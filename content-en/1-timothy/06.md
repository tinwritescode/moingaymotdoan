# 1 Timothy 6 — Contentment, the Snare of Wealth, and a Final Charge

## Story

Paul opens the final chapter addressing believers living under Roman slavery. Let all under the yoke of slavery regard their masters as worthy of full respect, so God's name and Christian teaching will never be maligned by outsiders. Those with believing masters must not show disrespect simply because they share brotherhood in Christ; instead let them serve even better, since those benefiting from their labor are beloved brothers in the faith (6:1–2).

Paul returns to unmasking the false teachers troubling Ephesus. Anyone teaching different doctrine who rejects the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and teaching aligned with godliness is puffed up with conceit and understands nothing. Obsessed with controversies and verbal hair-splitting, such people stir up envy, strife, slander, malicious suspicions, and endless friction among minds corrupted and stripped of truth—driven at bottom by treating godliness as a business for financial gain (6:3–5).

Paul turns their slogan inside out: godliness *paired with contentment* truly is great gain! For we brought nothing into this world, and we certainly cannot carry anything out of it. Therefore, if we have food and clothing, with these we shall be content (6:6–8). By contrast, those eager to get rich plunge into temptation, a snare, and countless foolish, harmful desires that sink people into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil; craving it, some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs (6:9–10).

In sharp contrast, Paul hails Timothy by a solemn Old Testament title: "man of God." Flee from all those traps, and pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, and gentleness. Fight the good fight of the faith; take hold of the eternal life to which you were called when you made the good confession before many witnesses (6:11–12).

Before God, who gives life to all things, and before Christ Jesus, who bore witness to the good confession before Pontius Pilate, Paul solemnly charges Timothy to keep the commandment unstained and free from reproach until the appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ. That appearing will be displayed at the appointed time by the blessed and only Sovereign—King of kings and Lord of lords, who alone possesses immortality, dwelling in unapproachable light whom no one has ever seen or can see. Paul seals this majestic tribute with an "Amen," ascribing honor and eternal dominion to Him (6:13–16).

Turning once more to finances, Paul instructs Timothy to charge those wealthy in this present age not to grow arrogant or fix their hope on uncertain riches, but to set their hope on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy. Urge them to do good, to grow rich in good works, and to be generous and ready to share—storing up a solid foundation for the future so they may take hold of what is truly life (6:17–19).

The closing appeal is crisp and urgent: "O Timothy, guard the deposit entrusted to you!" Turn away from irreverent empty chatter and the contradictions of what is falsely called "knowledge," by which some have swerved off course from the faith. Paul signs off wishing grace upon *you all*—plural in Greek, showing the letter was meant to be read aloud to the whole Ephesian church (6:20–21).

## Key Takeaways

**Contentment turns godliness into priceless treasure, while greed springs a deadly trap.** Paul never claims money itself is evil, only that "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (6:10). Once hearts lock onto getting rich at any price, people bend religion itself into a profit scheme and trade away their faith (6:5–10).

**Following Christ demands both running away from evil and chasing after good.** Three energetic verbs pack verses 11–12 into a rhythm for Christian ministry: *flee* greed, *pursue* godly character, and *fight* the good fight of faith holding fast to eternal life (6:11–12).

**Wealth is a tool for generosity, never a foundation for the soul.** Paul neither orders wealthy believers to feel ashamed nor demands total destitution; he calls them to two clear postures—trusting the living God instead of shifting assets, and growing rich in good deeds through eager generosity (6:17–19).

**Guarding the treasure of the gospel stands as every leader's highest duty.** Verse 20 distills the entire letter into one imperative: guard what has been entrusted to you. Amid endless trends claiming esoteric "knowledge," the Church's lifeline is protecting the simple, whole gospel intact (6:20–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**Perspective on possessions in 6:7 echoes Job and Ecclesiastes.** Stripped of children and fortune in a single day, Job confessed, "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return" (Job 1:21), while Ecclesiastes observes that a rich person leaves this world carrying nothing in his hand (Ecclesiastes 5:15). Remembering life's brevity cures the fever of greed.

**The title "man of God" (6:11) joins Timothy to Israel's great prophets.** That honored phrase marked Moses (Deuteronomy 33:1), Samuel (1 Samuel 9:6), Elijah, and Elisha (1 Kings 17:18). Calling his young protégé "man of God" places gospel ministry directly inside the noble lineage of God's ancient spokesmen.

**Storing up future treasure links straight to Jesus' Sermon on the Mount.** Urging the wealthy to lay up "a good foundation for the future" through open-handed giving (6:18–19) echoes Jesus' call to store treasures in heaven—safe from moth and thief—instead of anchoring hearts in fading earthly goods (Matthew 6:19–21; Luke 12:33).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Paul not summon slaves to revolt against slavery (6:1–2)?** In first-century Rome, enslaved people made up a huge share of the population, and uprisings ended in mass crucifixion. Writing to a tiny, politically powerless church, Paul never endorses slavery—having just condemned slave traders at the letter's start (1:10) and elsewhere advising slaves to seize freedom whenever available (1 Corinthians 7:21). Instead he plants a quiet seed transforming unequal structures from within: calling both master and slave beloved brothers inside one family of faith (cf. Philemon 1:16).

**Distinguish money itself from the love of money (6:10).** Verse 6:10 is frequently misquoted as "money is the root of all evil." In fact Paul specifies "*the love* of money," and most translators note "all *kinds* of evil"—meaning craving wealth opens the door to countless distinct sins, without claiming every sin on earth stems from cash.

**What was "what is falsely called knowledge" (6:20)?** In Greek, "knowledge" is *gnosis*. Earlier scholars sometimes linked this directly to second-century Gnosticism, whereas most modern scholars see earlier proto-Gnostic streams in Ephesus blending Jewish speculation with local mysticism—boasting secret insights loftier than the plain apostolic gospel.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople, distilled 6:6–8 into a memorable maxim: the richest person alive is not the one owning the most, but the one needing the least.
