# 1 Timothy 5 — Family Care, Widows, and Elders in the Church

## Story

Chapter 5 offers deeply practical guidance on how Timothy should relate to every age group in the congregation, treating the church like a close-knit extended family. Never rebuke an older man harshly; instead appeal to him as a father. Treat younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, and younger women as sisters, with complete purity (5:1–2).

Paul next devotes careful attention to supporting widows—among the most vulnerable people in the ancient world, lacking pensions or social safety nets. The church must honor and support widows who are genuinely left alone. Whenever a widow has children or grandchildren, those family members must first learn to practice godliness at home by repaying their parents, for that pleases God. A widow truly left on her own sets her hope on God and continues in prayer and supplication night and day, whereas one who lives solely for pleasure is dead even while alive (5:3–7). Paul speaks with startling bluntness about family duty: anyone who fails to provide for relatives—especially members of his own household—has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever (5:8).

To be enrolled on the official list of widows receiving ongoing church support—likely paired with dedicated prayer and service—a woman must be at least sixty years old, faithful as the wife of one husband, and well known for good deeds such as raising children, welcoming strangers, washing the feet of the saints, and helping the afflicted (5:9–10). Younger widows should not be placed on that list; when strong desires pull them away from their pledge to Christ, they will wish to remarry and incur blame for breaking their earlier vow. Besides, idle hours invite going from house to house as gossips and busybodies saying things they should not. Paul therefore advises younger widows to remarry, bear children, and manage their homes so adversaries have no opening for slander—noting some have already strayed after Satan. He restates the core rule: any believer who has widows in the family must care for them personally so the church is not overburdened and stays free to help widows truly alone (5:11–16).

Turning to church elders, Paul instructs that elders who direct the affairs of the church well deserve double honor—especially those laboring hard in preaching and teaching. He quotes two Scriptures grounding fair financial support for them: "You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain," and "The worker deserves his wages" (5:17–18; Deuteronomy 25:4; Luke 10:7).

Yet honor never means covering up misconduct. Paul lays out a fair process whenever an elder faces accusation: entertain no charge against an elder unless supported by two or three witnesses. Conversely, those persisting in sin must be rebuked publicly before all so the rest may stand in healthy fear (5:19–20). In the presence of God, Christ Jesus, and the elect angels, Paul solemnly charges Timothy to apply these rules without prejudice and without favoritism (5:21).

The chapter ends with seasoned pastoral reminders. Never lay hands hastily on anyone in ordination lest you share in another's sins; keep yourself pure (5:22). Right amid solemn church governance, Paul slips in a tender, fatherly health tip: stop drinking only water, and take a little wine for the sake of your stomach and frequent ailments (5:23). Returning to evaluating leaders, he notes that some people's sins stand obvious ahead of judgment while others trail behind; good deeds likewise shine clearly right away, and even quiet ones cannot stay hidden forever (5:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**The Church functions as a family where every generation meets respect and purity.** Appealing to older members like parents and peers like siblings shows that church leadership never operates as cold top-down command, but warm household affection anchored in moral integrity (5:1–2).

**Church compassion walks alongside family duty and wise stewardship.** Becoming a Christian never cancels children's duty to support aging parents; abandoning family betrays the gospel itself. Distinguishing widows backed by family from those genuinely alone directs church funds where help is needed most (5:3–16).

**Congregations owe leaders both generous support and impartial accountability.** "Double honor" combines heartfelt respect with fair financial provision for those laboring in teaching. Simultaneously, requiring two or three witnesses shields elders from reckless rumors, while public rebuke prevents churches from sweeping persistent sin under the rug (5:17–21).

**Appointing leaders calls for patience so time can test character.** Both hidden sins and quiet virtues eventually come into view. Leaders must never rush ordination on fleeting first impressions (5:22; 5:24–25).

## Deeper Meaning

**Caring for widows measures justice across both Testaments.** In Mosaic law, God names Himself defender of the fatherless and the widow (Deuteronomy 10:18), and prophets repeatedly indict Israel whenever widows go neglected (Isaiah 1:17). From Jerusalem's opening days, the early Church organized daily food distribution for widows (Acts 6:1–6), and James calls caring for orphans and widows pure, undefiled religion (James 1:27).

**Paul cites Jesus' words alongside the Old Testament under one title: "Scripture."** Introducing 5:18 with "For the Scripture says," Paul places Deuteronomy 25:4 side by side with Jesus' saying "The worker deserves his wages" from Luke 10:7. This offers one of the earliest testimonies that Jesus' teachings already carried full scriptural authority in the early Church.

**Two or three witnesses comes straight from Mosaic courtroom fairness.** Deuteronomy 19:15 barred convicting anyone on a lone witness's claim—a rule Jesus likewise applied to church discipline (Matthew 18:16). Carrying it into 1 Timothy 5:19 guards pastors against rumor-driven character assassination.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does advising Timothy to "take a little wine" (5:23) clash with warnings against drunkenness?** Not at all. Ancient water supplies were frequently contaminated and triggered stomach illness; mixing a splash of wine into water helped purify it and settle digestion. Paul prescribes modest medicinal use ("for the sake of your stomach") rather than endorsing drunkenness, which he just forbade in 3:3 and 3:8. The aside also hints that Timothy likely practiced strict abstinence—perhaps to stay above reproach or influenced by ascetic trends—and shows that even an apostle recommended ordinary medicine rather than expecting instant miraculous cures every time.

**What was the official "list of widows" (5:9)?** Scholars recognize more than an informal charity roster: this appears to be an enrolled order of older widows lacking relatives, fully supported by the church while committing their remaining years to dedicated prayer and ministry—explaining verse 12's reference to a "former pledge." That is why Paul discourages younger widows from taking a lifelong pledge and encourages them instead to remarry and build new families (5:14).

**What does "double honor" mean in 5:17?** The Greek noun *timē* encompasses both respect and honorarium/payment, just as in caring for widows at the chapter's start (5:3). Because verse 18 immediately quotes two texts about compensating workers, Paul clearly encompasses both genuine esteem and fair financial livelihood for elders devoting themselves to teaching.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople, posed an illuminating question on 5:23: why did Paul—an apostle who worked remarkable healings—prescribe a little wine instead of curing his beloved son's stomach instantaneously? God sometimes leaves lingering physical weakness in faithful servants, Chrysostom answered, to keep them humble and remind listeners that even eminent leaders share ordinary human frailty. *(Homilies on 1 Timothy, homily 16; Homilies on the Statues, homily 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom famously expounded 1 Timothy 5:23 at length in Homily 1 on the Statues as well as his 1 Timothy series -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, drew from 5:17 the classic Reformed model of two kinds of elders: ruling elders overseeing church governance and discipline, and teaching elders sharing governance while laboring primarily in preaching and teaching God's word. On 5:22 he warned churches against careless ordination, noting that rushing to lay hands implicates ordainers in future abuses. *(Commentaries on the Epistles to Timothy, on 1 Timothy chapter 5 verses 17 and 22)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's reading of 1 Timothy 5:17 distinguishing ruling and teaching elders laid the foundation for Presbyterian church polity -->

**John Stott**, twentieth-century Anglican pastor and expositor, marveled at Paul's down-to-earth realism across chapter 5: the Church is never an abstract theory club, but a living community making sure lonely widows eat, preachers receive honest pay, and discipline operates without bias—highlighting 5:18 as early testimony to the authority of Luke's Gospel. *(The Message of 1 Timothy and Titus, BST, 1996, on 1 Timothy chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: emphasizing pastoral practicality in chapter 5 and noting the Luke 10:7 citation in 5:18 are clear highlights of Stott's BST -->
