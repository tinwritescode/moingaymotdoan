# Malachi 4 — The Sun of Righteousness and the Promise of Elijah

## Story

In just six compact yet thunderous verses, chapter 4 closes not only the book of Malachi, but the entire Old Testament in traditional Christian canon order. The thought flows seamlessly from the closing promise of chapter 3 that people will clearly tell the righteous apart from the wicked (3:18). Right in the opening verse, the fiery day of judgment blazes into view: "For behold, the day is coming, burning like a furnace, when all the arrogant and every evildoer will be stubble; the day is coming when I will set them ablaze, says the LORD of Hosts. Not a root or branch will be left to them" (4:1). The arrogant wrongdoers whom skeptics had just called "blessed" and thriving in the previous chapter (3:15) vanish without a trace before God's just reckoning.

Yet that exact same blazing day breaks as a radiant, life-giving dawn for the faithful: "But for you who fear My name, the sun of righteousness will rise with healing in its wings, and you will go out and leap like calves from the stall" (4:2). Young calves bursting out of dark winter stalls to frisk across bright morning pasture capture sheer joy, freedom, and complete healing after long seasons of pressure. Meanwhile, wicked powers that once strutted over them are humbled completely: "Then you will trample the wicked, for they will be ashes under the soles of your feet on the day I am preparing" (4:3).

The final three verses (4:4–6) ring like a solemn last testament summarizing the entire Old Testament revelation, gathering its twin pillars—the Law and the Prophets—through their two towering figures, Moses and Elijah. First, God directs His people back to the foundation already laid: "Remember the law of My servant Moses, the statutes and ordinances I commanded him for all Israel at Horeb" (4:4). Next, He lifts their eyes forward with a mysterious pledge: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and awesome Day of the LORD" (4:5). That messenger's mission is deep reconciliation across generations: "And he will turn the hearts of the fathers to their children, and the hearts of the children to their fathers. Otherwise, I will come and strike the land with a curse" (4:6). And right on that expectant threshold, the Old Testament prophetic voice falls quiet—opening four hundred years of waiting until John the Baptist steps into the Judean wilderness and the Messiah's dawn breaks.

## Key Takeaways

**The very same Day of the LORD brings two opposite outcomes depending on the human heart.** Its heat incinerates proud evildoers like dry stubble in a furnace (4:1), yet rises as "the sun of righteousness" radiating healing warmth and liberty for all who revere God's name (4:2–3).

**While waiting for promises to unfold, staying faithful to the Word God has already given is our surest anchor.** Amid doubt and long stretches without dramatic signs, God never tells His people to chase wild speculation; He simply commands, "Remember the law of My servant Moses" (4:4). Obeying the light God has already revealed is always the best readiness for His coming day.

**True spiritual renewal always heals fractures inside families and across generations.** Remarkably, the hallmark of readiness for "the great and awesome Day of the LORD" is not political revolution, but turning "the hearts of the fathers to their children, and the hearts of the children to their fathers" (4:5–6). After Malachi indicted broken marriages and family betrayal back in chapter 2, renewal at the book's end returns home to mend households and restore the handing down of faith from parents to children.

**The Old Testament does not end with a closed door, but with an open promise leaning forward.** Verses 4:5–6 leave Israel's whole story leaning expectantly ahead: Moses delivered the law and the prophets spoke, yet redemption still awaits the forerunner messenger and the rising of "the sun of righteousness" over humanity.

## Deeper Meaning

**"The sun of righteousness" (4:2) rises with the birth of Jesus Christ.** Earlier in the Old Testament, God had been praised as "a sun and a shield" (Psalms 84:11). Four centuries after Malachi, right when his son John the Baptist was born, the priest Zechariah, filled with the Holy Spirit, burst into song directly echoing Malachi 4:2: "because of the tender mercy of our God, by which the Dawn will visit us from on high" (Luke 1:78). That healing light from heaven is the Messiah arriving to shine on those sitting in darkness and the shadow of death.

**The New Testament confirms that the promise of "Elijah the prophet" (4:5–6) came true in John the Baptist.** In the Old Testament, Elijah had been carried straight up to heaven in a whirlwind with chariots and horses of fire rather than dying an ordinary death (2 Kings 2:11), fueling Jewish expectation that he would return to herald the messianic age. Opening Luke's Gospel, the angel Gabriel quotes 4:6 directly to announce John's mission: "And he will go on before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to their children and the disobedient to the wisdom of the righteous—to make ready a people prepared for the Lord" (Luke 1:17). Jesus Himself twice affirmed plainly that John was the "Elijah" who had come in that spirit and power (Matthew 11:14; Matthew 17:10–13; Mark 9:11–13). And with breathtaking symmetry, the very two men closing the Old Testament in Malachi 4:4–5—Moses and Elijah—were the sole two figures who appeared speaking with Jesus on the Mount of Transfiguration!

**From "a curse" closing the Old Testament to "grace" closing the New.** Placing the Old Testament's closing line beside the New Testament's finale is deeply moving. The Old Testament stops in solemn suspense on the word "curse" (4:6), reminding us that law alone cannot rescue humanity from sin without repentance and a Savior coming to bear that curse for us. The New Testament answers from the far side of the cross in overflowing hope and love: "Amen. Come, Lord Jesus! The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all" (Revelation 22:20–21).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter and verse numbering differences between the Hebrew Bible and English translations.** In the original Hebrew Bible, these six verses do not stand as a separate chapter 4; they continue straight at the close of chapter 3 as verses 19–24, meaning Hebrew Malachi has only three chapters. The separate chapter 4 used in English Bibles (including the BSB) derives from the ancient Greek Septuagint and Latin Vulgate. Knowing this small detail helps readers see at once that 4:1 flows straight out of 3:18 and avoids confusion when commentaries cite Hebrew numbering.

**Why does the Old Testament end on "a curse" (4:6), and how does Jewish tradition handle it?** Ending a book with "Otherwise, I will come and strike the land with a curse" (4:6) feels startlingly abrupt. In synagogue liturgy across the centuries, readers never finish on 4:6: right after reading 4:6, they always repeat 4:5 aloud ("Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and awesome Day of the LORD") so the public reading closes on a promise rather than a curse. Note also that in the traditional Hebrew Bible (*Tanakh*) canon order, the final book is not Malachi, but 2 Chronicles (ending on King Cyrus's decree inviting exiles home to rebuild Jerusalem). The Christian church placed Malachi at the close of the Old Testament so the promise of Elijah in 4:5–6 bridges straight into John the Baptist opening Matthew's Gospel.

**Understanding "with healing in its wings" (4:2) and "trample the wicked" (4:3).** Speaking of the sun's "wings" (4:2) borrows a familiar ancient Near Eastern poetic metaphor for dawn's sunbeams fanning out across the horizon like protective bird wings bringing warmth and healing after cold night; it has nothing to do with pagan sun worship, instead exalting the LORD Himself as the source of justice and peace. Similarly, 4:3's picture of the righteous trampling the wicked like ashes underfoot is never a call for believers to practice vengeance or violence today, but apocalyptic poetry assuring the complete, permanent triumph of righteousness once God's judgment concludes.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, identifies "the sun of righteousness" rising with healing in its wings (4:2) as Jesus Christ—illuminating all who fear God and healing humanity's wounds of sin. On the promise to send Elijah in 4:5–6, Jerome notes the dual fulfillment familiar across early church fathers: fulfilled clearly first in John the Baptist coming in Elijah's spirit and power ahead of Christ's first coming, while many early fathers also awaited a concluding fulfillment prior to Christ's second coming. *(Commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Malachi chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Jerome reading the sun of righteousness in chapter 4 verse 2 of Christ and noting both John the Baptist and second-coming expectations for Elijah in verses 5–6; original Latin not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, points to the deep pastoral wisdom in the twin charge closing the Old Testament (4:4–6). Because God's people were entering several quiet centuries with no new prophets, God deliberately instructed them to "remember the law of My servant Moses" (4:4) so they would not stray into human inventions, while staying alert for restoration when the new "Elijah," John the Baptist, came calling them to repentance. For Calvin, setting reconciliation between parents and children (4:6) as the hallmark of Elijah's work shows that the true gospel always restores unity and love across the covenant community. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Malachi chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's comments on Moses's law preserving faith during the waiting era in chapter 4 verse 4 and John the Baptist fulfilling Elijah's role in verses 5–6; original lecture text not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses with delight over "calves from the stall" leaping into the light of "the sun of righteousness" (4:2). Believers in this world often feel shut inside cramped, dim, weary stalls, he writes, yet when Christ shines His healing grace into their hearts, they step out into bright freedom and youthful joy. He also observes how Moses and Elijah (4:4–5) stand side by side on the Old Testament's closing page to keep both sides together: reverence for God's righteous law and longing for the gospel's renewing grace. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Malachi chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's warm reflections on chapter 4 verse 2 and the pairing of Moses and Elijah in verses 4–5; original text on ccel.org not checked -->

**Joyce G. Baldwin**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, stresses that the final three verses (4:4–6) do far more than wrap up Malachi—they function as a formal epilogue to the entire Old Testament corpus of Law and Prophets.
