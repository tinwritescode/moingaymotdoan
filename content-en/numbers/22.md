# Numbers 22 — Balak Hires Balaam and the Talking Donkey

## Story

Israel marches on to encamp on the plains of Moab across the Jordan River, right opposite Jericho. Having watched Israel crush two Amorite kings, King Balak of Moab and all his people fall into deep dread. They warn the elders of Midian that this vast multitude will lick up everything around them like an ox licking up the grass of the field (22:1–4).

Afraid to fight head-on, Balak reaches for spiritual weapons. He sends elders of Moab and Midian carrying divination fees all the way north to Pethor near the Euphrates River to summon a celebrated diviner named Balaam to curse Israel. Balak believes that whomever Balaam blesses is blessed, and whomever he curses is cursed. Balaam asks the envoys to stay overnight while he consults the LORD. That night God gives an unmistakable answer: "You must not go with them. You must not curse this people, for they are blessed." Next morning, Balaam turns them down and sends the envoys home (22:5–14).

Balak refuses to give up. He dispatches a larger, more prestigious delegation promising lavish honor and anything Balaam asks if only he will come curse Israel. Balaam responds with impressive piety: even if Balak gave him a palace full of silver and gold, he could never go beyond the command of the LORD. Yet instead of sending them straight home in light of God's clear word, he invites them to stay another night to see whether the LORD might say anything else. That night God grants permission to go with them, warning strictly that he may do only what God tells him (22:15–20).

Early the next morning, Balaam eagerly saddles his donkey and rides off with the Moabite officials. Yet God's anger burns against the greed driving his journey. The angel of the LORD takes his stand right in the road with a drawn sword in hand. Balaam—the famed seer who claims spiritual vision—sees nothing at all, while his female donkey sees clearly. First she turns off the road into a field, and Balaam beats her back onto the path. Second, in a narrow lane between two vineyard walls, she presses close against the wall crushing Balaam's foot, and he beats her again. Third, in a tight spot with no room to turn right or left, she simply lies down beneath him, and Balaam flies into a rage beating her with his staff (22:21–27).

Then the LORD opens the donkey's mouth, and she speaks to Balaam: "What have I done to you that you have beaten me these three times?" So blinded by fury that he does not even blink at a talking donkey, Balaam snarls back that she has made a fool of him—if he had a sword in hand, he would kill her on the spot. Calmly the donkey reminds him she has faithfully carried him all his life and never behaved like this before (22:28–30).

At that instant, the LORD opens Balaam's eyes. Stunned, he sees the angel of the LORD standing straight ahead with drawn sword and falls facedown to the ground. The angel rebukes him for beating his donkey three times unjustly and tells him she actually saved his life: had she not turned aside, the angel would have killed Balaam and spared her. Balaam confesses his sin and offers to turn back if his journey displeases God. The angel tells him to continue with the men, speaking only the exact words God gives him (22:31–35).

Balak rides all the way to the Arnon border to welcome Balaam royally, mildly reproaching him for hesitating. Balaam repeats that he can utter only what God puts in his mouth. Balak prepares a feast, and the next morning leads Balaam up to the heights of Bamoth-baal overlooking the edge of Israel's camp below (22:36–41).

## Key Takeaways

**God quietly shields his people from plots they know nothing about.** Encamped peacefully down on the plains of Moab, Israel had no clue a conspiracy to curse them was brewing on the hills overhead. The LORD himself stepped in to dismantle the curse before it ever took breath (22:12; 22:22).

**Greed loves to disguise itself in pious speech.** Balaam's lips sounded noble: "Even if Balak gave me his palace full of silver and gold, I could not go beyond the command of the LORD" (22:18). Yet inviting the envoys to spend a second night after God had already said a clear *no* (22:12; 22:19) exposed a heart still aching for the hefty paycheck and hoping God would change his mind.

**Anger and greed can blind human beings worse than animals.** The episode's biting irony flips every role upside down: an internationally famous seer misses an angel blocking the road right in front of him and threatens to kill his own rescuer, while an ordinary donkey sees reality clearly and saves her master three times running (22:23–33).

**God holds the reins even over half-hearted instruments.** Although Balaam set out with corrupt motives, God kept firm grip on the bridle: permitting him to go while locking his mouth so not one syllable escapes outside God's sovereign will (22:35; 22:38).

## Deeper Meaning

**The New Testament holds up Balaam as a warning against ministry driven by greed.** The apostle Peter invokes the talking donkey directly to expose false teachers: they wandered off into the way of Balaam, "who loved the wages of wickedness, yet was rebuked for his transgression by a mute donkey speaking with a human voice, restraining the prophet's madness" (2 Peter 2:15–16). Jude likewise ranks Balaam's greed among classic destructive errors (Jude 1:11).

**God's power shatters external curses, so enemies try internal compromise.** Revelation recalls Balaam's legacy when warning the church at Pergamum against teachers promoting sexual immorality and idolatry—the sinister scheme Balaam later fed Balak once cursing Israel failed (Revelation 2:14; cf. Numbers 31:16). Across Scripture, when darkness cannot destroy God's people from outside, it strives to lure them into stumbling from within.

**Only the God who opens eyes grants true spiritual sight.** Humans remain blind to spiritual reality unless God opens their eyes (22:31; cf. 2 Kings 6:17). In the New Testament, only when Jesus opened the eyes of the two disciples on the road to Emmaus did they recognize him walking right beside them (Luke 24:31).

## Notes & Nuances

**How should we understand the talking donkey?** This is one of the Old Testament's most discussed moments, approached in two main ways. The classic reading—anchored in the plain text and New Testament citation—takes it as a genuine historical miracle: the text explicitly specifies "the LORD opened the donkey's mouth" (22:28) rather than claiming natural animal speech, and Peter affirms "a mute donkey spoke with a human voice" (2 Peter 2:16). For the Creator who fashioned human speech itself (Exodus 4:11), causing a beast to rebuke an arrogant seer lies effortlessly within his power and carries razor-sharp satire: a man paid to manipulate reality with words gets schooled by his own donkey. Some modern scholars instead read the scene as literary satire or an inward visionary experience of Balaam, noting that neither Balaam nor his two servants react with shock. Whichever approach one takes, the narrator's core message stands unanimous: however brilliant humans think themselves, greed renders them blinder than the animal beneath their feet.

**Why did God permit Balaam to go in verse 20, yet grow angry when he went in verse 22?** Careful reading unlocks the tension. First, God stated his clear preference: *do not go* (22:12). When Balaam stalled out of craving for gold (22:19), God granted conditional permission—letting Balaam's covetous heart reveal itself. Once Balaam rode eagerly off chasing "the wages of wickedness" (2 Peter 2:15), God's wrath confronted his inward motive head-on, intercepting him on the road to warn him he was playing with fire (22:20; 22:22).

**Was Balaam a true prophet of the LORD or a pagan diviner?** Scripture depicts Balaam as a complex figure. Non-Israelite by birth, Joshua 13:22 calls him straight out a *diviner*, and Balak's envoys brought fees for divination (22:7). Because the LORD's fame had spread across the Near East since the Exodus, Balaam knew the LORD's name and sought him as a potent deity. God temporarily used him as a mouthpiece to bless Israel, yet in the end Balaam sided with Israel's enemies to trap God's people and fell by the sword fighting alongside Midian (Numbers 31:8; 31:16).

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, notes wryly how Balaam answers his talking donkey without a flicker of astonishment: blind rage strips humans even of basic reason. **Joseph Butler**, eighteenth-century English bishop and philosopher, delivered one of history's most celebrated sermons using Balaam as the definitive portrait of *self-deception*. Balaam is no blatant atheist mocking God; rather, he tries to preserve spotless outward compliance while hunting every loophole to collect what his greed craves—a moral sickness common to every era. *(Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel, Sermon 7: Upon the Character of Balaam)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Sermon 7 Upon the Character of Balaam on self-deception in Fifteen Sermons is Bishop Joseph Butler's famous classic work -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, highlights the sparkling satirical artistry of the donkey episode: hired to topple an entire nation with his words, Balaam cannot even steer his own donkey down a path.
