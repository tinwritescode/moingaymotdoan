# Numbers 35 — Levitical Towns and the Six Cities of Refuge

## Story

Because the tribe of Levi receives no unified territory of its own, the LORD directs the other tribes to set aside forty-eight towns scattered throughout the land from their own inheritances, each ringed by generous pasturelands for Levite livestock. Tribes with larger allotments contribute more towns and smaller tribes fewer, keeping the share equitable (35:1–8).

Out of those forty-eight Levitical towns, God designates six special *cities of refuge*—three east of the Jordan and three west in Canaan. All six stand equally open to native Israelites and resident foreigners alike, giving anyone who accidentally kills another person a safe haven from *the avenger of blood* (the victim's nearest kinsman under ancient custom) until the assembly gives the accused a fair public trial (35:6; 35:9–15).

Next, the law draws a crisp line between intentional murder and accidental manslaughter. Anyone who strikes another fatally with an iron weapon, deadly stone, or wooden club, or shoves, hurls an object, or strikes someone out of malice and hostility, is a murderer and must face execution under the law (35:16–21). Conversely, if there was no prior hatred or malice—an accidental shove or dropping a heavy stone without seeing anyone below—the assembly must rescue the offender from the avenger of blood and return him safely inside his city of refuge (35:22–25).

The accidental manslayer must stay inside the borders of the city of refuge until the serving high priest dies. If he wanders outside the city limits before then and the avenger of blood kills him, the avenger incurs no bloodguilt. Once the high priest passes away, however, the person is completely free to return home to his family land (35:25–28).

The chapter seals four bedrock judicial safeguards: never execute anyone on the testimony of a single witness; never accept ransom money to spare a deliberate murderer's life; never accept ransom money to release a fugitive from a city of refuge early; because innocent bloodshed pollutes the very land in which the LORD himself dwells among his people (35:29–34).

## Key Takeaways

**God's servants were placed right inside everyday community life.** Rather than isolating Levites in a remote enclave, God scattered their forty-eight towns evenly among all twelve tribes (35:2–8). Thus God's instruction and spiritual oversight stayed woven right into daily life in every district.

**God's justice weaves firmness and mercy seamlessly together.** Cities of refuge simultaneously checked reckless blood feuds by sheltering accidental offenders (35:11–12; 35:22–25) while standing immovable against premeditated murder, refusing any bribe or payoff (35:31).

**Equal justice and solid evidence guard against wrongful verdicts.** The six refuge cities sheltered foreigners on identical terms with citizens (35:15). Requiring at least two corroborating witnesses for any capital conviction (35:30) raised a vital shield against false accusations and hasty verdicts.

**Human life is sacred because God dwells among his people.** The closing lines strike the deepest foundation of every pro-life law: unpunished bloodshed defiles the land because "I, the LORD, dwell among the Israelites" (35:33–34). Wherever God resides, human lives can never be bought off or held cheap.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus is the believer's eternal City of Refuge.** Hebrews draws straight on the Old Testament cities of refuge to describe our hope in Christ: we "who have fled to take hold of the hope set before us" find unshakable safety in him (Hebrews 6:18). Just as six refuge cities stood evenly accessible across both banks of the Jordan, Christ's salvation stands wide open within reach of everyone regardless of background.

**The death of our High Priest brings complete freedom.** Most striking of all, the accidental offender walked free from threat only upon the death of the anointed high priest (35:25; 35:28). That points unmistakably to Jesus, our eternal High Priest: his death on the cross settles our debt completely and sets us free from confinement to return home to our Father's inheritance (Hebrews 9:11–15).

**God's righteousness cannot be bought off with silver or gold.** Forbidding ransom payments for life (35:31–32) underlines that wealth and status never buy pardon for guilt. Peter echoes that exact truth in the New Testament: we were redeemed not with perishable things like silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ (1 Peter 1:18–19).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Mosaic law retain the role of *the avenger of blood*?** In ancient society without professional police departments or public prosecutors, tracking down killers fell to the victim's nearest kinsman (*go'el*—the exact same term used for the kinsman-redeemer in Ruth). Rather than abolishing that social mechanism overnight, Mosaic law harnessed it strictly under due process: once an accused person reached a city of refuge, the avenger could never act on his own, waiting for a public assembly trial backed by at least two witnesses (35:12; 35:24; 35:30).

**Reconciling the pastureland dimensions in verses 4 and 5.** Verse 4 measures *one thousand cubits* outward from the city wall, while verse 5 lists *two thousand cubits* along each outer side—east, south, west, and north (35:4–5). Commentators solve this simply: extending 1,000 cubits straight outward from each side of a central town creates an outer perimeter box measuring 2,000 cubits across each span, forming a neat greenbelt ample for Levite flocks.

**A careful translation note on verse 32 (35:32).** Some older translations read awkwardly as if barring ransom *after* the high priest's death, yet the Hebrew text and modern versions clarify it forbids taking ransom to let a fugitive return home *before* the high priest dies—for once the high priest dies, he already returns home freely without paying anything (35:28).
