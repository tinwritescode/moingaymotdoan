# Numbers 28 — Daily Sacrifices and Spring Festival Offerings

## Story

Before the new generation steps into the Promised Land, the LORD tells Moses to review the entire public sacrificial calendar for the nation so steady worship never falters. Every burnt offering comes paired with a grain offering of fine flour mixed with olive oil and a drink offering of wine—all ascending as "a food offering, a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (28:1–2; 28:5–7).

The anchor of the entire worship schedule is the daily offering: two unblemished year-old male lambs every single day—one offered in the morning and one at twilight. Established at Mount Sinai, this daily rhythm must continue unbroken every day of the year (28:3–8).

From there, the calendar widens across concentric cycles of time. Every Sabbath adds two extra male lambs atop the regular daily offerings (28:9–10). On the first day of every month (the New Moon), offerings expand significantly: two young bulls, one ram, and seven male lambs for burnt offerings, plus one male goat for a sin offering (28:11–15).

Moving into the major spring festivals, Passover falls on the fourteenth day of the first month, followed by seven days of eating unleavened bread beginning on the fifteenth. The first and seventh days are sacred assemblies with rest from ordinary work; throughout all seven days, priests present a major daily slate of bulls, a ram, seven lambs, and a sin-offering goat, all in addition to the regular morning and evening lambs (28:16–25).

The chapter concludes with the Feast of Weeks—the Day of Firstfruits—celebrated seven weeks after Passover. Once again, the people hold a sacred assembly, cease ordinary labor, and offer the full set of burnt, grain, and drink offerings together with a male goat for atonement (28:26–31).

## Key Takeaways

**Worship is not an occasional mood, but a steady rhythm of life.** One lamb at dawn, one lamb at dusk, week by week and month by month: the sacrificial schedule placed God squarely at the center of every day and season, reminding Israel that all time belonged to him (28:3–15).

**Big holidays never replace quiet daily faithfulness.** One refrain echoes all down the chapter: every Sabbath, New Moon, and grand festival offering must be presented "in addition to the regular burnt offering" (28:10; 28:15; 28:23; 28:31). Grand seasonal worship never gave anyone license to skip humble morning and evening devotion.

**God always receives our unblemished best.** The words "without blemish" appear at every single entry across the schedule (28:3; 28:9; 28:11; 28:19; 28:31). Israel brought neither lame castoffs nor leftovers, honoring God with whole, unmarred gifts.

**Festive joy always walks hand in hand with humility over sin.** From New Moons through Unleavened Bread and the Feast of Weeks, right alongside joyful burnt offerings always stood one male goat for a sin offering (28:15; 28:22; 28:30). Even on life's happiest feast days, humanity needs forgiveness to stand before a holy God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Endless repeated offerings point toward one final, perfect sacrifice.** Hebrews gazes straight at this relentless daily and annual schedule to highlight its limitation: if the blood of bulls and goats could truly erase sin once for all, priests would have stopped sacrificing long ago. Every offering was a shadow heralding Christ, who offered his own body once for all to perfect forever those who are being made holy (Hebrews 10:1–14).

**Jesus fulfills all three grand emblems in this chapter.** Two spotless lambs offered morning and evening prepared Israel's ears for John the Baptist's cry: behold the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29). Paul explicitly declares Christ to be our Passover Lamb who has been sacrificed (1 Corinthians 5:7). And the Feast of Weeks—Pentecost—became the birthday of the church when the Holy Spirit poured out to reap the new covenant's firstfruits (Acts 2:1–4).

**New Covenant worship turns all of life into a daily offering.** With no bronze altar left standing in Jerusalem, Paul urges believers to present their own bodies as living sacrifices, holy and pleasing to God—our true, everyday spiritual worship (Romans 12:1).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why repeat festival laws already given in Leviticus 23?** Leviticus 23 addressed the old generation at Mount Sinai, stressing each festival's meaning along with rest and community gatherings. Numbers 28–29 addresses the rising generation nearly forty years later right before crossing into Canaan, supplying the detailed official inventory of public sacrifices priests must offer on the nation's behalf at every single appointment.

**Why does verse 2 call offerings "My food"?** Modern readers might mistake this as meaning God needs human meals like pagan idols. Scripture explicitly rejects that idea: God never hungers or eats bulls' flesh and drinks goats' blood (Psalms 50:12–13). Calling sacrifices *food* was familiar ancient covenant imagery picturing Israel presenting their finest gifts as a table of fellowship before their King (28:2).

**Wine drink offerings in verses 7 and 14 (28:7; 28:14).** Pouring wine beside the altar alongside burnt meat and oil-mixed flour brought together Canaan's classic trio of blessings: livestock, grain, and wine. Commanding olive oil and wine while Israel still stood in the desert offered a quiet pledge that they truly would enter the land and reap its harvests.
