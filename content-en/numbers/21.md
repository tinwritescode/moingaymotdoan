# Numbers 21 — The Bronze Serpent and the First Victories

## Story

When the Canaanite king of Arad in the Negev hears Israel advancing along the trail the spies once traveled, he launches an attack and carries off captives. Israel vows to the LORD that if he delivers this people into their hand, they will devote their cities to complete destruction. The LORD answers their prayer and grants complete victory; they name the place Hormah—the very spot where their parents' generation had suffered humiliating defeat thirty-eight years earlier (21:1–3; cf. 14:45).

Next, Israel departs Mount Hor heading back toward the Red Sea to circle around Edom. Growing worn out along the detour, the people lose heart and speak once more against God and Moses: why drag them out of Egypt to die in a wilderness with no bread and no water? Then they utter bitter contempt for heaven's manna: "We detest this worthless food!" (21:4–5).

This time the LORD sends fiery serpents—venomous snakes whose bite burns like fire—slithering through camp and biting the people so that many Israelites die. Shaken awake by disaster, the people rush to Moses confessing plainly that they have sinned by speaking against the LORD and against him, begging him to pray that God will take the snakes away. Moses intercedes for the people (21:6–7).

God does not immediately make the snakes vanish; instead he prescribes a startling cure: "Make a fiery serpent and mount it on a pole. When anyone who is bitten looks at it, he will live." Moses casts a bronze serpent and lifts it high on a pole in camp; whenever a snake bites anyone and that person looks up at the bronze serpent, they live (21:8–9).

From that moment on, the mood of the narrative lifts into brisk forward momentum. Israel marches through stage after stage along Moab's eastern frontier to the Arnon Valley, quoting an ancient ballad from *the Book of the Wars of the LORD* to trace their route (21:10–15). Reaching Beer, God proactively provides water without waiting for a single complaint, and Israel breaks into a joyful folk song celebrating the bubbling well (21:16–20).

Next, Israel sends envoys to Sihon, king of the Amorites, courteously asking safe passage along the highway through his realm just as they had asked Edom. Not only does Sihon refuse—he mobilizes his entire army and attacks Israel at Jahaz. Israel crushes King Sihon completely and captures all his territory from the Arnon River to the Jabbok River, including his royal capital Heshbon, which Sihon had previously seized from Moab (21:21–32).

Advancing north toward Bashan, Israel faces King Og marching out with his whole army at Edrei. The LORD reassures Moses: "Do not fear him, for I have delivered him into your hand, along with all his people and his land." Israel wins a total victory over King Og and possesses the rich plateau of Bashan east of the Jordan River (21:33–35).

## Key Takeaways

**Growing disgusted with God's daily provision exposes an ungrateful heart.** Manna was a miracle feeding a whole nation every single morning for nearly forty years, yet road fatigue pushed Israel to scoff at it as "worthless food" they detested (21:5). Over-familiarity with God's blessings quickly turns daily wonders into dull routine and breeds resentment toward the Giver.

**God's rescue starts with honest confession and a simple look of faith.** Unlike earlier crises filled only with self-pitying panic, this time Israel spoke straight: "We have sinned by speaking against the LORD and against you" (21:7). And God's healing required neither crafting an antidote nor fighting off snakes—simply one humble act of faith: lifting one's eyes to what God hoisted high (21:8–9).

**God does not always remove hardship overnight, yet opens a way of life right inside it.** The people begged God to remove the snakes (21:7), yet God lifted up the bronze serpent to heal those bitten (21:8–9). Sometimes amid painful reminders of our own sin, God leaves our frailty visible so we learn day by day to lift our gaze to him and live.

**The new generation is learning to trust God and tasting its first triumphs.** The second half of chapter 21 marks a stirring turnaround: grumbling gives way to singing around a desert well (21:17–18), followed by decisive victories over the kings of Arad, Sihon, and Og (21:1–3; 21:21–35). God is forging the new generation's confidence right before they cross the Jordan into Canaan.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus himself chooses the bronze serpent as a direct picture of his cross.** Speaking to Nicodemus by night, Jesus declares: "Just as Moses lifted up the snake in the wilderness, so the Son of Man must be lifted up, that everyone who believes in Him may have eternal life" (John 3:14–15). Just as a serpent—emblem of judgment and death—was hoisted on a pole to heal the dying, sinless Jesus bore the likeness of our curse upon the cross so anyone lifting eyes of faith to him is rescued from sin's lethal venom (2 Corinthians 5:21; Galatians 3:13).

**The bronze serpent also warns against turning relics of faith into idols.** Centuries later, Israel kept that bronze serpent, named it Nehushtan, and began burning incense to it. Godly King Hezekiah smashed it to pieces, dismissing it bluntly as just *a piece of bronze* (2 Kings 18:4). Healing power never resided in metal—only in the living God trusted by those who looked up.

**Victories over Sihon and Og became Israel's timeless songs of praise.** Those two mighty Transjordan kings echo again and again across the Psalms as living proof that God's steadfast love endures forever (Psalms 135:10–12; 136:17–22)—an opening overture proving no earthly force could block God's promise of the land.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did God command fashioning a bronze snake when the Second Commandment bans idols?** The Second Commandment (Exodus 20:4–5) forbids crafting images *to worship*, not every divinely commissioned symbol or artwork (such as the golden cherubim atop the Ark). The bronze serpent in Numbers 21 was never an object of worship, merely a temporary focal sign directing faith toward God's healing promise. When later generations turned it into an idol, King Hezekiah rightly destroyed it (2 Kings 18:4).

**What was *the Book of the Wars of the LORD* (21:14)?** It was an ancient Israelite anthology of historical war poems chronicling Israel's marches and triumphs under God's lead, much like *the Book of Jashar* quoted in Joshua 10:13 and 2 Samuel 1:18. Though no longer extant, citing it shows that the Pentateuch drew responsibly on trusted contemporary historical songs and records.

**What were the *fiery serpents* (21:6)?** The Hebrew word *seraphim* ("fiery ones") likely describes either the reddish color of desert vipers native to the Arabah and Gulf of Aqaba, or the searing, burning inflammation caused by their venom. Even if they were natural desert snakes, their sudden invasion of camp at the precise hour of Israel's revolt was unmistakably God's disciplinary intervention.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, celebrates a marvelous paradox of grace: the very image of what brought death (the serpent) became the emblem God used to restore life.
