# Numbers 31 — War Against the Midianites

## Story

The LORD gives Moses one final assignment before his death: execute the LORD's vengeance upon the Midianites for the Baal of Peor crisis (cf. 25:16–18). Moses calls up one thousand men from each tribe—twelve thousand armed warriors in all. Phinehas the priest accompanies the army carrying the sacred articles and silver trumpets to sound the battle alarm (31:1–6).

Israel engages and defeats Midian, killing every fighting man, including the five kings of Midian—Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba. The diviner Balaam—once hired by Moab's king to curse Israel—also falls by the sword in this campaign. Israel's troops burn the enemy's towns and encampments, then march back to the plains of Moab bringing captive women, children, livestock, and all the plunder (31:7–12).

Moses, Eleazar the priest, and the tribal leaders walk outside camp to greet the returning force. Seeing that they have spared all the Midianite women, Moses grows furious with the army officers. He points straight to the heart of the crisis: these very women followed Balaam's counsel to seduce Israel into worshiping Baal of Peor and unleashed the deadly plague on the assembly. He orders them to execute the young boys and every woman who has slept with a man, sparing only the young girls who have never been intimate with a man (31:13–18).

Afterward, the entire army must stay outside camp for seven days of ritual purification. Every soldier who killed anyone or touched a dead body must purify himself and his captives on the third and seventh days. Eleazar publishes cleansing rules for captured goods: fireproof metals—gold, silver, bronze, iron, tin, and lead—must pass through fire and then cleansing water, while non-fireproof items are washed in water. Only on the seventh day, after washing their garments thoroughly, may the soldiers re-enter camp (31:19–24).

The LORD directs Moses, Eleazar, and the clan chiefs to inventory the plunder—675,000 sheep, 72,000 cattle, 61,000 donkeys, and 32,000 young girls—and split everything into two equal halves: one half for the twelve thousand soldiers who fought, and one half for the congregation who stayed behind. From the soldiers' half, one part in five hundred goes as a tribute to Eleazar the priest; from the assembly's half, one part in fifty goes to the Levites caring for the Tabernacle (31:25–47).

The chapter concludes with a moving debriefing: the army commanders check their rosters of all twelve thousand soldiers and report to Moses that "not one of us is missing." Overwhelmed with gratitude for God's miraculous protection and seeking atonement before the LORD, they voluntarily bring all the captured gold jewelry—totaling 16,750 shekels—as a memorial offering in the Tent of Meeting (31:48–54).

## Key Takeaways

**Victory belongs to God, never overwhelming numbers.** Israel boasted over six hundred thousand fighting men (26:51), yet Moses dispatched a symbolic force of just twelve thousand—one thousand per tribe—marching behind Phinehas's sacred trumpets (31:4–6). Returning without losing a single soldier proved unmistakably that victory came from God's grace rather than human military might (31:48–50).

**The tragic end of knowing truth yet selling out for money.** Balaam once spoke soaring prophecies over Israel and prayed to die the death of the righteous (23:10). Yet in the end he masterminded the sordid trap at Baal of Peor to please Israel's enemies and died by the sword right inside Midian's camp (31:8; 31:16).

**Even a commanded battle carries the defiling shadow of death.** Seven strict days of cleansing outside camp reminded returning warriors that shedding human blood is never clean or glorious (31:19–24). Anyone stepping off a battlefield had to halt outside camp and be purified before approaching the living God's dwelling.

**Fair sharing unites front-line fighters and home-front supporters.** Plunder split equally between soldiers in battle and families guarding camp, with both sides setting aside a grateful share for priests and Levites (31:27–30). The community's mission belonged to everyone together; no one kept the reward solely for themselves.

## Deeper Meaning

**Revelation unveils Balaam's real crime.** Only here in verse 16 do readers discover that Balaam himself engineered the temptation of chapter 25 (31:16). The New Testament repeatedly invokes his name as a warning against greedy teachers who twist truth and lure believers into moral compromise (2 Peter 2:15; Jude 1:11; Revelation 2:14).

**Shared reward across the body of Christ.** Dividing spoils evenly between front-line troops and rear support (31:27) was later affirmed by King David as a lasting statute in Israel (1 Samuel 30:24–25). In the New Testament, Paul speaks of the church in that exact spirit: planter and waterer, front-line worker and quiet supporter labor together as one body sharing together in the Lord's work (1 Corinthians 3:8; 12:22–26).

**The true warfare of God's people under the New Covenant.** Old Testament narratives depict physical swords confronting forces pulling Israel away from God. Under the New Covenant, God reveals clearly that believers wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against spiritual powers of darkness and temptation—armed solely with Scripture, faith, and prayer (Ephesians 6:12–17; 2 Corinthians 10:3–4).

## Notes & Nuances

**Reading the difficult question of *holy war* in chapter 31 honestly and fairly.** This stands among the hardest chapters in Scripture for modern consciences, especially the order to execute captive boys and women in verses 15 through 18 (31:15–18). Rather than dodging the tension or offering glib excuses, readers benefit from weighing the main perspectives thoughtful scholars and theologians have offered.

**Perspective one: an extraordinary judicial sentence under ancient theocracy.** Many conservative interpreters emphasize this was neither an imperial land grab nor ethnic bigotry—Moses' own wife and father-in-law Jethro were Midianites, and young Midianite girls were integrated into Israelite life. They view the campaign as a direct divine judgment (31:2–3) against the specific Midianite coalition that plotted to destroy Israel from within and cost 24,000 lives in chapter 25, uprooting active pagan seduction before Israel entered Canaan. Even so, many holding this view candidly admit that the fate of the young boys in verse 17 leaves a sobering moral weight not easily brushed aside (31:17).

**Perspective two: ancient warfare rhetoric and progressive revelation.** Other scholars situate chapter 31 inside harsh Bronze Age Near Eastern conflict and conventional ancient war chronicles employing ceremonial hyperbole (*total destruction*). Tellingly, just a few generations later in Judges 6–8, the Midianites reappear "like swarms of locusts" oppressing Israel—confirming chapter 31 targeted a localized Midianite confederation bordering Moab rather than exterminating an entire ethnic people. From this vantage, Scripture records candidly how God met a people shaped by violent antiquity, steadily lifting ethical horizons through the prophets until reaching full clarity in Jesus, who commands loving enemies and praying for persecutors (Matthew 5:44).

**Perspective three: distinguishing God's directive from Moses' harsh reaction.** Some commentators look closely at textual wording: in verse 2 God commands vengeance on Midian, and the army defeats the five kings and adult warriors (31:2; 31:7–8), whereas the angry order to kill captives in verses 14 through 18 comes strictly from Moses without the customary formula *the LORD spoke* (31:14–18). On this reading, Scripture sometimes chronicles stark human severity in its historical setting without endorsing every detail as an eternal moral norm.

**One non-negotiable truth for every reader today.** Whichever lens one emphasizes, mainstream Christian faith agrees unanimously: Old Testament wars belonged to a unique, unrepeatable historical epoch and must never be invoked to justify violence, prejudice, or religious warfare today.
