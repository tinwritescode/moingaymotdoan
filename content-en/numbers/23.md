# Numbers 23 — Balaam's First Two Blessings

## Story

Standing on the heights overlooking a corner of Israel's camp, Balaam instructs King Balak to build seven altars and prepare seven bulls and seven rams. Balak complies at once, and together they sacrifice a bull and a ram on every altar. Balaam then leaves Balak standing beside the burnt offerings while he walks up to a bare hilltop to see if the LORD will meet him (23:1–3).

God indeed meets Balaam, puts his very words into Balaam's mouth, and sends him back to Balak (23:4–5). Returning before the eager king and Moabite nobles, Balaam delivers his first oracle. Balak summoned him from the east to curse Jacob, he declares, yet "How can I curse whom God has not cursed? How can I denounce whom the LORD has not denounced?" He marvels at Israel as a people dwelling apart among the nations, countless as specks of dust, and concludes with a poignant, ironic wish: "Let me die the death of the righteous, and let my end be like theirs!" (23:6–10).

Balak explodes in dismay: I hired you to curse my enemies, and instead you have blessed them! Balaam replies that he can speak only what the LORD puts in his mouth. Guessing that perhaps the vantage point was unlucky or the sight of the whole camp rattled Balaam, Balak leads him to another spot—the field of Zophim atop Mount Pisgah, where only an edge of the camp is visible—and once again builds seven altars and offers seven bulls and seven rams (23:11–14).

Again Balaam steps aside to meet the LORD, and again God puts a message in his mouth. Returning to an impatient Balak demanding to hear what the LORD said, Balaam launches into a second oracle even bolder than the first. He opens with an unforgettable declaration of God's unchanging integrity: "God is not a man, that He should lie, nor a son of man, that He should change His mind. Does He speak and not act? Does He promise and not fulfill?" (23:15–19).

Balaam affirms that he has received a command to bless and cannot reverse it. He declares that God sees no misfortune or iniquity in Jacob, for the LORD their God is among them with the shout of a King. The God who brought them out of Egypt lends them the strength of a wild ox; no sorcery or divination holds power against Jacob. They will rise fierce as a lioness, unstoppable (23:20–24).

Deflated, Balak protests: "Neither curse them at all, nor bless them at all!" Balaam repeats once more that he must speak whatever the LORD says. Still refusing to quit completely, Balak proposes moving to a third summit—the peak of Peor overlooking the wasteland—hoping God might relent there. Once again they erect seven altars and sacrifice seven bulls and seven rams (23:25–30).

## Key Takeaways

**God cannot be bribed with sacrifices or manipulated by rituals.** Over and over Balak and Balaam erect seven altars, slaughter dozens of prized animals, and hop from peak to peak just like pagans coaxing fickle gods (23:1–2; 23:14; 23:29). Yet the LORD is moved neither by lavish offerings nor lucky vantage points; his decree to bless stands rock-solid.

**God's faithfulness is the bedrock of all faith.** Verse 19 captures the gulf separating God from humanity: mortals make promises they break and change course daily, yet "God is not a man, that He should lie, nor a son of man, that He should change His mind" (23:19). Israel stood secure not because they were flawless, but because the God who swore to bless them never goes back on his word.

**Before accusing enemies, God defends his people by grace.** Hearing "He has seen no iniquity in Jacob, nor observed distress in Israel" (23:21) startles anyone who just read Israel's endless grumbling in earlier chapters! Yet God views his people through the covering of covenant and atonement, refusing to let any pagan enemy stand in judgment over his redeemed.

**Wishing for a righteous death without living a righteous life is Balaam's tragedy.** Balaam uttered a moving prayer: let me die the death of the righteous (23:10). Yet he refused to abandon greed and walk the path of the righteous, dying instead by Israel's sword on the side of God's enemies (Numbers 31:8).

## Deeper Meaning

**God's unbreakable word anchors the entire New Testament.** The proclamation "God is not a man, that He should lie" (23:19) echoes in 1 Samuel 15:29, Titus 1:2, and Hebrews 6:18–19—affirming that because it is impossible for God to lie, his saving promise holds firm as *an anchor for the soul, firm and secure*.

**No one can condemn those whom God justifies.** Balaam's confession that no one can curse whom God blesses and that God counts no iniquity against Jacob (23:8; 23:21) foreshadows Paul's triumphant anthem in Romans 8:31–34: "If God is for us, who can be against us?" and "Who will bring any charge against God's elect?" Covered by Christ's atonement, God's people stand shielded from every accusation of the enemy.

**No dark spell or hostile power can prevail over God's people.** Verse 23 declares every omen and enchantment powerless against Israel (23:23). In the New Testament, that triumph reaches fullness in Christ, who disarmed every spiritual power and authority on the cross (Colossians 2:15; 1 John 4:4).

## Notes & Nuances

**Does verse 19 ("God does not change his mind") contradict passages saying God *relented* or *regretted*?** Not at all (cf. Genesis 6:6; 1 Samuel 15:11), for the two expressions highlight different truths. Saying God *does not change his mind* (23:19) affirms that his truthful nature and sworn covenant promises never waver or break like human fickleness. When Scripture uses human emotional language saying God *grieved* or *relented*—as when Nineveh repented (Jonah 3:10)—it expresses God's real sorrow over sin or his compassionate response when humans change course, never error or broken faith.

**How could verse 21 say God *sees no iniquity in Jacob* after all Israel's rebellions?** Two complementary readings illuminate the verse. First, the Hebrew nouns translated *iniquity* and *trouble* can also mean *misfortune* and *calamity*—meaning God permits no curse-driven disaster to overtake Israel. Second, taken morally, it celebrates forgiving covenant grace: God had already disciplined Israel himself, and facing an outside enemy he refused to count their sins against them or surrender them to Balak's curse, shielding them as a people justified under his covenant.

**Why erect exactly *seven* altars each time?** Across both Scripture and the ancient Near East, seven signified wholeness and perfection. Balak imagined that lavish, mathematically complete ceremonies would pressure the LORD into bending. Failing three straight times across twenty-one altars underscored how utterly useless manipulative worship is.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, pauses long over Balaam's wish in verse 10 to die the death of the righteous (23:10). **Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, repeatedly preached on verse 19 as an unshakable rock during storms (23:19). Humans break their word either from lack of wisdom (misjudging the future) or lack of strength (unable to deliver); because God is both all-wise and almighty, he never needs to lie or retract a single promise. *(Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit, sermon on Numbers chapter 23, verse 19)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: grounding God's immutability in 23:19 on omniscience and omnipotence is characteristic of Spurgeon's preaching; exact volume not checked -->
