# Numbers 24 — The Prophecy of the Star Out of Jacob

## Story

Standing a third time atop Mount Peor, Balaam realizes beyond all doubt that it pleases the LORD to bless Israel. Abandoning his quest for omens and divination altogether, he turns his face squarely toward the wilderness. Looking down across all of Israel camped orderly tribe by tribe, the Spirit of God suddenly comes upon him (24:1–2).

Balaam lifts up his third oracle, introducing himself as the man whose eye is opened, who hears the words of God and beholds visions of the Almighty. He bursts into radiant admiration: "How lovely are your tents, O Jacob, your dwelling places, O Israel!" He likens their camp to stretching valleys, lush riverside gardens, aloes and cedars planted beside flowing waters by the LORD's own hand. Water will overflow their buckets, their king will stand higher than Agag, and their kingdom will be exalted (24:3–7).

Continuing, he celebrates the might of Israel led out of Egypt by God—strong as a wild ox, majestic as a lion and lioness crouching at rest whom none dare rouse. Then he echoes the ancient covenant oath sworn to Abraham: "Blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you!" (24:8–9; cf. Genesis 12:3).

At this, Balak snaps completely. Striking his hands together in fury, the king lashes out at Balaam: I summoned you to curse my enemies, and now you have blessed them three straight times! Balak orders him home immediately, taunting bitterly that the LORD has kept Balaam from his rich reward (24:10–11).

Calmly Balaam recalls his opening words: even a house stuffed with silver and gold could not make him go past the LORD's word. Yet before departing, he voluntarily delivers a fourth oracle—unasked by Balak—foretelling what Israel will do to Moab in *the days to come* (24:12–14).

With spiritual sight sweeping across distant centuries, Balaam speaks a dazzling prophecy: "I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near. A star will come out of Jacob, and a scepter will rise out of Israel." That coming King will crush Moab, conquer Edom and Seir, and Israel will grow mighty, for "a ruler will come out of Jacob" (24:15–19).

Not stopping there, Balaam adds three brief oracles sealing the fate of neighboring powers: Amalek, though first among nations, will end in ruin; the Kenites, snug in rocky cliffside nests, will be carried captive by Assyria; and even mighty empires like Assyria will be raided by ships sailing from western coasts (Kittim) and come to destruction. His prophecies finished, Balaam rises to return home, and Balak goes quietly on his way (24:20–25).

## Key Takeaways

**When the Spirit of God moves, every human agenda bows.** Dropping his old divination tricks, a pagan seer yields to the Spirit of God and utters one of the loftiest messianic prophecies in the entire Old Testament (24:1–2; 24:17). God can commandeer any voice he chooses to herald his truth.

**Abraham's promise remains vibrant and expanding in the desert.** The third oracle's riverside garden imagery recalls Eden, while its closing line—"Blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you"—restates verbatim the covenant sworn to Abraham and Jacob (24:6; 24:9; cf. Genesis 12:3; 27:29). Forty rocky desert years have not dimmed God's oath by a shade.

**Empires rise and vanish; only the kingdom of the Ruler from Jacob endures.** The closing oracles sweep across powerhouse after powerhouse of the ancient world—Moab, Edom, Amalek, Kenites, Assyria—every one bound for collapse (24:17–24). Only the King bearing Israel's scepter holds the future of history.

**Seeing truth about the Savior clearly cannot save a disobedient heart.** Balaam speaks a heartbreaking line: "I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near" (24:17). He glimpsed the Star's brilliance from afar, yet greed pulled him right back into darkness—instigating the trap that ensnared Israel in the very next chapter (cf. Revelation 2:14).

## Deeper Meaning

**The prophecy of the *Star* and *Scepter* points directly to Jesus the Messiah.** In near history, verse 17 found an initial fulfillment in King David, who subdued Moab and Edom (2 Samuel 8:2; 8:14; Numbers 24:17). Yet its horizon stretched vastly past David: both ancient Jewish expectation and the early church saw the Messiah here. When Jesus was born, wise men arriving from the East—Balaam's very homeland—entered Jerusalem asking: "Where is the One who has been born King of the Jews? We saw His star in the east and have come to worship Him" (Matthew 2:2).

**Jesus calls himself the *Bright Morning Star* at the close of Scripture.** The star shining out of Numbers 24:17 comes to radiant completion on the final page of Revelation, where the risen Lord proclaims himself the Root and Offspring of David and "the bright Morning Star" (Revelation 22:16). The royal scepter of Judah (Genesis 49:10) and the Star of Jacob (24:17) meet completely in Jesus alone.

**God's Spirit may speak through flawed vessels, yet only personal faith saves.** Balaam's story echoes Jesus' sobering warning in Matthew 7:22–23: some who prophesied in the Lord's name still never belonged to him. Outward spiritual gifts never substitute for a heart that genuinely fears and obeys God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who was *Agag* in verse 7 (24:7)?** Verse 7 foretells that Israel's king "will be greater than Agag." Many scholars understand *Agag* as a royal title borne by Amalekite kings (comparable to *Pharaoh* in Egypt or *Abimelech* among Philistines), later borne by the Amalekite king in Saul's day (1 Samuel 15:8). Since Amalek dominated the southern desert at that time, rising higher than Agag meant Israel's monarchy would tower above contemporary regional powers.

**Why does verse 25 say Balaam went home, yet later he dies among the Midianites?** At the end of chapter 24, Balaam departs Peor heading home after Balak dismisses him (24:25). Yet either before leaving or by returning to the region, he counseled Moab and Midian to seduce Israel through women and idolatrous feasts at Baal of Peor (25:1–3; 31:16; Revelation 2:14). Still chasing the wealth he lost from Balak, he stayed or returned among Midian and was killed when Israel waged battle against them (31:8).

**What region was *Kittim* (24:24)?** *Kittim* originally designated Cyprus and western Mediterranean coastlines, later widening across Scripture to denote western maritime empires such as Greece and Rome (cf. Daniel 11:30). That brief oracle lifts the curtain from local Jordan Valley tribes out onto the sweeping rise and fall of ancient world empires.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, regards verse 17 as one of the clearest prophecies of Christ in the Pentateuch (24:17). He pauses sorrowfully over Balaam's admission—"I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near"—reminding readers that admiring Christ intellectually from a distance saves no one unless we draw near to him in faith and love. *(Exposition of the Old and New Testaments, on Numbers 24)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's famous application of 24:17 contrasting distant intellectual sight with nearness by faith is widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, explains that the *Star* and *Scepter* prophecy (24:17) first pointed to David's dynasty as an initial sketch before reaching eternal fulfillment in Christ. Having God place so exalted a prophecy on the lips of a greedy outsider like Balaam proves all the more forcibly that the promise came straight from God, not Israelite self-flattery. *(Harmony of the Law, on Numbers 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading messianic prophecies in two stages (David then Christ) and noting the evidential value of a foreign mouthpiece are characteristic of Calvin; exact text not checked -->

**Irenaeus**, second-century church father, linked the *Star out of Jacob* in Numbers 24:17 directly to the star guiding the eastern Magi in Matthew 2:2. Because Balaam's prophecy circulated across the East, Irenaeus reasoned, the wise men recognized the star heralding the birth of the King of the Jews. *(Against Heresies, Book 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: connecting Numbers 24:17 with the Magi's star in Matthew 2 is a famous point in Irenaeus and early fathers like Justin Martyr and Origen; exact chapter in Book 3 not verified -->
