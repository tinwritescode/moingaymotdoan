# Numbers 29 — The Great Festivals of the Seventh Month

## Story

Continuing the worship calendar from chapter 28, chapter 29 devotes itself entirely to the seventh month—the most sacred month on Israel's calendar, packing three grand festivals within a three-week span.

First comes the first day of the seventh month: a sacred assembly with rest from ordinary work and trumpets blasting across camp. On top of both the daily and monthly New Moon sacrifices, priests offer one young bull, one ram, and seven male lambs as burnt offerings along with one male goat for a sin offering, paired with exact grain and wine measures (29:1–6).

On the tenth day of the seventh month falls the Day of Atonement. The community gathers in sacred assembly, stops all regular work, and "humbles themselves"—fasting in solemn repentance. Alongside the high priest's special sanctuary atonement rites (detailed in Leviticus 16), the nation presents an extra slate of burnt, grain, and drink offerings plus a sin-offering goat (29:7–11).

The climax of the seventh month is the Feast of Tabernacles, beginning on the fifteenth day and lasting seven straight days. It boasts the most lavish slate of sacrifices all year: every single day calls for two rams, fourteen male lambs, and one sin-offering goat, while the number of young bulls steps down by exactly one each day—thirteen on day one, twelve on day two, down to seven on day seven, adding up to precisely seventy young bulls (29:12–34).

Following seven joyous festival days, the eighth day brings a solemn closing assembly. Israel rests from daily work and offers a simpler, intimate set of sacrifices: one bull, one ram, seven lambs, and one sin-offering goat (29:35–38). The chapter concludes with a clear summary: all these scheduled national sacrifices stand in addition to each individual's voluntary vows and freewill gifts—and Moses faithfully delivers to Israel every word the LORD commanded (29:39–40).

## Key Takeaways

**The seventh month traces the complete arc of spiritual life.** The order of its three festivals is deeply intentional: first trumpets awaken sleeping consciences (29:1), next comes fasting and cleansing from sin (29:7), and finally seven full days of celebration at the Feast of Tabernacles (29:12). Deepest joy always blossoms after sin has been forgiven before God.

**Harvest abundance calls for our boldest gratitude.** Tabernacles arrived right as autumn harvest filled Israel's barns with grain, olive oil, and new wine. At the very season when prosperity tempts human hearts into smug self-reliance, God invited the largest burst of offerings all year to declare that every good harvest came from his hand (29:12–34).

**Public worship never crowds out personal voluntary devotion.** Verse 39 explicitly notes that the entire public calendar operates "in addition to your vow offerings and freewill offerings" (29:39). Shared liturgy supplies strong structure, while God still delights in spontaneous personal gifts rising from each believer's own walk.

**Rest itself is a form of worship.** Every single occasion in the chapter—the first, tenth, fifteenth, and eighth closing day—opens with the charge "you shall do no ordinary work" (29:1; 29:7; 29:12; 29:35). Laying down tools to gather before God confesses that human life means far more than chasing bread.

## Deeper Meaning

**Hundreds of seventh-month sacrifices spotlight Christ's singular sufficiency.** Seven days of Tabernacles alone called for 70 bulls, 14 rams, 98 lambs, and 7 goats—on top of regular daily lambs. Hebrews sets that costly, endless cycle beside Jesus' single offering: precisely because hundreds of animal lives could never cleanse a human conscience, God's Son came to offer himself once for all, completing eternal redemption (Hebrews 10:1–14).

**Jesus cries out on the very final day of Tabernacles.** John's Gospel records that on the last and greatest day of the Feast of Tabernacles—that solemn eighth day of verse 35 (29:35)—Jesus stood in the temple courts crying aloud for everyone who thirsts to come to him and drink so rivers of living water would flow from within them (John 7:37–38). Israel's harvest festival thanking God for rain and crops reached its true fountain in Christ.

**Tabernacles points ahead to God dwelling forever with humanity.** Israel lived in leafy booths for seven days recalling how God camped among them in the desert (Leviticus 23:42–43). Revelation closes the Bible with that feast's final fulfillment: God's dwelling place is now among the people, he will dwell with them, they will be his people, and he will wipe every tear from their eyes (Revelation 21:3–4).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why do the Tabernacles bulls step down from thirteen to seven?** Adding thirteen down through seven yields exactly seventy bulls (29:13–32). Across Scripture, seventy represents all the nations of the earth listed in Genesis 10. Classical Jewish tradition (in the *Talmud*) read those seventy bulls as Israel's priestly intercession on behalf of all seventy nations worldwide—followed on the eighth day by one solitary bull celebrating intimate fellowship between God and his own people.

**Why such lengthy repetition throughout chapter 29?** To modern readers, spelling out the nearly identical sacrifice schedule day by day across verses 13 through 34 feels repetitive (29:13–34). In ancient liturgical prose, however, that steady cadence generates solemn grandeur, affirming that not a single day of the feast was rushed or cut short.

**A slight verse-numbering difference between chapters 29 and 30.** In the Hebrew Bible, verse 40 of chapter 29 serves as chapter 30 verse 1 (29:40; 30:1), shifting Hebrew verse numbers throughout chapter 30 by one compared to English translations. This stems solely from later verse numbering and alters nothing in the text.
