# Luke 6 — The Sermon on the Plain

## Story

One Sabbath, Jesus was passing through grainfields. His disciples plucked heads of grain, rubbed them in their hands, and ate them. Some Pharisees asked, "Why are you doing what is unlawful on the Sabbath?" He answered: haven't you read what David did when he and his companions were hungry? He entered the house of God, took the consecrated bread lawful only for priests to eat, ate it, and shared it with those with him. Then He declared, "The Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath" (6:1–5; 1 Samuel 21:1–6). They were not accused of theft—the Law allowed passersby to pluck neighbor's grain by hand (Deuteronomy 23:25)—but of "working" on the day of rest.

On another Sabbath He entered the synagogue to teach, where a man with a withered right hand was present. The scribes and Pharisees watched closely to see if He would heal on the Sabbath so they could find a charge against Him. Knowing their thoughts, He told the man with the withered hand to get up and stand in front of everyone. Then He asked them, "I ask you, which is lawful on the Sabbath: to do good or to do evil, to save life or to destroy it?" Looking around at them all, He told the man, "Stretch out your hand." He did so, and his hand was completely restored. Filled with fury, they began discussing with one another what they might do to Jesus (6:6–11). His question cuts deep: while He works to rescue a life, they plot to destroy one—right on the Sabbath.

In those days Jesus went up on a mountainside to pray, spending the entire night in prayer to God. When morning came, He called His disciples to Him and chose twelve of them, whom He also designated apostles: Simon, whom He named Peter, and his brother Andrew; James and John; Philip and Bartholomew; Matthew and Thomas; James son of Alphaeus, and Simon called the Zealot; Judas son of James, and Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor (6:12–16). Luke loves highlighting Jesus praying before turning points. Twelve men recall Israel's twelve tribes—a renewed Israel taking shape. Judas son of James is likely the same apostle Matthew and Mark call Thaddaeus.

Coming down with them, He stood on a level place. A great crowd of His disciples and a vast multitude from all Judea, Jerusalem, and the coastal region of Tyre and Sidon had gathered to hear Him and be healed of their diseases. Those troubled by impure spirits were cured, and the whole crowd tried to touch Him because power was coming out from Him and healing everyone (6:17–19). The sermon that follows parallels Matthew's Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), though briefer and delivered on level ground.

Lifting His eyes toward His disciples, He declared, "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God!" Blessed are you who hunger now, for you will be satisfied; blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh; blessed are you when people hate you, exclude you, and insult you because of the Son of Man, for great is your reward in heaven, just as their ancestors treated the prophets. Yet woe to you who are rich, for you have already received your comfort; woe to you who are well fed now, for you will go hungry; woe to you who laugh now, for you will mourn and weep; woe to you when everyone speaks well of you, for that is how their ancestors treated false prophets (6:20–26). Matthew records eight blessings; Luke pairs four blessings squarely opposite four woes, echoing Mary's song of bringing down rulers and lifting up the humble (1:52–53).

"Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you." If someone strikes you on one cheek, turn the other as well; if someone takes your cloak, do not withhold your tunic; give to everyone who asks you, and if anyone takes what belongs to you, do not demand it back. "Do to others as you would have them do to you." What credit is it if you love only those who love you? Even sinners do that. Doing good to those who do good to you, lending expecting repayment—sinners do the same. Instead, love your enemies, do good, and lend expecting nothing back; then your reward will be great and you will be sons of the Most High, because He is kind to the ungrateful and wicked. "Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful" (6:27–36). The ultimate reason is not bare morality, but family likeness: children resembling their Father.

Do not judge, and you will not be judged; do not condemn, and you will not be condemned; forgive, and you will be forgiven. Give, and it will be given to you: a good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over, will be poured into your lap, for with the measure you use, it will be measured back to you. He also told a parable: "Can a blind man lead a blind man? Will they not both fall into a pit?" A disciple is not above his teacher, yet everyone fully trained will become like his teacher (6:37–40). The blind guide and the teacher warn anyone aspiring to lead others: you will bring people straight to wherever you yourself stand.

Why do you look at the speck in your brother's eye yet miss the beam in your own? "You hypocrite, first take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye." No good tree bears bad fruit, nor does a bad tree bear good fruit; "each tree is known by its own fruit." Nobody picks figs from thornbushes or grapes from briers. A good person brings good things out of the good treasure stored in their heart, and an evil person brings evil out of evil treasure, "for out of the overflow of the heart, the mouth speaks" (6:41–45). Jesus never forbids helping a brother remove a speck; He simply insists we start with our own eye.

Finally He asks, "Why do you call Me 'Lord, Lord,' but not do what I say?" Whoever comes to Me, hears My words, and puts them into practice is like a man building a house who dug down deep and laid its foundation on rock. When a flood rose, the torrent struck that house yet could not shake it, because it was well built. But whoever hears My words and does not put them into practice is like a man who built a house on the ground without a foundation. The moment the torrent struck, that house collapsed, and its destruction was complete (6:46–49). From the outside, both houses look identical; only the flood reveals which one rests on bedrock.

## Key Takeaways

**The Sabbath exists to save lives and do good, not trap people in rigid rules.** Whether disciples rub grain to quiet hunger or a man with a withered hand stands inside the synagogue, Jesus restores the Sabbath's center: doing good and saving life, under the Son of Man who is Lord of the Sabbath (6:1–11).

**Jesus chooses the Twelve after a full night in prayer to build God's renewed community.** His twelve apostles bring wildly different people together—fishermen, a tax collector, a radical patriot named Simon the Zealot, and even Judas who will betray Him (6:12–16). Gathering God's people begins on its knees.

**The Sermon on the Plain sets four blessings opposite four woes, unveiling the kingdom's upside-down values.** The poor, hungry, weeping, and hated for Christ's sake are called blessed, while the rich, full, laughing, and widely applauded receive solemn warnings (6:20–26). Earthly comfort easily lulls hearts into complacency and closes them to God.

**The hallmark of truly belonging to Christ is uncalculated love and lived obedience.** Loving enemies, showing the Father's mercy, refusing harsh judgment, and putting His words into practice—that is the deep rock foundation standing firm when torrents strike, not mere lip service calling "Lord, Lord" (6:27–49).

## Deeper Meaning

**David's precedent with the consecrated bread puts mercy above ritual.** Hungry while fleeing for his life, David ate sacred loaves reserved strictly for priests and shared them with his men (6:3–4; 1 Samuel 21:1–6). Jesus stands squarely in the prophetic line proclaiming God desires mercy rather than sacrifice (Hosea 6:6).

**Twelve apostles mirror Israel's twelve tribes.** Choosing exactly twelve men (6:13) is a deliberate symbolic act: God is regathering His covenant people around the Messiah, an image still shining on the twelve foundation stones of the New Jerusalem (Genesis 49:28; Revelation 21:12–14).

**Four blessings and four woes echo the covenant paths of Deuteronomy.** Just as Moses laid blessings and curses before Israel (Deuteronomy 28:1–6; Deuteronomy 28:15–19), Jesus sets two ways of life and two destinies before His hearers, continuing Mary's song of reversal (1:52–53; 6:20–26).

**Being merciful like our Father echoes God's foundational self-revelation.** When the Lord declared His own name to Moses on Mount Sinai, His very first words were "compassionate and gracious" (Exodus 34:6). Jesus' disciples are called to mirror that exact mercy even toward the ungrateful and wicked (6:35–36).

## Notes & Nuances

**Are Luke's Sermon on the Plain and Matthew's Sermon on the Mount the same sermon?** Both share core teachings while differing slightly in setting: Matthew has Jesus climb a mountain, whereas Luke has Him come down to a level spot (6:17; Matthew 5:1). It may have been a flat plateau partway up a hillside, or Jesus simply preached His core message repeatedly across many towns and occasions.

**Why does Luke say flatly "poor" and "hungry" instead of Matthew's "poor in spirit" and "hunger for righteousness"?** Luke preserves the raw economic bite of Jesus' words: the gospel genuinely champions the materially poor and warns against the seductions of wealth (6:20–26; cf. Matthew 5:3; Matthew 5:6). Yet poverty does not automatically save anyone nor wealth automatically doom them—Luke himself celebrates generous people of means like Joanna and Susanna (8:3) and Zacchaeus (19:8–9).

**"Do not judge" (6:37) does not ban moral discernment.** Seconds later Jesus instructs us to test trees by their fruit (6:43–44), requiring clear-eyed judgment. What He forbids is harsh, self-righteous condemnation—nitpicking a brother's speck while ignoring the timber sticking out of our own eye (6:41–42).

**Understand the Sabbath debate within first-century Judaism.** Pharisees guarded Sabbath rules fiercely out of sincere reverence for God's command, classifying rubbing grain as harvesting and permitting Sabbath healing only in immediate life-threatening emergencies. Jesus does not abolish the Sabbath; He returns it to its true heart of giving life and doing good (6:9).
