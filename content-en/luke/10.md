# Luke 10 — Who Is My Neighbor?

## Story

After this, the Lord appointed seventy others and sent them two by two ahead of Him to every town and place He Himself was about to visit. He told them, "The harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few." Ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers into His harvest field. Go! I am sending you out like lambs among wolves. Carry no purse, no bag, no sandals, and greet no one along the road (10:1–4). Many ancient manuscripts read seventy-two rather than seventy in both verse 1 and verse 17. Both numbers recall the Table of Nations in Genesis 10—seventy nations in the Hebrew text and seventy-two in the Greek translation—signaling a mission aimed at the entire world.

Whatever house you enter, first say, "Peace to this house." If a person of peace is there, your peace will rest on them; if not, it will return to you. Stay in that same house, eating and drinking whatever they give you, "for the worker is worthy of his wages," and do not move around from house to house. Whenever you enter a town and are welcomed, eat what is set before you, heal the sick there, and tell them, "The kingdom of God has come near to you." Yet whenever a town refuses to welcome you, go into its streets and say: even the dust of your town clinging to our feet we wipe off against you; still be sure of this—the kingdom of God has come near. On that day it will be more bearable for Sodom than for that town (10:5–12). Paul later quotes the line about the worker's wages when addressing financial support for gospel ministers (1 Timothy 5:18).

"Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida!" If the miracles performed in you had been performed in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago, sitting in sackcloth and ashes. On the day of judgment it will be more bearable for Tyre and Sidon than for you. And you, Capernaum, will you be lifted up to heaven? No, you will be brought down to Hades! Whoever listens to you listens to Me; whoever rejects you rejects Me; and whoever rejects Me rejects the One who sent Me (10:13–16; Isaiah 14:13–15). Tyre and Sidon were notorious Gentile cities condemned by earlier prophets; Capernaum, Jesus' own base of ministry, witnessed the most and bears the heaviest responsibility.

The seventy returned with joy, saying, "Lord, even the demons submit to us in Your name." He replied, "I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven." I have given you authority to trample on snakes and scorpions and over all the power of the enemy, and nothing will harm you. Yet do not rejoice that the spirits submit to you, "but rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (10:17–20). Spiritual power is real, yet it is never the bedrock of joy. Unshakeable joy rests on what God has done for us, not on what we pull off for Him.

At that very hour Jesus rejoiced full of the Holy Spirit and declared, "I praise You, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because You have hidden these things from the wise and learned, and revealed them to little children." Yes, Father, for this was pleasing in Your sight. All things have been handed over to Me by My Father; no one knows who the Son is except the Father, and no one knows who the Father is except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him. Turning privately to His disciples, He said, "Blessed are the eyes that see what you see." Many prophets and kings longed to see and hear what you are seeing and hearing, yet did not (10:21–24). This is one of the rare places in the Gospels explicitly noting Jesus' overflowing joy—rejoicing in His Father, in the Spirit, and in humble little ones.

An expert in the Law stood up to test Him: "Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" Jesus asked: what is written in the Law? How do you read it? He answered, "Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your strength and with all your mind, and love your neighbor as yourself." Jesus said, "You have answered correctly; do this and you will live." Wanting to justify himself, however, the man asked Jesus, "And who is my neighbor?" (10:25–29; Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18). His question draws a fence: how far must my love stretch, and whom may I safely leave outside?

Jesus replied with a story: a man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho when he fell into the hands of robbers, who stripped him, beat him, and went away leaving him half dead. By chance a priest came down that same road; seeing him, he passed by on the other side. So too a Levite came to the place, looked at him, and passed by on the other side. Yet a Samaritan, as he traveled, came where the man was; seeing him, he took pity on him. Stepping over, he poured oil and wine on his wounds and bandaged them, lifted the man onto his own animal, brought him to an inn, and took care of him. The next day he took out two denarii, handed them to the innkeeper, and said: take care of him, and whatever more you spend I will repay you when I return (10:30–35). The road from Jerusalem to Jericho ran roughly fifteen miles, plunging over three thousand feet through barren hills notorious for bandits. Jews and Samaritans had nursed bitter hatred for centuries—just back in Luke 9 a Samaritan village refused Jesus shelter.

Jesus asked: which of these three do you think proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among robbers? The legal expert answered, "The one who showed him mercy"—unable even to bring himself to pronounce the word "Samaritan." Jesus told him, "Go and do likewise" (10:36–37). Jesus completely turned the question around. The lawyer asked "Who is my neighbor?"—who qualifies for my love. Jesus asks who *acted like* a neighbor—what kind of person must I become. And the model hero is the very enemy the lawyer despised.

As they continued on their way, He entered a village where a woman named Martha welcomed Him into her home. She had a sister called Mary, who sat at the Lord's feet listening to His teaching. Distracted by all the serving, Martha came up and said, "Lord, do You not care that my sister has left me to serve alone? Tell her to help me!" The Lord answered her, "Martha, Martha, you are worried and upset about many things, but only one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion, and it will not be taken away from her" (10:38–42). Sitting at a teacher's feet was the recognized posture of a student—a privilege rarely extended to women back then. Placed immediately after the Good Samaritan, the episode restores balance: active service matters deeply, yet listening comes first.

## Key Takeaways

**A disciple's truest joy rests not in impressive ministry wins, but in belonging to God.** The seventy return thrilled that demons obey them, yet Jesus anchors their joy deeper: rejoice not in spiritual power, "but rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (10:17–20).

**The secrets of God's kingdom open to simple, childlike hearts.** Jesus overflows with Spirit-filled joy seeing the Father pass over self-satisfied intellects to reveal Himself to humble little ones who trust Him (10:21–24).

**Neighbor-love breaks down every ethnic and religious boundary.** Facing the lawyer's fence-building question "Who is my neighbor?", Jesus tells the Good Samaritan to flip the question: stop drawing circles and become a neighbor yourself to anyone hurting beside your road (10:29–37).

**Listening to Christ is the one essential thing standing ahead of busy service.** Martha serves with generous intent yet loses her peace in anxious bustle, while Mary sitting at Jesus' feet listening has chosen "the good portion, and it will not be taken away from her" (10:38–42).

## Deeper Meaning

**Seventy messengers widen the mission from twelve tribes out to all nations.** While the Twelve in chapter 9 recall Israel's twelve tribes, the seventy (or seventy-two in early manuscripts) echo the Genesis Table of Nations and the seventy elders sharing Moses' burden in the desert (10:1; Genesis 10:1–32; Numbers 11:16–17).

**Capernaum's judgment echoes Isaiah's oracle against proud Babylon.** Asking "will you be lifted up to heaven? No, you will be brought down to Hades!" (10:15) borrows Isaiah's exact taunt against Babylonian arrogance (Isaiah 14:13–15). Receiving abundant light and shrugging it off carries heavy weight.

**The two great commandments capture the true heart of Moses' Law.** Pairing Deuteronomy's call to love God wholeheartedly (Deuteronomy 6:5) with Leviticus's command to love one's neighbor as oneself (Leviticus 19:18) wins Jesus' warm praise (10:27–28). God's Law was never cold bureaucracy; its heartbeat was always love.

**A Samaritan showing mercy on the Jericho road has an Old Testament forerunner.** Centuries earlier, men of Samaria anointed, clothed, mounted on donkeys, and escorted Judean captives safely back to Jericho (2 Chronicles 28:15). Jesus awakens that forgotten memory of mercy to shatter mutual hatred (10:33–34).

**The chapter's final two episodes embody the exact two commandments just quoted.** The Good Samaritan pictures loving one's neighbor in concrete action, while Mary at Jesus' feet pictures loving the Lord in undivided devotion (10:27–42).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did the priest and Levite walk right past the wounded man (10:31–32)?** Many assume they feared ceremonial defilement from touching an apparent corpse (Leviticus 21:1–3), though Luke notes they were traveling *down* from Jerusalem toward Jericho—meaning their temple shift may already have ended. Whether driven by purity scruples, fear of ambush, or plain apathy, the story insists no rule or caution excuses cold-heartedness.

**Feel the first-century shock value of making a Samaritan the hero.** Today "Good Samaritan" sounds cozy and safe, yet to first-century Jewish ears Samaritans were despised heretics and bitter rivals—a Samaritan village had just turned Jesus away at the end of chapter 9 (9:52–53). Elevating a Samaritan above priest and Levite was an earthquake.

**Martha and Mary never disparages practical service (10:38–42).** Jesus never faults Martha for cooking or welcoming guests; He gently points to the frantic anxiety robbing her of peace and souring her toward her sister. Equally striking, in a world that rarely welcomed women as rabbinic students, Jesus defends Mary's right to sit at His feet as a full disciple.

**Allegorical readings of the Good Samaritan should never eclipse its plain call.** Ancient Church Fathers read every element symbolically as Christ rescuing fallen humanity. While spiritually evocative, Jesus' own closing charge to the lawyer remains concretely ethical: "Go and do likewise" (10:37).

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa in the fourth and fifth centuries, crafted the most famous allegorical interpretation of the Good Samaritan: the traveler descending from Jerusalem is fallen Adam, the robbers are demons, the priest and Levite represent Old Testament Law powerless to heal, and the Samaritan is Christ Himself binding wounds with grace and entrusting the man to the inn of the church. *(Questions on the Gospels, book 2, question 19)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's famous allegorical reading of the Good Samaritan appears in Questions on the Gospels book 2 question 19 -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century French Reformer, rejected strained allegories of the Good Samaritan and held fast to Jesus' plain aim: shattering ethnic prejudice to demonstrate that any suffering human being is our neighbor. Turning to Martha's house, Calvin remarks Martha stumbled not by serving, but by letting outward hospitality crowd out listening to the gospel. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Luke 10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin is well known for dismissing patristic allegories of the Good Samaritan in favor of its direct ethical meaning -->

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, viewed the chapter's closing pair of stories as two inseparable dimensions of Christian life: the Samaritan embodies active love serving one's neighbor, while Mary embodies quiet faith listening to God's word—the very spring feeding every deed of love. *(Church Postil)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Luther links faith in Mary and love in the Samaritan across his Sunday sermons; exact sermon unchecked -->

**Amy-Jill Levine**, an American Jewish New Testament scholar, highlights the classic three-part rhythm of the Good Samaritan: after a priest and a Levite, first-century Jewish listeners naturally expected the third traveler to be an ordinary Israelite layperson. Swapping in a Samaritan—a religious enemy—shocked the lawyer so deeply that in 10:37 he could not even utter the word "Samaritan," calling him only "the one who showed him mercy." *(Short Stories by Jesus, 2014)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the priest-Levite-Israelite expectation and the lawyer's refusal to name the Samaritan are celebrated points in Short Stories by Jesus -->

**Joel Green**, an American New Testament scholar, underlines the social breakthrough in Mary "sitting at the Lord's feet listening to His teaching" (10:39): sitting at a rabbi's feet was the established posture of male Torah students, like Paul at the feet of Gamaliel (Acts 22:3). Defending Mary, Jesus openly breaks first-century gender barriers and affirms women as full disciples. *(The Gospel of Luke, NICNT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: highlighting the idiom "sitting at the feet" as formal discipleship posture for Mary is a notable point in Green's NICNT commentary -->
