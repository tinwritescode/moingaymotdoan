# Luke 18 — Two Men Went Up to the Temple

## Story

Jesus told His disciples a parable to show them that they should always pray and never lose heart. In a certain town there was a judge who neither feared God nor respected people. And there was a widow in that town who kept coming to him, pleading, "Grant me justice against my adversary." For a long time he refused. Finally he said to himself: Even though I neither fear God nor respect anyone, yet because this widow keeps bothering me, I will see that she gets justice, so that she will not eventually wear me out with her coming (18:1–5). A widow in that world had no husband, no wealth, and no clout; her only weapon was persistence.

The Lord said: Listen to what the unjust judge says. Will not God bring about justice for His chosen ones, who cry out to Him day and night? Will He keep putting them off? I tell you, He will swiftly grant them justice. "Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will He find faith on earth?" (18:6–8). God is not like that corrupt judge; this is a "how much more" argument. If even a crooked judge eventually acts, how much more will a righteous God! The closing question shifts the spotlight from God to us: He will prove faithful, yet will those who pray persevere to the end?

To some who trusted in their own righteousness and looked down on everyone else, He told this parable. Two men went up to the temple to pray, one a Pharisee and the other a tax collector. The Pharisee stood by himself and prayed, "God, I thank You that I am not like other men—swindlers, evildoers, adulterers—or even like this tax collector." I fast twice a week and give a tenth of everything I get (18:9–12). He is not lying: he genuinely went beyond what the Law demanded, which required fasting only once a year on the Day of Atonement. The problem is not his good deeds, but that his prayer revolves around himself and measures his worth against another person.

Standing at a distance, the tax collector would not even lift his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast and said, "God, have mercy on me, a sinner!" I tell you, this man went home justified rather than the other; "for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and the one who humbles himself will be exalted" (18:13–14). The Greek verb for "have mercy" here carries the resonance of "make atonement," echoing the sin offering being sacrificed right there in the temple. He brings nothing except his sin, and walks home declared righteous. Paul will write the entire letter to the Romans around this very truth.

People were also bringing babies to Jesus so He might touch them. When the disciples saw this, they rebuked them. Yet Jesus called the children to Him and said: Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them, for the kingdom of God belongs to such as these. "Truly I tell you, anyone who does not receive the kingdom of God like a little child will never enter it" (18:15–17). Placed right after the tax collector, the babies too have nothing to offer. The kingdom of God is received as a gift, never earned as a trophy.

A certain ruler asked Him, "Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" Jesus replied: Why do you call Me good? "No one is good except God alone." You know the commandments: Do not commit adultery, do not murder, do not steal, do not bear false witness, honor your father and mother. The man answered, "All these I have kept from my youth." Hearing this, Jesus told him, "One thing you still lack: sell everything you own and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. Then come, follow Me." At these words, the man became very sad, because he was extremely rich (18:18–23; Exodus 20:12–16). All five commandments Jesus listed deal with duties toward neighbors. The first commandment—having no other gods—He tests with the command to sell his possessions. Wealth had become this man's god.

Seeing how sad he had become, Jesus said, "How hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom of God!" Indeed, it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God! Those who heard asked, "Who then can be saved?" He answered, "What is impossible with man is possible with God." Peter said: Look, we have left our own homes to follow You. Jesus told them: Truly I tell you, no one who has left home or wife or brothers or parents or children for the sake of the kingdom of God will fail to receive many times more in this age, and in the age to come eternal life (18:24–30). The camel was the largest animal in the land, the needle's eye the smallest opening; Jesus is naming a sheer impossibility. Yet He will show that very impossibility happen in the next chapter, with a rich man named Zacchaeus.

Jesus took the Twelve aside and told them: Look, we are going up to Jerusalem, and everything written by the prophets about the Son of Man will be fulfilled. He will be handed over to the Gentiles, mocked, insulted, and spat upon; they will flog Him and kill Him, and on the third day He will rise again. Yet the disciples understood none of these things; this saying was hidden from them, and they did not grasp what He was talking about (18:31–34; Isaiah 53:3–8). This is the third and most detailed prediction of His passion. Still they do not comprehend. Only after the resurrection will He open their minds (Luke 24:45).

As Jesus approached Jericho, a blind man was sitting by the roadside begging. Hearing a crowd passing by, he asked what was happening. They told him, "Jesus of Nazareth is passing by." At once he cried out, "Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" Those at the front scolded him to be quiet, yet he shouted all the louder. Jesus stopped and ordered the man to be brought to Him. When he came near, Jesus asked, "What do you want Me to do for you?" He replied, "Lord, let me see again!" Jesus said to him, "Receive your sight; your faith has healed you." Immediately he regained his sight and followed Jesus, glorifying God, and all the people who saw it gave praise to God (18:35–43). The disciples have eyes yet cannot see what He is saying; the blind man "sees" that Jesus is the Son of David. His plea echoes the tax collector's cry: have mercy.

## Key Takeaways

**Prayer includes crying out for wrong to be made right.** The widow does not ask for wealth or health; she asks for justice (18:3). By using her story to teach about prayer, Jesus shows that prayer is not confined to quiet private devotion. It is also the cry of the oppressed who trust that a Judge is listening.

**Justification is God's verdict, not a score we give ourselves.** The Pharisee grades his own performance by comparing himself to the man standing nearby (18:11). The tax collector keeps no scorecard at all, asking only for mercy (18:13). The one who walks home declared righteous is the one who brought nothing to boast of (18:14).

**God's kingdom belongs to the empty-handed.** Immediately after the tax collector come infants carried to Jesus for His touch (18:15). Luke uses a word for very small babies—children who have accomplished nothing and kept no commandments. Jesus declares that whoever will not receive the kingdom like them cannot enter it (18:17).

**What is impossible for human beings is God's own work.** The ruler has kept the commandments since boyhood, yet still lacks one thing (18:21–22). Everyone listening asks, "Who then can be saved?" (18:26). Jesus does not lower the bar; He points to a different power: what is impossible with man is possible with God (18:27).

**The chapter opens and closes with someone who refuses to stop crying out.** At the start, a widow keeps pestering a judge; at the end, a blind beggar told to hush "shouted all the louder" (18:39). One appeals to a godless judge, the other to the Son of David. Both are heard. That relentless trust is the very faith Jesus wondered whether He would still find on earth (18:8).

## Deeper Meaning

**God is the defender of widows.** The law of Moses declares that God "executes justice for the fatherless and the widow" (Deuteronomy 10:18). He even warns that if anyone mistreats them and they cry out, "I will surely hear their cry" (Exodus 22:23). Listeners hearing the parable immediately recognize that this town judge acts like the exact opposite of God.

**The cry "How long?" echoes all the way to the end of Scripture.** In Revelation, the martyrs cry out loudly, asking "How long" until God judges and avenges their blood (Revelation 6:10). They are God's chosen ones crying out day and night, just as Jesus described in 18:7. His promise to bring them justice sustains the whole waiting church.

**The tax collector's prayer is the prayer of Psalm 51.** After his sin with Bathsheba, David prayed, "Have mercy on me, O God" (Psalms 51:1). He recognized that God does not desire sacrifices above all, but "a broken and contrite heart, O God, You will not despise" (Psalms 51:17). Beating his chest in the temple, the tax collector has nothing to bring except that heart.

**God looks with favor on the one who trembles at His word.** Through Isaiah, the LORD says He needs no temple built by human hands, for He made all things; the one He esteems is "the one who is humble and contrite in spirit, who trembles at My word" (Isaiah 66:2). Standing far off, unable to lift his eyes, the tax collector embodies that very posture (18:13).

**Blind eyes opening is the sign of God's coming.** Isaiah foresaw the day God arrives to save His people: "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened" (Isaiah 35:5). The blind man near Jericho calls Jesus "Son of David" (18:38)—the promised King. His restored sight confirms that his confession is true.

## Notes & Nuances

**The Pharisee in the parable was not a villain in the eyes of first-century listeners.** Fasting and tithing were respected marks of devotion (18:12), while tax collectors worked for Rome and frequently extorted their neighbors. The original audience would have been stunned by the verdict. Thanking God for not being like others even appears in ancient Jewish prayers, such as a rabbi's prayer recorded in the Talmud (Berakhot, folio 28b) thanking God for placing him in the house of study rather than idling at street corners. Thanking God for His grace is not wrong; the sin lies in using gratitude as a platform to look down on others (18:9).

**"Justified rather than the other" (18:14) has two readings.** Some older versions render the Greek "more than the other," as though the Pharisee received partial credit. Most commentators, however, understand the phrasing to mean "and not the other": one went home justified, the other did not. Likewise, "prayed about himself" in 18:11 can be translated "prayed to himself" or "prayed concerning himself." His prayer circled entirely around himself and never asked God for a single thing.

**Why does Jesus say God will grant justice "swiftly" when believers wait so long?** Some understand "swiftly" to mean suddenly once the appointed hour arrives. Others read it alongside Peter's reminder that with the Lord a day is like a thousand years, and apparent delay is God's patience giving people time to repent (2 Peter 3:8–9). Either way, verse 18:8 turns the question back on us: God will be faithful, yet will we keep faith while waiting?

**The blind man at Jericho: one or two, entering or leaving?** Luke mentions one blind man as Jesus "approached" Jericho (18:35); Mark names Bartimaeus as Jesus left the city (Mark 10:46); Matthew mentions two blind men (Matthew 20:30). Some explain that two Jerichos existed side by side—the old town and Herod's new city—so Jesus could be leaving one while approaching the other. Others suggest Luke uses "approaching" loosely and that Mark and Luke focus on the spokesman who was known by name. Minor variations like these are typical of independent eyewitness accounts and do not alter the event's meaning.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth-to-fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, preached on the Pharisee and the tax collector. He observed that the Pharisee went up to the temple to pray yet never asked God for anything—he only listed his own achievements. He acted like a sick patient visiting a doctor who shows off his healthy limbs while hiding his wounds. **Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, preached this parable as a classic lesson on justification by faith. In his view, the Pharisee was not condemned for doing good works, but for trusting in them to stand before God. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, wrote a harmony of the Gospels and thus addressed the Jericho healing accounts. He proposed that the blind man may have called out as Jesus entered the town without yet being summoned, and was healed as He departed, while also accepting that two blind men were present with Mark and Luke naming the better-known one. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on chapter 18 verses 35–43)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin harmonizing the accounts by placing the cry on entry and the healing on exit; exact wording unverified -->

**Kenneth Bailey**, an American scholar who spent decades in the Middle East, set this parable at the daily morning and afternoon atonement sacrifices in the temple, when crowds gathered for prayer. The Pharisee stood apart, perhaps to avoid ritual contact with the unclean, while the tax collector stood far off watching the sacrifice and praying, "Make atonement for me." For Bailey, the parable shows that atonement offered at the altar reaches only the person who knows he needs it. *(Through Peasant Eyes, 1980; Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes, 2008)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: setting the scene at the Tamid atonement sacrifice and rendering the prayer "be propitiated to me" are Bailey's well-known readings of Luke 18:9–14 -->

**Amy-Jill Levine**, American Jewish scholar, reminds readers that first-century hearers saw tax collectors as collaborators and traitors, not endearing humble folk. She pushes back against sermons that turn the Pharisee into a stereotype of "hypocritical Judaism."
