# Luke 23 — Father, Forgive Them

## Story

The whole assembly rose and led Jesus before Pilate. They began accusing Him: "We found this man subverting our nation, forbidding payment of taxes to Caesar, and claiming to be Christ, a king." Pilate asked Him, "Are you the King of the Jews?" Jesus answered, "You have said so." Pilate told the chief priests and the crowds, "I find no basis for a charge against this man." Yet they pressed harder: he stirs up the people with his teaching throughout all Judea, starting from Galilee all the way to here (23:1–5). Their accusation has been translated into political terms for a Roman governor's ears. The second claim is a flat lie: Jesus Himself had told them to give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar (Luke 20:25).

Hearing Galilee mentioned, Pilate asked whether the man was a Galilean. Learning that Jesus belonged to Herod's jurisdiction, he sent Him over to Herod, who was also in Jerusalem at the time. Herod was delighted to see Jesus, having wanted to meet Him for a long while after hearing reports about Him, and hoping to watch Him perform some sign. He questioned Jesus at length, yet Jesus gave him no answer. The chief priests and scribes stood there vehemently accusing Him. Herod and his soldiers treated Him with contempt, mocked Him, dressed Him in a splendid robe, and sent Him back to Pilate. That very day Herod and Pilate became friends, though previously they had been enemies (23:6–12; Isaiah 53:7). Luke alone records this encounter. Herod wanted a spectacle, and Jesus remained silent. Acts later echoes the scene, noting that Herod and Pontius Pilate conspired together against God's Anointed (Acts 4:27).

Pilate called together the chief priests, the rulers, and the people, and declared: you brought this man to me as one inciting rebellion; I examined him in your presence and found him guilty of none of your charges. Neither did Herod, for he sent him back to us. Nothing he has done deserves death, so I will punish him and release him. The entire crowd shouted together, "Away with this man! Release Barabbas to us!" Barabbas had been thrown into prison for an insurrection in the city and for murder (23:13–19). Verse 17—stating that Pilate had to release one prisoner to them at the festival—is missing from the earliest manuscripts and likely entered from Matthew 27:15 and Mark 15:6. Many translations enclose it in brackets or footnotes.

Wanting to release Jesus, Pilate addressed them again. They kept shouting back, "Crucify him! Crucify him!" A third time Pilate asked, "Why? What evil has this man done? I have found in him no grounds for death." I will punish him and let him go. Still, with loud shouts they kept demanding His crucifixion, and their voices prevailed. Pilate ruled that their demand be granted: he released the man jailed for insurrection and murder, whom they requested, and surrendered Jesus to their will (23:20–25). Three times Pilate declares Him innocent. Jesus is condemned for "inciting rebellion," while the actual rebel walks free. The innocent man dies in place of the guilty right inside the courtroom.

As they led Him away, they seized a man named Simon, from Cyrene, coming in from the countryside, and placed the cross on his shoulders to carry behind Jesus. A large crowd followed Him, including women who beat their breasts and wailed for Him. Turning to them, Jesus said, "Daughters of Jerusalem, do not weep for Me; weep instead for yourselves and for your children." For the days are coming when people will say, "Blessed are the barren, the wombs that never bore and the breasts that never nursed!" Then they will say to the mountains, "Fall on us," and to the hills, "Cover us!" "For if men do these things when the tree is green, what will happen when it is dry?" (23:26–31; Hosea 10:8). Simon carries the cross "behind" Jesus, embodying the picture of discipleship from Luke 9:23. On His way to execution, Jesus still grieves over a city headed for ruin. The green tree is Himself, innocent; if an innocent man receives such treatment, what awaits the guilty?

Two other men, both criminals, were also led out to be executed with Him. Reaching the place called the Skull, they crucified Him there along with the criminals, one on His right and the other on His left. Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing." Then they cast lots to divide His garments (23:32–34; Isaiah 53:12; Psalms 22:18). This prayer is absent from some early and weighty manuscripts, yet stands in most others; many scholars regard it as an authentic saying of Jesus even if debated in Luke's original text. Stephen, the first martyr, will pray almost the same words while being stoned (Acts 7:60).

The people stood watching. The rulers sneered, "He saved others; let him save himself if he is the Christ of God, the Chosen One!" The soldiers mocked Him too, stepping up to offer Him sour wine and saying, "If you are the King of the Jews, save yourself!" Above His head was an inscription reading, "This Is the King of the Jews." (23:35–38; Psalms 69:21). Three rounds of mockery, three demands to "save himself." They spoke truer than they realized: precisely because He refused to save Himself, He was able to save others.

One of the criminals hanging beside Him hurled insults at Him: "Aren't you the Christ? Save yourself and us!" But the other rebuked him: don't you fear God, since you are under the same sentence? We are punished justly, getting what our deeds deserve, "but this man has done nothing wrong." Then he said, "Jesus, remember me when You come into Your kingdom." Jesus answered him, "Truly I tell you, today you will be with Me in Paradise." (23:39–43). The last person to recognize Him as King is a dying criminal with nothing to offer and no time left to mend his life. He asks only to be remembered, and receives a promise for "today."

By then it was about the sixth hour—noon—and darkness covered the whole land until the ninth hour, because the sun failed. The curtain of the temple was torn down the middle. Jesus cried out in a loud voice, "Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit!" Having said this, He breathed His last. Seeing what had taken place, the centurion praised God and said, "Surely this was a righteous man." When all the crowds who had gathered for the spectacle saw what happened, they went home beating their breasts. Meanwhile, all who knew Him, including the women who had followed Him from Galilee, stood at a distance watching these things (23:44–49; Psalms 31:5). His final words in Luke come from a psalm Jewish children learned to pray before sleep, with the word "Father" added at the start. The curtain guarding the Most Holy Place splits open: the way into God's presence stands open (Hebrews 10:19–20).

Now there was a man named Joseph, a member of the Council, a good and upright man who had not consented to their decision and action. He came from the Jewish town of Arimathea and was waiting for the kingdom of God. He went to Pilate and asked for Jesus' body. Taking it down, he wrapped it in linen cloth and placed it in a tomb cut into the rock, where no one had ever been laid. It was Preparation Day, and the Sabbath was about to begin. The women who had come with Jesus from Galilee followed behind, saw the tomb, and noted how His body was laid. Returning home, they prepared spices and perfumes. On the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment (23:50–56; Isaiah 53:9). They saw plainly which tomb it was and how the body rested inside; there could be no mistake about the grave. And they prepared spices for a dead body: none of them expected a resurrection.

## Key Takeaways

**Five witnesses attest His innocence.** Luke lines up testimonies one after another: Pilate states three times that he finds no guilt in Him (23:4; 23:14; 23:22), Herod finds none either (23:15), the criminal declares "this man has done nothing wrong" (23:41), and the centurion proclaims, "Surely this was a righteous man" (23:47). He does not die for sins of His own. Readers are led straight to the question: whose sins, then, is He dying for?

**Jesus practices the exact command He taught.** Early in the Gospel, He told disciples to "pray for those who mistreat you" (6:28). From the cross, while soldiers are still dividing His clothes, He prays, "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (23:34). Loving one's enemies is no distant ideal; He lives it at the hardest conceivable moment.

**The "today" of salvation.** Luke favors the word "today": the angel announces, "Today in the town of David a Savior has been born to you" (2:11), and Jesus tells Zacchaeus, "Today salvation has come to this house" (19:9). Its final appearance comes on the cross: "Today you will be with Me in Paradise" (23:43). The last person He saves before dying is a criminal who can only ask to be remembered.

**He dies as a Son trusting His Father.** Matthew and Mark preserve the cry, "Why have You forsaken Me?" (Mark 15:34). Luke records another final utterance: "Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit!" (23:46). The two sayings do not contradict each other; both come from the Psalms. Luke wants readers to see that even amid darkness, Jesus still addresses God as Father and entrusts Himself to Him.

**Not everyone stands against Him.** The rulers and a gathered crowd clamor for crucifixion (23:18; 23:21). Yet Luke also notes women weeping and beating their breasts for Him (23:27), onlookers standing quietly while rulers scoff (23:35), crowds returning home beating their breasts in grief (23:48), and a Sanhedrin member who dissented from the council's vote (23:51). Luke refuses to paint an entire nation as a single hostile bloc.

## Deeper Meaning

**Entrusted, and redeemed.** Psalm 31 is the prayer of a sufferer ringed by enemies and deserted by friends, who nevertheless prays, "Into Your hand I commit my spirit," and immediately adds, "You have redeemed me" (Psalms 31:5). Jesus quotes only the first half (23:46). The second half expresses what He trusts will follow: the Father will redeem Him from death. While being stoned, Stephen echoes the same surrender, addressing it directly to Jesus: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59).

**Green wood and dry wood.** Ezekiel prophesied a fire sweeping over Judah that would consume "every green tree and every dry tree" (Ezekiel 20:47). Jesus draws on that pair of images on the road to Calvary (23:31). Green wood still holds sap and resists burning; dry wood catches instantly. If an innocent man suffers this fate, what will happen when disaster hits the city?

**Paradise is the reopened garden.** The word "Paradise" originally meant a walled garden, and the Greek Old Testament uses it for the Garden of Eden (Genesis 2:8). Paul speaks of being caught up "to Paradise" (2 Corinthians 12:4), and Revelation promises the victor the right to eat from the tree of life "in the Paradise of God" (Revelation 2:7). The promise made to the dying criminal is a promise of the garden humanity lost.

**The Righteous One.** The Roman centurion calls Jesus "a righteous man" (23:47). In Luke's second volume, that phrase becomes a title. Peter tells the people of Jerusalem, "You disowned the Holy and Righteous One and asked that a murderer be released to you" (Acts 3:14). What a Roman officer blurts out at His death becomes a confession on the lips of the early Church.

**Noon turned to night.** Amos announced a day of divine judgment: "I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight" (Amos 8:9). Luke records darkness covering the whole land from noon until three in the afternoon (23:44–45). It is the darkness of judgment, yet the one enduring it is wholly innocent. Jesus had already named it "the power of darkness" (22:53).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who bears responsibility for Jesus' death?** Luke identifies each party plainly: the chief priests and leaders bring Him forward and press charges, Pilate knows He is innocent yet gives in, Herod mocks Him, and a crowd summoned to the scene shouts for crucifixion (23:13; 23:23–25). Yet Luke never indicts the Jewish people as a whole: Jesus, the apostles, the weeping women, and Joseph of Arimathea are all Jews. In Acts, Peter says they acted "in ignorance" (Acts 3:17), matching the prayer in 23:34. Blaming the entire Jewish people brought untold suffering across history; in 1965 the Catholic Church formally repudiated that charge. Christian theology likewise teaches that Jesus died for everyone's sins, leaving no one room to point a finger at others.

**Who is included in "forgive them," and did Luke write that sentence?** As noted above, 23:34 is missing from several early manuscripts. As for "them," some understand the Roman soldiers nailing Him to the cross, who truly did not grasp what they were doing. Others include the Jewish leaders and the crowd, echoing Peter's later words (Acts 3:17). The prayer does not excuse their wrong as harmless; it asks the Father to pardon a real sin.

**Where does "today" belong in the sentence?** Ancient Greek manuscripts lacked punctuation, so a few readers punctuate 23:43 as "Truly I tell you today, you will be with Me in Paradise," arguing that the criminal enters Paradise only at the final resurrection. Almost all commentators read it in the usual way: "Truly I tell you, today you will be with Me." Everywhere else Jesus says "Truly I tell you" without adding "today," and the man asked to be remembered "when You come into Your kingdom" (23:42)—to which Jesus replies not in the distant future, but today.

**Was the darkness a solar eclipse?** Passover falls at full moon, when a solar eclipse is astronomically impossible. Certain manuscripts of 23:45 use a Greek term that can mean "the sun was eclipsed" or simply "the sun failed," prompting ancient debate. Some propose a dust storm or heavy cloud cover; others view it as a direct supernatural act of God. Luke offers no scientific explanation. He simply shows creation itself growing dark as the Son of God dies.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, late fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, preached two sermons on the cross and the thief. He marveled that while the disciples had fled, a dying criminal looked at a crucified, ridiculed man and recognized a King. According to Chrysostom, Jesus led a robber—not an apostle—as the first entrant into Paradise so that no sinner would ever despair of salvation. *(Homilies on the Cross and the Thief)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Chrysostom's two sermons De cruce et latrone and core theme; exact wording and accessibility of full English text not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, ranked the dying criminal's faith among the most extraordinary examples of faith in all Scripture. Seeing no miracle and no outward glory—only a man dying beside him—he still believed Jesus possessed a kingdom. Calvin also drew on the promise "today" to affirm that believers' souls are with Christ immediately after death. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Calvin praising the thief's faith here; less certain whether he develops the intermediate-state argument at this exact verse -->

**Philo of Alexandria**, first-century Jewish philosopher who lived during Pilate's tenure, described Pilate as inflexible, cruel, and notorious for bribery, insults, robberies, and executions without trial. That portrait reminds readers that Luke's Pilate is no gentle magistrate; Luke himself knew of Pilate's brutality (13:1). Pilate recognized Jesus' innocence yet still surrendered Him to keep the crowd quiet and protect his own office. *(On the Embassy to Gaius)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Philo's characterization of Pilate in Embassy to Gaius is frequently quoted; pairing it with Luke is standard commentary practice -->

**Raymond Brown**, American Catholic priest and biblical scholar, closely weighed the prayer "Father, forgive them" (23:34). He considered it plausible that Luke originally included the line, suggesting later scribes may have deleted it after Jerusalem's fall because they assumed God had not forgiven the city or felt reluctant to imagine forgiveness for Jesus' killers. He also noted how naturally the prayer pairs with Stephen's dying prayer in Acts. *(The Death of the Messiah, 1994)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Brown favoring Lukan originality for verse 34 and explaining possible scribal omission; exact wording not checked -->

**The Second Vatican Council**, gathering Catholic bishops in Rome from 1962 to 1965, issued a declaration on the Church's relations with other religions. The document declared that what happened during Jesus' passion cannot be blamed indiscriminately on all Jews alive then, nor on Jews today, and forbade portraying the Jewish people as rejected or accursed by God. It affirmed instead that Christ underwent His suffering for the sins of all humanity. *(Nostra Aetate, 1965, section 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the teaching of Nostra Aetate section 4 on responsibility for Jesus' death is widely known -->
