# Luke 8 — He Who Has Ears to Hear

## Story

Afterward Jesus traveled from town to town and village to village, proclaiming the good news of the kingdom of God. The Twelve were with Him, along with certain women who had been healed of evil spirits and sicknesses: Mary called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out; Joanna, the wife of Chuza, manager of Herod's household; Susanna; and many others. They provided for Him out of their own means (8:1–3). What an unlikely team: a woman once tormented by seven demons walking beside the wife of a royal palace official. These very women will stand near the cross and arrive first at the empty tomb (23:49; 24:10).

As a great crowd gathered from towns everywhere, He spoke a parable. A sower went out to sow his seed. Some fell along the path, was trampled underfoot, and birds devoured it. Some fell on rock, sprouted, and withered for lack of moisture. Some fell among thorns, which grew up with it and choked it. Still other seed fell on good soil, grew, and yielded a hundredfold crop. Calling out, He said, "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (8:4–8).

His disciples asked what this parable meant. He replied: to you it has been given to know the mysteries of the kingdom of God, while to others I speak in parables, "so that though seeing, they may not see; though hearing, they may not understand" (8:9–10; Isaiah 6:9–10). The seed is the word of God. Those along the path are people who hear, and then the devil snatches the word from their hearts lest they believe and be saved. Those on rock welcome the word with joy when they hear it, yet have no root; they believe for a while, and in a time of testing fall away. Those among thorns hear, yet as they go along they are choked by life's worries, riches, and pleasures, and never mature. Seed in good soil represents those with an honest and good heart who hear the word, hold fast to it, and with perseverance bear fruit (8:11–15). Only Luke adds "with perseverance": good soil is not a single emotional response, but clinging to God's word across the years.

Nobody lights a lamp and hides it under a jar or a bed; they put it on a stand so those coming in can see the light. Nothing is hidden that will not come into the open. "Therefore consider carefully how you listen"—for whoever has will be given more, while whoever does not have will lose even what they think they have. His mother and brothers came looking for Him yet could not get near Him because of the crowd. Someone told Him: Your mother and brothers are standing outside wanting to see You. He answered, "My mother and brothers are those who hear the word of God and carry it out" (8:16–21). This entire section turns on one action: listening. He does not disown His family; He opens His family wide to everyone who listens and obeys.

One day He stepped into a boat with His disciples and said: let us cross to the other side of the lake. As they sailed, He fell asleep. A furious windstorm swept down onto the lake, filling the boat with water and putting them in real danger. The disciples woke Him: "Master, Master, we are perishing!" Rising, He rebuked the wind and the raging waves; they subsided, and all became calm. He asked them, "Where is your faith?" Filled with fear and amazement, they asked one another, "Who is this? He commands even the winds and the water, and they obey Him!" (8:22–25). That haunting question—"Who is this?"—echoes through these chapters until Peter answers in Luke 9:20.

They sailed to the region of the Gerasenes across the lake opposite Galilee. Stepping ashore, Jesus met a man from the town possessed by demons. For a long time he had worn no clothes and lived not in a house, but among the tombs. People had repeatedly bound him with chains and shackles under guard, yet he snapped the bonds and was driven by the demon into solitary places. Seeing Jesus, he cried out, fell at His feet, and shouted, "What do You want with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? I beg You not to torture me!" Jesus asked him, "What is your name?" "Legion," he answered, because many demons had entered him. And they begged Him repeatedly not to order them into the Abyss (8:26–31). Ancient manuscripts vary on the region's name—Gerasenes, Gadarenes, or Gergesenes; Matthew writes Gadarenes (Matthew 8:28)—all designating the predominantly Gentile eastern shore.

Nearby a large herd of pigs was feeding on the hillside. The demons begged Him to let them enter the pigs, and He gave them permission. Coming out of the man, the demons entered the pigs, and the herd rushed down the steep bank into the lake and drowned. The herdsmen fled and spread the news in town and countryside. People poured out to see what had happened, and finding the man from whom the demons had gone sitting at Jesus' feet, dressed and in his right mind, they were afraid. Eyewitnesses reported how the demon-possessed man had been saved. Then the entire population of the Gerasene region begged Jesus to leave them, seized with great fear. Boarding the boat, He turned back (8:32–37). They saw one human being restored and a herd of pigs wiped out—and asked Him to go away.

The rescued man begged to go with Him, yet Jesus sent him away saying, "Return home and describe how much God has done for you." So the man went throughout the whole town proclaiming how much Jesus had done for him (8:38–39). Jesus says "what God has done"; the man tells "what Jesus has done." Luke sets the two lines side by side without comment. This healed man becomes the first missionary in Gentile lands.

When Jesus returned, a crowd welcomed Him, for everyone had been waiting for Him. A synagogue ruler named Jairus came, fell at Jesus' feet, and pleaded with Him to come to his house, because his only daughter, about twelve years old, was dying. As Jesus went, crowds pressed tightly around Him. A woman who had suffered from bleeding for twelve years, whom no one could heal, came up behind Him and touched the fringe of His cloak; instantly her bleeding stopped. Jesus asked, "Who touched Me?" Everyone denied it, and Peter remarked: Master, the crowds are pressing and crowding around You! Jesus insisted: someone touched Me, for I know power has gone out from Me (8:40–46). Some early manuscripts omit the clause that she "had spent all she had on doctors" in verse 43. Under Mosaic law, her condition rendered her ritually unclean for twelve years—the exact lifespan of Jairus's daughter (Leviticus 15:25).

Seeing she could not go unnoticed, the woman came trembling, fell at His feet, and declared in front of everyone why she had touched Him and how she had been instantly healed. He told her, "Daughter, your faith has healed you. Go in peace." While He was still speaking, someone arrived from the synagogue ruler's house saying, "Your daughter is dead; do not bother the Teacher anymore." Hearing this, Jesus told Jairus, "Do not be afraid; just believe, and she will be healed" (8:47–50). Stopping for an unnamed woman seemed to cost Jairus's daughter her last chance—yet Jesus refuses to let Jairus give up.

Reaching the house, He let no one enter with Him except Peter, John, James, and the child's father and mother. Everyone outside was weeping and mourning for her. He said, "Stop weeping; she is not dead, but asleep." They laughed at Him, knowing she was dead. Taking her by the hand, He called out: child, get up! Her spirit returned, she stood up at once, and He ordered them to give her something to eat. Her parents were astonished, and He charged them to tell no one what had happened (8:51–56). Telling them to feed her confirms she is flesh-and-blood alive, not a phantom—and reveals His tender eye for a freshly awakened child's simplest need.

## Key Takeaways

**Women stand as faithful disciples and vital financial partners in Jesus' ministry.** Luke explicitly names Mary Magdalene, Joanna wife of Herod's household manager, and Susanna—healed women who travel alongside Him and support the mission from their own resources (8:1–3). In God's kingdom, women are full participants, never bystanders.

**How we listen to God's word shapes the harvest of an entire life.** The sower, the lamp on its stand, and Jesus' definition of His true family all converge on one urgent plea: watch carefully how you listen, hold the word in an honest heart, and persevere in living it out (8:15–21).

**Jesus holds sovereign power over four forces humanity cannot tame: storms, demons, chronic disease, and death.** Four consecutive miracles spanning the lake, the tombs of Gerasa, and Jairus's bedroom (8:22–56) drive the disciples' stunned question straight to the heart: "Who is this?" (8:25).

**Real faith reaches for Jesus right inside fear and utter helplessness.** Both a woman exhausted after twelve years of illness and a father facing his twelve-year-old daughter's death run completely out of options—and Jesus pauses personally for each one (8:41–55).

## Deeper Meaning

**Rebuking wind and waves recalls the Lord's sovereignty in the Psalms.** Old Testament praise celebrates God alone calming roaring storms and hushing ocean waves (Psalms 107:28–29). The scene on the lake (8:24–25) quietly answers the disciples' question: the One asleep in their boat wields the Creator's own command.

**The purpose of parables links directly to Isaiah's prophetic calling.** Quoting "though seeing, they may not see; though hearing, they may not understand" (8:10; Isaiah 6:9–10), Jesus shows that God's word acts like a mirror: hungry hearts grow brighter, while shut hearts grow darker.

**The rescued Gerasene becomes an early herald to the Gentile world.** A massive herd of pigs signals Gentile territory across the lake (8:26; 8:32). Remarkably, Jesus tells him to recount what *God* did, and he proclaims across town what *Jesus* did (8:39)—Luke quietly placing Jesus' saving work on the exact plane of God's own work.

**Jesus steps across two of the Law's strictest purity barriers to bring life.** Mosaic law declared chronic bleeding ceremonially unclean (Leviticus 15:25–27) and contact with a corpse defiling (Numbers 19:11). Neither the woman's touch nor clasping a dead girl's hand defiles Jesus; instead His life heals them both (8:44–55).

**The repeated number twelve hints at Israel's twelve tribes.** Twelve years of bleeding meet twelve years of childhood (8:42–43). Interweaving the rescue of two "daughters" signals Jesus coming to heal and restore God's entire covenant people.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why let demons enter and drown the pigs (8:32–33)?** Modern readers naturally feel concern for the animals and the villagers' loss. Scripture never trivializes creatures, yet draws an unmistakable hierarchy of worth: freeing a tormented human being living among graves outweighs an entire herd, while the plunging herd offers visible proof to both the man and his neighbors that the legion of spirits is truly gone.

**Jesus is neither cold nor dismissive toward His mother and brothers in 8:19–21.** He simply declares that God's kingdom family stretches wider than biology, united by hearing and obeying God's word. His own mother and brothers later join the believers praying together in the early church (Acts 1:14).

**Why ask "Who touched Me?" and bring the shy woman forward publicly (8:45–47)?** Not to embarrass her. After twelve years carrying shame and slipping behind Him in secret for fear of rejection, Jesus calls her out so the entire crowd hears she is fully restored—giving back her dignity and calling her "Daughter" (8:48).

**Why tell the Gerasene man to spread the news (8:39) yet silence Jairus's family (8:56)?** In Galilee, volatile messianic excitement easily sparked political misunderstandings, leading Jesus to curb publicity. Across the lake in Gentile territory that danger did not exist, freeing the healed man to testify openly at home.

**Ambrose**, fourth-century bishop of Milan, reads the storm-tossed boat of 8:22–25 as a portrait of the church crossing history's rough waters: Christ appears to sleep whenever disciples let their faith grow drowsy, yet the moment we wake our faith and cry out to Him, the storm subsides. *(Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, book 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the storm-tossed boat as the church is Ambrose's familiar reading in book 6; exact original phrasing unchecked -->

**Cyril of Alexandria**, fifth-century bishop, pauses over Jesus saying "I know that power has gone out from Me" when the bleeding woman touches His hem (8:46). Cyril sees vivid evidence that Christ's human flesh is intimately united with His divine nature, so that even a faith-filled touch of His garment receives healing life. *(Homilies on Luke)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Cyril is renowned for using Luke 8:46 to demonstrate the life-giving union of divinity and humanity in Christ's flesh -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century French Reformer, reflects on the Gerasenes begging Jesus to leave (8:37): greed and fear of losing property make people prize pigs above the salvation of their souls. Whenever Christ's power threatens comfortable self-interest, many politely show Him the door. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Luke 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Calvin's well-known commentary thrust on the Gerasenes prioritizing pigs over Christ; exact wording unchecked -->

**Richard Bauckham**, a British New Testament scholar, closely studies the named women of Luke 8:1–3—Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Susanna. Ancient writers rarely recorded women's personal names unless they were respected eyewitnesses known within the community; in particular, Joanna, wife of Herod's steward, may well have supplied Luke with inside details about Herod's court. *(Gospel Women, 2002; Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2006)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Bauckham's analysis of Joanna and the named women of Luke 8:1–3 is one of the best-known arguments in Gospel Women and Jesus and the Eyewitnesses -->

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, details ceremonial impurity rules surrounding prolonged uterine bleeding and daily contact boundaries.
