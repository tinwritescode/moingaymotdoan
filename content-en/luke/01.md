# Luke 1 — Two Announcements

## Story

Luke opens his book with a formal dedication, just as Greek historians of his era did. He writes to Theophilus, a man of rank. Many others had already undertaken to compile accounts of the events fulfilled among them, handed down by those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and servants of the word. Therefore, having carefully investigated everything from the start, Luke decides to write an orderly account so Theophilus may know the certainty of the things he has been taught (1:1–4). Luke never claims to have been an eyewitness himself; he is an investigator, and his Gospel is a careful historical report.

The story begins not in Bethlehem, but in the temple. During the reign of King Herod, there lived a priest named Zechariah from the division of Abijah, and his wife Elizabeth, a descendant of Aaron. Both were righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the Lord's commandments and statutes. Yet they had no child, because Elizabeth was barren, and both were well along in years. While his division was on duty, Zechariah was chosen by lot to enter the sanctuary of the Lord and burn incense while the gathered crowd prayed outside (1:5–10). A priest might receive that privilege only once in an entire lifetime. Today was already the greatest day of Zechariah's life—and it was about to grow far greater than he imagined.

An angel of the Lord appeared, standing to the right of the altar of incense. Zechariah was startled and gripped with fear. The angel said, "Do not be afraid, Zechariah, because your prayer has been heard." Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you are to name him John. He will be a joy and delight to you, and many will rejoice at his birth. He will be great in the sight of the Lord, never drinking wine or strong drink, and filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother's womb. He will turn many of the people of Israel back to the Lord their God, going before Him in the spirit and power of Elijah to turn the hearts of fathers to their children, to turn the disobedient to the wisdom of the righteous, and to make ready a people prepared for the Lord (1:11–17; Malachi 4:5–6). Malachi, the last prophetic book, had promised Elijah would return. After four hundred years of prophetic silence, that promise begins coming true.

Zechariah asked: how can I be sure of this, since I am an old man and my wife is well along in years? The angel answered: I am Gabriel, who stands in the presence of God, sent to speak to you and bring you this good news. Because you did not believe my words, you will be silent and unable to speak until the day these things happen. Meanwhile the people waiting outside wondered why he stayed so long in the sanctuary. Coming out unable to speak, he kept making signs to them, and they realized he had seen a vision. When his term of service ended, he returned home. Soon afterward Elizabeth conceived, stayed in seclusion for five months, and said: the Lord has done this for me, looking kindly upon me to take away my disgrace among the people (1:18–25). In that world, childlessness carried bitter social stigma for a woman. Elizabeth receives not only a child, but restored honor.

In the sixth month, Gabriel was sent to a small Galilean town called Nazareth, to a virgin named Mary pledged in marriage to Joseph, a descendant of David. The angel greeted her: "Greetings, you who are highly favored! The Lord is with you." Deeply troubled, Mary wondered what kind of greeting this might be. Do not be afraid, Mary, the angel said, for you have found favor with God. You will conceive and give birth to a son, and you are to name Him Jesus. He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High; the Lord God will give Him the throne of His father David. He will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and His kingdom will never end (1:26–33; 2 Samuel 7:12–16). First the angel visited an aging priest inside the capital's temple; next he visits a young girl in an obscure village.

Mary asked: how will this be, since I am a virgin? Zechariah's question sprang from doubt; Mary's asks how it will happen. The angel answered: the Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you, so the holy child to be born will be called the Son of God. Even Elizabeth your relative, who was called barren, is now in her sixth month of pregnancy, "For nothing will be impossible with God." Mary answered, "I am the Lord's servant. May it happen to me according to your word." Then the angel departed from her (1:34–38). For an unwed girl to turn up pregnant risked losing her husband, her reputation, and even her life. Still, Mary says yes.

Mary hurried to a town in the hill country of Judea, entered Zechariah's house, and greeted Elizabeth. The instant Elizabeth heard Mary's greeting, the baby leaped in her womb, and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit and cried out: blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb! "And why am I so honored, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?" Blessed is she who has believed that the Lord's word to her will be fulfilled (1:39–45). Even before birth, John begins his life's work—pointing to the One coming after him.

Mary responds with a song: "My soul magnifies the Lord," and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior, for He has looked with favor on the humble state of His servant. From now on all generations will call me blessed, for the Mighty One has done great things for me; holy is His name, and His mercy extends to those who fear Him from generation to generation. He has performed mighty deeds with His arm, scattered the proud in the thoughts of their hearts, brought down rulers from their thrones, lifted up the humble, filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty. He has helped His servant Israel, remembering to be merciful to Abraham and his descendants forever. Mary stayed with Elizabeth about three months and then returned home (1:46–56; 1 Samuel 2:1–10). This song echoes Hannah's song, mother of Samuel, and sounds the keynote for Luke's entire Gospel: God turns the world's order upside down.

When the time came, Elizabeth gave birth to a son, and neighbors and relatives rejoiced with her. On the eighth day, at his circumcision, they were about to name him Zechariah after his father. His mother spoke up, "No! He shall be called John." They protested that no one in her family bore that name, and made signs to the father to ask what he wanted to name the boy. Asking for a writing tablet, Zechariah wrote, "His name is John." Everyone was amazed. Immediately his mouth was opened, his tongue was loosed, and he began praising God. Fear came upon all their neighbors, news spread across the hill country of Judea, and everyone who heard took it to heart, asking: what then will this child become? For the Lord's hand was with him (1:57–66). Nine months of silence break the moment Zechariah obeys—his first written word is the name the angel commanded.

Filled with the Holy Spirit, Zechariah prophesied. He praised the Lord God of Israel for visiting and redeeming His people, raising up a mighty Savior in the house of David as the prophets long foretold—rescuing them from their enemies, remembering His holy covenant and the oath sworn to Abraham, so they might serve Him without fear in holiness and righteousness all their days. Then turning to his newborn son, he sang: and you, child, will be called a prophet of the Most High, going before the Lord to prepare His ways, to give His people knowledge of salvation through the forgiveness of their sins, because of the tender mercy of our God, by which the sunrise from on high will visit us, to shine on those living in darkness and in the shadow of death, and to guide our feet into the path of peace. The child grew and became strong in spirit, living in the wilderness until the day he appeared publicly to Israel (1:67–80; Isaiah 9:2; Malachi 4:2). Most of Zechariah's song praises the coming Savior, with only a brief stanza for his own son—he has grasped who his boy really is.

## Key Takeaways

**Christian faith stands on real historical events.** Luke opens not with moral maxims or myth, but with a historian's method: interviewing original eyewitnesses and setting events down in order so Theophilus can trust the certainty of what he learned (1:1–4). The gospel welcomes scrutiny.

**God chooses people on the margins to launch His greatest work.** Salvation's story does not start in royal palaces or prestigious circles, but with an elderly childless couple and a young girl in an obscure Galilean village (1:7; 1:26–27). Just when people feel forgotten or insignificant, God steps onto the stage.

**God's kingdom flips the world's familiar hierarchy upside down.** Mary's song unveils how God reigns: pulling down rulers from thrones, lifting the humble, filling the hungry, and sending the rich away empty (1:51–53). That great reversal runs right through the whole Gospel of Luke.

**God's rescue starts with forgiveness of sins.** In Zechariah's prophecy, deliverance from enemies is woven tightly together with knowing salvation through the forgiveness of sins, freeing God's people to serve Him unafraid and walk in peace (1:71; 1:74–79).

## Deeper Meaning

**Four hundred years of waiting for Elijah break open.** The Old Testament's final prophet closed with the promise that Elijah would come to turn fathers' hearts back to their children (Malachi 4:5–6). Gabriel announces John will go before the Lord in Elijah's spirit and power, turning the page after centuries of silence (1:17).

**Jesus inherits the eternal throne promised to David's house.** The angel's promise that God will give the child David's throne and an endless kingdom links directly back to God's covenant with King David (1:32–33; 2 Samuel 7:12–16).

**The Most High overshadowing Mary recalls the glory cloud on the tabernacle.** Long ago, God's cloud of glory overshadowed the tent of meeting so He could dwell among Israel in the desert (Exodus 40:34–35). Now that very power overshadows Mary so God's Son can take human flesh and dwell among us (1:35).

**Mary's song echoes Hannah's song from the days of the judges.** Both women lift up praise to the God who lifts the lowly and humbles the arrogant (1:46–55; 1 Samuel 2:1–10). Luke places Mary squarely in the lineage of Israel's great women of faith.

**The sunrise from on high fuses prophetic promises of light.** Zechariah hails the coming One as the dawn breaking from heaven to light up those sitting in darkness and death's shadow, blending twin promises from Isaiah and Malachi (1:78–79; Isaiah 9:2; Malachi 4:2).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why was Zechariah struck mute while Mary was not?** Both questioned the angel (1:18; 1:34). Yet Zechariah asked "How can I be sure?" demanding an extra sign out of unbelief despite Scripture's own stories of aged Abraham and Sarah conceiving (Genesis 21:1–2). Mary simply asked how an unprecedented virgin birth would happen—and immediately yielded in obedience (1:38).

**Was Theophilus a real individual or a symbolic name?** *Theophilus* means "lover of God," and Luke addresses him as "most excellent," an official courtesy title for Roman dignitaries (1:1). Most scholars think he was an actual patron underwriting the book's publication, while his fitting name invites every God-loving reader to listen in.

**Should Mary's song be read spiritually or socially?** Some read toppling rulers and feeding the hungry purely as spiritual metaphors for saving souls. Others read them as a direct manifesto for earthly justice for the poor. Throughout Luke, both strands belong inseparably together: the gospel transforms human hearts while demanding concrete justice for the vulnerable.

**Christian traditions honor Mary differently, yet unite around her faith.** Catholics hold Mary in high veneration, whereas Protestants stay cautious about later Marian dogmas. Still, Luke's text highlights two truths every Christian celebrates: Mary calls herself "the Lord's servant," and Elizabeth blesses her as "she who has believed" (1:38; 1:45).
