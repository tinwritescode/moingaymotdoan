# Luke 3 — A Voice in the Wilderness

## Story

Once again Luke anchors the story squarely in world history, this time naming seven powerful figures. It is the fifteenth year of Emperor Tiberius's reign. Pontius Pilate governs Judea, Herod rules Galilee, his brother Philip rules Iturea and Traconitis, Lysanias rules Abilene, and Annas and Caiaphas serve as high priests. Passing over all those corridors of power, the word of God comes instead to John, son of Zechariah, in the wilderness (3:1–2). The fifteenth year of Tiberius falls around AD 28 or 29.

John travels through the whole region around the Jordan, preaching a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins, just as Isaiah wrote: a voice of one calling in the wilderness, prepare the way for the Lord, make straight paths for Him; every valley will be filled in, every mountain and hill brought low, crooked roads made straight, rough ways made smooth, and all flesh will see God's salvation (3:3–6; Isaiah 40:3–5). Matthew and Mark stop at the first verse of Isaiah's quote; Luke extends it to "all flesh." From the start, he wants readers to know this gospel belongs to everybody.

To the crowds streaming out for baptism, John speaks without flinching: "You brood of vipers, who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?" Produce fruit worthy of repentance. Do not start telling yourselves Abraham is our father, for God can raise up children for Abraham from these very stones. The axe already lies at the root of the trees; every tree that fails to produce good fruit will be cut down and thrown into the fire (3:7–9). Family heritage rescues no one; John demands real change in how people live.

The crowds ask: what then should we do? John replies: whoever has two tunics must share with someone who has none, and whoever has food must do the same. Tax collectors arriving to be baptized ask too, and he instructs them, "Collect no more than you are authorized." Soldiers ask, and he tells them: extort no one by threats or false accusations, and be content with your wages (3:10–14). Only Luke preserves these practical answers. John tells neither tax collectors nor soldiers to quit their jobs; he tells them to practice their jobs honestly.

People are filled with expectancy, wondering silently whether John might be the Christ. John answers everyone: I baptize you with water, yet One more powerful than I is coming, the straps of whose sandals I am not worthy to untie. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire. His winnowing fork is in His hand to clear His threshing floor, gathering wheat into His barn while burning chaff with unquenchable fire. With many other exhortations, John preaches the good news to the people (3:15–18). Untying sandal straps was a lowly slave's task—yet the prophet revered by the whole nation insists he is unworthy even to do that for the One coming behind him.

Herod the tetrarch, however, stung by John's rebukes over Herodias his brother's wife and all his other wicked deeds, adds one more evil to them all: locking John up in prison (3:19–20). Luke reports John's imprisonment even before recounting Jesus' baptism, clearing John off the stage so Jesus steps wholly into the center. Mark tells the fuller story of John's execution (Mark 6:17–29).

When all the people were being baptized, Jesus was baptized too. As He was praying, heaven opened, the Holy Spirit descended on Him in bodily form like a dove, and a voice came from heaven: "You are My beloved Son; in You I am well pleased" (3:21–22; Psalms 2:7; Isaiah 42:1). Only Luke notes that Jesus was praying—throughout his Gospel, decisive moments in Jesus' life unfold right while He prays.

Beginning His ministry, Jesus was about thirty years old, thought to be the son of Joseph. Luke then traces His genealogy backward from Joseph through Zerubbabel, Nathan son of David, David, Boaz, Judah, Jacob, Isaac, Abraham, Noah, Enoch, and Seth all the way to Adam—"Adam, the son of God" (3:23–38). Matthew's genealogy starts at Abraham and moves through Solomon; Luke's runs through David's son Nathan and climbs all the way to Adam (Matthew 1:1–17). Various explanations have been proposed—such as one tracking Joseph's line and the other Mary's—yet Luke's core point shines unmistakably: Jesus belongs not solely to Israel, but to the entire human family descended from Adam.

## Key Takeaways

**God's word arrives in the wilderness, not inside royal palaces.** Luke parades a roster of political and religious powerbrokers from Caesar to the high priests, then abruptly swings the spotlight onto one lone man in the desert (3:1–2). True history pivots on God's word, not earthly thrones.

**Real repentance shows up in everyday fairness.** John strips away complacency over Abraham's family tree and demands tangible fruit (3:8). His instructions hit daily life concretely: share spare clothes and food, stop overcharging taxes, stop bullying and extorting the weak (3:11–14).

**Jesus' public ministry begins in prayer and heavenly confirmation.** Luke alone mentions Jesus praying as heaven opens, the Spirit descends, and the Father affirms His beloved Son (3:21–22). Jesus steps into His mission not on human drive, but in deep communion with Father and Spirit.

**The gospel reaches all of humanity, refusing narrow boundaries.** Luke purposefully carries Isaiah's prophecy down to "all flesh will see God's salvation" (3:6) and climbs Jesus' family tree all the way to Adam (3:38). The Savior belongs to every human being.

## Deeper Meaning

**The wilderness cry rekindles Isaiah's hope of homecoming.** Centuries earlier, Isaiah promised God would carve a highway across the desert to bring exiles home (Isaiah 40:3–5). John proclaims that word anew as a far greater rescue dawns—now embracing all nations (3:4–6).

**The heavenly voice at the Jordan unites two Old Testament portraits in one Person.** "You are My beloved Son; in You I am well pleased" (3:22) fuses the royal coronation psalm of the Messiah with Isaiah's Suffering Servant (Psalms 2:7; Isaiah 42:1). God's true King will reign through service and sacrifice.

**Baptism with the Holy Spirit and fire previews Pentecost.** John announces the Coming One will baptize not merely with water, but with Spirit and fire (3:16). Luke himself will recount its fulfillment in his second volume, when the Holy Spirit rests on the disciples like tongues of fire (Acts 2:3–4).

**Climbing back to Adam sets the stage for the wilderness testing ahead.** Ending the genealogy with "Adam, the son of God" (3:38), Luke moves straight into Jesus' temptation in chapter 4. The first Adam fell inside a lush garden; the Last Adam stands firm in a barren desert to restore humanity (Romans 5:14; 1 Corinthians 15:45).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why do Luke's and Matthew's genealogies differ?** Matthew traces David's line through King Solomon down to Jacob, father of Joseph (Matthew 1:6–16), while Luke runs through David's son Nathan down to Heli (3:23–31). The two most common explanations are that Luke records Mary's line (with Heli as Joseph's father-in-law), or that both track Joseph's lineage with a levirate marriage raising heirs for a childless brother under Old Testament law (Deuteronomy 25:5–6).

**Why are two high priests named—Annas and Caiaphas (3:2)?** Under Jewish law only one man officially held the high priesthood at a time—then Caiaphas. Yet Annas, his father-in-law, had previously held the office before Roman authorities removed him and continued wielding immense authority behind the scenes, so people still honored him with the title (John 18:13).

**Does baptism "with fire" mean judgment or purification?** The very next sentence depicts burning chaff with unquenchable fire (3:16–17), leading many scholars to hear judgment separating wheat from chaff. Read alongside Acts 2:3, however, fire also evokes the purifying power of the Holy Spirit.

**John tells neither tax collectors nor soldiers to abandon their professions.** His advice in 3:12–14 shows repentance does not necessarily require retreating from society into a desert, but practicing integrity, refusing greed, and defending the weak right inside one's daily calling.

## What Commentators Say

**Irenaeus**, late second-century bishop of Lyon, saw Luke's backward genealogy from Jesus to Adam (3:23–38) as a centerpiece of his doctrine of recapitulation—summing up all things in Christ. Jesus gathers the entire human story from Adam into Himself to restart and heal everything Adam broke. *(Against Heresies, book 3, chapter 22)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Irenaeus explicitly uses Luke's 72-generation reverse genealogy in book 3 chapter 22 to illustrate recapitulation -->
