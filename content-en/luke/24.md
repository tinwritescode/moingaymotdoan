# Luke 24 — Were Not Our Hearts Burning Within Us?

## Story

On the first day of the week, at early dawn, the women went to the tomb carrying the spices they had prepared. They found the stone rolled away from the entrance, and going inside they did not find the body of the Lord Jesus. While they stood perplexed, suddenly two men in dazzling clothes stood beside them. Terrified, the women bowed their faces to the ground. The two men asked, "Why do you look for the living among the dead?" He is not here; He has risen. Remember what He told you while still in Galilee: the Son of Man must be delivered into the hands of sinful men, be crucified, and on the third day rise again. Then they remembered His words (24:1–8). The women came to anoint a corpse. The angels do not produce new proofs; they urge them to remember what He already said.

Returning from the tomb, the women reported all these things to the Eleven and to all the others. They were Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and the other women with them. Yet their report struck the apostles as nonsense, and they did not believe them. Even so, Peter got up and ran to the tomb; stooping down, he saw only the linen strips lying there, and went home wondering what had happened (24:9–12). In that era, women's testimony carried little weight in court. Anyone inventing a resurrection story would hardly have chosen women as the primary witnesses. Verse 12 is omitted in one ancient manuscript, yet appears in nearly all others, including very early witnesses.

That very same day, two of the disciples were walking to a village called Emmaus, sixty stadia—about eleven kilometers—from Jerusalem. They were talking with each other about everything that had happened. As they conversed and debated, Jesus Himself drew near and walked alongside them, though their eyes were kept from recognizing Him. He asked, "What are these things you are discussing as you walk along?" They stopped, faces downcast. One of them, named Cleopas, answered: are you the only visitor in Jerusalem who does not know the things that have happened there in these days? (24:13–18). They are walking away from Jerusalem, turning their backs on the place where everything fell apart—and the Lord joins them on that very road.

"What things?" He asked. They replied: the things about Jesus of Nazareth, a prophet mighty in deed and word before God and all the people; how our chief priests and rulers handed Him over to be sentenced to death and crucified Him. "We had hoped that He was the one who was going to redeem Israel," and besides all that, it is now the third day since these things happened. Some women among us also amazed us: they went to the tomb early in the morning, did not find His body, and came back saying they had seen a vision of angels who said He was alive. Some of our companions went to the tomb and found it just as the women said, yet Him they did not see (24:19–24). "We had hoped"—past tense. They had heard every piece of evidence, yet hope had died along with Him.

Jesus told them, "How foolish you are, and how slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken!" "Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and then enter His glory?" Then, starting with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them what was said about Himself throughout all the Scriptures. As they approached the village where they were heading, He acted as though He would go farther. But they urged Him strongly, "Stay with us, for it is nearly evening and the day is almost over." So He went in to stay with them (24:25–29). Every reader longs to know which passages He cited. Luke does not list them, though the speeches in Acts offer strong clues: Psalm 16, Psalm 110, and Isaiah 53.

When He sat at table with them, He took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and began handing it to them. Instantly their eyes were opened and they recognized Him, and He vanished from their sight. They said to each other, "Were not our hearts burning within us while He talked with us on the road and opened the Scriptures to us?" That very hour they got up and returned to Jerusalem, where they found the Eleven and those gathered with them saying, "The Lord has truly risen and has appeared to Simon!" Then the two recounted what had happened on the road, and how they had recognized Him when He broke the bread (24:30–35). The verbs mirror both the feeding of the five thousand and the Last Supper (Luke 9:16; Luke 22:19). The guest becomes the host of the meal. Eleven kilometers in the dark, and they walk straight back that same night.

While they were still telling this, Jesus Himself stood among them and said, "Peace be with you!" Startled and frightened, they supposed they were seeing a spirit. He asked why they were troubled and why doubts rose in their hearts. "Look at My hands and My feet; it is I Myself. Touch Me and see, because a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see I have." Saying this, He showed them His hands and feet. While they still could not quite believe it for sheer joy and amazement, He asked if they had anything to eat. They gave Him a piece of broiled fish, and He took it and ate it right in front of them (24:36–43). Luke underscores that Jesus rose in a real body, not as a disembodied ghost or hallucination: He can be touched, bears His wounds, and eats food. A few phrases here, including the greeting of peace in verse 36 and all of verse 40, are missing from one ancient manuscript, though present in almost all others.

Then He told them: this is what I told you while I was still with you—everything written about Me in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms must be fulfilled. Then He opened their minds to understand the Scriptures. He explained: it is written that the Christ will suffer and rise from the dead on the third day, and that repentance for the forgiveness of sins will be proclaimed in His name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem. You are witnesses of these things. I am sending upon you what My Father promised; stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high (24:44–49). Law, Prophets, and Psalms represent the three divisions of the Hebrew Scriptures: every part points to Him. Verses 47–49 sketch the outline of Acts: the Spirit arrives, and the gospel moves outward from Jerusalem to all nations.

He led them out as far as Bethany, lifted His hands, and blessed them. While He was blessing them, He parted from them and was carried up into heaven. They worshiped Him and returned to Jerusalem with great joy, spending their time continually in the temple praising God (24:50–53). Luke's Gospel opened in the temple, with the priest Zechariah unable to pronounce a blessing on the people because he had been struck mute (Luke 1:21–22). It closes with Jesus raising His hands in blessing, and His disciples filling the temple with praise to God. One ancient manuscript lacks the phrases "was carried up into heaven" and "worshiped Him," though Luke narrates the ascension in detail at the opening of Acts (Acts 1:9–11).

## Key Takeaways

**No one believes immediately.** The women at the tomb are "perplexed" (24:4); the apostles dismiss their report as "nonsense" (24:11); the two travelers to Emmaus are called "slow of heart to believe" (24:25); even when He stands right among them, the disciples still hesitate out of sheer joy (24:41). Luke does not portray credulous followers waiting eagerly for a miracle. He portrays defeated people whose hope had collapsed, gently led step by step into faith by the risen Lord.

**The cross is not where the story breaks, but where it makes sense.** The two disciples assumed His death disproved their hope that He would redeem Israel (24:21). Jesus responds, "Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and then enter His glory?" (24:26). What looked like catastrophe was the very path Scripture foretold. Suffering is not erased, but placed at the center of God's saving design.

**The Lord is recognized at the table.** Along the entire walk, the two disciples hear Him explain Scripture without realizing who He is. Recognition dawns only when He breaks the bread (24:30–31; 24:35). Scripture makes their hearts "burn" (24:32), while the shared meal opens their eyes. Luke sets those two moments side by side: Scripture opened and bread broken are the twin places where the risen Lord meets His disciples.

**Resurrection means a real body, not a phantom.** The disciples think they are looking at a spirit (24:37). Jesus invites them to touch Him, displays His hands and feet, and eats fish before their eyes (24:39–43). The hope Luke announces is not a soul escaping the body, but this very body raised to new life by God.

**The Gospel closes in mission and joy.** From Jerusalem, repentance and forgiveness of sins will be heralded to "all nations" (24:47). Yet before setting out, they must wait for power from on high (24:49). The very people who hid in grief three days earlier return to the city "with great joy" and stay continually in the temple praising God (24:52–53).

## Deeper Meaning

**Eyes opened at a meal.** In the Garden of Eden, after eating the forbidden fruit, "the eyes of both of them were opened," and they saw their nakedness (Genesis 3:7). At Emmaus, as two disciples eat bread placed in their hands by Jesus, "their eyes were opened and they recognized Him" (24:31). Scripture's first meal opened human eyes to shame; this meal opens human eyes to the risen Lord.

**The breaking of bread in the early Church.** In Acts, Luke records that the first believers "devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer" (Acts 2:42). That phrase "breaking of bread" matches the exact wording in 24:35. The supper at Emmaus is more than one evening's event; it establishes the pattern for every gathering of the Church around the Lord's table.

**On the third day.** The angels remind the women of Jesus' word that the Son of Man must be crucified "and on the third day rise again" (24:7). Hosea had spoken of God's people, "On the third day He will raise us up, that we may live in His presence" (Hosea 6:2). What the prophet hoped for the whole nation, Jesus fulfills first in Himself.

**A light for the nations.** In Isaiah, God tells His Servant that restoring the tribes of Jacob is "too small a thing": "I will also make you a light for the nations" (Isaiah 49:6). Aged Simeon had held the infant Jesus in the temple and sung of that very light (2:32). At the Gospel's close, the risen Lord commissions His disciples to bring forgiveness to "all nations" (24:47).

**A priest raising His hands to bless.** After presenting the sacrifices, "Aaron lifted up his hands toward the people and blessed them" (Leviticus 9:22). Jesus departs from His disciples with that exact gesture, "lifting up His hands and blessing them" (24:50). The sacrifice is complete; now comes the blessing. His final act on earth is not reproach, but benediction.

**The Father's promise.** Joel foretold, "I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh" (Joel 2:28). Jesus calls this "what My Father promised" (24:49). On the day of Pentecost, Peter stands up to preach and quotes that very passage from Joel (Acts 2:16–17). Luke closes with the promise; Acts opens with its fulfillment.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did they fail to recognize Him?** Luke simply says their eyes "were kept from recognizing Him" (24:16), as though something held their sight back. Some think grief blinded them, or simply that nobody expects to meet someone who died. Others see God intentionally withholding recognition until the right moment, so that they would know Him through Scripture and the breaking of bread rather than surface appearance alone. His risen body may also have been transformed: in John's Gospel, Mary Magdalene likewise fails to recognize Him at first (John 20:14–15).

**Galilee or Jerusalem?** Matthew and Mark record an appointment to meet Jesus in Galilee (Mark 16:7; Matthew 28:16). Luke situates all the resurrection appearances in and around Jerusalem, and here the angels say, "Remember how He spoke to you while He was still in Galilee" (24:6), rather than telling them to travel there. This need not be a contradiction: Jesus appeared repeatedly over forty days in multiple locations. Each evangelist selects scenes fitting his narrative focus. For Luke, Jerusalem is the pivot where redemption occurs and the starting point from which the gospel goes out (24:47).

**Did He ascend on Easter Sunday itself?** Read straight through, chapter 24 can make every event look compressed into a single day: empty tomb at dawn, Emmaus in the afternoon, appearance at evening, and ascension right after. Yet Luke himself opens Acts by explaining that Jesus appeared to the apostles "over a period of forty days" (Acts 1:3). The author of both books knew the timeline well. At the close of Luke he summarizes tightly to round off the Gospel; at the start of Acts he unpacks the interval to launch the sequel.

**What kind of body is a resurrection body?** Jesus possesses flesh and bones, eats food, and can be touched (24:39–43). Yet He also "vanished from their sight" (24:31) and stood suddenly in the middle of the room (24:36). Paul describes it as a "spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44)—not a body made of mist or vapor, but a body animated by the Spirit and no longer subject to death. It is the same body, still carrying nail marks, yet gloriously transformed.

**Where was Emmaus?** Most manuscripts place the village sixty stadia—around eleven kilometers—from Jerusalem (24:13), while a few read one hundred sixty stadia. Scholars have proposed several sites, and no identification is certain. Perhaps that uncertainty suits the story: Emmaus can stand for any road down which discouraged people walk away.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, late fourth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, preached repeatedly on Emmaus during Easter week. He urged listeners not to lament that they cannot see Christ physically as the disciples did, since the Lord chose to make Himself known in the breaking of bread—which the Church still celebrates. **Gregory the Great**, late sixth-century bishop of Rome, preached on Emmaus and noted that the two disciples did not recognize Christ while He explained Scripture, yet knew Him when they set a table for the stranger. From this he concluded that hearing God's word alone is not enough; it must be put into practice, and welcoming strangers is a place where believers encounter Christ Himself. *(Forty Gospel Homilies, homily 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled homily 23 of Gregory on Luke 24 highlighting hospitality; exact homily number unverified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, noted that when Jesus began "with Moses" to interpret the things concerning Himself (24:27), He taught that the entire Old Testament points toward Christ. Anyone who reads the Law and the Prophets without finding Christ misses their true aim. Calvin also observed that Christ's "opening their minds" (24:45) proves human readers need the Spirit's illumination to grasp Scripture. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the themes that all Scripture points to Christ and requires the Spirit to open minds are typical of Calvin here; exact wording not checked -->

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, recorded that after the war of AD 70, Emperor Vespasian settled eight hundred military veterans at a place called Emmaus not far from Jerusalem. Many scholars draw on this detail in searching for Luke's village, pointing toward the area of Motza west of Jerusalem. Still, the distances preserved in Josephus's manuscripts and Luke's do not align cleanly, leaving the location debated. *(The Jewish War, book 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Josephus mentioning Vespasian settling veterans at Emmaus in book 7; exact distance readings across Josephus manuscripts unverified -->

**N. T. Wright**, British bishop and New Testament scholar, wrote that on the Emmaus road Jesus did not merely string together isolated proof texts. Instead, He retold the entire story of Israel—a story passing through exile and suffering on its way to redemption—and showed how it reaches its climax in the crucified and risen Messiah. Wright also emphasized that Gospel resurrection accounts contain unexpected touches unlikely to be fabricated at the time, such as the risen Lord eating fish and going unrecognized at first. *(The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Wright discusses Emmaus and unusual features of resurrection narratives in this work; summary of Israel's story drawn from memory without checking chapter -->
