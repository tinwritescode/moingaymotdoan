# Luke 7 — The One Forgiven Much

## Story

After finishing His words in the hearing of the people, Jesus entered Capernaum. There a Roman centurion had a servant whom he valued highly, who was sick and near death. Hearing about Jesus, he sent some Jewish elders to Him, begging Him to come heal his servant. They pleaded earnestly with Jesus: this man deserves Your help, "for he loves our nation and has built our synagogue." So Jesus went with them (7:1–6). An officer of the occupying army defended by Jewish elders—a Gentile God-fearer.

When He was not far from the house, the centurion sent friends to tell Him, "Lord, do not trouble Yourself, for I am not worthy to have You come under my roof." That is why I did not even consider myself worthy to come to You; "But just say the word, and my servant will be healed." For I myself am a man under authority, with soldiers under me: I tell one to go and he goes, another to come and he comes. Hearing this, Jesus marveled at him and, turning to the crowd following Him, declared, "I tell you, not even in Israel have I found such great faith." When the messengers returned home, they found the servant completely well (7:6–10). The elders called him "worthy"; he called himself "unworthy." The very man claiming zero merit wins praise for the greatest faith.

The next day He went to a town called Nain, accompanied by His disciples and a large crowd. Approaching the town gate, a dead man was being carried out—the only son of his mother, and she was a widow—with a sizable crowd from the town walking with her. Seeing her, the Lord felt deep compassion for her and said, "Do not weep." Stepping forward, He touched the bier, and the bearers stood still. He said: young man, I say to you, get up! The dead man sat up and began to talk, and Jesus gave him back to his mother (7:11–15). Nobody asked Him for a thing; compassion moved Him first. Losing her only son stripped a widow of her last means of survival. Giving him back to his mother echoes nearly word for word Elijah's miracle at Zarephath (1 Kings 17:23).

Awe gripped everyone, and they glorified God: "A great prophet has appeared among us! God has visited His people!" News about Jesus spread across Judea and the entire surrounding region. John's disciples told him all about these things. Summoning two of them, John sent them to the Lord to ask, "Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?" (7:16–20). John sits locked in prison (3:20). The prophet who heralded a Coming One gripping a winnowing fork (3:17) now hears reports of healing and dining with sinners, and longs to know for sure.

At that very hour Jesus cured many of diseases, afflictions, and evil spirits, and gave sight to many who were blind. Then He answered them: go report to John what you have seen and heard: "The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor." And He added, "Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me" (7:21–23; Isaiah 35:5–6; Isaiah 61:1). Rather than answering with a blunt yes or no, He invites John to match His works against Isaiah—the exact passage He read aloud in Nazareth.

After John's messengers departed, Jesus spoke to the crowds about John: what did you go out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken by the wind? A man dressed in fine clothes? People wearing splendid robes and living in luxury live in royal palaces. What then did you go out to see—a prophet? Yes, and more than a prophet! He is the one Scripture speaks of: "Behold, I will send My messenger ahead of You." Among those born of women there is none greater than John; yet the least in the kingdom of God is greater than he. All the people—even tax collectors—acknowledged God's justice, having been baptized by John; meanwhile the Pharisees and experts in the Law rejected God's purpose for themselves by refusing his baptism (7:24–30; Malachi 3:1).

To what can I compare the people of this generation? They are like children sitting in the marketplace calling to one another: "We played the flute for you, and you did not dance; we sang a dirge, and you did not weep." John the Baptist came neither eating bread nor drinking wine, and you say he has a demon. The Son of Man came eating and drinking, and you say, "Look at this glutton and drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!" Yet wisdom is proved right by all her children (7:31–35). People determined not to listen find faults either way: fasting looks crazy, feasting looks loose.

One of the Pharisees, named Simon, invited Jesus to dinner, so He went to the Pharisee's house and reclined at table. In that town lived a woman widely known as a sinner. Learning Jesus was dining at the Pharisee's house, she brought an alabaster jar of perfume. Standing behind Him at His feet weeping, she began wetting His feet with her tears, wiping them with her hair, kissing them, and pouring perfume over them. Watching this, the Pharisee thought to himself: if this man were a prophet, he would know who is touching him and what kind of woman she is—that she is a sinner (7:36–39). At ancient banquets guests reclined on low couches with their feet angled back away from the table, letting her reach His feet.

Jesus spoke up: "Simon, I have something to tell you." A moneylender had two debtors: one owed five hundred denarii, the other fifty. Neither had the money to pay, so he freely canceled both debts. Which of them will love him more? Simon answered: I suppose the one who had the bigger debt canceled. Turning toward the woman, Jesus said to Simon, "Do you see this woman?" I entered your house and you gave Me no water for My feet, no welcoming kiss, no oil for My head; yet she has wet My feet with her tears, wiped them with her hair, has not stopped kissing My feet, and poured perfume on them. Therefore her many sins have been forgiven, for she has loved much; whereas whoever is forgiven little loves little. Then He told her, "Your sins are forgiven." The other dinner guests muttered among themselves, "Who is this who even forgives sins?" Yet Jesus said to the woman, "Your faith has saved you; go in peace" (7:40–50). Simon wondered whether Jesus knew who she was—He knew her completely, and He knew Simon too. As the parable makes unmistakable, her overflowing love flows from forgiveness already received, never as payment to earn it.

## Key Takeaways

**The greatest faith appears in the most surprising place: a Roman officer claiming zero merit.** Jewish elders lobby for the centurion because he built their synagogue, yet he insists he is unworthy even to host Jesus and asks simply for a spoken word (7:4–7). Complete confidence in the authority of Christ's word astonishes Jesus Himself (7:9).

**Jesus' compassion moves first toward the utterly broken before anyone even asks.** Meeting the funeral of the widow of Nain's only son—her sole lifeline—He steps straight up, tells her not to weep, touches the bier, and calls the young man back to life (7:12–15).

**Christ never scolds the honest bewilderment of a faithful servant.** When imprisoned John asks if Jesus really is the Coming One, Jesus gently points to healing signs fulfilling Scripture and then honors John publicly before the crowds (7:19–28).

**Whoever realizes how much they have been forgiven overflows with love.** Inside Simon the Pharisee's dining room, a woman with a ruined reputation pours out gratitude through tears, hair, and perfume—exposing the gulf between a heart transformed by grace and cold religious respectability (7:36–50).

## Deeper Meaning

**The miracle at Nain's gate echoes Elijah raising the widow of Zarephath's son.** Luke reports Jesus handing the boy back to his mother using nearly identical phrasing to the Old Testament (7:15; 1 Kings 17:23). Small wonder the stunned crowd declares a great prophet has arisen and God has visited His people (7:16).

**Jesus' message to John gathers Isaiah's signs of the Messianic age.** Opening blind eyes, making the lame walk, unstopping deaf ears, and preaching good news to the poor (7:22) fulfill Isaiah's promises of divine rescue (Isaiah 35:5–6; Isaiah 61:1). Jesus helps John see that God's kingdom arrives first in healing mercy ahead of final judgment.

**Honoring John ties directly to the closing prophecy of the Old Testament.** Naming John the forerunner preparing the way (7:27) quotes Malachi (Malachi 3:1), marking John as the hinge closing the old covenant era and swinging open God's new kingdom.

**The two debtors prove grace always comes first and sparks love.** Neither debtor can pay a penny, so both debts are canceled freely, and the greater cancellation wakes deeper love (7:41–43)—mirroring the New Testament's core truth: we love because God first loved us (1 John 4:19).

**Forgiving and blessing the woman continues the thread of Christ's divine authority.** Just as with the paralytic in chapter 5 (5:20–24), dinner guests whisper in shock over who dares forgive sins (7:49), while Jesus confirms that faith in Him brings real salvation and peace (7:50).

## Notes & Nuances

**Do not confuse the anointing woman of Luke 7 with Mary Magdalene or Mary of Bethany.** Older Western tradition blended this unnamed woman in 7:37 together with Mary, Lazarus's sister (John 12:3), and Mary Magdalene, who first appears right at the start of the next chapter (8:2). Scripture never equates them: Luke 7 takes place early in Galilee at a Pharisee's dinner, quite distinct from the Bethany anointing right before Passion Week (Mark 14:3–9).

**The woman did not purchase forgiveness with her love; she loved extravagantly because she had been forgiven.** Verse 7:47—"her many sins have been forgiven, for she has loved much"—can easily be read backward if pulled out of context. The preceding parable of the two debtors (7:41–43), the balancing line at the end of verse 47—"whoever has been forgiven little loves little"—and verse 7:50—"Your faith has saved you"—all show fierce love is the fruit and evidence of grace already received.

**John the Baptist's doubt was genuinely human and understandable.** Locked in Herod's dungeon after announcing a fiery Messiah clearing the threshing floor (3:17), hearing that Jesus healed people and dined with sinners without striking down oppressors left him longing for personal confirmation from Jesus Himself.

**Calling the least in God's kingdom greater than John (7:28) speaks of redemptive privilege, not moral rank.** Jesus just praised John as unsurpassed among all earlier prophets. Yet John stood on the doorstep pointing toward the Messiah, whereas even the humblest believer living after the cross and resurrection enjoys the full reality prophets only glimpsed from afar.

## What Commentators Say

**Ambrose**, fourth-century bishop of Milan, reads the widow of Nain (7:11–15) on two rich levels: first as Jesus' concrete tenderness toward a grieving mother, and also as an image of the church weeping like a mother over children dead in sin until Christ touches them and calls them back to life. *(Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, book 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Ambrose's reading of the widow of Nain as the church weeping over spiritually dead children appears in book 5 of his commentary on Luke -->

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa in the fourth and fifth centuries, preached on Simon's dinner and the two debtors (7:36–50) to clarify pure grace. Because neither debtor had anything to repay, nobody brings merit before God; forgiving grace always moves first, and the woman's passionate devotion blossoms out of gratitude. *(Sermons)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Augustine's argument on the two debtors and grace preceding love in his Luke 7 preaching; exact sermon numbering unverified -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century French Reformer, explains John sending his disciples to Jesus (7:19) not as John losing his own faith, but as a shepherd wanting his followers to see Jesus' deeds firsthand so they would transfer their allegiance to the Messiah before John's execution. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Luke 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: arguing John sent his disciples for their own faith rather than out of personal doubt is Calvin's signature interpretation of Luke 7:19 -->

**Kenneth Bailey**, an American scholar who spent decades living in the Middle East, illuminates the hospitality customs inside Simon's home (7:44–46). Ancient Middle Eastern etiquette called for a host welcoming an honored teacher to provide foot-washing water, a greeting kiss, and olive oil; Simon coldly withheld all three minimum courtesies to keep Jesus at arm's length, while the scorned woman supplied every one with her tears, hair, and precious perfume. *(Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes, 2008)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Bailey's analysis of Middle Eastern hospitality norms at Simon's dinner is a famous chapter of Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes -->

**Gregory the Great**, sixth-century pope, preached on the anointing woman in 7:36–50 that everything she had once used in service of sin—her eyes, her hair, her perfume—she now offered back in tearful repentance to serve her Lord.
