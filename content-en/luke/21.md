# Luke 21 — Not One Stone Left upon Another

## Story

Jesus looked up and saw rich people putting their gifts into the temple treasury. He also saw a poor widow drop in two tiny copper coins. He declared, "Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put in more than all the others." Those others gave out of their surplus, whereas she, out of her poverty, put in all she had to live on (21:1–4). Moments earlier, He had warned against those who "devour widows' houses" (Luke 20:47). God does not measure an offering by the amount given, but by what remains afterward.

Some people were talking about the temple, admiring how it was adorned with fine stones and votive gifts. Jesus said that the days were coming when not a single stone of what they saw would be left upon another; every one would be thrown down. They asked Him, "Teacher, when will these things happen, and what will be the sign that they are about to take place?" (21:5–7). Rebuilt by Herod the Great, the temple stood among the grandest structures in the ancient world, incorporating stone blocks more than ten meters long. In AD 70, Roman armies burned and demolished it.

He answered: watch out that you are not deceived, for many will come in My name claiming, "I am he," and "The time is near." Do not go after them. When you hear of wars and uprisings, do not panic, for these things must happen first, yet the end will not come right away. Nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom; there will be great earthquakes, famines, and plagues in various places, along with terrifying sights and great signs from heaven (21:8–11). His opening reply is not a timetable, but a caution: do not be misled, and do not give way to fear. Such upheavals are not signs of the end, but the ordinary reality of a fallen world.

Yet before all of that, people will seize and persecute you for My name's sake, handing you over to synagogues and prisons and dragging you before kings and governors. "This will become your opportunity to bear witness." So settle it in your hearts not to worry ahead of time about how to defend yourselves, because I will give you words and wisdom that none of your adversaries will be able to resist or refute. You will be betrayed even by parents, brothers, relatives, and friends, and they will put some of you to death. Everyone will hate you because of My name. "Yet not a single hair of your head will perish." "By your patient endurance you will gain your lives." (21:12–19). Those final two statements sit side by side in striking tension: some will be killed, and yet not one hair will be lost. The body can be harmed, but their true selves remain safe in God's hands. Acts, Luke's second volume, records each of these things coming true for the apostles.

When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, recognize that its desolation is near. Then those in Judea must flee to the mountains, those inside the city must get out, and those in the countryside must not enter it. "For these are days of vengeance, to fulfill all that is written." How dreadful it will be for pregnant women and nursing mothers in those days! Great distress will fall upon the land and wrath upon this people. They will fall by the edge of the sword and be led away captive among all nations, and Jerusalem will be trampled by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled (21:20–24). Luke speaks more plainly than Matthew and Mark: armies surrounding the city. According to the fourth-century historian Eusebius, Christians in Jerusalem left the city before the siege closed in, fleeing to Pella across the Jordan River.

There will be signs in the sun, moon, and stars, and on earth nations will be in anguish and perplexity at the roaring and tossing of the sea. People will faint from terror as they anticipate what is coming upon the world, for the heavenly powers will be shaken. "Then they will see the Son of Man coming in a cloud with power and great glory." When these things begin to happen, "stand up and lift up your heads, because your redemption is drawing near." (21:25–28; Daniel 7:13–14). One event produces two opposite responses: the world is overcome with dread, while the disciples lift their heads. What terrifies the world is glad tidings for those who belong to Him.

He told them a parable: look at the fig tree and all the trees. As soon as they sprout leaves, you can see for yourselves that summer is near. In the same way, when you see these things happening, know that the kingdom of God is near. Truly, this generation will not pass away until all these things take place. "Heaven and earth will pass away, but My words will never pass away." (21:29–33). The saying about "this generation" ranks among the hardest verses in the Gospels. Some understand it as referring to the fall of Jerusalem, which came within forty years; others take "generation" to mean an entire people or humankind as a whole.

Be careful, or your hearts will be weighed down with carousing, drunkenness, and the anxieties of this life, and that day will spring upon you suddenly like a trap, for it will come upon everyone living across the whole face of the earth. "Therefore keep watch at all times and pray that you may have strength to escape all that is about to happen, and to stand before the Son of Man." (21:34–36). The discourse closes not with a calendar, but with a summons to stay awake. The greatest danger is not persecution, but drifting asleep under the weight of everyday concerns.

Each day Jesus taught in the temple, and each night He went out to stay on the hill called the Mount of Olives. Early in the morning, all the people came to the temple to listen to Him (21:37–38). These are the final days before Passover. One group of manuscripts places the account of the woman caught in adultery—familiar from John 7:53 and 8:1–11—right after verse 38, probably because the setting matches: staying on the Mount of Olives and teaching the people in the temple at dawn.

## Key Takeaways

**People admire fine stones; Jesus notices a widow.** Within the very same temple, onlookers marvel at the "fine stones and offerings" (21:5), while Jesus watches an overlooked woman with two of the smallest coins in circulation (21:1–2). Every massive stone will come crashing down (21:6). The widow's gift is remembered and commended by Him.

**Do not trust anyone claiming "the time is at hand."** Luke records that impostors will announce, "The time is near" (21:8). Jesus' first warning targets not outside enemies, but voices using His name to stir disciples into panic or feverish excitement. Wars, earthquakes, and famines will come, yet "the end will not come at once" (21:9).

**Persecution becomes an arena for testimony, and the Lord supplies the words.** Before the cosmic upheavals, disciples will be hauled before synagogues, kings, and governors (21:12). Jesus does not promise they will escape trouble. He promises that He Himself will give them "words and wisdom" (21:15), and declares that steady endurance—holding firm over the long haul—will preserve their lives (21:19).

**The city's ruin is real, yet bounded.** Luke paints a stark picture of besieging armies, refugees fleeing to the hills, and captives led away (21:20–24). Still, the verse pivots on the word "until": Jerusalem is trampled by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled (21:24). Catastrophe does not get the last word in history.

**Lift your head and stay alert.** While the world faints in terror, disciples are told to "stand up and lift up your heads" (21:28). Even so, the final peril Jesus highlights is not fear, but dissipation, drunkenness, and daily worries dulling the heart (21:34). People seldom lose their faith through one dramatic wave of persecution; far more often, it erodes gradually under ordinary distractions.

## Deeper Meaning

**The widow of Zarephath.** At the outset of His ministry, Jesus recalled the widow of Zarephath to whom Elijah was sent (4:25–26). She had only "a handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug," yet she baked bread for the prophet first (1 Kings 17:12–13). The temple widow acts in the same spirit, giving her very last portion. Luke frames His Gospel near its beginning and near its close with two destitute widows whom God notices.

**Wisdom no opponent can withstand.** The promise of 21:15 unfolds in Luke's second book. When opponents debated Stephen, "they could not stand up against his wisdom or the Spirit by whom he spoke" (Acts 6:10). Shortly afterward, they stoned him to death. The promise held true, and still he died—just as 21:16 foretold.

**Not a hair will fall.** When King Saul intended to execute Jonathan, the people swore that "not one hair of his head shall fall to the ground" (1 Samuel 14:45). That Hebrew idiom expressed complete safety. Jesus applies that exact idiom to disciples who may nonetheless be put to death (21:16–18). He had already assured them that every hair on their heads is numbered (12:7).

**Fulfilling all that is written.** Jesus calls the siege "days of vengeance, to fulfill all that is written" (21:22). Moses had warned that if Israel broke the covenant, a distant nation would "besiege you in all your cities" until the tall, fortified walls they trusted fell down (Deuteronomy 28:52). Hosea likewise announced, "The days of punishment have come" (Hosea 9:7). Jesus reads the looming crisis through the lens of the prophets.

**Paul's "until."** Luke states that Jerusalem will be trampled until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled (21:24). Paul similarly writes that a partial hardening has come upon Israel "until the full number of the Gentiles has come in," adding, "and in this way all Israel will be saved" (Romans 11:25–26). Both writers speak of a limited season and keep the door open for hope beyond it.

**A snare over all the earth.** Isaiah pictures the day the Lord judges the world: "Terror and pit and snare await you, O inhabitants of the earth" (Isaiah 24:17). Jesus takes up the image of a closing trap and warns that the day will come "upon all who live on the face of the whole earth" (21:34–35). The warning reaches far beyond one city; it addresses everyone.

## Notes & Nuances

**Praise for the widow, or lament?** Most readers hear Jesus commending her: she gave the least yet gave more than everyone else (21:3). Some interpreters read the episode differently, noting that Jesus has just condemned scribes who "devour widows' houses" (20:47) and immediately predicts the destruction of the very temple taking her money (21:6). On that reading, He laments a religious system that drains a poor woman of "everything she had to live on" (21:4). The two readings do not cancel each other out: the Lord sees her devotion and also sees the injustice surrounding her.

**Luke separates the city's fall from the coming of the Son of Man.** Where Matthew and Mark speak of the "abomination of desolation" set up in the holy place, Luke speaks plainly of armies surrounding Jerusalem (21:20), making the warning intelligible to non-Jewish readers. Luke also inserts an interval between the two events: the city is trampled "until" the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled (21:24), and only then come the heavenly signs and the Son of Man's arrival (21:25–27). Accordingly, many commentators conclude that Luke does not place the end of history immediately after AD 70.

**Do not use this chapter to claim God rejected the Jewish people.** Over the centuries, some Christians treated the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 as proof that God had cast off the Jewish people altogether. Luke says nothing of the kind. The one delivering this prophecy has just wept over the city (19:41), and on the way to the cross He tells the daughters of Jerusalem to weep for themselves and their children (23:28). Those urged to flee in 21:21 are themselves Jews, and the word "until" in 21:24 signals that the judgment is not permanent.

**"Do not worry beforehand" is not an excuse to skip preparation.** People sometimes appeal to 21:14–15 to argue that preachers need not prepare sermons. Yet the context is a courtroom interrogation, where arrested disciples cannot know what charges or questions will be thrown at them. The promise belongs to witnesses on trial for Christ's name, not to careless speakers. Earlier, Luke wrote that the Holy Spirit would teach them what to say (12:12); here Jesus says, "I will give you" the words (21:15). Both statements point to the same reality.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, witnessed the AD 70 siege of Jerusalem as a Roman captive. He recounts how the general Titus built a siege wall encircling the entire city so no one could slip out, after which famine claimed vast numbers of lives. He puts the tally at ninety-seven thousand captives and over one million dead, though modern historians regard his death toll as inflated. **The Talmud**, the great compendium of rabbinic teaching, asks why the second temple was destroyed even though the people studied Torah and performed deeds of charity. Its answer is causeless hatred among the people themselves. Thus Jewish tradition itself read the catastrophe of AD 70 not as a political accident, but as a call to repentance. *(Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 9b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the explanation citing causeless hatred in Yoma 9b is very well known; pairing it with Luke 21 is the editor's comparison -->

**Augustine**, late fourth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, replied to a fellow bishop inquiring about the end of the world. He pointed out that where Matthew and Mark refer to the "abomination of desolation," Luke explicitly writes of armies encircling Jerusalem. From this he concluded that the passage addresses Jerusalem's destruction, and warned against using it to calculate the date of Christ's return. *(Letter 199, to Hesychius)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled that Letter 199 to Hesychius uses Luke 21:20 to clarify the abomination passage; exact phrasing not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, observed regarding the widow that God weighs offerings not by their external value, but by the heart of the giver. He drew comfort from this for the poor: even a modest gift remains precious in the Lord's sight. He also reminded the wealthy not to imagine that giving generously suffices when they part only with their excess. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the theme that God weighs the heart rather than the sum reflects Calvin's reading here; exact wording and remark on the rich not verified -->

**C. H. Dodd**, British New Testament scholar, wrote a landmark article on Luke's predictions of Jerusalem's fall. He demonstrated that nearly every detail in 21:20–24 and 19:43–44 draws on Old Testament language describing the Babylonian capture of the city, rather than reporting fresh eyewitness details from AD 70. Consequently, he argued, these verses cannot be used to prove that Luke wrote after AD 70. *("The Fall of Jerusalem and the Abomination of Desolation," Journal of Roman Studies, 1947)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Dodd's article title, publication year, and core argument are widely cited in discussions of Luke's dating -->
