# Luke 20 — Questions in the Temple

## Story

One day, as Jesus was teaching the people in the temple and proclaiming the gospel, the chief priests and scribes, together with the elders, confronted Him and asked, "Tell us by what authority You are doing these things, or who gave You this authority?" He answered: I will also ask you a question: "John's baptism—was it from heaven or from men?" They discussed it among themselves: if we say "from heaven," He will ask why we did not believe him; if we say "from men," all the people will stone us, because they are convinced John was a prophet. So they answered that they did not know. Jesus told them, "Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things" (20:1–8). Those three groups together made up the Sanhedrin, the supreme Jewish council. They demanded to know His credentials, yet refused to speak honestly because they cared more about saving face than about truth.

Then He began to tell the people a parable. A man planted a vineyard, rented it out to tenant farmers, and went away for a long time. At harvest time he sent a servant to collect his share of the fruit, but the tenants beat him and sent him away empty-handed. He sent another servant; they beat him, treated him shamefully, and sent him away empty-handed too. He sent a third; they wounded him and threw him out. The owner of the vineyard said, "What shall I do? I will send my beloved son; perhaps they will respect him!" Yet when the tenants saw the son, they plotted together: "This is the heir! Let us kill him, so the inheritance will be ours." So they threw him out of the vineyard and killed him (20:9–15; Isaiah 5:1–7). The vineyard was Isaiah's familiar image for Israel; the servants were the prophets. "Beloved son" echoes the very words spoken from heaven at Jesus' baptism (Luke 3:22).

What then will the owner of the vineyard do to them? He will come and destroy those tenants and give the vineyard to others. Hearing this, the people exclaimed, "May it never be!" Looking directly at them, Jesus asked: Then what does this Scripture mean: "The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone"? Everyone who falls on that stone will be broken to pieces, and the one on whom it falls will be crushed. That very hour the scribes and chief priests looked for a way to lay hands on Him, because they realized He had told this parable against them—yet they feared the people (20:15–19; Psalms 118:22; Isaiah 8:14–15; Daniel 2:34–35). The murdered son of the parable becomes the cornerstone of the Psalm. Rejection does not get the final word.

Watching Him closely, they sent spies pretending to be sincere men, hoping to trap Him in His words so they could hand Him over to the authority and jurisdiction of the governor. They asked Him, "Teacher, we know that You speak and teach rightly and show no partiality, but truly teach the way of God. Is it lawful for us to pay taxes to Caesar or not?" Perceiving their craftiness, He said: Show Me a denarius. Whose image and inscription are on it? They answered, "Caesar's." He replied, "Then give back to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's." Unable to catch Him in anything He said in front of the people, they marveled at His answer and fell silent (20:20–26). The trap had two edges: say "yes" and alienate the crowd; say "no" and get reported to Rome for sedition. His answer rises above both. A coin stamped with Caesar's image belongs back to Caesar; human beings stamped with God's image (Genesis 1:27) belong back to God.

Next came some Sadducees, who deny there is a resurrection, asking Him: Teacher, Moses wrote for us that if a man's brother dies leaving a wife but no children, the man must marry the widow and raise up offspring for his brother. Now there were seven brothers. The first married a woman and died childless; the second and then the third married her, and in the same way all seven died leaving no children. Finally the woman died too. In the resurrection, therefore, whose wife will she be, since all seven were married to her? (20:27–33; Deuteronomy 25:5–6). The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy, accepting only the five books of Moses as binding Scripture. Their riddle was designed to make resurrection sound ridiculous.

Jesus answered, "The sons of this age marry and are given in marriage," yet those considered worthy to attain that age and the resurrection from the dead neither marry nor are given in marriage. Nor can they die anymore, for they are like the angels and are sons of God, being sons of the resurrection. As for the dead being raised, even Moses showed this in the passage about the burning bush, where he calls the Lord the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. "Now He is not the God of the dead, but of the living, for to Him all are alive." Some of the scribes replied, "Teacher, You have spoken well." And they no longer dared to ask Him any more questions (20:34–40; Exodus 3:6). He meets them on their own ground, using Moses' own writings. God does not bind His eternal name to people who have vanished into nothing; He remains their God because they still live before Him.

Then He asked them: How can people say that the Christ is the Son of David? For David himself says in the Book of Psalms, "The Lord said to my Lord: 'Sit at My right hand until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet.'" Since David calls Him "Lord," how then can He be David's son? (20:41–44; Psalms 110:1). Having answered every test, Jesus poses His own question. The Messiah is both David's descendant and David's Lord—a riddle solved only when one recognizes who Jesus truly is.

While all the people were listening, He said to His disciples: Beware of the scribes, who like to walk around in long robes, love respectful greetings in the marketplaces, the best seats in the synagogues, and the places of honor at banquets. They devour widows' houses and for show make long prayers. "They will receive greater condemnation" (20:45–47). This warning leads straight into the very next scene: a poor widow dropping two tiny coins into the temple treasury (Luke 21:1–4).

## Key Takeaways

**The question about authority is a question about John.** Jesus is not dodging when He replies by asking about John's baptism (20:4). John had pointed straight to the Mightier One coming after him (3:16). Anyone unwilling to speak honestly about John will not accept the truth about the One to whom John pointed. The leaders hide behind "we don't know" to protect themselves (20:7), and Jesus refuses to force truth on those determined not to receive it.

**The vineyard owner keeps hoping until the very end.** Even after three servants are beaten and turned away, the owner says, "perhaps they will respect him!" (20:13). Luke alone preserves that word "perhaps." It sounds like a father clinging to hope against every grim sign. The parable portrays God not as a quick-tempered landlord, but as One who absorbs repeated wrongs before finally acting in judgment.

**Resurrection is not merely an extended version of this life.** The Sadducees assumed any afterlife would simply continue present life—complete with marriage disputes. Jesus explains that the coming age is a whole new mode of existence: those raised "can no longer die" and are "sons of the resurrection" (20:36). Luke also adds the striking line, "for to Him all are alive" (20:38): those who have died remain alive to God.

**The Messiah is greater than the title "Son of David."** People expected a descendant of David who would restore the earthly kingdom. Jesus points out that David himself addressed this coming One as "Lord" (20:42–44). The Christ is not merely another king in David's royal line, but One before whom David himself bows.

**Outward piety can walk hand in hand with exploitation.** The scribes prized flowing robes, marketplace honors, front seats, and lengthy prayers—yet they "devour widows' houses" (20:46–47). Jesus does not fault prayer; He exposes religious theater used to cover preying on the most defenseless, warning that they "will receive greater condemnation."

## Deeper Meaning

**A vineyard expected to yield justice.** Isaiah's Song of the Vineyard ends with the explanation: the vineyard is the house of Israel, and God "looked for justice, but saw bloodshed; for righteousness, but heard cries of distress" (Isaiah 5:7). Jesus' parable builds directly on that song, and this chapter closes with the plight of widows whose homes are devoured (20:47). The fruit the vineyard owner seeks is that very justice.

**Mocked messengers.** Second Chronicles sums up Israel's history before Jerusalem's first fall: God "sent word to them through His messengers again and again," yet they "mocked God's messengers" (2 Chronicles 36:15–16). The three servants in the parable compress that entire tragedy into three verses (20:10–12).

**Paul echoes the teaching on taxes.** Writing to believers living in Caesar's very capital, Paul teaches, "Pay everyone what you owe him: taxes to whom taxes are due" (Romans 13:7). His word "pay back" matches Jesus' command in 20:25. Yet in the very next verse, Paul adds that the one debt we can never finish paying is the debt to love one another (Romans 13:8).

**Those who sleep in the dust will awake.** Daniel contains the Old Testament's clearest statement on resurrection: "Many who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life" (Daniel 12:2). Because the Sadducees did not recognize Daniel as authoritative law, Jesus skips it and draws His proof straight from the books of Moses they accepted (20:37).

**Peter solves the riddle of David.** No one in the temple answers Jesus' closing question (20:44). In Luke's second volume, the answer rings out on Pentecost: Peter quotes Psalm 110 and declares that God has made this Jesus "both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:34–36). Risen and seated at the Father's right hand, He is indeed David's Lord.

**Preying upon widows.** Isaiah rebuked unjust lawmakers who "make widows their prey" (Isaiah 10:2). Jesus' denunciation of the scribes in 20:47 stands squarely in that prophetic tradition. This is not an outsider attacking Israel, but the voice of Israel's own prophets speaking from within.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who are the "others" to whom the vineyard is given?** For centuries many read "others" (20:16) as the Gentiles, claiming God cast off the Jewish people. Yet Luke explicitly names who recognized themselves as the target: "the scribes and the chief priests" (20:19), while the ordinary crowds exclaimed, "May it never be!" (20:16). The vineyard itself remains Israel; what changes is its leadership. Several commentators see "others" as the apostles—Jewish leaders who will guide God's people in the Book of Acts.

**Even His answer about Caesar was twisted against Him.** Luke notes the spies' goal: to "hand Him over to the authority and jurisdiction of the governor" (20:20). Jesus plainly told them to give Caesar what belongs to Caesar. Yet days later, standing before Pilate, His accusers charged Him with "forbidding payment of taxes to Caesar" (23:2). Luke's readers know the charge is a blatant lie. Nor does 20:25 carve life into two equal halves: Caesar gets a coin, whereas God receives the whole person.

**Is an afterlife without marriage a sorrowful thought?** Many read 20:35 and wonder whether spouses who loved each other for a lifetime will become strangers. Notice how Luke links marriage directly to mortality: people in this age marry to continue life because everyone dies—and levirate marriage existed solely because of death (20:28). Once people "can no longer die" (20:36), that necessity vanishes. Jesus never says love is erased; He shows that the Sadducees' riddle starts from a false premise, projecting the rules of a dying world onto the age of resurrection.

**Jesus is not denying that He is David's descendant.** His question in 20:41–44 might sound like a rejection of the title. Yet Luke himself recorded the angel's promise that God "will give Him the throne of His father David" (1:32) and traced Jesus' genealogy through David (3:31). The question does not discard the title "Son of David"; it shows that the title by itself is too small.

## What Commentators Say

**Irenaeus**, second-century bishop of Lyon, appealed to "He is not the God of the dead, but of the living" (20:38) against teachers who claimed the Old Testament God differed from the Father of Jesus. Jesus identifies the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as the Giver of life—the very Father He proclaims. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, observed that the answer about Caesar shows how obeying civil authorities in paying taxes does not undermine devotion to God. Jesus distinguishes earthly government from His spiritual kingdom, correcting those who imagine faithfulness to God requires rebelling against rulers. Yet civil power has a boundary, and it must halt wherever God's authority begins. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: distinguishing the two realms and setting limits to civil authority is characteristic of Calvin on this verse; exact wording unverified -->

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, recounts that around AD 6, when Rome conducted a census to tax Judea, Judas the Galilean led an uprising, preaching that paying Roman taxes amounted to accepting mortal masters in place of God. He also describes the Sadducees as holding that the soul perishes with the body and recognizing only the written Law. Both reports illuminate why the tax question was explosive and why the Sadducees mocked resurrection. *(The Jewish War, Book 2; Antiquities of the Jews, Book 18)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Judas the Galilean's revolt and the description of the Sadducees appear in both works in these books -->

**Joachim Jeremias**, German New Testament scholar, set the vineyard parable in first-century Galilee, where absentee landlords and resentful peasants were common. He explained the tenants' logic: seeing the son arrive alone, they assumed the owner had died, so killing the heir would leave the estate ownerless and allow the occupants to claim it by squatters' rights. Their calculation was legally plausible in that world yet fatally blind—for the owner was still alive. *(The Parables of Jesus)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recalls Jeremias's explanation of why the tenants thought killing the heir would gain them the estate; exact page location unverified -->

Wright**, British bishop and New Testament scholar, suggests that "give back" in 20:25 may echo the dying words of Mattathias, founder of the Maccabean revolt, urging his sons to "pay back" the Gentiles in full. Hearers, he proposes, could catch two layers in Jesus' reply: a peaceful compliance on the surface, alongside a quiet reminder that Caesar too answers to God's judgment.
