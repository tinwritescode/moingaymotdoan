# Luke 22 — The Last Supper and the Mount of Olives

## Story

The Feast of Unleavened Bread, called the Passover, was approaching. The chief priests and the scribes were looking for a way to put Jesus to death, because they feared the crowds. Then Satan entered Judas, called Iscariot, one of the Twelve. Judas went to confer with the chief priests and the officers of the temple guard about how he might hand Jesus over to them. They were delighted and agreed to give him money. He consented and began looking for an opportunity to betray Him away from the crowd (22:1–6). The devil had left Jesus "until an opportune time" after the wilderness temptation (Luke 4:13). Now he returns, working through one of Jesus' closest companions.

Then came the day of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover lamb had to be sacrificed. Jesus sent Peter and John ahead, saying, "Go and prepare the Passover for us to eat." They asked where He wanted them to prepare it. He replied that when they entered the city, a man carrying a jar of water would meet them; they should follow him into the house he entered and tell the owner, "The Teacher asks: Where is the guest room where I may eat the Passover with My disciples?" He would show them a large upstairs room already furnished; they were to prepare the meal there. They went, found everything just as He had said, and prepared the Passover (22:7–13; Exodus 12:1–14). Carrying water was ordinarily women's work, so a man bearing a water jar made an unmistakable signal. Jesus arranged the location discreetly so Judas would not know in advance where they would dine.

When the hour came, Jesus reclined at the table with the apostles and said, "I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer." For He would not eat it again until it finds fulfillment in the kingdom of God. Taking a cup, He gave thanks and told them to take it and divide it among themselves, for from now on He would not drink of the fruit of the vine until the kingdom of God comes. Then He took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and gave it to them, saying, "This is My body, given for you; do this in remembrance of Me." In the same way, after the meal, He took the cup and said, "This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is poured out for you." (22:14–20; Jeremiah 31:31–34). Luke mentions two cups, one before the bread and one afterward, fitting the sequence of cups in a Passover meal. One ancient manuscript stops at "This is My body," omitting the rest of verse 19 and verse 20, yet almost all manuscripts include those lines, and the wording closely matches Paul's account (1 Corinthians 11:23–25).

Yet look, He continued, the hand of the one betraying Me is with Mine on the table. The Son of Man is going as it has been determined, but woe to the man who betrays Him! The disciples began asking one another which of them would do such a thing. Then a dispute broke out among them over which of them should be regarded as the greatest. Jesus told them that Gentile kings lord it over their subjects, and those wielding authority call themselves "Benefactors." You are not to be like that; instead, the greatest among you must become like the youngest, and the leader like the one who serves. Who is greater, the one reclining at table or the one serving? Is it not the one at table? "Yet I am among you as one who serves." (22:21–27). Moments after He gives them His body, they wrangle over rank. He corrects them not with a lecture, but by pointing to His own life.

You are the ones who have stood by Me in My trials. So I confer a kingdom on you, just as My Father conferred one on Me, so that you may eat and drink at My table in My kingdom and sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel. "Simon, Simon, Satan has asked to sift all of you like wheat." Yet I have prayed for you, Simon, that your faith may not fail; and once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers. Peter protested, "Lord, I am ready to go with You both to prison and to death." Jesus answered, "I tell you, Peter, the rooster will not crow today until you deny three times that you know Me." (22:28–34). In Greek, the pronoun "you" in verse 31 is plural—Satan demanded to sift them all—while in verse 32 it is singular, because Jesus prayed personally for Peter. He knew Peter would stumble and had already assigned him work once he rose again.

He asked them: when I sent you out without purse, bag, or sandals, did you lack anything? They answered, "Nothing." He told them: but now, whoever has a purse should take it, and likewise a bag; and whoever has no sword should sell his cloak and buy one. For the Scripture saying, "He was numbered with the transgressors," must be fulfilled in Me. They said, "Look, Lord, here are two swords." He replied, "That is enough." (22:35–38; Isaiah 53:12). The season of friendly hospitality is over; they are about to live amid hostility. Still, He is not assembling an army—within hours He will forbid using the sword. "That is enough" likely brings the conversation to a close because they took His metaphor literally.

Jesus went out as usual to the Mount of Olives, and the disciples followed Him. Reaching the place, He told them, "Pray that you will not enter into temptation." He withdrew about a stone's throw away, knelt down, and prayed: "Father, if You are willing, take this cup from Me; yet not My will, but Yours be done." An angel from heaven appeared to strengthen Him. In anguish He prayed even more earnestly, and His sweat became like great drops of blood falling to the ground (22:39–44). Verses 43 and 44 are absent from several early and major manuscripts, though second-century writers such as Justin Martyr and Irenaeus already knew them. Scholars remain divided on whether these verses belong to Luke's original text or entered later.

Rising from prayer, He went back to the disciples and found them asleep, exhausted from sorrow. He asked, "Why are you sleeping? Get up and pray so that you will not fall into temptation." While He was still speaking, a crowd approached. Judas, one of the Twelve, led the way and stepped up to kiss Him. Jesus asked, "Judas, are you betraying the Son of Man with a kiss?" Sensing what was about to unfold, those around Him asked, "Lord, should we strike with the sword?" One of them struck the high priest's servant and cut off his right ear. Jesus answered, "No more of this!" Then He touched the servant's ear and healed him (22:45–51). Luke alone recounts this healing, and only Luke—the physician—notes that it was the right ear. His final healing miracle before the cross is performed for a member of the arresting party.

Then Jesus addressed the chief priests, the officers of the temple guard, and the elders who had come for Him: have you come out with swords and clubs as if catching a bandit? Day after day I was with you in the temple courts, and you did not lay hands on Me. "But this is your hour, and the power of darkness." Seizing Him, they led Him away to the house of the high priest. Peter followed at a distance. They kindled a fire in the middle of the courtyard and sat down together, and Peter sat among them. A servant girl saw him seated in the firelight, looked closely at him, and said, "This man was with him too." Peter denied it: "Woman, I do not know him." A little later someone else saw him and said, "You are one of them too." He replied, "Man, I am not." About an hour later, another man insisted, "Surely this fellow was with him, for he is a Galilean too." (22:52–59). His accent gave him away: Galileans spoke differently from residents of Jerusalem.

Peter replied, "Man, I do not know what you are talking about!" Even as he was speaking, a rooster crowed. The Lord turned and looked straight at Peter. Peter remembered what the Lord had told him, that before the rooster crowed today he would deny Him three times. He went outside and wept bitterly. Meanwhile, the men guarding Jesus mocked and beat Him. They blindfolded Him and demanded, "Prophesy! Who hit you?" And they hurled many other insults at Him (22:60–65). Only Luke records that the Lord turned and looked at Peter. Peter wept not under a sentence of condemnation, but beneath a single glance—and the Lord had already prayed for him.

At daybreak, the council of the elders of the people, both chief priests and scribes, assembled and brought Him before the Sanhedrin. They demanded, "If you are the Christ, tell us." He replied, "If I tell you, you will not believe," and if I ask you a question, you will not answer. "But from now on, the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God." They all asked, "Are you then the Son of God?" He answered, "You yourselves say that I am." They declared, "What further testimony do we need? We have heard it ourselves from his own lips." (22:66–71; Psalms 110:1; Daniel 7:13). His answer is an indirect yes. They sit in judgment over Him now, yet He speaks of the day He sits at God's right hand as Judge.

## Key Takeaways

**He deeply longed to share this meal with them.** Luke opens the supper with Jesus' heartfelt longing: "I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer" (22:15). This is no cold ritual, but a farewell meal shared by a teacher with those He loves. The phrase "for you" sounds twice, over the bread and over the cup (22:19–20): the approaching death is a gift offered for the very people seated at that table.

**The greatest person is the one serving at table.** Only Luke places the quarrel over greatness right inside the Last Supper (22:24). Jesus observes that earthly kings love to be styled "Benefactors," ruling over subjects and expecting gratitude in return (22:25). Among disciples it must be otherwise: the greatest must take the place of the youngest. The ground of that ethic is not an abstract rule, but Jesus Himself: "I am among you as one who serves" (22:27).

**The Lord prays for the one about to fall.** Before Peter ever denies Him, Jesus has already prayed for him and entrusted him with a task once he recovers (22:32). At the third denial, "the Lord turned and looked at Peter" (22:61). That look needed no words; it awakened his memory and broke him into tears. Peter's failure is held within prior prayer and immediate mercy.

**Pray so you do not enter temptation.** Luke frames the scene on the Mount of Olives with the exact same command at start and finish: "Pray that you will not enter into temptation" (22:40; 22:46). Between those bookends, Jesus kneels and surrenders His will to the Father's (22:41–42). The one who prays stands steady when arrest comes; those who sleep swing a sword, flee, and deny Him.

**The hour of darkness is real, yet it is only an hour.** When arrested, Jesus tells His captors, "This is your hour, and the power of darkness" (22:53). Satan has entered Judas (22:3) and demanded to sift the disciples (22:31). Yet Luke calls it an "hour"—something with a beginning and an end. Even in that hour, Jesus reaches out to heal a servant's ear (22:51).

## Deeper Meaning

**Counted among the lawless.** This is the only place in his Gospel where Luke shows Jesus directly quoting Isaiah's song of the Suffering Servant: "He was numbered with the transgressors" (22:37; Isaiah 53:12). Those words come true the very next day when He is crucified between two criminals (23:33). That same verse in Isaiah adds that the Servant "interceded for the transgressors," and from the cross Jesus will pray for those nailing Him there (23:34).

**Do this in remembrance.** When establishing Passover, God instructed Israel, "This day shall be a memorial for you" (Exodus 12:14). Year after year, Jews ate the meal to remember how the Lord brought them out of Egypt. Jesus echoes that exact pattern: "Do this in remembrance of Me" (22:19). From this night onward, the disciples' meal remembers a new deliverance.

**Sifted without losing a single grain.** In the book of Job, Satan has to obtain permission before testing Job, and God sets strict boundaries around him (Job 1:12). Here too Satan "demanded" to sift the disciples (22:31), meaning he had to ask. Amos used the image of shaking Israel in a sieve while promising that "not a pebble will fall to the ground" (Amos 9:9). The disciples will be shaken hard, yet none will be lost.

**A kingdom given to the saints.** Daniel foresaw the day when kingdom and dominion "will be given to the people of the saints of the Most High" (Daniel 7:27). Jesus tells His fragile disciples, "I confer on you a kingdom, just as My Father conferred one on Me" (22:29). The very men who have just argued over greatness are promised a seat at His table, though only after learning to serve.

**An angel strengthens Him, as with Elijah.** When Elijah fled in despair and wished to die, an angel woke him, gave him food, and told him, "The journey is too much for you" (1 Kings 19:7). In Gethsemane, the angel likewise does not remove the ordeal, but strengthens Jesus (22:43) to walk the road ahead.

**The restored disciple strengthens his brothers.** Jesus instructed Peter that once he turned back, he must strengthen his brothers (22:32). Luke shows that charge fulfilled. After rising from the dead, the Lord appears privately to Simon (24:34), and on Pentecost it is Peter who rises before the crowds to preach (Acts 2:14). The man who denied his Master three times becomes the first public witness to Him.

## Notes & Nuances

**What are the two swords for?** Jesus' saying, "Whoever has no sword should sell his cloak and buy one" (22:36), has been read in several ways. The most common reading sees figurative language warning that the season of welcome has ended and disciples will now face hostility; they misunderstand Him literally, and "That is enough" cuts the exchange short (22:38). Others take it literally as permission for self-defense while traveling. Still others link it to the Isaiah quotation immediately following: carrying weapons contributes to Jesus' circle being "numbered with the transgressors" (22:37). Whichever reading one prefers, within hours He forbids using the sword and heals the wounded servant (22:51). This passage cannot be invoked to justify violence by the Church.

**Sweat "like" drops of blood.** Verse 22:44 states that His sweat "became like great drops of blood falling to the ground." The word "like" indicates that this may be a simile—sweat pouring down as thick and heavy as drops of blood. Others believe real blood mingled with His sweat, pointing to a rare medical condition triggered by extreme distress. The text does not force either conclusion. What is unmistakable is the depth of His inward agony.

**If Satan entered Judas, is Judas still responsible?** Luke writes that "Satan entered Judas" (22:3), yet also narrates Judas deliberately plotting with the leaders, agreeing to take money, and hunting for a chance to hand Jesus over (22:4–6). Jesus declares that the Son of Man goes "as it has been determined," and immediately adds, "woe to that man by whom He is betrayed!" (22:22). Scripture holds both truths together: God's sovereign purpose and genuine human responsibility. Peter makes the exact same point at Pentecost (Acts 2:23).

**Was the Last Supper a Passover meal?** Luke states clearly that it was: Jesus sends disciples to prepare the Passover and eats it with them (22:8; 22:15). Yet John notes that on the morning of Jesus' trial, His accusers had not yet eaten the Passover (John 18:28; John 19:14). Several explanations have been offered: different Jewish groups may have followed slightly different calendars; Jesus may have eaten the meal a day early; or John may frame the chronology to highlight Jesus dying at the exact hour the Passover lambs were slaughtered. No single solution has won universal agreement.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the collection of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, describes four cups of wine during the Passover meal, with the third cup drunk after supper alongside the blessing after meals. Many commentators draw on this custom to explain Luke's two cups: the cup in 22:17 corresponds to one of the earlier cups, while the cup "after supper" in 22:20 corresponds to the post-meal cup. Paul calls the communion cup "the cup of blessing" (1 Corinthians 10:16), likely echoing that very title. *(Mishnah, Pesachim, chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the four cups and post-meal blessing in Pesachim chapter 10 are well established; identifying verse 20 with the third cup is common though not universal -->

**Ambrose**, fourth-century bishop of Milan, reflected on Peter's tears in 22:62. He remarked that he reads of Peter weeping, yet does not read of Peter offering excuses or explanations. In Ambrose's view, tears wash away a fault that words struggle to confess, and Peter wept because the Lord had looked upon him. *(Exposition of the Gospel according to Luke, book 10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Ambrose's observation that he reads of Peter's tears rather than words of defense is very famous from book 10 of his Luke commentary -->

**Pope Boniface VIII**, in a papal bull issued in 1302, appealed to the remark "here are two swords" (22:38) to argue that the Church holds both spiritual and temporal authority, with the temporal sword to be wielded at the Church's direction. Earlier writers such as Bernard of Clairvaux had already advanced this reading. Today virtually no biblical scholar—Catholic or otherwise—interprets the verse that way; its history illustrates how far a difficult line can be pulled from its setting. *(Unam Sanctam, 1302)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Unam Sanctam's use of the two swords in Luke 22:38 for the two-powers doctrine is well known; the observation on modern readers is general -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, remarked that the disciples' mention of two swords reveals how slow they were to grasp Jesus' meaning. In his reading, Jesus spoke figuratively about the coming spiritual struggle, and "That is enough" was a dismissal of their dullness rather than satisfaction with two weapons. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled that Calvin reads the sword saying figuratively and criticizes the disciples' misunderstanding; exact wording not checked -->

**Bruce Metzger**, American New Testament textual scholar, documented the Greek editorial committee's judgments on the two textual cruxes in this chapter. For 22:19–20, the committee retained the longer text because nearly all manuscripts contain it. For 22:43–44, they judged that the two verses were probably not written by Luke, yet kept them inside double brackets as a very ancient tradition about Jesus. *(A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: these textual decisions and the retention of verses 43–44 in double brackets are well-known entries in Metzger's commentary -->

**Raymond Brown**, American Catholic priest and biblical scholar, closely examined the verses about the angel and sweat like blood. He observed that manuscript evidence pulls strongly in both directions, and leaned toward viewing the verses as authentically Lukan, suggesting later copyists might have removed them because a Jesus needing angelic help looked too vulnerable. *(The Death of the Messiah, 1994)* <!-- confidence: low | verified: no | reason: certain that Brown analyzes verses 43–44 in depth in this volume, but uncertain which side he ultimately favors -->
