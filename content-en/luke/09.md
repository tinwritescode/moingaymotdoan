# Luke 9 — Setting His Face Toward Jerusalem

## Story

Summoning the Twelve together, Jesus gave them power and authority over all demons and to cure diseases, and sent them out to proclaim the kingdom of God and heal the sick. He told them, "Take nothing for the journey—no staff, no bag, no bread, no money, no second tunic." Whatever house you enter, stay there until you leave that town. Where people refuse to welcome you, shake the dust off your feet as you leave as a testimony against them. Setting out, they traveled from village to village, proclaiming the good news and healing people everywhere (9:1–6). Traveling empty-handed forces them to rely on hospitality and trust the One sending them.

Herod the tetrarch heard about everything happening and was perplexed, because some said John had risen from the dead, others that Elijah had appeared, and still others that one of the ancient prophets had come back to life. Herod said, "I beheaded John, so who is this I hear such things about?" And he kept trying to see Jesus (9:7–9). Luke skips narrating John's execution, letting John's killer reveal it himself. Herod will finally meet Jesus—only on the day of His trial (23:8).

Returning, the apostles reported all they had done. Jesus took them privately toward a town called Bethsaida, yet the crowds found out and followed Him. Welcoming them, He spoke to them about the kingdom of God and healed those needing care. Late in the afternoon the Twelve urged Him to send the crowd away to surrounding villages and countryside for lodging and food, since they were in a remote spot. He replied, "You give them something to eat." We have only five loaves and two fish, they protested, unless we go buy food for all these people—about five thousand men were there (9:10–14). Aside from the Resurrection, this is the sole miracle recorded in all four Gospels.

Telling His disciples to seat them in groups of about fifty, they did so. Taking the five loaves and two fish, looking up to heaven, He blessed them, broke them, and gave them to the disciples to set before the crowd. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and they picked up twelve baskets of leftover broken pieces (9:14–17). Those exact four actions—taking, blessing, breaking, giving—will reappear at the Last Supper and at Emmaus (22:19; 24:30). Once God fed Israel with manna in the desert; here a vast crowd eats their fill in a barren place again.

Once while Jesus was praying alone with His disciples nearby, He asked them, "Who do the crowds say I am?" Some say John the Baptist, others Elijah, others an ancient prophet risen again, they answered. He asked, "But what about you? Who do you say I am?" Peter replied, "The Christ of God." Strictly warning them to tell no one, He added that the Son of Man must suffer many things, be rejected by the elders, chief priests, and scribes, be killed, and on the third day be raised (9:18–22). The question haunting both disciples and Herod—"Who is this?"—now has its answer. Yet the instant He is confessed as Christ, He unveils the cross.

Then He told everyone, "If anyone wants to come after Me, he must deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow Me." Whoever wants to save his life will lose it, yet whoever loses his life for My sake will save it. What good is it for someone to gain the whole world yet lose or forfeit themselves? Whoever is ashamed of Me and My words, the Son of Man will be ashamed of them when He comes in His glory and in the glory of the Father and the holy angels. Truly, some standing here will not taste death before they see the kingdom of God (9:23–27). Luke alone inserts the word "daily": taking up the cross is not a single heroic gesture, but a daily way of living.

About eight days later, He took Peter, John, and James up onto a mountain to pray. As He prayed, the appearance of His face changed, and His clothes became dazzling white. Two men, Moses and Elijah, appeared in glory talking with Him, speaking of His departure that He was about to fulfill in Jerusalem. Peter and his companions were heavy with sleep, yet staying awake they saw His glory and the two men standing beside Him. As the men began leaving, Peter said: Master, it is good for us to be here; let us make three shelters—one for You, one for Moses, and one for Elijah—not knowing what he was saying (9:28–33). The Greek word rendered "departure" is literally *exodos*—exodus. Just as Moses led Israel out of Egypt, Jesus is about to lead a greater exodus in Jerusalem.

While Peter was still speaking, a cloud enveloped them, and the disciples grew afraid as they entered the cloud. A voice spoke from the cloud: "This is My Son, whom I have chosen. Listen to Him!" When the voice finished, Jesus was found alone. The disciples stayed silent and told no one in those days anything of what they had seen (9:34–36; Deuteronomy 18:15). Moses long ago promised God would raise up a prophet like him to whom Israel must listen; the voice from the cloud identifies that Prophet unmistakably.

The next day, coming down the mountain, a large crowd met Him. A man shouted from the crowd: Teacher, I beg You to look at my son, my only child! A spirit seizes him, making him suddenly shriek, convulsing him until he foams at the mouth, bruising him and scarcely letting him go. I begged Your disciples to cast it out, yet they could not. Jesus answered, "O unbelieving and perverse generation, how long must I remain with you and put up with you?" Bring your son here. Even as the boy approached, the demon dashed him to the ground in violent convulsions. Jesus rebuked the unclean spirit, healed the boy, and gave him back to his father—and everyone marveled at the majesty of God (9:37–43). Straight down from mountaintop splendor, He steps right beside a desperate father and helpless disciples.

While everyone still marveled at all He did, He told His disciples, "The Son of Man is about to be delivered into the hands of men." Yet they did not understand this statement—its meaning remained hidden from them, and they were afraid to ask Him about it. Soon an argument broke out among them over which of them was the greatest. Knowing the thoughts of their hearts, Jesus took a little child, stood him beside Himself, and declared: whoever welcomes this little child in My name welcomes Me, and whoever welcomes Me welcomes the One who sent Me, "For whoever is the least among all of you, he is the greatest." John spoke up: Master, we saw someone driving out demons in Your name and tried to stop him because he does not follow along with us. Jesus answered, "Do not stop him, for whoever is not against you is for you" (9:43–50). He talks about betrayal; they bicker over rank. The gap between Master and students has never yawned wider.

As the days drew near for Him to be taken up, Jesus resolutely set His face to go to Jerusalem. He sent messengers ahead into a Samaritan village to prepare a place for Him, yet the villagers would not welcome Him because His face was set toward Jerusalem. Seeing this, James and John asked, "Lord, do You want us to call down fire from heaven to consume them?" Turning, He rebuked them, and they went on to another village (9:51–56; 2 Kings 1:10–12). Verse 51 launches the longest section of Luke's Gospel—the journey to Jerusalem running all the way to chapter 19. Traditional Bibles include extra words in verses 55–56 ("You do not know what kind of spirit you are of," "just as Elijah did," and "for the Son of Man did not come to destroy men's lives, but to save them"), found in many later manuscripts though absent from the earliest Greek copies.

Walking along the road, someone told Him: I will follow You wherever You go. Jesus answered, "Foxes have dens and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has no place to lay His head." To another He said: follow Me. Let me first go bury my father, the man replied. Jesus answered, "Let the dead bury their own dead; but you go and proclaim the kingdom of God." Still another said: I will follow You, Lord, yet first let me say goodbye to my family. Jesus told him, "No one who puts his hand to the plow and then looks back is fit for the kingdom of God" (9:57–62; 1 Kings 19:19–21). Elijah once allowed Elisha to kiss his parents goodbye; Jesus demands even more than Elijah. Luke never tells us whether these three men followed—leaving the decision hanging directly over the reader.

## Key Takeaways

**Messiahship is welded permanently to the cross, never to easy glory.** The instant Peter confesses Him as God's Christ, Jesus spells out His suffering, death, and third-day resurrection (9:20–22). Disciples walk that exact same road: denying self and taking up their cross "daily" (9:23).

**The Transfiguration reveals Christ's true glory ahead of His crucifixion.** Before Peter, James, and John, His face blazes with splendor, Moses and Elijah converse with Him about His coming exodus in Jerusalem, and the Father's voice commands, "Listen to Him!" (9:28–36).

**Kingdom greatness is measured by welcoming the weak and renouncing vengeance.** While disciples squabble over status, try shutting down outsiders, and beg to call fire on Samaritans, Jesus places a little child at His side and rebukes their narrow, violent instincts (9:46–56).

**From 9:51 onward, Luke's entire Gospel turns steadily toward Jerusalem, demanding wholehearted commitment.** The three closing encounters show that walking with Jesus leaves zero room for half-measures or glancing backward (9:57–62).

## Deeper Meaning

**Five loaves and two fish recall both wilderness manna and Elisha's miracle.** God sustained Israel with desert manna (Exodus 16:4) and used Elisha to feed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves with leftovers to spare (2 Kings 4:42–44). Jesus feeds five thousand men and gathers twelve leftover baskets—echoing Israel's twelve tribes (9:12–17).

**Speaking of His "departure" links the cross directly to the Exodus.** Luke's Greek word in 9:31 is *exodos*. Jesus' death and resurrection in Jerusalem accomplish the ultimate exodus, liberating humanity from bondage to sin and death (Exodus 12:41–42).

**Moses and Elijah fade away, leaving Jesus alone as the Prophet greater than Moses.** Moses represents the Law and Elijah the Prophets; as the Father declares, "This is My Son, whom I have chosen. Listen to Him!" (9:35), the ancient promise of a coming prophet like Moses reaches complete fulfillment (Deuteronomy 18:15).

**Calling down fire recalls Elijah—only for Jesus to overturn it.** Elijah once called heavenly fire down on King Ahaziah's troops (2 Kings 1:10–12). Rebuking James and John (9:54–55), Jesus reveals that the Son of Man comes to rescue rejecters, not incinerate them.

**Urgency surpassing even Elisha's farewell.** Elijah let Elisha pause to bid his parents farewell (1 Kings 19:19–21). Setting His face toward Jerusalem, Jesus uses the image of a plowman refusing to glance backward (9:61–62) to signal the absolute urgency of God's kingdom.

## Notes & Nuances

**When would some standing there "see the kingdom of God" before dying (9:27)?** Commentators suggest several fulfillments: many link it directly to the Transfiguration eight days later (9:28–36), where three disciples beheld His majesty; others point to the Resurrection, Pentecost, or Jerusalem's fall in AD 70. Luke placing the Transfiguration immediately next makes the first link remarkably natural.

**"Let the dead bury their own dead" (9:60) does not promote neglecting parents.** Elsewhere Jesus fiercely defends honoring and caring for parents (Mark 7:10–13). Scholars of Jewish custom point out the man's father may not even have died yet—he wanted to wait home until his father eventually passed—or he wished to stay for secondary bone-burial a year later. Either way, Jesus uses stark speech to warn against postponing God's call.

**Luke's distinctive word "daily" in taking up one's cross (9:23).** Under Rome, carrying a cross meant a condemned prisoner walking to execution. Adding "daily" reminds readers that discipleship is not one dramatic rush of courage, but surrendering self-rule again and again in ordinary daily moments.

**Longstanding religious hostility explains the Samaritan village's snub (9:52–53).** Samaritans worshiped on Mount Gerizim and rejected Jerusalem's temple, shutting their doors to pilgrims heading there. Rather than retaliating, Jesus walks quietly to the next village—and in the very next chapter casts a Samaritan as the supreme hero of neighborly love (10:33).
