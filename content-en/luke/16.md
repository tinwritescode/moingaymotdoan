# Luke 16 — The Rich Man and Lazarus

## Story

Jesus told His disciples: There was a rich man whose manager was accused of wasting his possessions. The master summoned him and asked: What is this I hear about you? Turn in an account of your management, because you can no longer be manager. The manager thought to himself: What shall I do now that my master is taking away my job? I am not strong enough to dig, and I am ashamed to beg. I know what I will do so that, when I lose my position, people will welcome me into their homes (16:1–4). The parable opens like the tale of a cornered schemer with nowhere left to run.

He summoned each of his master's debtors one by one. He asked the first: How much do you owe my master? The man answered, "A hundred measures of olive oil." The manager said: Take your bill, sit down quickly, and write fifty. Then he asked another: And how much do you owe? He replied, "A hundred measures of wheat." The manager told him: Take your bill and write eighty (16:5–7). A hundred measures of oil represented the yield of a hundred olive trees—a massive debt. Some scholars propose that the portion he cut was his own interest or commission, though the text does not specify.

The master commended the dishonest manager because he had acted shrewdly, for the sons of this age are more shrewd in dealing with their own generation than the sons of light. And I tell you, "Use worldly wealth to make friends for yourselves, so that when it is gone, they will welcome you into eternal dwellings" (16:8–9). The master is not praising fraud; he is praising sharp foresight—the man knew his time was up and used the resources still in his hands to secure his future. Disciples likewise know that earthly wealth will run out; they should use it for what lasts forever. This remains one of the hardest parables to interpret, and commentators have never reached complete agreement.

"Whoever is faithful with very little will also be faithful with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much." If you have not been trustworthy in handling worldly wealth, who will entrust true riches to you? And if you have not been trustworthy with someone else's property, who will give you property of your own? No servant can serve two masters. "You cannot serve both God and money" (16:10–13). The Aramaic word rendered "money" is *mammon*, meaning wealth. Jesus speaks of it as a rival master demanding allegiance.

The Pharisees, who loved money, heard all this and ridiculed Him. He said to them: You are the ones who justify yourselves before men, but God knows your hearts, "for what is prized among men is detestable before God." The Law and the Prophets were proclaimed until John; since that time, the good news of the kingdom of God is being preached, and everyone is forcing his way into it. Yet it is easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for a single stroke of a pen to drop out of the Law. Anyone who divorces his wife and marries another woman commits adultery, and the man who marries a divorced woman commits adultery (16:14–18). These sayings may sound disconnected at first, yet they hang together: a new era has dawned, but that does not mean the Law is discarded. The word on divorce illustrates the point: Jesus upholds the Law more rigorously—not more loosely—than those who bend it.

There was a rich man who dressed in purple and fine linen and feasted lavishly every day. At his gate lay a poor man named Lazarus, covered with sores, longing to eat what fell from the rich man's table; even the dogs came and licked his sores (16:19–21). The rich man goes unnamed, while the poor man has a name—the only named character in any of Jesus' parables. Lazarus means "God helps." The world never cared to learn his name, yet God knows it.

The poor man died and was carried by the angels to Abraham's side. The rich man also died and was buried. In Hades, where he was in torment, he looked up and saw Abraham far away, with Lazarus by his side. He called out, "Father Abraham, have mercy on me and send Lazarus to dip the tip of his finger in water and cool my tongue, because I am in agony in this fire!" Abraham answered: Child, remember that in your lifetime you received your good things, while Lazarus received bad things; now he is comforted here, and you are in agony. Besides all this, a great chasm has been fixed between us and you, so that no one can cross from one side to the other (16:22–26). Even here, the rich man still treats Lazarus like an errand boy. In life, only a gate separated them; now an uncrossable chasm lies between them.

The rich man said: Then I beg you, father, send Lazarus to my father's house, for I have five brothers, so he can warn them lest they also come to this place of torment. Abraham replied, "They have Moses and the Prophets; let them listen to them!" He answered: No, father Abraham, but if someone goes to them from the dead, they will repent. Abraham told him, "If they do not listen to Moses and the Prophets, they will not be convinced even if someone rises from the dead" (16:27–31). The closing line looks far ahead: One will indeed rise from the dead, and many will still refuse to believe. As for Moses and the Prophets, they had already spoken plainly about how to treat a poor brother lying at one's gate (Deuteronomy 15:7–11).

## Key Takeaways

**Worldly people often plan for their earthly future with more alertness than disciples plan for eternity.** The manager realized his livelihood was about to vanish and acted immediately, without stalling (16:3–4). Jesus does not commend dishonesty; He commends the clearheaded realism of someone who knows the clock is ticking. Disciples know everything in their hands will one day be returned—the question is whether we are preparing for that day.

**Money is never just a tool; it wants to be a master.** Jesus sets wealth alongside God as two rival lords competing for one servant (16:13). Nobody can straddle the fence. How we handle small sums day by day reveals which master we are really serving (16:10).

**God looks at the heart, not at human applause.** The Pharisees sneered at His teaching on money (16:14). Jesus replied that what society admires can be detestable in God's sight (16:15). Religious reputation and wealth hailed as divine blessing can both mask a heart ruled by greed.

**The rich man knew Lazarus's name.** During life, the story never records him speaking a word to Lazarus. Yet from torment he calls the beggar by name and asks to send him on an errand (16:24). That means he saw him every day at his own front gate and chose to walk past. His sin was not some dramatic atrocity; it was stepping past a suffering neighbor every day as though there were nothing to do.

**Scripture is already enough; miracles cannot replace a willing heart.** The rich man assumed his brothers needed a startling sign (16:30). Abraham answered that whoever ignores Moses and the Prophets will not believe even a resurrection (16:31). What is missing is not evidence, but a readiness to obey.

## Deeper Meaning

**No one can limp between two opinions.** On Mount Carmel, Elijah challenged Israel: "How long will you waver between two opinions?" (1 Kings 18:21). The people wanted to worship both the LORD and Baal, the storm god of rain and harvests. Mammon is the Baal of every age—the rival god promising abundance. Verse 16:13 confronts listeners with the exact same choice.

**The LORD looks at the heart.** When Samuel went to anoint a king, he was swayed by the eldest brother's impressive appearance. The LORD told him, "Man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). Jesus speaks that same truth to those who "justify yourselves before men" (16:15).

**Amos had already condemned the rich man's lifestyle.** The prophet described people lounging on beds of ivory, eating choice calves, drinking wine by the bowlful, "yet they do not grieve over the ruin of Joseph" (Amos 6:4–6). The rich man in the parable feasted daily while ruin lay right outside his gate. Moses and the Prophets—the very Scriptures Abraham invoked—had made this unmistakably clear.

**This great reversal was announced at the very start of Luke's Gospel.** Mary sang that God "has filled the hungry with good things, but has sent the rich away empty" (1:53). Jesus warned, "Woe to you who are rich, for you have already received your comfort!" (6:24). In 16:25 Abraham echoes those words almost directly: the rich man received his good things, and now Lazarus is comforted.

**A real Lazarus truly rose from the dead, and people still refused to believe.** John's Gospel tells how Jesus called Lazarus of Bethany out of his tomb. Far from believing, the chief priests plotted to kill Lazarus too, because many were putting their faith in Jesus on his account (John 12:10–11). Abraham's closing words in 16:31 came true down to the letter.

**The risen One opens Moses and the Prophets.** At the close of Luke's Gospel, on the road to Emmaus, the risen Jesus "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets" explained to two disciples what the Scriptures said about Himself (24:27). The One who rises from the dead does not come to replace Scripture, but to open it.

## Notes & Nuances

**The parable of the dishonest manager has several major interpretations.** First, he cheats one more time, and the praise targets his sheer resourcefulness—much as Jesus uses an unjust judge to teach about prayer (18:6). Second, he cancels only his own added commission or interest, making his action less fraudulent than it looks. Third, he gambles on his master's generosity. Another wrinkle is that the word "master" in verse 8 could refer either to the rich employer or to Jesus Himself, since Greek uses the same word (*kyrios*). As for "unrighteous wealth," it probably does not mean stolen money, but earthly wealth in general—a resource entangled with an unjust world.

**"Everyone is forcing his way into it" (16:16) is difficult to translate.** Some understand it positively: crowds are eagerly pressing into God's kingdom. Others take it passively: people are being strongly urged into it, like the guests at the great banquet (14:23). Still others see a reference to violent opposition directed against the kingdom. The parallel saying in Matthew speaks more explicitly of violence (Matthew 11:12).

**The rich man and Lazarus is not a literal map of the afterlife.** "Abraham's side," Hades, the chasm, fire, and a drop of water were familiar Jewish imagery for speaking about death. Some readers take the account as a literal description of the intermediate state before the final resurrection. Most commentators think Jesus draws on imagery His listeners already knew to drive home a sober truth: how we live now has lasting consequences, and after death the door to change closes. One should not build a full doctrine of the afterlife from story details like the dead conversing across a chasm.

**The rich man is not condemned simply for owning wealth, nor Lazarus saved merely for being poor.** Abraham himself, holding Lazarus at his side, was "very rich" (Genesis 13:2). The parable never lists Lazarus's virtues, yet his name means "God helps," picturing someone with no one else to lean on. The rich man is condemned because he lived as though neither God nor the suffering neighbor at his gate existed.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople famed for his preaching, delivered an entire series of sermons on Lazarus and the rich man. Refusing to share with the poor, he declared, is theft, because the surplus we cling to belongs to them, not us. **Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, preached on this passage in his Sunday sermons. He taught that "Abraham's bosom" is not a place on a map, but God's promise to Abraham in which believers rest while awaiting the last day. He also stressed that the rich man was condemned not for his riches, but for his unbelief—an unbelief exposed by his indifference to the poor. *(Church Postil, sermon for the First Sunday after Trinity)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Luther interpreting "Abraham's bosom" as God's promise in his sermon on Luke 16:19–31; exact phrasing on the rich man's unbelief unverified -->

**Joseph Fitzmyer**, American Jesuit priest and New Testament scholar, argued that the manager did not defraud his master further when reducing the bills. Rather, he wrote off his own built-in commission that had been added to each invoice. By forfeiting his personal profit, he won the debtors' goodwill without costing his master a cent—explaining why the master praised him. *(The Gospel According to Luke, Anchor Bible, 1981–1985)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Fitzmyer adopts the commission reading of the manager and authored a dedicated study on it; exact supporting steps unverified -->

**Kenneth Bailey**, an American scholar who spent decades living in the Middle East, read the manager through village culture. The master neither jails nor punishes him, merely dismissing him—clearly a generous man. The manager bets everything on that generosity. Once the debts are slashed, the whole village will hail the master as noble, and he cannot reverse the reductions without losing honor. The lesson, Bailey argues, is that disciples too must stake their entire future on their Master's mercy. *(Poet and Peasant, 1976)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading the manager as wagering on his master's mercy is Bailey's well-known thesis in Poet and Peasant -->

**The Jerusalem Talmud**, the rabbinic commentary compiled in Galilee, preserves a strikingly similar tale. A wealthy tax collector named Bar Ma'yan dies and receives a lavish citywide funeral, while a poor Torah scholar dies unnoticed. Afterward, in a dream, the scholar is seen resting in a watered garden, while Bar Ma'yan stands beside a river straining his tongue toward water he cannot reach.
