# Luke 17 — Where Are the Other Nine?

## Story

Jesus said to His disciples: Things that cause people to stumble are bound to come, but woe to the one through whom they come! It would be better for him to have a millstone hung around his neck and be thrown into the sea than to cause one of these little ones to stumble. "Watch yourselves." If your brother sins, rebuke him; and if he repents, forgive him. Even if he sins against you seven times in a day, and seven times returns to you saying, "I repent," you must forgive him (17:1–4). Rebuke and forgiveness walk hand in hand. Seven times in a single day is a way of saying: without limit.

The apostles said to the Lord, "Increase our faith!" The Lord replied: If you have faith as small as a mustard seed, you can say to this mulberry tree, "Be uprooted and planted in the sea," and it will obey you (17:5–6). They asked for more faith because His command to forgive sounded impossibly hard. He answers that the issue is not the quantity of faith, but the One toward whom faith is directed. Even a tiny seed of genuine faith placed in God can accomplish the impossible.

Suppose one of you has a servant plowing or tending sheep. Will he say to the servant when he comes in from the field, "Come along now and sit down to eat"? Won't he rather say, "Prepare my supper, get yourself ready, and wait on me while I eat and drink; after that you may eat and drink"? Does he thank the servant because he did what he was told to do? So you also, when you have done everything you were commanded, should say, "We are unworthy servants; we have only done our duty" (17:7–10). This reflects ordinary master-servant customs of the day, not a portrait of how God treats His people—back in Luke 12:37, the Master Himself serves His servants at table. Here Jesus reminds disciples that serving God never puts Him in our debt.

On His way to Jerusalem, Jesus traveled along the border between Samaria and Galilee. As He entered a village, ten men with leprosy met Him. Standing at a distance, they raised their voices and called out, "Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!" When He saw them, He said, "Go, show yourselves to the priests." And as they went, they were cleansed (17:11–14; Leviticus 13:45–46). Lepers were required to keep their distance and call out so others would stay clear. Unlike an earlier occasion (Luke 5:13), He does not touch them; He sends them to the priests while the disease is still on their skin. They walk away in faith and are healed on the road.

One of them, seeing that he was healed, turned back, praising God in a loud voice, and fell facedown at Jesus' feet, thanking Him. And he was a Samaritan. Jesus asked, "Were not all ten cleansed? Where are the other nine? Was no one found to return and give praise to God except this foreigner?" Then He said to him, "Rise and go; your faith has made you well" (17:15–19). Leprosy had forced a Samaritan and nine Jews into a shared exile of outcasts. All ten were cleansed, yet only one heard the words "your faith has saved you." The other nine received healthy skin; this man received a restored relationship with God.

Asked by the Pharisees when the kingdom of God would come, Jesus answered: The kingdom of God does not come with observable signs, nor will people say, "Look, here it is," or "There it is"; for indeed, the kingdom of God is in your midst (17:20–21). The Greek phrase here can be rendered "within you"—meaning inside the heart, as older translations render it—or "in your midst," which many modern scholars prefer, because Jesus is addressing the Pharisees: the kingdom has already arrived in the very person of Jesus standing before them.

He said to His disciples: The days are coming when you will long to see one of the days of the Son of Man, and you will not see it. People will tell you, "Look, there He is!" or "Here He is!" Do not go running off after them. For just as lightning flashes and lights up the sky from one end to the other, so will the Son of Man be in His day. Yet first He must suffer many things and be rejected by this generation (17:22–25). His return will need no tour guide; everyone will see it as unmistakably as lightning. Yet before glory comes the cross.

Just as it was in the days of Noah, so it will be in the days of the Son of Man: people were eating, drinking, marrying, and being given in marriage up to the day Noah entered the ark, and the flood came and destroyed them all. It was the same in the days of Lot: people were eating and drinking, buying and selling, planting and building; yet on the day Lot left Sodom, fire and sulfur rained down from heaven and destroyed them all. It will be just like this on the day the Son of Man is revealed (17:26–30; Genesis 7:6–23; Genesis 19:23–25). Nothing on that list is wicked—eating, marrying, buying, building are everyday life. The peril is living as if that day will never come.

On that day, let no one on the housetop come down to get his goods inside the house, and let no one in the field turn back. "Remember Lot's wife!" Whoever tries to preserve his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life will preserve it. I tell you, on that night two people will be in one bed; one will be taken and the other left. Two women will be grinding grain together; one will be taken and the other left. The disciples asked Him, "Where, Lord?" He replied, "Where there is a dead body, there the vultures will gather" (17:31–37; Genesis 19:26). Lot's wife had already escaped the city, yet her heart remained behind, and she looked back. Verse 36—about two men in the field—does not appear in the earliest manuscripts and was likely copied from Matthew 24:40. The closing line is a proverb: when judgment comes, it arrives as surely as vultures gather over a carcass.

## Key Takeaways

**Disciples must watch out for one another.** Jesus begins with a stern warning against causing the vulnerable to stumble (17:1–2), and follows immediately with "Watch yourselves" (17:3). Following Christ means we cannot act as if a neighbor's spiritual life is none of our concern. We must have the courage to speak up when a brother sins, and the readiness to forgive when he turns back.

**Faith is not a power to boast about, but the quiet obedience of a servant.** The apostles ask for more faith as if asking for extra capital (17:5). Jesus responds with two back-to-back images: a mustard seed, followed by a servant who comes in from the field and still serves dinner (17:6–8). Real faith does the work God assigns and then says, "we have only done our duty" (17:10).

**The other nine never disobeyed Jesus.** They did exactly what He told them: they went to show themselves to the priests (17:14). Only the Samaritan turned back—pausing his errand to return to the One who sent him (17:15–16). It is possible to obey God's instructions while forgetting the Giver. Gratitude is the place where a gift becomes a relationship.

**God's kingdom has already arrived, yet is still coming.** To the Pharisees, Jesus says the kingdom is not something to track down here or there (17:20–21). To the disciples, He speaks of a future day arriving suddenly like lightning (17:24). The kingdom is already present in Jesus, while its full unveiling still lies ahead. Disciples live between those two moments.

**Looking back can cost everything.** Lot's wife had already left the city and was nearly safe (17:32). Jesus links her memory to the person on a rooftop tempted to go back inside for belongings (17:31). The danger is not possessions themselves, but a heart still glued to them when God calls us forward.

## Deeper Meaning

**Naaman was another foreign leper who turned back.** After washing seven times in the Jordan and being cleansed, the Syrian commander returned to stand before Elisha and confess the God of Israel (2 Kings 5:14–15). Right at the opening of His ministry in Nazareth, Jesus had recalled him: in Elisha's day, none of Israel's lepers was cleansed, "only Naaman the Syrian" (4:27). The Samaritan in 17:16 steps straight into Naaman's footsteps.

**The law of Moses already commanded rebuking one's neighbor.** Leviticus instructs, "Do not hate your brother in your heart. Rebuke your neighbor frankly" (Leviticus 19:17). The two halves belong together: staying silent while a brother destroys himself can be a disguised form of hatred. The command to "rebuke him" in 17:3 springs not from irritation, but from the same love that teaches us to forgive.

**The servant has nothing to boast of.** Speaking of his own preaching ministry, Paul says, "I have no reason to boast, because I am compelled to preach" (1 Corinthians 9:16). His attitude matches the servant's confession in 17:10. Serving God is never lending Him a favor; it is answering grace already received.

**"The Son of Man" in His day draws on Daniel's vision.** Daniel saw "one like a son of man" coming with the clouds of heaven and receiving an everlasting kingdom (Daniel 7:13–14). When Jesus speaks of "the day of the Son of Man" (17:24), anyone steeped in Scripture hears that vision. Yet He adds a line Daniel never stated: first the Son of Man must suffer many things (17:25).

**The warning not to look back comes straight from Lot's rescue.** The angels urged Lot's family, "Flee for your lives! Do not look back" (Genesis 19:17). Paul writes like a runner on the track: "forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead" (Philippians 3:14). Someone pulled out of Sodom cannot sprint forward while staring backward.

## Notes & Nuances

**"The kingdom of God is within you" (17:21) carries three main interpretations.** First, inside the human heart, as rendered by older versions and many Church Fathers—Russian novelist Leo Tolstoy even borrowed this verse for the title of his book on nonviolence. Second, "in your midst," meaning present in Jesus Himself, since He is addressing Pharisees who have not yet believed in Him. Third, "within your reach," waiting only to be received. Most modern scholars favor the second, though the three need not exclude one another: the kingdom arrives in the person of Jesus and enters the hearts of those who welcome Him.

**Were the other nine saved?** All ten were cleansed, and Jesus did not revoke the healing of the nine (17:14). The Greek verb in 17:19 is the same word Luke uses for the bleeding woman, often translated "your faith has healed you" (8:48). For that reason, some hold that Jesus' words to the Samaritan say nothing different from His words to other healed individuals. Yet in context, only this man returns, glorifies God, and falls at Jesus' feet; most commentators therefore see a deeper salvation here than merely cured skin.

**Who are "these little ones"?** No children appear in the scene at 17:2. Some understand the phrase to mean literal children, as in Matthew 18; many take it to mean fragile or new believers who are easily shaken. Either way, the warning is equally grave: leading a vulnerable person away from God is something Jesus treats with utmost seriousness.

**Is the one "taken" rescued or judged?** Many readers connect 17:34–35 with believers being caught up beforehand. In Luke, however, the disciples immediately ask, "Where, Lord?" and Jesus answers with corpses and vultures (17:37). Some therefore argue that being "taken" means swept away in judgment, like the people in Noah's flood. These brief sayings do not provide a detailed timetable of end-time events; what is unmistakable is that the coming day will divide people living side by side.

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, a theologian in Carthage, North Africa, around AD 200, wrote against Marcion, who claimed Jesus came to overthrow the law of Moses and the Old Testament God. Tertullian pointed to the ten lepers: Jesus sent them to the priests precisely as Mosaic law required, proving He honored the Law rather than destroying it. He also noted a contrast: Elisha healed one leper, whereas Jesus healed ten at once with a single word. *(Against Marcion, Book 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Book 4 cites the ten lepers to show Jesus upheld Mosaic law; the comparison with Elisha is recalled from the same section, unverified -->

**Augustine**, fourth-to-fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, read leprosy as a picture of false doctrine mixed with truth, like mottled patches across the skin. The ten sent to show themselves to the priests represent people who must be examined and received by the church, while the one returning in gratitude pictures humility—the grace that makes healing complete. *(Questions on the Gospels, Book 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Augustine reading leprosy as mixed heresy in Quaestiones Evangeliorum Book 2; details on the returning Samaritan unverified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, appealed to verse 17:10 to refute the notion that people can perform extra good works beyond God's commands and transfer surplus merit to others. If doing everything commanded still leaves us unworthy servants, he argued, no one has spare merit to trade. Everyone stands before God not on earned credit, but on grace alone. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 3, chapter 14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin cites the "unworthy servants" saying against works of supererogation in Institutes Book 3 chapter 14 -->

**The Mishnah**, the compendium of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, records Rabbi Eliezer saying that eating the bread of a Samaritan is like eating pork. That remark reveals how deep the rift ran between Jews and Samaritans. Only shared affliction could throw nine Jews and one Samaritan together in a single band: cast out of every village alike, they had only one another. *(Mishnah, Shevi'it 8.10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Rabbi Eliezer's saying on Samaritan bread in Shevi'it chapter 8 is widely cited; the connection to Luke 17 is editorial commentary -->

**Joseph Fitzmyer**, American Jesuit priest and New Testament scholar, closely examined the Greek phrase in 17:21, weighing all three renderings: inside, in your midst, and within reach.
