# Luke 19 — The King on a Colt

## Story

Jesus entered Jericho and was passing through the city. A man there named Zacchaeus was a chief tax collector and was very rich. He tried to see who Jesus was, but could not because of the crowd, since he was short. So he ran ahead and climbed a sycamore tree to see Him, because Jesus was about to pass that way. When Jesus reached the spot, He looked up and said to him, "Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for I must stay at your house today." Zacchaeus hurried down and welcomed Him joyfully. Seeing this, everyone began to grumble: "He has gone to be the guest of a sinner!" (19:1–7). A wealthy, prominent official running and climbing a tree like a child—Zacchaeus threw away all dignity just to get a glimpse of Him. Jesus calls him by name and invites Himself into his home.

Standing before the Lord, Zacchaeus said: Look, Lord, half of my possessions I give to the poor, and if I have cheated anyone of anything, I will repay it fourfold. Jesus said to him, "Today salvation has come to this house, because this man too is a son of Abraham. For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (19:8–10; Exodus 22:1). In the previous chapter, a rich ruler walked away grieving; here a rich man gives away with joy. The impossibility of Luke 18:27 has just happened. Mosaic law required fourfold restitution only for stealing a sheep; Zacchaeus voluntarily applies the heaviest penalty to himself. Verse 10 may well summarize Luke's entire Gospel.

While they were listening to this, He went on to tell a parable, because He was near Jerusalem and the people supposed the kingdom of God was going to appear at once. A nobleman traveled to a distant country to receive a kingdom for himself and then return. Before leaving, he called ten of his servants, gave them ten minas, and instructed them: Put this money to work until I come back. Yet his citizens hated him and sent a delegation after him to say, "We do not want this man to reign over us!" (19:11–14). First-century listeners would have immediately recalled Archelaus, son of Herod the Great, who sailed to Rome to claim the kingship of Judea while a Jewish delegation followed to protest his rule.

After receiving the kingdom and returning, he summoned the servants to find out what each had gained. The first came and said: Master, your mina has earned ten more. The master replied, "Well done, good servant! Because you have been faithful in a very small matter, take charge of ten cities." The second came and said: Master, your mina has made five more. The master answered, "You take charge of five cities" (19:15–19). The reward dwarfs the original assignment: one coin traded for ten cities.

Then another came and said: Master, here is your mina; I have kept it tucked away in a handkerchief, because I was afraid of you, since you are a harsh man—you withdraw what you did not deposit and reap what you did not sow. The master replied, "I will judge you by your own words, you wicked servant!" If you knew I was such a harsh man, why didn't you put my money in the bank so that on my return I could collect it with interest? Then he told those standing by: Take his mina away and give it to the one who has ten. They said: Master, he already has ten! I tell you that to everyone who has, more will be given, while from the one who has nothing, even what he has will be taken away. As for those enemies of mine who did not want me to reign over them—bring them here and slay them in front of me (19:20–27). The third servant lost nothing, yet risked nothing either. Fear paralyzed him because he misjudged his master's character. The final sentence is stark: the parable closes with judgment on those who reject the King—and immediately afterward, Jesus walks into the very city about to reject Him.

Having said this, Jesus walked ahead of the crowd, going up to Jerusalem. As He approached Bethphage and Bethany at the Mount of Olives, He sent two of His disciples ahead, saying: Go into the village ahead of you; as you enter, you will find a colt tied there that has never been ridden. Untie it and bring it here. If anyone asks why you are untying it, say, "The Lord needs it." Those who were sent went and found it just as He had told them. As they were untying the colt, its owners asked: Why are you untying the colt? They answered, "The Lord needs it." They brought it to Jesus, threw their cloaks on the colt, and put Jesus on it. As He rode along, people spread their cloaks on the road (19:28–36; Zechariah 9:9; 2 Kings 9:13). Zechariah had foretold it: your King comes humbly, riding on a colt. Spreading cloaks on the road was an act of royal acclaim.

When He came near the slope going down the Mount of Olives, the whole multitude of disciples began to rejoice and praise God loudly for all the miracles they had seen: "Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord! Peace in heaven and glory in the highest!" Some Pharisees in the crowd said to Him, "Teacher, rebuke Your disciples!" He replied, "I tell you, if they keep silent, the stones will cry out" (19:37–40; Psalms 118:26). Their shout recalls the angels' song at Bethlehem (Luke 2:14). There it was "peace on earth"; here it is "peace in heaven," as if the city below is about to turn peace away.

As He drew near and saw the city, He wept over it and said, "If only you had known on this day what would bring you peace!" Yet now it is hidden from your eyes. Days will come upon you when your enemies will build an embankment around you, surround you, and hem you in on every side; they will dash you and your children to the ground and will not leave one stone upon another, "because you did not recognize the time of your visitation" (19:41–44). In AD 70, Roman armies besieged and leveled Jerusalem just as He described. Amid the cheering, the King weeps. Jerusalem's very name was widely linked with "peace," yet the city failed to recognize peace when He arrived.

Jesus entered the temple and began driving out those who were selling there, saying, "It is written: 'My house will be a house of prayer,' but you have made it 'a den of robbers.'" Every day He taught in the temple. The chief priests, the scribes, and the leaders of the people sought to destroy Him, yet they could not find a way to do it, because all the people hung on His words (19:45–48; Isaiah 56:7; Jeremiah 7:11). Luke keeps the cleansing of the temple remarkably brief. Afterward the temple becomes a classroom: the One once found there at age twelve (Luke 2:46) now teaches daily in His Father's house.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus seeks the person everyone else has written off.** Zacchaeus tries to catch sight of Jesus, yet it is Jesus who stops, calls him by name, and invites Himself to his house (19:5). The crowd mutters because to them he is simply "a sinner" (19:7). The closing verse explains everything: "For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (19:10). Lost people do not have to find their own way back; Someone comes searching for them.

**Salvation shows up in a person's wallet.** Zacchaeus says nothing about his feelings or theology. He talks about money: half his estate to the poor, fourfold restitution to anyone he wronged (19:8). Jesus responds immediately, "Today salvation has come to this house" (19:9). He does not buy salvation with cash; rather, once salvation enters the house, it transforms how he holds money and treats those he exploited.

**While the King is away, His servants must get to work.** People expected God's kingdom to materialize immediately (19:11). The parable of the minas answers them: the King is away for a season before returning, and in the meantime each servant must put his trust to productive use. The third servant is condemned not for losing money, but for letting fear paralyze him—fear born from a distorted view of his master (19:21).

**The King enters His city and weeps.** Surrounded by shouts of "Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord!" (19:38), Jesus gazes on Jerusalem and breaks into tears (19:41). He knows the city will reject Him and foresees what awaits it. His announcement of judgment is spoken through tears, never gloating.

**The crowds listen while the leaders plot to kill Him.** After clearing the merchants out, Jesus teaches in the temple every day (19:45–47). The chief priests and scribes look for a way to eliminate Him yet cannot, because "all the people hung on His words" (19:48). Luke draws a sharp line between these two groups and maintains it all the way to the cross.

## Deeper Meaning

**The Shepherd searching for lost sheep.** Through Ezekiel, God rebuked shepherds who fed only themselves and promised to shepherd the flock Himself: "I will search for the lost" (Ezekiel 34:16). Jesus' statement in 19:10 carries that exact promise. In chapter 15 He told of a shepherd leaving ninety-nine sheep to track down one stray (15:4–6). In Jericho, that stray sheep has a name: Zacchaeus.

**Children of Abraham.** John the Baptist had warned the crowds not to boast of descent from Abraham, since God could raise up children for Abraham from stones (3:8). Jesus, by contrast, calls the bent-over woman a "daughter of Abraham" (13:16) and Zacchaeus a "son of Abraham" (19:9). Two despised outsiders are restored to full membership in God's family.

**The stones will cry out.** Habakkuk warned the builder of a house funded by injustice that "the stone will cry out from the wall" as a witness against him (Habakkuk 2:9–11). Jesus declares that if the disciples stay silent, the stones will cry out (19:40). Praise for the King cannot be silenced—and just a few verses later He warns that the city's very stones will be thrown down until not one rests on another (19:44).

**The time of visitation.** At the opening of the Gospel, the priest Zechariah sang that God "has visited and redeemed His people" (1:68). When Jesus raised the widow's son at Nain, the crowds likewise exclaimed, "God has visited His people" (7:16). The lament in 19:44 reveals the tragedy of that visitation: God came near, and His city failed to recognize Him.

**A prophet's tears.** Jeremiah wept over Jerusalem before Babylon destroyed it: "Oh, that my head were a spring of water, and my eyes a fountain of tears!" (Jeremiah 9:1). Jesus stands squarely in that prophetic line. He announces judgment as one who loves the city, never as its enemy.

**The Lord comes to His temple.** Malachi promised, "Suddenly the Lord you are seeking will come to His temple," and asked who could endure the day of His coming (Malachi 3:1–2). Luke shows Jesus riding straight from the Mount of Olives into the temple (19:45). Cleansing the temple brings that prophecy into view—both mercy and reckoning.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is verse 19:27 talking about, and does it sanction violence?** The line "bring them here and slay them in front of me" (19:27) is among the harshest sentences in the parables. Most commonly, the nobleman represents Jesus returning as King, and the verse points to final judgment on those who reject His reign—some also linking it to Jerusalem's fall in AD 70, predicted just verses later. Because the parable borrows the real-world mold of an earthly ruler like Archelaus, not every detail mirrors God's character; a small minority even read the story inversely, viewing the ruler as a tyrant and the third servant as a whistleblower refusing to enrich him. However one reads it, the verse never puts a sword in disciples' hands. Moments later, this very King weeps over the city rejecting Him (19:41), and on the night of His arrest He heals the ear of a servant struck by a disciple's blade (22:51).

**Is Zacchaeus repenting, or defending his record?** In Greek, Zacchaeus speaks in the present tense: *I give*, *I repay*. Most readers take this as a decisive, on-the-spot commitment: transformed by Jesus' visit, he pledges a new way of life from this moment forward. Some scholars read the verbs as habitual present: he has long been generous and is defending himself against the crowd's grumbling. On that second view, the story becomes Jesus vindicating a misunderstood man rather than a sinner's conversion. The first reading fits far better with the punchline about seeking and saving "the lost" (19:10).

**Minas are not talents.** In Matthew's parable, each servant receives a different number of talents, "each according to his ability" (Matthew 25:15). In Luke, ten servants each receive exactly one mina—roughly three months' wages, far smaller than a talent—and Luke weaves in the subplot of a nobleman claiming a throne amid citizen opposition. Some take these as two separate parables told on different occasions; others see one parable adapted by each evangelist. In Luke, everyone starts with the same stake; the difference lies in what each does with it.

**Was the prophecy of AD 70 spoken beforehand or shaped afterward?** The description of enemies throwing up embankments and encircling the city (19:43) closely matches the Roman siege of AD 70. Since many scholars date Luke's Gospel after that year, some suspect the wording was sharpened in hindsight. Others note that nearly every phrase comes directly from Old Testament prophecies of Babylon's siege, such as "I will camp against you all around; I will besiege you with towers and set up siege works against you" (Isaiah 29:3)—no hindsight after AD 70 was needed to speak in those terms.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa at the close of the fourth century, preached on Zacchaeus and the sycamore tree. The crowd, he said, represents what blocks small people from seeing Jesus, so Zacchaeus had to climb a tree people looked down upon in order to see Him. He linked that tree to the cross, which the world dismisses as foolishness (1 Corinthians 1:23): whoever wants to see the Lord must be willing to climb what others mock. *(Sermons, homily on Zacchaeus)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls an Augustine sermon on Zacchaeus connecting the sycamore tree with the foolishness of the cross; exact sermon number and phrasing unverified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, emphasized that Zacchaeus's generosity and fourfold restitution did not purchase salvation; they were the fruit of newfound faith. He also pointed out that Zacchaeus gave away half—not all—of his goods, showing this passage lays down no blanket rule requiring believers to give away everything they own. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: treating works as evidence rather than cause of salvation is characteristic of Calvin; recalls his point on "half," exact location and phrasing unverified -->

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, recounts that after Herod the Great died, his son Archelaus traveled to Rome to ask Emperor Augustus to confirm his royal title, while a Jewish embassy followed to beg Rome not to let him rule them. Augustus granted him governance over part of the territory without the title of king. Many commentators note that Jesus' audience near Jericho would instantly think of this episode when hearing the parable in 19:12–14. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 17; The Jewish War, Book 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Archelaus's journey to Rome and the opposing Jewish delegation are well attested in these books of Josephus; connecting them to Luke 19 is a widespread reading -->

**Joseph Fitzmyer**, American Jesuit priest and New Testament scholar, read Zacchaeus's statement in 19:8 as describing his existing practice. Rather than announcing a brand-new conversion pledge, Zacchaeus states what he already habitually does to answer the crowd's murmurings. Fitzmyer therefore sees the scene primarily as Jesus vindicating an unfairly maligned man rather than a sudden change of life. *(The Gospel According to Luke, Anchor Bible, Vol. 2, 1985)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Fitzmyer as a leading representative of the vindication reading of Zacchaeus; exact wording unverified -->

**N. T. Wright**, British bishop and New Testament scholar, contends that the parable of the minas is not primarily about Jesus' return at the end of time. First-century hearers, he argues, would hear it as the story of Israel's God returning to Zion, as the prophets promised, to inspect what His people had done with their calling. And when Jesus rides a colt into Jerusalem and enters the temple right after the parable, that very return is taking place. *(Jesus and the Victory of God, 1996)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Wright reads the minas/talents parables in this book as God's return to Zion; exact chapter not recalled -->
