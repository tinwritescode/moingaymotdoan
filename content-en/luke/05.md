# Luke 5 — Nets Full of Fish

## Story

Jesus stood beside the Lake of Gennesaret—the Sea of Galilee—with a crowd pressing in around Him to hear the word of God. Spotting two boats moored near the shore while the fishermen were out washing their nets, He stepped into Simon's boat, asked him to push out a little from land, and sat down to teach the crowds from the boat (5:1–3). A fishing boat becomes His pulpit.

When He finished speaking, He told Simon, "Put out into deep water and let down your nets for a catch." Simon answered, "Master, we have worked hard all night without catching anything. But because You say so, I will let down the nets." When they did so, they caught such a huge shoal of fish that their nets began to tear. They signaled their partners in the other boat to come help, and they filled both boats so full that they began to sink (5:4–7). Simon is a seasoned fisherman; a traveling preacher tells him to cast nets in broad daylight after an empty night. He obeys before he understands.

Seeing this, Simon Peter fell at Jesus' knees and cried, "Go away from me, Lord; I am a sinful man!" He and all his companions were astonished at the catch of fish they had taken, including James and John, the sons of Zebedee, Simon's partners. Jesus said to Simon, "Do not be afraid; from now on you will catch men." Pulling their boats up on shore, they left everything and followed Him (5:8–11). Peter shifts from calling Him "Master" to calling Him "Lord." Like Isaiah in the temple, he beholds holiness first and immediately sees his own sin (Isaiah 6:5)—and like Isaiah, he receives his calling on the spot.

While Jesus was in one of the towns, a man covered with leprosy came along. Seeing Jesus, he fell facedown and begged Him, "Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean." Jesus reached out His hand and touched him: "I am willing. Be clean!" Immediately the leprosy left him. Jesus ordered him not to tell anyone, only to go show himself to the priest and offer the sacrifices Moses commanded for his cleansing as a testimony to them. Yet news about Him spread all the more, and large crowds came together to hear Him and be healed of their sicknesses; meanwhile Jesus often withdrew to lonely places and prayed (5:12–16; Leviticus 14:1–32). Under the Law, touching a leper transmitted uncleanness; here cleanness flows the opposite direction, from Jesus into the sick man.

One day as He was teaching, Pharisees and teachers of the Law sat watching—men who had come from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem—and the power of the Lord was with Him to heal. Some men came carrying a paralyzed man on a mat and tried to bring him inside to lay him before Jesus. Finding no way through the crowd, they climbed onto the roof, removed the tiles, and lowered him on his mat right into the center in front of Jesus. Seeing their faith, Jesus said, "Friend, your sins are forgiven." The scribes and Pharisees began thinking to themselves, "Who is this man who speaks blasphemy? Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (5:17–21).

Knowing their thoughts, Jesus asked: which is easier to say, "Your sins are forgiven," or "Get up and walk"? So that you may know the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, He told the paralyzed man, "I tell you, get up, pick up your mat, and go home." Immediately he stood up in front of them, picked up what he had been lying on, and went home glorifying God. Everyone was amazed, praised God, and filled with awe declared, "We have seen remarkable things today!" (5:22–26). The scribes' premise was completely right: only God forgives sins. The visible miracle testifies to the invisible one.

After this, Jesus went out and saw a tax collector named Levi sitting at his tax booth. He told him, "Follow Me." Leaving everything behind, Levi got up and followed Him. Then Levi held a great banquet for Jesus at his house, where a large crowd of tax collectors and others reclined at table with them. The Pharisees and their scribes grumbled to His disciples: why do you eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners? Jesus answered, "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick. I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance" (5:27–32). Matthew tells this same episode and calls the tax collector Matthew (Matthew 9:9).

They said to Him: John's disciples often fast and pray, and so do the disciples of the Pharisees, while Yours keep eating and drinking. Jesus replied: can you make the wedding guests fast while the bridegroom is still with them? The time will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them; in those days they will fast. Then He told them a parable: no one tears a piece out of a new garment to patch an old one, or they will ruin the new garment and the patch will not match the old. Nor does anyone pour new wine into old wineskins, or the new wine will burst the skins, spilling the wine and ruining the skins; new wine must be poured into new wineskins. And no one after drinking old wine wants the new, for they say, "The old is better" (5:33–39). Only Luke preserves that closing sentence—a candid window into human nature: the familiar feels comfortable, so many refuse to taste the new life Jesus brings.

## Key Takeaways

**Encountering God's holiness exposes our sin, yet His grace calls us into mission instead of driving us away.** After the miraculous catch, Peter begs Jesus to leave him because he is sinful, yet Jesus tells him not to fear and calls him to catch people (5:8–11). Genuine repentance leads to leaving everything behind to follow Him.

**Jesus holds authority to forgive sins, not merely heal bodies.** Healing the paralyzed man lowered through the roof, He purposefully pronounces forgiveness first before commanding him to walk home (5:20–25). The visible cure validates the invisible power belonging solely to God.

**The gospel seeks those who know they need a doctor, not those convinced they are already well.** Calling Levi the tax collector and sharing meals with social outcasts irritates the religious elite (5:27–30). Jesus replies plainly that He came to call sinners to repentance (5:31–32).

**The new life Christ brings cannot be squeezed into brittle old molds.** Through the bridegroom's feast, fresh cloth, and fresh wineskins, Jesus reveals that His arrival launches a joyful new age calling for hearts willing to be made new (5:34–38).

## Deeper Meaning

**Peter's reaction by the lake echoes Isaiah's encounter in the temple.** Witnessing divine power, Peter cries out that he is a sinful man (5:8), matching Isaiah's cry before the Lord's throne (Isaiah 6:5). Both receive tender reassurance and an immediate commission (5:10; Isaiah 6:8).

**Touching the leper overturns the Law's flow of uncleanness.** Under Mosaic regulations, touching leprosy spread defilement, and healed lepers had to obtain priestly verification before rejoining society (Leviticus 14:1–4). Jesus honors the Law by sending him to the priest while proving His own holiness overpowers every uncleanness (5:13–14).

**The title "Son of Man" wielding authority to forgive links to Daniel's vision.** Calling Himself the Son of Man with authority on earth to forgive sins (5:24) recalls the heavenly figure "like a son of man" given everlasting rule over every nation (Daniel 7:13–14).

**The bridegroom image invokes God's marriage covenant with His people.** In the Old Testament, God portrays Himself as Israel's husband (Hosea 2:19; Isaiah 62:5). Calling Himself the bridegroom and foreseeing the day He will be "taken away" (5:34–35), Jesus hints both at His divine identity and at the looming shadow of the cross.

## Notes & Nuances

**Is Peter's calling here the same event told by Matthew and Mark?** Mark 1:16–20 gives a brief account of Jesus calling Simon, Andrew, James, and John along the shore, while Luke 5:1–11 adds teaching from the boat and the astonishing catch. Many scholars see the same occasion told in fuller detail by Luke; others think Jesus called them across multiple encounters before they left fishing permanently.

**Saying He did not come to call "the righteous" (5:32) does not imply the Pharisees were actually sinless.** Jesus uses gentle irony: only people aware they are sick seek out a doctor. Anyone convinced they already possess plenty of righteousness locks themselves out from grace.

**"The old is better" in 5:39 appears only in Luke.** Jesus is not agreeing that old ways outshine the gospel; He offers a sharp, human observation about how easily attachment to familiar habits makes people resist the new wine God offers.

**Read the Pharisees in this chapter fairly.** In 5:17 they traveled great distances just to listen to Him, and their alarm in 5:21 sprang from a completely sound conviction that only God forgives sins. Tension erupts because they have not yet recognized that Jesus bears God's own authority.

**Cyril of Alexandria**, fifth-century bishop, reflects on Jesus stretching out His hand to touch the leper (5:13). Mosaic law forbade touching lepers to avoid defilement, yet because Christ's flesh is the very fountain of life and holiness, uncleanness cannot touch Him; instead His holiness flows outward and instantly heals the sick man. *(Homilies on Luke)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Cyril's explanation that Christ's touch transmits holiness instead of contracting defilement is a celebrated theme in his Homilies on Luke -->

**Ambrose**, fourth-century bishop of Milan, reads the two boats filled to sinking with fish (5:2–7) as a picture of the church gathering both Jews and Gentiles together in the net of the gospel. On Peter pleading for Jesus to depart, Ambrose observes that humble awareness of our own sinfulness is the true doorway to receiving Christ's commission. *(Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, book 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Ambrose's symbolic reading of the two boats and Peter's humility; exact original phrasing not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century French Reformer, reads the old wineskins and "the old is better" (5:37–39) pastorally: understanding the fragility of brand-new disciples, Jesus refrains from forcing strict fasting routines on them before they are spiritually ready to carry them. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Luke 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Calvin's pastoral interpretation of fasting and new wineskins; exact wording unchecked -->

**Darrell Bock**, an American New Testament scholar, highlights how Luke clusters five consecutive controversies from mid-chapter 5 into early chapter 6—forgiveness of sins, dining with tax collectors, fasting, and two Sabbath disputes—pressing readers straight toward the central question: who is Jesus to claim such authority? *(Luke, BECNT, volume 1, 1994)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the five-controversy cycle spanning Luke 5:17 to 6:11 is a standard structural insight in Bock's BECNT commentary -->
