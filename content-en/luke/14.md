# Luke 14 — The Great Banquet

## Story

One Sabbath, when Jesus went to dine at the house of a prominent Pharisee, those present were watching Him closely. Right in front of Him was a man suffering from dropsy. Jesus asked the experts in the law and the Pharisees, "Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath or not?" They remained silent. So He took hold of the man, healed him, and sent him away. Then He asked them: If one of you has a son or an ox that falls into a well on the Sabbath day, will you not immediately pull him out? They had no answer (14:1–6). Luke recounts yet another Sabbath healing, following Luke 6:6–11 and Luke 13:10–17. Some ancient manuscripts read "donkey" instead of "son"; the BSB, following other ancient manuscripts, reads "son or ox."

Noticing how the invited guests were choosing the places of honor at the table, He told them a parable. When someone invites you to a wedding feast, do not take the place of honor, lest a more distinguished guest has been invited and the host comes and says to you, "Give this man your seat." Then in humiliation you will have to take the lowest place. Instead, when you are invited, go and sit in the lowest place, so that when your host comes he will tell you, "Friend, move up to a better place." Then you will be honored in front of all your fellow guests. "For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and the one who humbles himself will be exalted" (14:7–11; Proverbs 25:6–7). Jesus is not merely teaching table etiquette. In God's kingdom, the Host assigns the seats; guests do not claim them.

He also spoke to the man who had invited Him: When you host a luncheon or dinner, do not invite your friends, your brothers, your relatives, or your rich neighbors, lest they invite you back and you be repaid. Rather, when you give a banquet, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, and the blind, and you will be blessed, because they cannot repay you; you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous (14:12–14). In that world, banquets formed a web of social reciprocity: invite someone, and they owe you an invitation in return. Jesus tells him to break that cycle and invite people who can never pay him back.

Hearing this, one of those reclining at the table said to Him, "Blessed is everyone who will eat at the feast in the kingdom of God!" Jesus replied with a parable: a man was preparing a great banquet and invited many guests. At the banquet hour, he sent his servant to tell those who had been invited, "Come, for everything is now ready." Yet all alike began to make excuses. The first said: I have bought a field and must go see it; please excuse me. Another said: I have bought five yoke of oxen and am going to try them out. Still another said: I have just married a wife, so I cannot come (14:15–20). The guest who offered the pious toast assumed everyone sitting there had a guaranteed seat. The parable reveals that the first invited guests never show up. By custom, guests accepted the initial invitation beforehand and were summoned once the food was ready; refusing at that hour was an insult to the host. Besides, nobody buys a field sight unseen or buys oxen without testing them first.

The servant returned and reported this to his master. Angry, the master ordered him: Go out quickly into the streets and alleys of the town, and bring in the poor, the crippled, the blind, and the lame. The servant said: Sir, what you ordered has been done, and there is still room. The master told him: Go out to the highways and hedges and compel them to come in, so that my house will be full. For I tell you, not one of those men who were invited will taste my banquet (14:21–24). The new guest list matches the exact list Jesus just urged His host to invite. The second invitation stays inside the city, while the third goes beyond the walls—foreshadowing the gospel spreading from Israel to the Gentiles. "Compel" here means warmly insisting to people who assume they do not belong, not using force—though tragically, in later history, some twisted this phrase to justify religious coercion.

Large crowds were traveling with Jesus, and turning to them He said: If anyone comes to Me and does not hate his father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters—yes, even his own life—he cannot be My disciple. "And whoever does not carry his cross and follow Me cannot be My disciple" (14:25–27). "Hate" is a Semitic idiom of comparison meaning to love far less; Matthew records "loves father or mother more than Me" (Matthew 10:37). Jesus is not telling anyone to abandon family duty, but placing allegiance to Himself above every other bond.

Suppose one of you wants to build a tower. Won't you first sit down and estimate the cost to see if you have enough money to complete it? Otherwise, once you lay the foundation and cannot finish, everyone who sees it will ridicule you, saying, "This man began to build and wasn't able to finish!" Or what king going to war against another king will not first sit down and consider whether with ten thousand men he can face the one coming against him with twenty thousand? If he cannot, he will send a delegation while the other is still far away and ask for terms of peace. In the same way, any of you who does not give up everything he has cannot be My disciple (14:28–33). Large crowds follow Him, yet Jesus is not thrilled by big numbers. He wants them to count the cost beforehand.

"Salt is good, but if the salt loses its saltiness, with what will it be seasoned?" It is fit neither for the soil nor for the manure pile; it is thrown out. "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (14:34–35). Salt in that region often came from the Dead Sea mixed with other minerals; when the true salt leached out, only tasteless powder remained. A disciple who loses the essence of discipleship is just the same.

## Key Takeaways

**A human being matters more than any rule about rest.** Jesus enters the Pharisee's house under watchful eyes, with a sick man standing right before Him (14:1–2). He does not wait until the Sabbath ends. His question is disarmingly simple: if your own child fell into a well, who would wait until tomorrow (14:5)? This sick man is someone's child too.

**Our seat is assigned by the Host, not seized by us.** Guests scramble for the top seats out of fear of being overlooked (14:7). Jesus points to a deeper freedom: the one who takes the low place has nothing to fight for, having entrusted his honor to God. "Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and the one who humbles himself will be exalted" (14:11).

**True generosity gives without expecting a return.** Inviting people who will invite you back is merely an exchange (14:12). Inviting the poor, the crippled, the lame, and the blind is giving the way God gives—with no strings attached. There is a reward, yet God Himself will pay it at the final day (14:13–14).

**People rarely reject God through glaring crimes; they reject Him through ordinary things.** A field, oxen, and a new marriage are all good things (14:18–20). None of the three guests says, "I hate the host." They simply have other priorities. The parable exposes how even good things can become walls keeping us from God.

**Following Jesus is a lifelong commitment, so count the cost first.** Immediately after flinging the banquet doors wide open to all, Jesus turns to the crowds and speaks plainly about the cost (14:25–33). Both truths belong together: the invitation is free, yet accepting it means handing your whole life to the Host.

## Deeper Meaning

**God is the One who lifts up the humble.** Verse 14:11 echoes Mary's song before Jesus' birth: God "has brought down rulers from their thrones, but has exalted the humble" (1:52). Paul saw Jesus Himself live out those exact words: He "humbled Himself and became obedient to death," and therefore God exalted Him to the highest place (Philippians 2:8–9).

**A lame man dining at the king's table goes back to David.** Once enthroned, David searched for Jonathan's surviving family and invited Mephibosheth, crippled in both feet, to eat at the king's table for the rest of his life (2 Samuel 9:13). Mephibosheth had nothing to repay, and David invited him out of covenant faithfulness to his friend. The banquet in Jesus' parable opens its doors to people just like him (14:21).

**The guests' excuses sound like exemptions from military service.** The law of Moses excused a man who had built a new house, planted a new vineyard, or become betrothed from going out to battle (Deuteronomy 20:5–7), and granted a newly married man a full year at home (Deuteronomy 24:5). Yet those were rules for war. The three guests invoke wartime exemptions as if summoned to fight, when in fact they have been invited to a feast.

**"Come, for everything is now ready" echoes Isaiah's invitation.** The prophet cries out, "You who have no money, come, buy and eat!" (Isaiah 55:1), and then asks why people spend their labor on what does not satisfy (Isaiah 55:2). The new field and new oxen of the three guests are precisely such things.

**Elisha owned twelve yoke of oxen and still left them behind at God's call.** When Elijah threw his cloak over him, Elisha was plowing with twelve pairs of oxen (1 Kings 19:19). He slaughtered a yoke of oxen, burned the plowing equipment to cook the meat for the people, and set out to follow Elijah (1 Kings 19:21). The guest in the parable had only five yoke of oxen, yet could not even make it to dinner (14:19).

## Notes & Nuances

**"Hating" father and mother is a comparative idiom, as Scripture itself proves.** Genesis says Jacob "loved Rachel more than Leah," and the very next verse states that Leah was "hated"—often rendered "unloved" (Genesis 29:30–31). Jacob did not despise Leah; he simply loved her less. Jesus Himself rebuked those who used religious vows as an excuse to avoid caring for their parents (Mark 7:10–12). Luke also includes "even his own life" (14:26): no one is commanded to practice self-loathing, so "hate" here means placing every loyalty—even oneself—decisively below Christ.

**Pulling an animal from a well on the Sabbath was debated among Jews of the time.** The Jewish community at Qumran near the Dead Sea forbade pulling an animal out of a pit on the Sabbath, whereas Pharisaic legal teachers allowed much more leeway. Jesus' question (14:5) therefore appeals to what the Pharisees themselves practiced. He does not accuse them of being uniquely harsh; He asks why the compassion they show an animal does not extend to a human being.

**The teaching on seating is not a clever strategy for winning praise.** Read in isolation, 14:10 might sound like a tactic for getting promoted in front of the room. Yet Jesus calls it a "parable" (14:7), and the closing maxim in 14:11 speaks of what God does. Someone who takes a low seat hoping to be applauded is still scrambling for status—only more subtly.

**Does "give up everything" mean selling all one's possessions?** Some take it literally, and Luke does record disciples who "left everything and followed Him" (5:11). Yet Luke also tells of women who supported Jesus out of their own means (8:3), and Zacchaeus gave away half his wealth (19:8). The mainstream reading is that a disciple no longer treats himself as owner of what he possesses. One may keep or give away, yet Christ alone is Master.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth-to-fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, appealed to the phrase "compel them to come in" in verse 23 to justify Roman imperial laws forcing the Donatists, a breakaway movement in North Africa, back into the church. At first he opposed coercion, then changed his mind when he saw many who were pressured eventually embrace unity sincerely. Later centuries used this interpretation to excuse religious persecution, and today almost all commentators reject it. *(Letter 185, To Boniface, on the Correction of the Donatists)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's use of "compelle intrare" against the Donatists and his change of mind in Letter 185 to Boniface are widely documented -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, remarked on verses 12–14 that Jesus is not forbidding hospitality toward friends and relatives. It is a comparative statement condemning banquets thrown for self-serving return, while pointing out that genuine generosity reaches those who have nothing to give back. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on chapter 14 verses 12–14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin routinely reads "do not X, but Y" sayings comparatively; confident in his main point here, exact wording unverified -->

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, German pastor executed by the Nazis in 1945, opens a chapter of his classic work with Luke 14:26. When Christ calls, he writes, each person must stand alone before Him. Jesus severs our direct, unmediated ties to parents, spouse, children, and nation, and steps in as Mediator between us and them. From then on we relate to those we love only through Him—and precisely for that reason we love them more truly. *(The Cost of Discipleship, chapter "Discipleship and the Individual")* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the chapter "Discipleship and the Individual" opens with Luke 14:26 and centers on Christ stepping as Mediator between the believer and loved ones -->

**Joel Green**, American New Testament scholar, reads all of chapter 14 against the backdrop of ancient dining culture, where seating charts and guest lists mapped out social rank. Jesus is not offering politeness tips; He is dismantling the whole honor-and-reciprocity system. The table of God's kingdom does not sort people by status, and it welcomes those deemed unworthy of an invitation. *(The Gospel of Luke, NICNT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident that Green reads Luke's meal scenes through ancient honor and reciprocity systems; exact phrasing on this chapter unverified -->

**The Talmud**, the great collection of rabbinic commentary, preserves a ruling that if an animal falls into a water channel on the Sabbath, people may bring pillows and bedding to place under it so it can climb out on its own. The sages sought ways to keep the Sabbath without letting an animal die. This shows that the mercy toward livestock Jesus invokes in verse 5 was a real feature of Jewish life. *(Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat, folio 128b)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Rav's ruling on pillows and bedding for an animal in water at Shabbat 128b, frequently cited on Luke 14:5; exact details unverified -->
