# Luke 4 — In the Synagogue at Nazareth

## Story

Full of the Holy Spirit, Jesus returned from the Jordan and was led by the Spirit into the wilderness, where for forty days He was tempted by the devil. He ate nothing during those days, and at the end of them He was hungry. The devil said: if You are the Son of God, tell this stone to become bread. Jesus replied: it is written, "Man shall not live on bread alone" (4:1–4; Deuteronomy 8:3). The genealogy just ended with Adam, the son of God. Adam faced temptation inside a garden and fell; this Son of God faces temptation in a wilderness and stands firm.

Leading Him up to a high place, the devil showed Him all the kingdoms of the world in an instant and said: I will give You all their authority and splendor, for it has been handed over to me and I can give it to anyone I want. If You worship me, it will all be Yours. Jesus answered: it is written, worship the Lord your God and serve Him only (4:5–8; Deuteronomy 6:13). The devil pitches a crown without a cross.

Then the devil led Him to Jerusalem, set Him on the pinnacle of the temple, and said: if You are the Son of God, throw Yourself down from here, for it is written that He will command His angels to guard You and lift You up in their hands so You will not strike Your foot against a stone. Jesus answered: it is said, do not put the Lord your God to the test. Having finished every temptation, the devil left Him until an opportune time (4:9–13; Psalms 91:11–12; Deuteronomy 6:16). Luke places the temple temptation last—different from Matthew's order—likely because his entire Gospel drives toward Jerusalem. And "until an opportune time" ominously hints the enemy will return (22:3).

Returning to Galilee in the power of the Spirit, news about Him spread across the whole countryside. He taught in their synagogues, praised by everyone. Then He came to Nazareth, where He had grown up. As was His custom, on the Sabbath He entered the synagogue and stood up to read. Handed the scroll of the prophet Isaiah, He unrolled it to the place where it is written: "The Spirit of the Lord is on Me, because He has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor"; He has sent Me to proclaim freedom for the captives, recovery of sight for the blind, to set the oppressed free, and to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor (4:14–19; Isaiah 61:1–2). He stops reading right in the middle of the sentence—just before the words "and the day of vengeance of our God."

Rolling up the scroll, He handed it back to the attendant and sat down. Every eye in the synagogue was riveted on Him. He began: "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing." All spoke well of Him and marveled at the gracious words pouring from His lips, yet asked: isn't this Joseph's son? (4:20–22). This is Jesus' opening sermon in Luke, and that Isaiah text acts as His mission manifesto: the poor, the captive, the blind, the oppressed.

He told them: surely you will quote Me the proverb, "Physician, heal yourself!" and say, do here in Your hometown the things we heard You did in Capernaum. Truly, no prophet is accepted in his own hometown. He reminded them of two stories from Scripture. In Elijah's days, when the sky stayed shut for three and a half years and severe famine struck the land, there were many widows in Israel, yet Elijah was sent to none of them—only to a widow in Zarephath in the region of Sidon. And in Elisha's days, many in Israel had leprosy, yet none was cleansed except Naaman the Syrian (4:23–27; 1 Kings 17:8–16; 2 Kings 5:1–14). Both recipients of God's mercy were Gentiles.

Hearing this, everyone in the synagogue flew into a rage. Jumping up, they drove Him out of town and hauled Him to the brow of the hill on which the town was built, intending to throw Him off the cliff. Yet He walked straight through the crowd and went on His way (4:28–30). They loved hearing promises of grace—until grace reached outside their own circle. From His very first sermon, the shadow of rejection falls across His road.

Going down to Capernaum, a town in Galilee, He taught them on the Sabbath. People were astonished at His teaching because His word carried authority. Inside the synagogue a man possessed by an unclean demon cried out: ah! What do You want with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have You come to destroy us? I know who You are—the Holy One of God! Jesus rebuked it: be quiet and come out of him! Throwing the man down before them all, the demon came out without injuring him. Amazement seized everyone as they asked: what word is this? With authority and power He commands unclean spirits, and they come out! News of Him spread everywhere in the surrounding region (4:31–37). Nazareth failed to recognize Him; inside Capernaum's synagogue, even a demon knows exactly who He is.

Leaving the synagogue, He entered Simon's house. Simon's mother-in-law lay suffering from a high fever, and they appealed to Him for her. Bending over her, He rebuked the fever, and it left her; immediately she got up and began serving them. At sunset, people brought to Him everyone sick with various diseases, and laying His hands on each one, He healed them. Demons also came out of many, shouting: You are the Son of God! Yet He rebuked them and would not allow them to speak, because they knew He was the Christ (4:38–41). Even in a pressing crowd, He lays gentle hands on each sick person one by one.

At daybreak He went out to a solitary place. Crowds went searching for Him and, finding Him, tried to keep Him from leaving. Yet He told them, "I must preach the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns as well, because that is why I was sent." And He kept preaching in the synagogues of Judea (4:42–44). Traditional translations read "Galilee" following later manuscripts, whereas the earliest Greek copies read "Judea," likely used broadly for the whole Jewish homeland.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus launches His ministry by rejecting power without the cross.** Across forty desert days, the devil tempts Him to use divine power for Himself, trade worship for world empire, and test God with a spectacle off the temple roof (4:1–12). Three times He chooses quiet obedience to Scripture.

**The Nazareth sermon serves as Jesus' manifesto for all of Luke.** Reading Isaiah and declaring "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing," He places good news for the poor and freedom for the captive and oppressed at the heart of His calling (4:18–21).

**Grace crossing ethnic borders ignites the first hostility.** Hometown neighbors cheer His gracious speech until He reminds them God helped a Sidonian widow and a Syrian general ahead of Israel—and instantly they march Him toward a cliff (4:22–29). Religious pride struggles fiercely with a God who loves outsiders too.

**Miracles confirm His authoritative word, yet never replace preaching the kingdom.** In Capernaum He casts out demons and heals Simon's mother-in-law and crowds of sick people, yet slips away at dawn because His core mission is proclaiming the good news of God's kingdom from town to town (4:31–44).

## Deeper Meaning

**Forty days in the desert answer Israel's forty years of testing.** Every Scripture Jesus quotes against Satan comes straight from Deuteronomy, recalling exact tests where Israel stumbled in the wilderness (4:4; 4:8; 4:12; Deuteronomy 8:3; Deuteronomy 6:13; Deuteronomy 6:16). Where Israel fell, God's Son stands unshaken.

**"The year of the Lord's favor" recalls the Year of Jubilee.** Isaiah 61:1–2 draws directly on the Jubilee law: every fiftieth year, slaves went free, debts were canceled, and ancestral land returned to poor families (Leviticus 25:8–10). Jesus announces that the ultimate season of liberation has arrived in Himself (4:18–21).

**Elijah and Elisha foreshadow the gospel turning to the Gentiles.** The widow of Zarephath and Naaman the Syrian (4:25–27; 1 Kings 17:8–16; 2 Kings 5:1–14) establish a pattern pulsing through Luke and Acts: whenever insiders turn the message away, God's grace widens out to other nations (Acts 13:46).

**Leaving "until an opportune time" foreshadows Jerusalem's final clash.** The desert battle was only round one; verse 4:13 leaves an ominous pause until the adversary returns on betrayal night, when Satan enters Judas (22:3; 22:53).

**Exorcisms prove God's kingdom is pushing back darkness.** The demon's panicked cry in 4:34 confirms that Jesus' arrival directly threatens Satan's dominion—just as Jesus will later spell out (11:20).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Luke order the three temptations differently from Matthew?** Matthew puts the mountaintop offer of world kingdoms third (Matthew 4:8–10), while Luke saves the temple pinnacle in Jerusalem for last (4:9–12). Each author arranges the scene to highlight his overarching theme—Luke steers his entire Gospel purposefully toward Jerusalem and its temple.

**Jesus stops reading Isaiah 61 mid-sentence to spotlight grace.** Right after "the year of the Lord's favor" in Isaiah 61:2 comes "and the day of vengeance of our God." Rolling up the scroll right before vengeance (4:19–20), Jesus marks His first coming as the season of saving grace, leaving judgment for the final day.

**"The poor" in 4:18 embraces both real material poverty and spiritual dependence.** In Luke, the poor are never reduced to a tidy metaphor; they are truly hungry, sick, and marginalized neighbors—and often their very lack of earthly props leaves them more open to receive God's rescue.

**Why silence demons calling Him "the Christ" (4:41)?** Many contemporaries expected a political warrior Messiah who would crush Rome by force. Jesus refuses to let His title be hijacked or publicized by evil spirits before He defines it Himself through the cross.

**Cyril of Alexandria**, fifth-century bishop, highlights the quiet note that Jesus attended the Sabbath synagogue "as was His custom" (4:16). Honoring Scripture and gathered worship, He unrolled the prophet's scroll to show that the entire Old Testament points toward Himself and comes to completion in Him. *(Homilies on Luke)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Cyril's exposition of the Nazareth synagogue scene in his Homilies on Luke; exact homily number unverified -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century French Reformer, reflects on Nazareth's sudden fury (4:28–29): religious pride cannot tolerate God's sovereign freedom to bless outsiders. As long as they assumed grace was their private privilege, they applauded Him; the instant grace extended to Gentiles, they tried to murder Him. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Luke chapter 4 verses 24–30)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Calvin's emphasis on Nazareth's anger at sovereign grace extending to Gentiles; exact original wording unchecked -->

**Joel Green**, an American New Testament scholar, identifies the Nazareth synagogue episode (4:16–30) as the programmatic key unlocking all of Luke. It defines Jesus' mission in Jubilee terms of liberating the poor and excluded while establishing the plot of the whole book: rejected at home, the gospel expands to outsiders. *(The Gospel of Luke, NICNT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: treating Luke 4:16–30 as programmatic for Luke's entire theology is a central thesis of Green's NICNT commentary -->

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, prescribes Sabbath synagogue liturgy pairing a reading from the Law of Moses with a selection from the Prophets.
