# Luke 15 — Lost and Found

## Story

Now all the tax collectors and sinners were drawing near to hear Jesus. The Pharisees and scribes began to grumble: "This man welcomes sinners and eats with them!" So He told them this parable (15:1–3). The entire chapter answers that single complaint. Three parables, one theme: something lost, something found, and a joyful celebration.

Suppose one of you has a hundred sheep and loses one of them. Does he not leave the ninety-nine in the open pasture and go after the lost sheep until he finds it? And when he finds it, he joyfully puts it on his shoulders. Returning home, he calls together his friends and neighbors and says, "Rejoice with me, for I have found my lost sheep!" I tell you, in the same way, there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous people who do not need to repent (15:4–7; Ezekiel 34:11–16). The shepherd does not wait for the sheep to wander back. He goes searching, and when he finds it, he does not drive it home with a rod—he carries it.

Or what woman who has ten silver coins and loses one does not light a lamp, sweep the house, and search carefully until she finds it? When she finds it, she calls her friends and neighbors together and says, "Rejoice with me, for I have found my lost coin!" In the same way, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of God's angels over one sinner who repents (15:8–10). Ten silver coins could represent a poor family's entire savings or a woman's dowry. Homes in that era were dark, with few windows and dirt floors; finding a single coin meant lighting a lamp and sweeping every corner. In the first parable the searcher is a man, in the second a woman; both picture God.

Then He said: A certain man had two sons. The younger son said to his father, "Father, give me my share of the estate." So the father divided his property between them. Not many days later, the younger son gathered everything he had, traveled to a distant country, and there squandered his wealth in wild living (15:11–13). Demanding one's inheritance while the father was still alive was tantamount to saying, "I wish you were dead." Yet the father still divided his estate.

After he had spent everything, a severe famine struck that country, and he began to be in need. So he went and hired himself out to a citizen of that country, who sent him into his fields to feed pigs. He longed to fill his stomach with the carob pods the pigs were eating, yet no one gave him anything. Then he came to his senses and said, "How many of my father's hired servants have plenty of bread, and here I am dying of hunger!" I will get up and go to my father and say to him, "Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son; treat me as one of your hired servants" (15:14–19). For a Jew, herding pigs was the most unclean work imaginable. He had hit rock bottom—lower than the pigs, for at least the pigs got fed.

So he got up and went to his father. While he was still a long way off, his father saw him, was filled with compassion, ran to him, threw his arms around his neck, and kissed him. The son said to him, "Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son." But the father told his servants: Quick! Bring the best robe and put it on him. Put a ring on his finger and sandals on his feet. Bring the fattened calf and kill it, and let us feast and celebrate, "for this son of mine was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found!" And they began to celebrate (15:20–24). The father saw him "while he was still a long way off"—he had been watching the road for a long time. For an elderly Middle Eastern patriarch to lift his robes and run through the village was undignified, yet he ran. Before the son can even finish his rehearsed request to become a hired servant, the father cuts him off with robe, ring, and sandals—the marks of a full son in the household. Some ancient manuscripts include the request to be treated as a hired servant in verse 21; the BSB omits it.

Meanwhile, the older son was in the field. As he approached the house, he heard music and dancing, so he called one of the servants and asked what was going on. The servant told him: Your brother has come home, and your father has killed the fattened calf because he has him back safe and sound. The older brother became angry and refused to go in. His father came out and pleaded with him. He answered his father: Look, all these years I have slaved for you and never disobeyed a single command of yours, yet you never even gave me a young goat so I could celebrate with my friends. But when this son of yours comes home, who has devoured your property with prostitutes, you kill the fattened calf for him! (15:25–30). He says "this son of yours," not "my brother." He is just as far from his father as the younger boy—only he stayed right inside the house.

The father replied, "Son, you are always with me, and everything I have is yours." Yet it was fitting to celebrate and rejoice, "because this brother of yours was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found" (15:31–32). The father went out to welcome the younger son, and now he goes out to plead with the older one. The parable stops right there: we never learn whether the older brother goes inside. The Pharisees and scribes standing there listening are the older brother, and the story leaves the door open for them.

## Key Takeaways

**God is the One who searches, not the One who sits waiting.** The shepherd leaves ninety-nine sheep and goes out; the woman lights a lamp and sweeps; the father runs down the road (15:4; 15:8; 15:20). All three parables begin from the side of the one who suffered loss, not the one who wandered away. Jesus eating with sinners is God Himself going out to search.

**A single person is worth an entire search.** One out of a hundred, one out of ten, one out of two. By sheer math, losing one looks minor. Yet to the owner, what is missing is never "one percent"; it is my sheep, my coin, my child.

**Heaven rejoices and invites others to join the party.** Each parable culminates in a celebration, and every time the owner calls friends and neighbors: "Rejoice with me" (15:6; 15:9). God's joy is meant to be shared. Anyone who refuses to rejoice with God when a sinner comes home locks himself outside the feast.

**There are two ways to be lost from the father.** The younger son leaves home; the older son stays home while his heart stays distant. The older son boasts that he "never disobeyed" (15:29), yet talks like a resentful servant demanding wages. Neither truly knows the father: one treats him as an ATM to drain, the other as a boss to appease.

**The father gives before the son can prove himself.** He runs out while the boy is still far away, before a single word of apology is spoken (15:20). The robe, ring, and sandals are not prizes for a polished confession; they are gifts for a child who came home. Repentance here is simply turning back; the father handles the rest.

## Deeper Meaning

**God sings over His people.** Zephaniah glimpses a startling scene: God Himself takes delight in His people and "will rejoice over you with singing" (Zephaniah 3:17). Luke 15 speaks three times of God's joy, in heaven and before the angels (15:7; 15:10). God does not merely pardon those who return; He delights in them.

**The law of Moses had a statute for a rebellious son.** If a son was "stubborn and rebellious," a "glutton and a drunkard," his parents were to bring him before the elders at the city gate (Deuteronomy 21:18–21). Jesus' audience knew that statute. The father in the parable does the exact opposite: instead of dragging his son to the town gate for judgment, he runs down the road to bring him home.

**The father's embrace mirrors Esau's embrace.** When Jacob returned home after years of deceit and flight, he braced for his brother's revenge. Instead, "Esau ran to meet him and embraced him, threw his arms around his neck, and kissed him. And they wept" (Genesis 33:4). Luke's wording in 15:20 nearly replays that moment. Here, however, it is the father who runs out, while the older brother refuses to welcome his sibling.

**A ring and fine robe are emblems of restored authority.** When Pharaoh placed Joseph over Egypt, he took the signet ring from his own hand, put it on Joseph's finger, and dressed him in garments of fine linen (Genesis 41:42). A ring often served as the family seal. The father does not merely let the boy back indoors; he restores him to his full standing as a son.

**"Dead and alive again" is Paul's language for every saved person.** Paul writes that even when we were dead in our trespasses, God, "rich in mercy," made us alive with Christ (Ephesians 2:4–5). The father's exclamation in 15:24 is more than a relieved parent's figure of speech; it describes what happens to everyone who returns to God. Near the end of this journey, Jesus will summarize His whole mission: the Son of Man came "to seek and to save the lost" (19:10).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who are the "ninety-nine righteous people who do not need to repent"?** Verse 15:7 is read in two main ways. One detects gentle irony: no one is truly without need of repentance, and Jesus means those who think themselves righteous, like the older brother. The other reads it straightforwardly: there are faithful people who genuinely walk with God, just as the father tells the older son, "you are always with me" (15:31). Either way, the point stands: God does not love the ninety-nine any less, yet He celebrates uniquely when the lost one is found.

**Is the younger son's repentance sincere, or merely hunger?** He "comes to his senses" when his stomach is empty, thinking first of the servants' bread (15:17). Some wonder whether his speech is a calculated script to get a meal. The parable never settles the question, because the father never stops to audit the boy's motives. Perhaps that is precisely the point: human repentance is often mixed, and God's grace does not wait until our motives are spotless before running to meet us.

**Does the parable teach forgiveness without the cross?** Some cite this story to claim God forgives effortlessly, requiring no atonement. Yet a single parable never tells the entire gospel; it answers a specific complaint—why Jesus eats with sinners (15:2). Moreover, even within the story, forgiveness is costly: the father absorbs the loss of his estate and bears public shame before the village. And the One telling this story is walking straight toward Jerusalem to die.

**The Pharisees here are not cartoon villains.** They were alarmed that Jesus dined with tax collectors, men who commonly enriched themselves by extorting their own people on Rome's behalf. Sharing a table made a public statement about one's company, and they cared deeply about Israel's holiness. Jesus does not tell them they are wrong to take sin seriously; He shows they have forgotten that God seeks sinners. And the father goes out to plead with the older brother too (15:28): the invitation to the banquet stands open to them as well.

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, a theologian in Carthage, North Africa, around AD 200, wrote on these three parables during his later, rigorous period. Some appealed to the prodigal son to argue that the church should readmit believers guilty of grave sins like adultery. Tertullian pushed back, insisting that the lost sheep and younger son represent pagans coming to God for the first time, not baptized Christians who fall away. **Augustine**, fourth-to-fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, interpreted every detail of the prodigal son allegorically. The distant country is forgetfulness of God; the foreign citizen who sends him to tend pigs is the devil; the ring is the seal of the Holy Spirit; and the fattened calf is Christ Himself, slain to make a feast for returning sinners. *(Questions on the Gospels, Book 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Augustine's allegorical reading of the prodigal son in Quaestiones Evangeliorum Book 2, including the calf as Christ; exact details unverified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, had little patience for allegorizing every prop. In his view, the robe, ring, and sandals do not hide secret symbols; they simply paint the father's heart vividly. The central truth of the parable is that God gladly forgives the penitent, and those who consider themselves righteous must not begrudge His grace. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on chapter 15 verses 11–32)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin routinely rejects piece-by-piece allegorizing; confident in his main point on God's readiness to forgive, exact comments on robe and ring unverified -->

**Henri Nouwen**, Dutch Catholic priest, wrote an entire book after spending hours before Rembrandt's painting *The Return of the Prodigal Son*. At first he saw himself in the younger son, then recognized he resembled the older brother far more—dutiful, hardworking, yet quietly resentful. Finally he realized the deepest calling is to become like the father, learning to welcome and forgive. He noticed that the father's two hands in Rembrandt's painting look different: one masculine, one feminine. *(The Return of the Prodigal Son, 1992)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the progression from younger son to older brother to father, plus the observation about the two hands, forms the core structure of the book -->

**Tim Keller**, pastor in New York, suggested calling this the parable of the "two lost sons." The younger is lost through reckless rebellion; the older is lost through rule-keeping, using his obedience to claim leverage over his father. Keller points out that "prodigal" means recklessly extravagant—and the most extravagant spender in the story is the father, pouring out his love. The story, he notes, cries out for a true older brother willing to go search for the younger one, and Jesus is that brother. *(The Prodigal God, 2008)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the two lost sons, the meaning of "prodigal" applied to the father, and Jesus as the true elder brother are the central arguments of the book -->

**Amy-Jill Levine**, American Jewish scholar, argues that if we hear all three parables as first-century Jewish listeners did, the focus is not repentance—after all, sheep and coins cannot repent. The focus is counting: the shepherd with a hundred notices one missing; the father has two sons. The question for the reader, she suggests, is: who is missing from our house, and have we gone looking for them? She also strongly critiques sermons that turn the older brother into a caricature of "the Jews." *(Short Stories by Jesus, 2014)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: emphasizing counting and searching rather than repentance, and warning against anti-Jewish readings of the older brother, are Levine's well-known arguments on Luke 15 -->
