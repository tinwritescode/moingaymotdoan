# Luke 13 — The Fig Tree and the Narrow Door

## Story

At that time some people told Jesus about the Galileans whose blood Pilate had mixed with their sacrifices. He replied: Do you think these Galileans were worse sinners than all the other Galileans because they suffered this fate? "No, I tell you. But unless you repent, you too will all perish." Or those eighteen who were killed when the tower of Siloam collapsed on them—do you think they were more guilty than all the others living in Jerusalem? No; unless you repent, you too will all perish (13:1–5). Two tragedies: one caused by human violence, one by an accident. He rejects the assumption that whoever suffers catastrophe must be guiltier than everyone else. The question is not "what did they do?" but "what about you?"

Then He told a parable: a man had a fig tree planted in his vineyard. He came looking for fruit on it and found none, so he told the keeper of the vineyard: Look, for three years now I have come looking for fruit on this fig tree and haven't found any. Cut it down! Why should it use up the soil? The gardener answered: Sir, leave it alone again this year, until I dig around it and fertilize it. If it bears fruit next year, fine; if not, you can cut it down (13:6–9). The parable ends in suspense: the tree gets one more year, and no one knows whether it will bear fruit. The call to repent just given has a time limit—and that time limit is grace.

On a Sabbath He was teaching in one of the synagogues. A woman was there who had been disabled by a spirit for eighteen years; she was bent over and could not straighten up at all. Seeing her, Jesus called her over and said, "Woman, you are set free from your disability." He placed His hands on her, and immediately she straightened up and began to praise God. Indignant because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath, the synagogue leader told the crowd, "There are six days for work. So come and be healed on those days, and not on the Sabbath" (13:10–14). He did not dare confront Jesus directly, so he turned to scold the crowd—and indirectly the woman who had just been healed.

The Lord answered him: You hypocrites! Doesn't each of you on the Sabbath untie his ox or donkey from the stall and lead it out to water? "Then should not this daughter of Abraham, whom Satan has kept bound for eighteen long years, be released from her bondage on the Sabbath day?" When He said this, all His adversaries were humiliated, while the whole crowd rejoiced at all the glorious things He was doing (13:15–17). An ox is untied every single day; this woman had been bound for eighteen years. He calls her a "daughter of Abraham," a rare title that places her as a full member of the covenant. Because the Sabbath commemorates liberation from Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15), it is the most fitting day of all to set someone free.

He said: What is the kingdom of God like? To what can I compare it? It is like a mustard seed that a man took and planted in his garden; it grew and became a tree, and the birds of the air nested in its branches. Again He said: What shall I compare the kingdom of God to? It is like leaven that a woman took and mixed into three measures of flour until all of it was leavened (13:18–21). Both parables make the same point: a small beginning, a vast result. Three measures of flour is an enormous quantity—enough to bake bread for a hundred people.

Jesus went through towns and villages, teaching as He made His way toward Jerusalem. Someone asked Him, "Lord, will only a few people be saved?" He answered, "Make every effort to enter through the narrow door," because many, I tell you, will try to enter and will not be able. Once the master of the house gets up and closes the door, you will stand outside knocking and pleading, "Lord, open the door for us." He will reply, "I do not know where you are from." Then you will say, "We ate and drank with You, and You taught in our streets." But He will say, "I tell you, I do not know where you are from. Depart from Me, all you evildoers!" (13:22–27; Psalms 6:8). The question was about other people: how many will be saved? The answer is directed straight at the questioner: strive to enter. Knowing Him from a distance, hearing His teaching, or sharing a table is still not the same as being known by Him.

There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth when you see Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and all the prophets in the kingdom of God, but you yourselves are thrown out. People will come from east and west and north and south, and will recline at the table in the kingdom of God. "And indeed, some who are last will be first, and some who are first will be last" (13:28–30; Isaiah 49:12). Right on the heels of the warning, a banquet opens wide to people from every corner of the earth. Those who assumed they were inside find themselves outside, and vice versa.

At that very hour, some Pharisees came to Him and said, "Get away from here, because Herod wants to kill You." He replied, "Go tell that fox, 'Look, I will keep driving out demons and healing people today and tomorrow, and on the third day I will reach My goal.'" Nevertheless, I must keep going today and tomorrow and the next day, for it is not possible for a prophet to perish outside of Jerusalem (13:31–33). A fox is cunning yet small—not a lion. Jesus does not flee from Herod; His timetable is set by God, and it leads to Jerusalem.

"O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who kills the prophets and stones those sent to her!" How often I have longed to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, but you were unwilling! Look, your house is left to you desolate. I tell you, you will not see Me again until you say, "Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!" (13:34–35; Psalms 118:26). A tender maternal image, offered to the very city about to kill Him. A few chapters later, the crowd of disciples will shout those very words from the Psalm as He enters the city (19:38).

## Key Takeaways

**Another person's tragedy is a reminder to ourselves, not an occasion for judgment.** People brought up Pilate's atrocity like breaking news, perhaps expecting Jesus to condemn Pilate or explain why God allowed it. He does neither. He turns the story back on His listeners, saying twice, "unless you repent" (13:3; 13:5). Every person lives just as close to death, so every person has the same urgent need to turn back to God.

**Grace both waits and gets to work.** The gardener does not merely ask for one more year for the fig tree. He promises to dig around the roots and apply fertilizer—actively working so the tree might bear fruit (13:8). The extra time God gives is not passive neglect. Yet the parable is also candid: a tree that keeps refusing to bear fruit will be cut down (13:9).

**Jesus notices the person no one else sees.** The bent-over woman asks for nothing. Jesus Himself sees her, calls her forward, and lays His hands on her (13:12–13). For eighteen years she had looked only at the ground; now she stands straight and praises God. To the synagogue leader she is a scheduling violation; to Jesus she is a "daughter of Abraham" (13:16).

**A question about others is answered with an invitation to oneself.** Someone asks whether only a few will be saved, and Jesus refuses to give a number (13:23–24). He turns curiosity into urgency: the door is still open, though a moment will come when it shuts. And the guests reclining at the banquet will include people from all four directions whom the questioner never expected (13:29).

**A closed door is never because God did not want to open it.** The warnings in this chapter are severe: weeping, gnashing of teeth, a desolate house. Yet the heart of Jesus comes through in the closing lament: "How often I have longed to gather your children together," "but you were unwilling" (13:34). The barrier is the human will, not the heart of God.

## Deeper Meaning

**God looking for figs is a familiar prophetic image.** Hosea records that God found Israel's ancestors like "the early fruit on the fig tree in its first season" (Hosea 9:10). Micah laments that he searches everywhere and finds not even "an early fig" that his soul craves (Micah 7:1). When Jesus tells of an owner coming three years in a row looking for fruit and finding none, His listeners know this is the story of God and His people.

**The name Siloam returns in John's Gospel alongside the same question about sin.** There, the disciples ask whether a man born blind sinned or his parents did, and Jesus answers, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned" (John 9:2–3). Then He sends the man to wash in the pool of Siloam, and he comes back seeing (John 9:7). Both stories dismantle the same reflex: seeing someone suffer and assuming they must have sinned.

**Unbinding someone on the Sabbath fulfills what Jesus announced at Nazareth.** At the outset of His ministry, He read from Isaiah that the Lord had sent Him "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (4:18). Isaiah himself declared that the fast God chooses is "to undo the bonds of the yoke" (Isaiah 58:6). The woman bound for eighteen years is one of those captives, and God's day of rest is the truest day to loose her chains.

**"Daughter of Abraham" has a brother in chapter 19.** Luke alone records two moments where Jesus restores a marginalized person as a child of Abraham. The second is Zacchaeus, the despised tax collector: "Today salvation has come to this house, because this man too is a son of Abraham" (19:9). A disabled woman and a scorned man—both are placed back at the heart of the covenant family.

**The banquet from four directions echoes an ancient promise of gathering the scattered.** Psalm 107 praises God for gathering His redeemed "from east and west, from north and south" (Psalms 107:3). In the Psalm, that meant scattered Israelites returning home. Jesus takes up the image for the banquet of God's kingdom, and Luke's second volume shows that door swinging wide to the Gentiles as well (Acts 10:34–35).

**Jerusalem already had a history of killing prophets.** In Jeremiah's day, the prophet Uriah spoke against the city; King Jehoiakim had him hauled back from Egypt and "struck him down with the sword" (Jeremiah 26:20–23). When Jesus says a prophet cannot perish outside Jerusalem (13:33), He knows He is stepping into that exact line.

## Notes & Nuances

**Outside of Luke, no surviving historical source mentions these two tragedies.** The Jewish historian Josephus records several bloody crackdowns by Pilate—such as when he raided the temple treasury to fund an aqueduct and had soldiers club protesters to death. Yet Josephus mentions neither the Galileans killed during their sacrifices nor the tower of Siloam. Silence in other sources does not mean the events never happened; the massacre fits Pilate's well-documented brutality.

**Jesus does not say catastrophe is never connected to sin.** Scripture does contain moments when God judges sin through disaster. What Jesus rejects is using suffering as a scoreboard to measure who is more sinful than whom. The phrase "perish in the same way" (13:3) has two main readings: a concrete historical warning, since roughly forty years later vast numbers in Jerusalem died by Roman swords and collapsing walls; and eternal ruin for those who refuse to repent. Many commentators hold both together.

**Illness and evil spirits in the story of the bent-over woman.** Luke writes that she "had a spirit of infirmity" (13:11), and Jesus says Satan had kept her bound (13:16). Yet Jesus performs no exorcism here; He lays hands on her and heals her. Later in the same chapter He distinguishes between "driving out demons" and "healing people" (13:32). The mainstream view is that Luke sees all sickness, in a broad sense, as part of the bondage Jesus came to break; he is not claiming that everyone who is sick is demon-possessed.

**Are the Pharisees in verse 31 friends or foes?** Some argue they brought a genuine warning. Not every Pharisee opposed Jesus: in Luke some invite Him to dine (11:37), and in Acts Gamaliel defends the apostles (Acts 5:34–39). Others suspect they wanted to scare Him out of Herod's territory, perhaps even acting on Herod's behalf. Jesus' reply answers either motive: no threat will divert Him from His road.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth-to-fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, preached on the barren fig tree. He read the tree as humanity, and the owner's three visits as three eras: before the law, under the law, and under grace. Digging around the roots stands for teaching the humility of repentance, while the manure represents sorrow for sin—something unpleasant to look at that nonetheless makes the tree bear fruit. *(Sermons, homily on Luke chapter 13)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls the three eras and manure as repentance from an Augustine sermon on Luke 13; uncertain of the exact sermon number, so omitted -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer, observed on verses 1–5 that people eagerly treat another's misfortune as proof of guilt and quietly congratulate themselves. In his view, God does not punish the worst offenders first; He allows some to suffer as examples so the rest will recognize they deserve no less and see that God's patience is waiting for their repentance. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on chapter 13 verses 1–5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Calvin's main warning here against judging others' calamities; exact wording not verified -->

**Joel Green**, American New Testament scholar, highlights the repeated verb "untie" in verses 15 and 16: if people untie an ox, how much more should this woman be unbound. In his reading, the healing does not merely straighten her spine; it restores her rightful standing in God's covenant community under the title "daughter of Abraham." He connects the scene back to Jesus' Nazareth proclamation of release for the captives. *(The Gospel of Luke, NICNT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Green writes extensively on this passage, including a dedicated study, emphasizing liberation and status restoration; exact page-level details not verified -->

**Darrell Bock**, American New Testament scholar, reads "today and tomorrow, and on the third day" (13:32) as an idiom for a brief, divinely appointed span of time rather than three literal calendar days. He stresses the word "must" in verse 33—a favorite term of Luke's for divine necessity: Jesus' journey to Jerusalem is ordained by God, so Herod's threat cannot alter it. *(Luke, Baker Exegetical Commentary, 1994–1996)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading "three days" as a short appointed span and "must" as divine necessity reflects Bock and mainstream Luke scholarship; exact phrasing unverified -->

**The Mishnah**, the compendium of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, rules that whenever there is even a doubt that human life is in danger, Sabbath restrictions are set aside to save it. It even gives the example of a collapsed building: rescuers must dig through the rubble immediately, even when unsure whether anyone lies beneath. Jewish teachers were therefore not callous toward suffering on the Sabbath. The synagogue leader followed that same reasoning: since the woman's condition was not life-threatening, it could wait until tomorrow. Jesus does not quarrel with that principle; He replies that eighteen years is already far too long to wait. *(Mishnah, Yoma 8.6–7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the principle that doubtful danger to life overrides Sabbath and the rubble example in Yoma chapter 8 are widely attested; the connection to Luke 13 is editorial commentary -->
