# Galatians 3 — The True Children of Abraham

## Story

Paul turns directly to his readers with the sharpest rebuke in the letter: "O foolish Galatians, who has bewitched you?" Right before their eyes Jesus Christ had been publicly portrayed as crucified. He puts one blunt question to them: did they receive the Spirit by works of the law, or by hearing with faith? Are they really so foolish—having begun by the Spirit, are they now trying to finish by human flesh? Did they suffer so much for nothing—if indeed it was for nothing? Does God supply the Spirit and work miracles among them because they obey the law, or because they believe what they heard? (3:1–5). Paul appeals not first to abstract logic, but to their own lived experience.

Next he opens Scripture, just as he will later do in Romans. Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness. Therefore those who live by faith are Abraham's true children. Foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, Scripture announced the gospel ahead of time to Abraham: "All nations will be blessed through you." Consequently, those who rely on faith share blessing alongside faithful Abraham (3:6–9; Genesis 15:6; Genesis 12:3). The agitators insisted Gentiles needed circumcision to join Abraham's family; Paul answers that faith has already made them his children.

By contrast, everyone relying on works of the law stands under a curse, for Scripture declares: "Cursed is everyone who does not continue to do everything written in the Book of the Law." Plainly no one is justified before God by the law, since "the righteous will live by faith." The law does not operate on the principle of faith; rather, "the one who does these things will live by them" (3:10–12; Deuteronomy 27:26; Habakkuk 2:4; Leviticus 18:5). The law demands flawless obedience across the board; whoever chooses to stand on law must fulfill every single clause.

"Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us," because Scripture says, "Cursed is everyone who is hung on a tree." He did this so that Abraham's blessing might flow to the Gentiles in Christ Jesus, and so that through faith we might receive the promised Spirit (3:13–14; Deuteronomy 21:23). The cross—which Jews viewed as proof that God had cursed a man—becomes in Paul's reading the exact place where the curse was absorbed and lifted.

Paul borrows an everyday legal example. Once a human covenant is ratified, nobody may cancel it or tack on new clauses. Now the promises were spoken to Abraham and to his offspring. Scripture does not say "and to offsprings," pointing to many, but "and to your offspring," referring to one—namely Christ. Therefore the law, arriving four hundred thirty years later, cannot annul a covenant previously ratified by God so as to void the promise. For if the inheritance rests on law, it no longer rests on promise; yet God graciously granted it to Abraham through a promise (3:15–18; Genesis 22:18; Exodus 12:40). A later code of rules can never turn an unconditional promise into a conditional bargain.

Why then was the law given? It was added because of transgressions until the promised Offspring arrived. It was put in place through angels by the hand of a mediator—and a mediator does not act for one party alone, whereas God is one. Does the law then contradict God's promises? Certainly not! Had a law been given capable of imparting life, righteousness would indeed have come by the law. Instead, Scripture locked everything under sin so that what was promised through faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe (3:19–22). Verse 20 ranks among the most enigmatic lines in Paul's writings, inspiring hundreds of proposed explanations.

Before faith came, we were kept under custody of the law, penned in until the coming faith was revealed. "So then, the law became our guardian until Christ came, in order that we might be justified by faith." Once faith has arrived, however, we are no longer under a guardian (3:23–25). The Greek noun here (*paidagōgos*) refers to a household slave charged with escorting and supervising a child on the way to school; once the child matures, that custodian's job ends.

For in Christ Jesus you are all children of God through faith. All of you who were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham's offspring—heirs according to the promise (3:26–29). Christ is the single Offspring; everyone united to Him shares that inheritance.

## Key Takeaways

**The Christian life continues the exact way it starts.** The Galatians received the Spirit through "hearing with faith," never through law-keeping (3:2). Paul challenges them: having started by the Spirit, why attempt to reach maturity by human effort (3:3)? Nobody enters by grace only to finish by earning merit.

**Abraham's true family consists of believers.** Abraham was counted righteous simply by trusting God (3:6). Belonging to his household therefore hinges not on a physical mark on the body, but on sharing Abraham's faith (3:7–9). Gentiles are never outsiders hovering in Abraham's courtyard; they are full children.

**Christ bore the curse in our place.** The law pronounces a curse on everyone falling short of perfect obedience (3:10). Hanging on the wooden cross, Jesus stepped beneath that very curse so blessing could pour out upon us (3:13–14). What looked like divine rejection turned out to be God's rescue.

**The law was good, yet temporary.** Far from fighting God's promise (3:21), the law served like a household custodian escorting a minor until Christ's arrival (3:24–25). Its role mattered deeply, yet it was never designed to remain permanent supervisor.

**In Christ, dividing walls collapse into unity.** Through baptism, Jew and Greek, enslaved and free, man and woman alike "put on Christ" (3:27–28). Earthly distinctions still exist in daily life, yet they no longer determine who holds a full seat at God's family table.

## Deeper Meaning

**Abraham believed long before circumcision.** Paul quotes Genesis 15:6 in 3:6: "Abram believed the LORD, and He credited it to him as righteousness." Circumcision does not arrive until Genesis 17. In Romans Paul underscores that exact sequence: Abraham was declared righteous "not after, but before he was circumcised" (Romans 4:10–11).

**The gospel was announced beforehand to Abraham.** Right from the opening promise, Gentiles stood in God's heart: "All peoples on earth will be blessed through you" (Genesis 12:3). Paul explicitly calls that pledge "the gospel" preached in advance (3:8). Gentile inclusion was never Plan B, but God's original intent.

**The righteous live by faith.** Verse 3:11 quotes the prophet Habakkuk speaking on the brink of Babylon's invasion: "The righteous shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4). Reappearing in Romans 1:17 and Hebrews 10:38, this single sentence later sparked Martin Luther's spiritual breakthrough.

**Cursed on a tree.** Mosaic law required burying executed criminals hung on a tree before nightfall, "because anyone hung on a tree is under God's curse" (Deuteronomy 21:23). Earlier, that very verse likely fueled Paul's fierce persecution of Christians: how could a crucified man possibly be God's chosen Messiah? Now he sees that verse unlocking the heart of the cross.

**The law mediated through angels.** Deuteronomy pictures the Lord descending upon Sinai "with ten thousands of holy ones" (Deuteronomy 33:2). The Greek Old Testament explicitly mentions angels at His right hand, echoed by Stephen telling Israel they "received the law as delivered by angels" (Acts 7:53). Paul highlights this contrast: the law arrived via intermediaries, whereas God spoke His promise directly.

**Erasing ancient hierarchies.** Colossians sounds a sister refrain: "Here there is no Greek or Jew," neither "slave nor free" (Colossians 3:11). A traditional Jewish morning prayer recorded in later rabbinic sources thanked God for not creating the speaker a Gentile, a slave, or a woman. Many scholars hear Paul—schooled in that very heritage—deliberately turning those status markers upside down.

## Notes & Nuances

**The singular "offspring."** Paul builds an argument on Scripture saying "offspring" rather than "offsprings" (3:16). Modern readers sometimes wonder if that stretches grammar, since Hebrew *zera* is a collective noun covering many descendants. Paul knows that perfectly well—in 3:29 he applies the exact same singular word to all believers as "Abraham's offspring." His point is theological: all the promises converge in one representative figure, Christ, and radiate outward from Him to everyone united to Him.

**Paul never calls the law evil.** He explicitly denies it: "Is the law then contrary to the promises of God? Certainly not!" (3:21). What the law could not do was impart spiritual life. Elsewhere in Romans he calls the law "holy, righteous, and good" (Romans 7:12). Reading Galatians as an attack on the Old Testament completely misreads Paul.

**The puzzle of verse 3:20.** "Now a mediator implies more than one, yet God is one." Likely Paul contrasts a two-party contract mediated by Moses—which collapses whenever Israel fails its half—with God's unilateral promise to Abraham, backed solely by the one unchanging God. Even so, this remains one of the most variously interpreted lines in the New Testament.

**Does 3:28 abolish every earthly distinction?** Elsewhere Paul still addresses husbands and wives, masters and slaves (Ephesians 5:22–33; Ephesians 6:1–9). Verse 3:28 proclaims equal standing before God and equal membership inside Christ's body without erasing Jewish identity or womanhood. Churches continue debating how far its trajectory reaches into social institutions such as slavery and women's ministry roles.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther** wrote on verse 13 that Christ did not merely absorb our penalty from a polite distance; He took humanity's sin so deeply upon Himself that under the law He stood in the place of the greatest sinner on earth—thief, murderer, and adulterer alike. Finding sin upon Him, the law put Him to death, and through His death the world was cleansed. *(Commentary on Galatians, 1535, on chapter 3 verse 13)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's vivid statement naming Christ the "greatest sinner" in Galatians 3:13 is among the most famous passages in his commentary -->

**N. T. Wright** read 3:10–14 against the grand narrative of Israel. The covenant curses of Deuteronomy 27–28 had fallen in exile, a condition many first-century Jews felt had not truly ended. As Israel's representative Messiah, Jesus absorbed that national curse on the cross so Abraham's blessing could finally spill outward to the nations. *(The Climax of the Covenant, 1991, chapter on Galatians chapter 3 verses 10–14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Wright's "continuing exile" reading of Galatians 3:10–14 is a landmark chapter of this book -->

**J. Louis Martyn**, American New Testament scholar, reconstructed the message of the rival teachers in Galatia as a rival sermon on Abraham—painting Abraham as father strictly to those who accept circumcision and Torah observance. Rather than dodging their proof texts, Paul re-reads the very same Genesis passages to show they center on faith, turning chapter 3 into both instruction and a clash over how to read Scripture. *(Galatians, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Martyn reconstructs "the Teachers'" Abraham preaching; exact section location unchecked -->

**Daniel Boyarin**, American Jewish scholar, portrayed Paul as "a radical Jew." In his view, 3:28 expresses Paul's passionate dream of one universal humanity transcending every division—yet at a cost, rendering distinctive Jewish practices like circumcision and dietary laws superfluous. Boyarin both admires Paul's vision and cautions that a universalism demanding everyone "be one" can crowd out communities wishing to preserve their distinct identity. *(A Radical Jew: Paul and the Politics of Identity, 1994)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Galatians 3:28 and critique of Pauline universalism stand at the center of Boyarin's book -->

**John Calvin** understood the "guardian" (*paidagōgos*) of 3:24 as picturing the Church's childhood under Old Testament ceremonies and shadows; once Christ arrived, God's people reached adulthood. At the same time, Calvin maintained that the moral law continues serving believers as a mirror and a practical guide for faithful living. *(Commentary on Galatians, on chapter 3 verse 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Calvin interprets the paidagōgos as the Church's minority under ceremonial law; the ongoing uses of the law are well known from his Institutes though less certain how fully he develops them at this exact verse -->
