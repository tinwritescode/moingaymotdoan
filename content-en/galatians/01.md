# Galatians 1 — No Other Gospel

## Story

Paul opens the letter by defending his apostolic calling: an apostle not sent from human beings nor through any human agent, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father who raised Him from the dead. Joined by all the brothers with him, he writes to the churches of Galatia, wishing them grace and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, who gave Himself for our sins to rescue us from the present evil age according to the will of our God and Father—to whom be glory forever and ever (1:1–5). Already inside his greeting, Paul states the heart of what he is about to defend: Christ has already done everything needed to rescue us.

In Paul's other letters, a warm thanksgiving follows the greeting. Here there is none. Paul cuts straight to the crisis: "I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting the One who called you in the grace of Christ and turning to a different gospel." In truth there is no other gospel; only certain troublemakers are unsettling their minds and trying to twist the gospel of Christ. Even if Paul himself—or an angel out of heaven—should preach a gospel contrary to what he proclaimed to them, let that person be accursed! He says it twice to remove all doubt (1:6–9). As later chapters show, these agitators insisted Gentile believers must also be circumcised and observe Mosaic law.

Paul challenges them: am I now trying to win human approval, or God's? Am I trying to please people? If I were still trying to please people, I would not be a servant of Christ (1:10). Critics may have accused him of trimming away the law just to make his message easier for Gentiles to swallow. His blistering tone right from the opening lines refutes that charge on the spot.

Next he traces his own story to establish one bedrock fact: the gospel he preaches did not originate with human beings. He neither received it from any person nor was taught it; rather, it came through a direct revelation of Jesus Christ. They had heard of his earlier life in Judaism: how fiercely he persecuted the church of God and tried to destroy it, advancing in Judaism far beyond peers his own age and burning with supreme zeal for his ancestral traditions (1:11–14; Acts 8:3). Someone on that track could never have invented this gospel on his own.

Yet when God—who set him apart from his mother's womb and called him by His grace—was pleased to reveal His Son in him so that he might preach Him among the Gentiles, Paul consulted no human being and did not go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before him; instead, he went away into Arabia and afterward returned to Damascus (1:15–17; Jeremiah 1:5). Jeremiah likewise was set apart before birth; Paul narrates his conversion in the cadence of an Old Testament prophet's call.

Only three years later did he go up to Jerusalem to get acquainted with Cephas—Peter—staying with him fifteen days. He saw none of the other apostles except James, the Lord's brother. Before God he swears he is not lying in what he writes (1:18–20). Fifteen days is far too brief to be catechized in an entire gospel, yet plenty of time for two men to talk deeply about Jesus.

Afterward he traveled to the regions of Syria and Cilicia, remaining personally unknown by sight to the churches of Judea in Christ. They only kept hearing reports: "The man who formerly persecuted us is now preaching the faith he once tried to destroy." And they glorified God because of him (1:21–24). The very churches he had terrorized never questioned his gospel; they praised God for it.

## Key Takeaways

**The gospel was not invented by human minds.** Paul insists from line one that his apostleship comes not from human commission, nor did he learn his gospel from human teachers, but "through a revelation of Jesus Christ" (1:1; 1:11–12). What human beings design, others are free to edit; what comes straight from God, no one may alter.

**There is only one gospel.** The agitators in Galatia were not denying Jesus outright; they simply tacked on a few legal rules. Paul warns that adding conditions turns the message into another gospel—which is no gospel at all (1:6–7). Because the gospel announces what Christ has already finished, adding requirements strips away its good news.

**The message outranks every messenger.** Even Paul himself—or a heavenly angel—would stand under curse if preaching a rival gospel (1:8). Believers must never embrace a message simply because its speaker seems impressive or saintly; every messenger must be tested against the gospel itself.

**Christ's servant does not live for applause.** "If I were still trying to please people, I would not be a servant of Christ" (1:10). Everyone serving God feels pressure to soften hard edges to keep listeners comfortable. Paul chooses to answer to one Master alone.

**Grace transforms a destroyer into a herald.** Paul never hides his violent past attempting to wreck the church (1:13). The very believers he persecuted end up praising God for him (1:23–24). His own biography stands as living proof that salvation comes by grace, never human merit.

## Deeper Meaning

**Called like an ancient prophet.** Saying God set him apart from his mother's womb to proclaim His Son among the nations (1:15–16) echoes Jeremiah's call: "Before you were born I set you apart" (Jeremiah 1:5). It also resonates with Isaiah's Servant, called "from the womb" to serve as a light to the nations (Isaiah 49:1–6).

**Testing messengers despite signs.** Mosaic law warned that even if a prophet performs genuine wonders yet leads the people after other gods, they must refuse to listen (Deuteronomy 13:1–3). Paul applies that exact standard: even an angel descending from heaven holds zero authority to revise the gospel (1:8).

**Two intersecting ages.** Christ gave Himself "to rescue us from the present evil age" (1:4). First-century Jewish thinkers divided history into two epochs: this present age marked by sin and oppression, and the age to come when God makes everything new. Paul announces that through the cross, the age to come has already broken into the present—what he calls "a new creation" at the letter's close (Galatians 6:15).

**Paul's unmatched zeal.** He recalls being "extremely zealous" for ancestral traditions (1:14). Writing to Philippi, he specifies what that zeal looked like: "as for zeal, persecuting the church; as for righteousness under the law, blameless" (Philippians 3:5–6). Grace rescued not a lax outsider, but the most fiercely devout contender of his generation.

**Two perspectives on the same years.** Acts likewise recounts Paul in Damascus, traveling up to Jerusalem where Barnabas introduces him to the apostles, and departing for his hometown Tarsus in Cilicia (Acts 9:19–30). The two narratives view the same stretch from distinct angles: Luke highlights the church's warm embrace of Paul, while Paul demonstrates that he did not derive his gospel from Jerusalem.

## Notes & Nuances

**Where was "Arabia"?** Not the modern Arabian Peninsula, but the Nabataean kingdom stretching south and east of Damascus under King Aretas (2 Corinthians 11:32–33). Paul never specifies what he did there—some picture quiet solitude and study, while others think he preached immediately, explaining why King Aretas's governor later sought his arrest in Damascus.

**Apparent tension with Acts.** Acts reports Barnabas bringing Paul "to the apostles" (Acts 9:27), whereas Paul swears he met only Cephas and James (1:18–20). Most readers resolve this simply: Luke writes in broad summary terms, while Paul speaks with courtroom precision under oath. Both accounts agree the visit was brief.

**"Anathema" is not a petty curse.** In 1:8–9 Paul uses the Greek word *anathema*, denoting a person or object handed completely over to God's judgment. Rather than venting personal spite, Paul consigns anyone distorting the gospel to divine reckoning—speaking harshly because the eternal salvation of entire churches hangs in the balance.

**James "the Lord's brother."** Most Protestant readers understand James as Jesus' younger half-brother, born to Mary and Joseph. Following Jerome, Catholic tradition interprets "brother" broadly as cousin, while Eastern Orthodox tradition views James as Joseph's son from an earlier marriage. Because Greek *adelphos* can span wide kin ties, no single verse settles the historical debate.

**Which churches were "Galatia"?** Galatia designated a large Roman province in central Asia Minor (modern Turkey). Scholars divide between two hypotheses: the southern provincial cities—Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe—founded on Paul's first missionary journey (Acts 13–14), or ethnic Galatian communities farther north. Which view one adopts directly affects when the letter is dated.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, late fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople famed for his preaching, pointed out that in verse 6 Paul does not accuse them of abandoning him, but of deserting "the One who called you." **Krister Stendahl**, Swedish bishop and Harvard professor, argued that Paul's account in verses 15–16 should not be labeled a "conversion" in the modern psychological sense. Paul neither switched religions nor described a tormented conscience; he narrated a prophetic call—paralleling Jeremiah and Isaiah's Servant—commissioning him to carry the gospel to Gentiles. *(Paul Among Jews and Gentiles, 1976, chapter "Call Rather Than Conversion")* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the "call rather than conversion" thesis grounded in Galatians 1:15–16 is Stendahl's most famous contribution -->

**N. T. Wright**, British bishop and New Testament scholar, noted that "zeal" in verse 14 recalls Phinehas and Elijah, famed for militant zeal for God. On that basis he proposed that when Paul went into "Arabia," he may have journeyed to Mount Sinai just as Elijah fled there after slaying the prophets of Baal (1 Kings 19:8–15), receiving a fresh commission before returning to Damascus (1:17). *("Paul, Arabia, and Elijah," 1996)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this forms the central thesis of Wright's 1996 article in Journal of Biblical Literature -->

Lightfoot**, nineteenth-century English scholar and bishop, already considered the link between Arabia and Mount Sinai, noting Paul himself later writes "Hagar stands for Mount Sinai in Arabia" (Galatians 4:25). **Paula Fredriksen**, American historian of early Judaism and Christianity, argued that when Paul "persecuted" the church he likely administered synagogue disciplinary measures such as floggings against Jewish Christ-followers. Their offense, she suggested, was welcoming uncircumcised Gentiles into synagogue networks without circumcision, risking political friction with pagan neighbors and civic authorities—and after his call, Paul remained a Jew who himself endured those exact synagogue lashings (2 Corinthians 11:24). *(Paul: The Pagans' Apostle, 2017)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Fredriksen interprets Paul's persecution as synagogue discipline tied to Gentile inclusion; exact chapter and link with 2 Corinthians 11:24 unchecked -->
