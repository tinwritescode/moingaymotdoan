# Galatians 2 — Paul and Peter

## Story

Fourteen years later, Paul went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, bringing Titus along as well. Going up in response to a revelation, he laid out privately before those esteemed as leaders the gospel he proclaims among the Gentiles, making sure he was not running—or had not run—in vain. Even Titus, a Greek traveling with him, was not compelled to be circumcised (2:1–3). Titus stood right inside Jerusalem as living proof: an uncircumcised Gentile whom the apostles still welcomed as a brother.

Tension broke out because certain false brothers slipped in secretly to spy on the freedom believers have in Christ Jesus and drag them back into slavery. Paul refused to yield to them for even a moment, so that the truth of the gospel might stay preserved for the Galatians themselves (2:4–5). He speaks as though the battle fought in Jerusalem years earlier was waged specifically to guard the readers holding his letter.

As for those held in high esteem—whatever they were makes no difference to Paul, since God shows no partiality—those leaders added nothing to his message. On the contrary, recognizing that he had been entrusted with preaching the gospel to the uncircumcised just as Peter had been entrusted with preaching to the circumcised, and seeing the same God at work powerfully through both, James, Cephas, and John—those reputed to be pillars—recognized the grace given to Paul and extended the right hand of fellowship to Paul and Barnabas: they would go to the Gentiles, while the Jerusalem pillars went to the circumcised. Their sole request was to remember the poor, the very thing Paul was eager to do (2:6–10). Not two rival gospels, but one gospel serving two mission fields.

Next Paul recounts a painful confrontation. When Cephas visited Antioch, Paul opposed him straight to his face because he stood clearly in the wrong. Before certain men arrived from James, Cephas freely ate meals alongside Gentile believers. Once those men showed up, however, he drew back and separated himself, afraid of the circumcision party. The rest of the Jewish believers joined his hypocrisy, until even Barnabas was swept along (2:11–13). In that culture, sharing a table declared everyone part of one family; pulling back from the table told Gentile believers they were still second-class.

Seeing that their conduct did not walk in step with the truth of the gospel, Paul confronted Cephas publicly before everyone: if you, though a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you force Gentiles to follow Jewish customs? We ourselves are Jews by birth and not "Gentile sinners," yet we know that a person is not justified by works of the law, but through faith in Jesus Christ. So we too put our faith in Christ Jesus to be justified by faith in Christ and not by works of the law, "because by works of the law no one will be justified." (2:14–16). Paul never records Peter's reply. Yet later at Jerusalem, it is Peter himself who stands up to defend Gentile liberty (Acts 15:7–11).

Paul presses the logic home. If while seeking to be justified in Christ we ourselves are branded "sinners" for eating with Gentiles, does that make Christ an agent of sin? "Certainly not!" If I rebuild the very barrier I tore down, I expose myself as a lawbreaker. For through the law I died to the law so that I might live for God (2:17–19).

Then arrives the verse many regard as the beating heart of the letter: "I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me." The life he now lives in the body he lives by faith in the Son of God, who loved him and gave Himself for him. He refuses to nullify the grace of God, "for if righteousness comes through the law, then Christ died for nothing." (2:20–21). That is what hangs on the line in Galatia—not a minor ritual, but the very meaning of the cross.

## Key Takeaways

**One gospel, two mission fields.** The Jerusalem apostles added zero conditions to Paul's message (2:6). Recognizing that the exact same God dispatched Peter to Jewish communities and Paul to Gentiles, they clasped hands in partnership (2:7–9). What differed was geography and audience, never the gospel itself.

**Freedom in Christ is worth defending fiercely.** Paul refused to cave "even for a moment" to pressure demanding Titus's circumcision (2:4–5). He saw clearly that conceding once in Jerusalem would saddle Gentile believers everywhere with that yoke.

**The dinner table tests our grasp of the gospel.** Peter never preached a single false sentence in Antioch; he merely switched tables (2:12). Yet Paul diagnosed that move as failing to walk "in line with the truth of the gospel" (2:14). The gospel shapes not only what we recite in creeds, but whom we welcome at table.

**Justified by faith, not works of the law.** Verse 16 repeats that verdict three times in one breath. Justification means God declaring us righteous and welcoming us into His covenant people—a standing received strictly by trusting Christ, never by keeping Mosaic regulations, even for lifelong Jews like Peter and Paul.

**Christ lives within me.** Faith goes far deeper than mental agreement with doctrine. Believers have died with Christ and now draw life from His own indwelling presence (2:20)—rooted in deeply personal love: the Son of God "loved me and gave Himself for me."

## Deeper Meaning

**Which Jerusalem visit is this?** Acts records Paul and Barnabas visiting Jerusalem both to deliver famine relief (Acts 11:29–30) and later to attend the Jerusalem Council over circumcision (Acts 15:1–2). Many scholars identify 2:1–10 with the Acts 15 council because the cast and issue match closely. Others match it to the earlier famine-relief visit, dating Galatians prior to the council—noting that otherwise Paul surely would have cited the council's formal decree to settle the argument.

**Peter had already learned this lesson once.** Inside Cornelius's house, Peter testified that God had shown him "not to call any person impure or unclean" (Acts 10:28). Returning to Jerusalem, he immediately faced criticism: "You went into the house of uncircumcised men and ate with them!" (Acts 11:3). Back in Antioch, that exact same critique and fear pulled him backward again.

**Paul echoes the Psalms.** Writing "by works of the law no one will be justified" (2:16) adapts David's prayer: "No one living is righteous before You" (Psalms 143:2). Paul draws on that same verse in Romans, explaining that "through the law comes knowledge of sin" (Romans 3:20).

**Crucified with Christ.** What Paul states personally in 2:20 applies to every believer in Romans: "our old self was crucified with Him" (Romans 6:6). Baptism enacts that death and entry into new life (Galatians 3:27).

**Remembering the poor.** The lone request from the Jerusalem pillars (2:10) became a defining passion of Paul's ministry: gathering generous relief offerings across Gentile churches for poor believers in Jerusalem (Romans 15:26; 1 Corinthians 16:1–3). For Paul, that collection proved tangibly that Jews and Gentiles belong to one family.

## Notes & Nuances

**Was Paul disrespectful toward the Jerusalem apostles?** Referring to "those who seemed influential" sounds brisk (2:6). Yet he never disparages them; he proudly records their handshake of fellowship (2:9). His point is strictly that his gospel's authority does not rest on human endorsement—and invoking their stature highlights how completely they stood behind his message.

**Paul never opposed Jews observing Jewish customs.** He never ordered Peter to abandon Jewish heritage; he rebuked Peter for "forcing Gentiles to live like Jews" (2:14). Paul himself later circumcised Timothy, whose mother was Jewish, to avoid stumbling blocks among Jewish listeners (Acts 16:3). What Paul fiercely opposed was turning Torah observance into an entry ticket into God's family.

**What are "works of the law"?** Two major readings exist. The classic Reformation reading sees all human moral and religious deeds performed to earn favor with God. A more recent reading emphasizes boundary-marking Torah practices separating Jews from Gentiles—circumcision, dietary codes, and Sabbath rules—fitting the Antioch table conflict directly. Many scholars observe that the two readings complement rather than cancel each other.

**"Faith in Jesus Christ" or "the faithfulness of Christ"?** The Greek phrase in 2:16 and 2:20 can translate either as our faith directed toward Christ or Christ's own faithful obedience. Traditional versions favor the first, though literal renderings sometimes echo the second ("faith of the Son of God"). Reading it as Christ's faithfulness never removes personal human faith, since verse 16 explicitly says, "we too have believed in Christ Jesus."

**Who prevailed at Antioch?** Paul omits the immediate outcome. Some scholars suspect that had he carried the room cleanly, he would have said so—and suggest Paul afterward moved on from Antioch into more independent mission work. Even so, Peter later championed Gentile freedom publicly (Acts 15:7–11) and even calls Paul "our beloved brother" (2 Peter 3:15).

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, late fourth-century Latin Bible translator, followed Origen's earlier view that the Antioch clash was staged by mutual arrangement between the two apostles. On this theory, Peter did not actually err, but allowed Paul to rebuke him publicly so Jewish onlookers would learn the lesson without hard feelings. *(Commentary on Galatians, on chapter 2 verse 11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jerome's "simulated disagreement" reading of Galatians 2:11–14 sparked his celebrated correspondence debate with Augustine -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa, wrote firmly to push back against Jerome. If Paul swore under oath that he was not lying (1:20) while narrating a staged play, Scripture would contain falsehood and readers could no longer trust what was true. For Augustine, Peter genuinely stumbled and Paul genuinely rebuked him—and Peter shone in true humility by accepting correction from a younger apostle. *(Letters to Jerome, Letters 28 and 40)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the Augustine–Jerome debate over Galatians 2 is famous across Letters 28, 40, and 82 -->

**Martin Luther** dwelt intensely on the pronoun "me" in verse 20, urging readers to pronounce that word with fierce faith and claim it personally—never doubting they belong inside that "me": the Son of God loved *me* and gave Himself for *me*. *(Commentary on Galatians, 1535, on chapter 2 verse 20)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's exhortation to read "me" with vigorous personal faith in Galatians 2:20 is widely quoted -->

**James Dunn**, British New Testament scholar, coined the phrase "the New Perspective on Paul" in a landmark 1983 lecture centered on verse 2:16. In his view, "works of the law" primarily denote identity markers separating Jew from Gentile such as circumcision and food laws—meaning Paul attacked using boundary badges to keep Gentiles out, rather than attacking legalistic merit-earning. *(The New Perspective on Paul, 1983)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Dunn's 1983 Manson Memorial Lecture centered on Galatians 2:16 and coined the name New Perspective -->

**Richard Hays**, American New Testament scholar, argued that the key Greek phrase in 2:16 and 2:20 means "the faithfulness of Jesus Christ"—Jesus' own faithful obedience unto death by which believers are justified. That reading frames Galatians as a grand story of what Christ accomplished rather than merely a lesson about human mental assent. *(The Faith of Jesus Christ, 1983)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this forms the central thesis of Hays's dissertation on Galatians 3:1–4:11, addressing 2:16 and 2:20 directly -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observed that Peter stumbled not out of doctrinal heresy, but out of fear of people. Calvin also drew a pointed ecclesial lesson: if even Peter could be corrected openly by a fellow apostle, no human leader in the Church stands above the correction of truth. *(Commentary on Galatians, on chapter 2 verses 11–14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Calvin traces Peter's slip to fear rather than false doctrine and applies the scene against papal supremacy; exact wording unchecked -->
