# Galatians 4 — Sons, Not Slaves

## Story

Paul continues his portrait of an heir. As long as an heir remains a child, even though he owns the entire estate, he is no different from a slave, living under guardians and trustees until the date set by his father. So it was with us: while still minors, we were enslaved under the elemental principles of the world (4:1–3).

Yet when the fullness of time arrived, God sent forth His Son—born of a woman, born under the law—to redeem those under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. And because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of His Son into our hearts, crying out, "Abba! Father!" Thus you are no longer a slave, but a son—and if a son, then an heir through God (4:4–7; Romans 8:15–17). God sends His Son, then sends the Spirit of His Son: the first sending makes us children; the second lets us know we are children.

Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to beings that by nature are not gods at all. Now that you have come to know God—or rather, to be known by God—how can you turn back again to weak and worthless elemental principles, eager to be enslaved to them all over again? You are observing special days, months, seasons, and years! Paul fears for them, worrying that his hard labor among them may have been in vain (4:8–11). Strikingly, Paul places adopting Mosaic festival calendars on the same footing as their former pagan idolatry: both mean marching backward into slavery.

Then his tone shifts from crisp debate to tender appeal. Paul pleads with them: become like me, since I became like you. They never wronged him. They recall how he first preached the gospel to them because of a bodily illness. Even though his physical ailment posed a trial for them, they neither scorned nor despised him, welcoming him instead like an angel of God—like Christ Jesus Himself. What happened to all that joy? He testifies that back then, had it been possible, they would have torn out their own eyes and handed them to him. Have I now become your enemy simply by telling you the truth? (4:12–16). Nobody knows what illness afflicted Paul; that remark about eyes leads many to suspect an eye disease (Galatians 6:11).

Those rival teachers court you eagerly, yet for no good motive; they want to shut you off from Paul so that you will court them instead. Being courted for good is always fine, not only when Paul stands present. Then he addresses them with a mother's tenderness: "My little children, for whom I am again in the anguish of childbirth until Christ is formed in you!" How he wishes he could be right there with them and change his tone, because he is perplexed about them (4:17–20). The fierce urgency of this letter is the cry of a parent terrified of losing children.

Closing the chapter, Paul turns to the Law itself to address those eager to live under the Law. Scripture records that Abraham had two sons—one by the slave woman, one by the free woman. The slave woman's son was born according to the flesh; the free woman's son was born through promise. Paul draws an allegory: the two women represent two covenants. One comes from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery—that is Hagar. Hagar stands for Mount Sinai in Arabia and corresponds to present-day Jerusalem, living in slavery together with her children. By contrast, the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother. As Scripture declares: "Rejoice, O barren woman who bears no children; break forth and cry aloud, you who are not in labor—for the children of the desolate one will be more than those of the one who has a husband!" (4:21–27; Genesis 16:15; Genesis 21:2; Isaiah 54:1). It is a stunning reversal: Paul aligns those boasting of Sarah's lineage on Hagar's side.

Now you, brothers and sisters, like Isaac, are children of promise. Just as back then the son born according to the flesh persecuted the son born according to the Spirit, so it happens today. Yet what does Scripture say? "Cast out the slave woman and her son, for the son of the slave woman shall never share the inheritance with the son of the free woman." Therefore, brothers and sisters, we are children not of the slave woman, but of the free woman (4:28–31; Genesis 21:9–10). Sarah's ancient demand becomes counsel for the Galatian churches: expel the agitators trying to drag you back under bondage.

## Key Takeaways

**Elevated from slaves to sons.** An underage heir lives scarcely better than a servant even though the estate belongs to him (4:1–2). When Christ arrives, God adopts believers as full children and heirs (4:5–7). Paul wants the Galatians to realize that retreating under Mosaic law means voluntarily marching back into the nursery.

**God sends His Son, then sends His Spirit.** His Son comes born of a woman and under the law to buy our freedom (4:4–5). Next the Son's Spirit enters our hearts crying "Abba! Father!" (4:6). Believers are not merely granted the legal title of children; we are given a child's living voice.

**Known by God.** Mid-sentence Paul revises himself: they have come to know God—"or rather to be known by God" (4:9). Our deepest security in faith never hinges on how much we grasp about God, but on the fact that He knows and claims us.

**A pastor's fierce affection.** Paul is no cold debater. He reminds them how eagerly they once cared for him—willing even to give him their own eyes (4:15)—and compares himself to a mother enduring labor pains all over again until Christ takes shape in them (4:19). His goal is never winning personal loyalty, but seeing Christ formed in their lives.

**Children of promise walk free.** Ishmael and Isaac illustrate two contrasting ways of seeking belonging: human effort versus God's promise (4:23). Believers, like Isaac, are born of promise and belong to "the Jerusalem above" (4:26–28).

## Deeper Meaning

**At just the right time.** Verse 4:4 declares that God sent His Son when the appointed time reached fullness. Jesus opened His own ministry announcing that the time was fulfilled and God's kingdom had drawn near (Mark 1:15). History never drifts aimlessly; God keeps His own clock.

**Abba—Jesus' own intimate address.** In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed, "Abba, Father" (Mark 14:36). Greek-speaking churches preserved that Aramaic word untouched, as Paul repeats in Romans: "by Him we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15). Adopted believers pray with the exact same intimate word Jesus used with His Father.

**Sarah, Hagar, and two sons.** Paul draws directly from Genesis 16 and 21. His quote in 4:30 comes from Sarah's plea to Abraham: "Get rid of that slave woman and her son" (Genesis 21:10). Paul attributes those words directly to "Scripture," because God Himself instructed Abraham to listen to Sarah's voice (Genesis 21:12).

**The barren woman rejoices.** Verse 4:27 cites Isaiah 54:1, comforting ruined Jerusalem after exile: "More are the children of the desolate woman than of her who has a husband." Sarah herself had long been barren. Paul sees Isaiah's vision unfolding as Gentiles from every corner join the citizens of the new Jerusalem.

**The heavenly Jerusalem.** Hebrews likewise assures believers they have come to "the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem" (Hebrews 12:22), and Revelation culminates with that holy city descending from heaven (Revelation 21:2). Believers' true homeland sits not on an earthly map, but in the city God builds.

**Observing calendar days.** Paul worries over their keeping "days and months and seasons and years" (4:10)—likely Sabbaths, new moons, and Jewish festivals urged by the agitators. Colossians sounds the exact same note: such observances "are a shadow of the things to come, whereas the substance belongs to Christ" (Colossians 2:16–17).

## Notes & Nuances

**What are "the elemental principles of the world"?** The Greek term (*stoicheia*) in 4:3 and 4:9 can denote basic ABCs taught to children; the four classical elements (earth, water, air, fire) thought to compose the cosmos; or spiritual forces believed to govern stars and seasons. Astonishingly, Paul groups both Jewish Torah observance and pagan idolatry under the same umbrella: both represent forms of bondage before Christ's arrival.

**What ailment afflicted Paul?** He mentions preaching to them initially because of "a bodily illness" (4:13) and their readiness to tear out their eyes for him (4:15). Many conjecture an eye affliction—possibly linked to his "thorn in the flesh" in 2 Corinthians 12:7—while others propose malaria or another malady. Of course, verse 4:15 may simply be vivid hyperbole for wholehearted devotion. Nobody knows for certain.

**Does Paul twist Scripture arbitrarily with allegory?** He openly states, "Now this may be interpreted allegorically" (4:24). Without denying the historical reality of Genesis, he traces a recurring pattern across God's dealings. Pairing Hagar with Mount Sinai and present Jerusalem startles readers because physically Jews descend from Sarah—showing Paul is contrasting two modes of belonging (bondage versus promise), not ranking two ethnic groups.

**Hagar is no villain.** In Genesis, Hagar is a mistreated slave woman whom God notices so tenderly that she names Him "the God who sees me" (Genesis 16:13). Reading Galatians 4 should never erase Genesis 16. Paul uses Hagar strictly as a figure for covenant bondage, never passing moral judgment on her person.

**Why does Paul say Ishmael "persecuted" Isaac?** Verse 4:29 states that Ishmael persecuted Isaac, whereas Genesis 21:9 simply says Ishmael was "laughing" or "mocking." Paul likely draws on Jewish interpretive traditions already reading that Hebrew verb as taunting or mistreatment, applying it to agitators relying on human effort harassing believers born of promise.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom** observed that in verse 24 Paul uses the word "allegory" slightly loosely: what he actually practices is typology—historical events in Genesis foreshadowing later spiritual realities. **Martin Luther** dwelt movingly on the cry "Abba! Father!" in verse 6. Amid temptation and despair, believers may feel only a faint sigh inside their hearts—yet Luther assured them that this Spirit-born sigh sounds like a thunderous cry in God's ears, drowning out every accusation of sin and Satan until heaven and earth ring with it. *(Commentary on Galatians, 1535, on chapter 4 verse 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's celebrated passage on the faint sigh sounding as a mighty cry to God on Galatians 4:6 is well known -->

**Midrash Genesis Rabbah**, a classical Jewish commentary on Genesis, debates the verb "laughing/mocking" in Genesis 21:9: some rabbis read it as idolatry or immorality, while another imagined Ishmael shooting arrows at Isaac and claiming he was only joking. **J. Louis Martyn** interpreted "the elemental principles of the cosmos" in verses 3 and 9 as the ancient pairs of cosmic opposites—such as hot and cold, wet and dry—believed to structure the world. In his view, the law likewise split humanity into binary pairs (Jew/Gentile, clean/unclean)—a divided cosmos now ended in Christ, enabling Paul to proclaim neither Jew nor Greek (3:28). *(Galatians, Anchor Bible, 1997, comment on "the elements of the cosmos")* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Martyn includes an excursus interpreting stoicheia as binary pairs; specific examples cited from memory -->

**Phyllis Trible**, American Old Testament scholar, examined Hagar's ordeal among Scripture's "texts of terror"—a woman used and cast aside. She noted how Galatians 4 casts Hagar once more onto the side of bondage and echoes her expulsion, urging readers never to lose sight of the real Hagar whom God saw and called by name. *(Texts of Terror, 1984, chapter on Hagar)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Trible's Hagar chapter in Texts of Terror; recalled her noting Paul's use of Hagar in Galatians 4 though exact length of discussion unchecked -->
