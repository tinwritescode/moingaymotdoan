# Song of Solomon 6 — Reunion in the Garden and the One and Only

## Story

After the woman's rapturous portrait at the close of chapter 5, the daughters of Jerusalem flip completely from skepticism to eagerness. They ask the fairest among women where her beloved has gone and which way he turned, so they can help her look for him (6:1). Right then the answer flashes clear in her mind: he has not wandered off somewhere strange; he has gone down to his garden, to the beds of spices, to browse in the gardens and gather lilies (6:2). Once more she speaks her steady pledge of belonging—this time placing her own gift of herself first: "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine" (6:3).

Without a single word of scolding over the missed encounter of chapter 5, the man's voice greets her warmly when they reunite. He praises her as lovely as Tirzah, graceful as Jerusalem, and awe-inspiring as an army with banners (6:4). He asks her to turn her eyes away because her gaze overwhelms him, then repeats the cherished praises of chapter 4—her hair cascading like a flock of goats down Mount Gilead, her teeth bright and even like freshly washed ewes, and her cheeks behind her veil glowing like halves of a pomegranate (6:5–7).

He lifts her far above every woman of a royal court: even if there are sixty queens, eighty concubines, and young women without number, his dove and flawless one is unique—her mother's only daughter, the cherished favorite of the one who bore her. Every young woman—even queens and concubines alike—sees her, calls her blessed, and praises her (6:8–9).

A voice of wonder rings out—either the chorus or the man himself—admiring her as she shines forth like the dawn, fair as the moon, bright as the sun, and majestic as an army with banners (6:10). Next come two brief, mysterious verses whose speaker varies across translations (some assign them to the man, others to the woman): going down to the walnut grove to see the new greenery of the valley, whether the vines have budded and the pomegranates are in bloom, and suddenly feeling swept away amid noble chariots (6:11–12). Closing the chapter, the companions call eagerly: "Return, return, O Shulammite! Return, return, that we may gaze upon you!"—to which another voice asks why they would gaze upon her as on the dance of Mahanaim (6:13).

## Key Takeaways

**Forgiveness and warm welcome heal fractured moments.** After the tearful missed encounter of chapter 5, the man never throws her mistake back in her face or punishes her with cold silence. He repeats the exact tender praises from their wedding day (6:5–7; cf. 4:1–3) so she knows his love stands completely intact.

**True love is always an exclusive choice.** Set beside the huge royal court of sixty queens and eighty concubines, the man declares that his beloved "is unique"—his one and only (6:8–9). Marital joy never grows from collecting options, but from wholehearted loyalty to one person.

**Sincere admiration draws others to love as well.** Back in chapter 5, the young women questioned what made her beloved special; once they hear her speak of him from an overflowing heart, they instantly want to search for him beside her (6:1). Honest devotion persuades far more powerfully than argument.

**Belonging matures from receiving to giving.** In chapter 2 she sang, "My beloved is mine and I am his" (2:16); now in chapter 6, having weathered the fear of losing him, she reverses the order: "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine" (6:3). Love deepens when we begin by giving ourselves first.

## Deeper Meaning

**Restoring grace after failure.** The man refusing to hold her hesitation against her and greeting her with his original words of love (6:4–7) mirrors God's heart toward faltering people. In the New Testament, Jesus restores Peter after his threefold denial with gracious questions anchored in love (John 21:15–17).

**The church as one unique body before Christ.** The portrait of the bride as "the only one" (6:9) connects richly with New Testament teaching on the unity of the church: though gathered from every nation, the church remains the one, undivided bride of Christ (Ephesians 4:4–6; Ephesians 5:25–32).

**The radiant majesty of God's bride.** Describing the bride as glowing like dawn, moon, and sun and awesome as an army with banners (6:10) anticipates the New Jerusalem in Revelation—both adorned like a bride for her husband and shining with the radiant glory of God (Revelation 21:2; Revelation 21:10–11).

## Notes & Nuances

**Two challenging verses in 6:11–12.** Verse 6:12 in Hebrew is one of the most puzzling lines in the entire Song of Solomon, producing varied renderings (such as *chariots of my willing people*, *chariots of a prince*, or *chariots of Amminadib*). Even so, the emotional drift of 6:11–12 shines clearly: while strolling through a spring grove, love surges up suddenly and sweeps the speaker off their feet.

**The name "Shulammite" (6:13).** This is the only spot in the whole book where the woman receives a specific title: *Shulammite*. Scholars offer two leading explanations: either the feminine counterpart of *Solomon* (from the Hebrew root for *peace*), meaning *woman of peace* or *Solomon's bride*; or a resident of the northern Israelite village of Shunem (later pronounced Shulem).

**Tirzah and Jerusalem (6:4).** Tirzah served as the famously scenic capital of Israel's northern kingdom before Samaria was built, while Jerusalem stood as the royal capital of the south. Pairing both cities praises her beauty as rivaling the two proudest jewels of the whole land.
