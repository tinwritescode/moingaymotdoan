# Song of Solomon 2 — Spring Arrives and the Beloved Calls

## Story

The woman begins by comparing herself to an ordinary wildflower growing in open fields: "a rose of Sharon," "a lily of the valley" (2:1). Immediately the man catches her words and lifts her far above every rival: among the young women, she stands out like a lily blooming among thorns (2:2). Answering back, she compares him among young men to an apple tree spreading its branches in a dense forest; she delights to sit in his shade and taste his sweet fruit (2:3).

She goes on describing his tender care: he brings her into the banquet hall, and his banner over her is love. Overwhelmed with longing, she asks to be sustained with raisin cakes and refreshed with apples, because she is "faint with love" (2:4–5). Nestled in his arms, his left hand rests beneath her head while his right hand embraces her. Right inside that quiet embrace, she turns to charge the daughters of Jerusalem—by the gazelles and does of the field—not to arouse or awaken love until it so desires (2:6–7).

Suddenly the scene shifts to quick, eager footsteps. The woman exclaims as she hears her beloved coming, leaping across the mountains and bounding over the hills as swiftly as a gazelle or young stag. He stands outside her wall, gazing through the windows and peering through the lattice (2:8–9). He calls her to rise and come away with him, for the cold winter and rains have passed; blossoms cover the earth, the season of singing has come, the cooing of doves echoes through the land, fig trees ripen their early fruit, and blossoming vines spill their fragrance (2:10–13).

The man calls her his dove tucked away in the clefts of the rock and hidden cliffside ledges, asking to see her lovely face and hear her sweet voice (2:14). Another voice—perhaps the couple together or friends chiming in—urges everyone to catch the little foxes ruining the vineyards just as the vines burst into bloom (2:15). The woman closes the scene with a complete pledge of belonging—"My beloved is mine and I am his"—inviting him to turn back to her like a young stag on the hills until the day breaks and the shadows flee (2:16–17).

## Key Takeaways

**Love makes someone ordinary feel utterly unique.** The woman sees herself as a simple wildflower in the meadow, yet to her beloved she outshines everyone around her (2:1–2). Genuine love does not wait for perfection before cherishing someone; loving eyes confer deep worth.

**True love blends intensity with patience.** Even after sweet closeness in each other's arms, the woman warns the young women never to stir up love before its proper season (2:6–7). Deep passion is not careless impulse; it knows how to wait for the right moment.

**Love carries the fresh breath of spring.** When the beloved arrives at the door, all creation seems to wake from winter chill: flowers bloom, birds sing, vines release their scent (2:10–13). Healthy love draws people out of winter isolation into vibrant new life.

**Small threats must be addressed early.** The vivid plea to catch "the little foxes that ruin the vineyards" (2:15) reminds us that even a flourishing relationship can suffer damage from small neglects, misunderstandings, or temptations if left unchecked.

**Belonging runs equally in both directions.** The pledge "My beloved is mine and I am his" (2:16) shows that love in the Song is mutual, wholehearted self-giving—never one-sided possession.

## Deeper Meaning

**The banner of love and God's sheltering care.** The picture of the beloved leading her to the banquet table under a banner of love (2:4) recalls God's watchful care in the Psalms, preparing a table for His people (Psalms 23:5). God's love does not merely rescue from danger; it invites us into joyful fellowship.

**The covenant language of mutual belonging.** "My beloved is mine and I am his" (2:16) echoes the foundational covenant pledge ringing through Scripture: God will be His people's God, and they will be His people (Exodus 6:7; Jeremiah 31:33). In the New Testament, Jesus speaks of that same mutual abiding between Himself and believers (John 15:4–5).

**Called out of winter.** The bridegroom summoning his bride to step out of winter into springtime (2:10–13) has led generations of Christians to hear the call of Christ lifting humanity out of the winter of sin and fear into new creation (2 Corinthians 5:17).

## Notes & Nuances

**"A rose of Sharon" describes the woman, not the man (2:1).** Many beloved hymns use *Rose of Sharon* and *Lily of the Valleys* as poetic titles for Jesus. Reading the flow of Song of Solomon 2:1–2, however, the speaker of verse 1 is the woman modestly describing herself as an everyday meadow flower—to which the man responds in verse 2 that she is like a lily among thorns.

**"Little foxes" carry rich layers of meaning (2:15).** In ancient Near Eastern poetry, a *vineyard* frequently symbolized the woman herself or the couple's budding romance. Thus the little foxes represent subtle nuisances, outside intruders, or small fissures within a blossoming relationship that can spoil its fruit if ignored.

**The book's first refrain (2:7).** Here appears the first of three charges *not to awaken love* until its proper time. Calling on gazelles and wild does—graceful, sensitive, easily startled creatures—adds a tender, solemn rhythm to guarding love's timing.
