# Song of Solomon 1 — Opening Lines and the Call of Love

## Story

The poem opens with a superscription naming it the finest of all songs, linked with King Solomon (1:1). Instantly, without preface or narrator, the woman's voice breaks in filled with longing: she wants him to kiss her, for his love is "more delightful than wine," and his fragrance and name make all the young women adore him. She begs him to draw her away so they can run together, recalling the joy of the king bringing her into his chambers, while a chorus of young women—the daughters of Jerusalem—joins in praising their love (1:2–4). Because the original Hebrew never prints speaker labels above the lines, readers distinguish the woman, the man, and the chorus of friends through grammatical gender and number, which explains why translations occasionally assign a few lines differently.

The woman turns to the daughters of Jerusalem to speak of her sun-darkened skin: she is dark as the tents of Kedar, yet lovely as the curtains of Solomon. She tells them not to stare at her swarthy complexion; her brothers grew angry with her and forced her to tend the vineyards, leaving her no time to look after *her own vineyard*—that is, herself (1:5–6). Then she asks the one her soul loves where he pastures his flock and rests them at noon, so she will not have to wander veiled like a stranger around the flocks of his companions (1:7).

A reply—either from the man or from the chorus—calls her "fairest of women" and advises her simply to follow the tracks of the flock and graze her young goats beside the shepherds' tents (1:8). Next the man speaks words of admiration: he compares her to a prized mare among Pharaoh's chariots, praising her cheeks framed with ornaments and her graceful neck lined with jewels. The chorus—or the man along with his friends—promises to craft her ornaments of gold studded with silver (1:9–11).

The scene shifts to the two lovers side by side. The woman says that while the king rests at his table, her perfume of nard sends out its fragrance. To her, her beloved is like a sachet of myrrh resting close against her heart, like a cluster of henna blossoms blooming in the vineyards of En-gedi (1:12–14). The man responds with short, enraptured praise: how beautiful she is, with eyes gentle as doves (1:15). She answers back in delight at how handsome and charming he is, pointing to their own private shelter amid nature: fresh green grass is their bed, towering cedars form the beams of their house, and pine branches arch overhead as rafters (1:16–17).

## Key Takeaways

**Love speaks openly without hiding behind embarrassment.** Right from the opening verse, the woman takes the initiative to express her longing and delight (1:2). The book never shrinks from emotion or physical affection, treating attraction between man and woman as good and natural.

**True beauty is not measured by outsiders' standards.** Self-conscious for a moment about skin tanned from fieldwork, the woman still declares with quiet confidence, "I am dark, yet lovely" (1:5–6). In her beloved's eyes she stands as the fairest among women, with nothing able to diminish her worth (1:8; 1:15).

**Spoken praise nourishes love.** Again and again the two lovers notice what is precious in each other and say it aloud—eyes, scent, presence, and grace (1:9–16). Love in the Song of Solomon is never silent, unspoken sentiment; it is an active conversation steeped in admiration.

**Love can flourish even in humble surroundings.** Their sanctuary does not require a gold-plated palace; a bed of green grass beneath cedar boughs becomes a rich home (1:16–17). What makes them wealthy is simply being together.

## Deeper Meaning

**An echo of love at creation.** Watching man and woman gaze on each other with pure delight and zero shame brings back the atmosphere of Genesis before sin entered the world (Genesis 2:23–25). The Song of Solomon reminds us that marital love is a wholesome gift from the Creator, never something lesser or shameful.

**The bridegroom and bride imagery spanning all of Scripture.** Old Testament prophets repeatedly picture God's covenant with His people through marriage, like a bridegroom rejoicing over his bride (Isaiah 62:4–5; Hosea 2:19–20). In the New Testament, Paul calls marriage a profound mystery pointing to Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:25–32), and Revelation brings the entire story of redemption to its climax at the wedding supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7; Revelation 21:2).

**Love calls for undivided belonging.** Inside an ancient world where kings kept sprawling harems, the opening chapter focuses intensely on exclusive devotion between two people calling each other *my darling* and *my beloved* (1:13–16). In the same way, biblical faith invites single-hearted devotion to one God alone.

## Notes & Nuances

**Three major ways readers approach the Song of Solomon.** Across millennia, readers have engaged this book along three main paths. The literal reading celebrates the Song as love poetry honoring romantic, embodied married love created by God. Jewish tradition has long read it allegorically as God's covenant love for Israel, reciting it during Passover. Classic Christian tradition—led by Origen, Bernard of Clairvaux, and later Charles Spurgeon—read it allegorically as the love between Christ and the church, or Christ and the individual believer. These three avenues need not fight each other: honoring the literal beauty of married love naturally opens the heart to contemplate the greater love that human marriage reflects (Ephesians 5:32).

**A recurring refrain guarding love's timing.** Three times across the book, a solemn charge is spoken to the daughters of Jerusalem: *do not arouse or awaken love* until it so desires (2:7; 3:5; 8:4). That refrain paces the entire Song: love is passionate and wonderful, yet it deserves patience until its proper season arrives rather than being rushed prematurely.

**The title and Solomon's name.** "The Song of Songs" is a Hebrew superlative meaning *the greatest song of all*, parallel to *Holy of Holies* or *King of kings* (1:1). The Hebrew phrase linking it to Solomon can mean written by Solomon, dedicated to Solomon, or belonging to the royal poetic tradition associated with his court.

**The mare among Pharaoh's chariots (1:9).** Modern readers may raise an eyebrow at comparing a beloved woman to a horse. In the ancient world, Pharaoh's war chariots were pulled exclusively by stallions; releasing one spirited mare amid a team of chariot stallions would throw the whole company into instant commotion. It is a playful compliment to her breathtaking allure, while also picturing a royal steed adorned with dazzling ornaments (1:10–11).

## What Commentators Say

**Rabbi Akiva**, second-century Jewish sage, famously defended the Song of Solomon's place in the biblical canon when rabbis debated the sacred writings. **Origen**, third-century theologian of Alexandria, pioneered the spiritual reading of the Song in the early church. **Bernard of Clairvaux**, twelfth-century French abbot, devoted eighty-six sermons to the Song of Solomon and lingered deeply over 1:2. He traced three stages of drawing near to God: kissing the feet in repentance, kissing the hand to receive grace, and finally longing for "the kisses of his mouth"—deep, peaceful, intimate union with God. *(Sermons on the Song of Songs, opening sermons)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Bernard's threefold image of kissing feet, hand, and mouth in his opening sermons is among the most celebrated passages in medieval biblical exposition -->

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Puritan pastor, read "I am dark, yet lovely" (1:5) as an honest portrait of the believer: outwardly weathered and sunburned by trials and flaws, yet made genuinely lovely in God's sight through the grace of Christ. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Song of Solomon 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: fits Matthew Henry's familiar allegorical reading of Song of Solomon 1:5, though exact wording was not re-checked -->
