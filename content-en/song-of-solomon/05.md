# Song of Solomon 5 — A Missed Encounter at Night and a Portrait of the Beloved

## Story

The opening verse of chapter 5 actually belongs directly to the invitation at the close of chapter 4. The man replies with overflowing joy: he has entered his garden, gathered his myrrh and spice, eaten his honeycomb, and drunk his wine and milk. Then a chorus of blessing—perhaps friends or the poet himself—cheers the newlyweds on to eat, drink, and drink deeply of their love (5:1).

From verse 2 onward, the mood shifts abruptly into a tense nighttime episode feeling halfway between an anxious dream and a painful real memory. The woman tells how she was asleep yet her heart stayed awake, when she heard her beloved knocking outside. He calls her with four tender names in a row—my sister, my darling, my dove, my flawless one—asking her to open the door because his head is drenched with dew and his hair damp with the night mist (5:2). Yet she hesitates with petty excuses: she has already taken off her robe—how can she put it back on? She has washed her feet—how can she get them dusty again? (5:3).

When her beloved reaches his hand through the latch opening, her heart leaps within her. She rises to open the door, her hands dripping liquid myrrh onto the handles of the bolt. Yet when she swings the door open, he has already turned and vanished (5:4–6). Her heart sinks in shock. She runs out looking for him without finding him, calls his name and gets no answer. Worse still, the city watchmen making their rounds find her, strike her, bruise her, and tear off her cloak (5:6–7). Wounded and desperate, she begs the daughters of Jerusalem: if they find her beloved, tell him she is "faint with love" (5:8).

The daughters of Jerusalem answer back with curiosity—and a hint of challenge: how is her beloved better than any other, that she charges them so earnestly? (5:9). That question unleashes the most radiant portrait of the man anywhere in the book. She describes him as "dazzling and ruddy, outstanding among ten thousand": his head is purest gold, his wavy hair black as a raven, his eyes clear and gentle like doves beside flowing streams, his cheeks fragrant like beds of spice, his lips like lilies dripping flowing myrrh (5:10–13).

She goes on describing his frame through precious, enduring materials: arms like rods of gold set with jewels, torso like polished ivory inlaid with sapphires, legs sturdy as alabaster pillars set on bases of pure gold, his bearing stately as Lebanon and its finest cedars (5:14–15). Finally, she gathers everything into one proud summation: his speech is sweetness itself, and "he is altogether lovely"—this is her beloved, and this is her friend (5:16).

## Key Takeaways

**Small carelessness can trigger painful distance.** The woman does not turn her beloved away because love died—only because she cannot be bothered to slip on a robe or dust her feet (5:2–3). Yet one moment of sluggishness costs her his presence and brings loneliness and bruises (5:6–7).

**Real love must navigate misunderstandings and wounds.** Unlike fairy-tale romances that smooth over every snag, the Song does not hide missed cues, late regrets, or harsh encounters in the dark (5:6–7). Even right after the joyous wedding feast of 5:1, marriage still requires alertness and eagerness to welcome one another.

**Hardship clarifies how precious our beloved truly is.** Precisely while separated and questioned by outsiders, the woman sees her beloved more vividly than ever (5:9–16). She does not offer vague compliments; she names feature after feature that makes him stand out among ten thousand.

**Lasting romance walks hand in hand with friendship.** In the closing verse, she calls him not only "my beloved" but also "my friend" (5:16). The strongest love flourishes when a spouse is also one's closest companion.

## Deeper Meaning

**The knock at the door in Revelation.** The scene of the beloved standing outside knocking and calling for the door to open (5:2) carries a striking echo in Jesus' words to the church in Laodicea in Revelation: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20). Both moments warn how easily complacency and comfort can make us slow to welcome the One standing right at our threshold.

**Staying awake for the Bridegroom.** Her hesitation in 5:3 causing the beloved to depart also echoes Jesus' teaching to stay watchful for the Bridegroom's coming, never letting drowsiness or carelessness cost us the hour of meeting Him (Matthew 25:1–13).

**"Outstanding among ten thousand" and bearing witness to Christ.** When the young women ask how her beloved differs from any other (5:9), her glowing reply (5:10–16) has reminded generations of Christians of the question the world asks the church: *What makes your Lord any different?* The most convincing answer never comes from dry debate, but from a heart that genuinely knows and loves the One it follows (1 Peter 3:15).

## Notes & Nuances

**The chapter break between 4:16 and 5:1.** Chapter divisions were added to the Bible centuries after the books were written. Here, 5:1 is the immediate response to the bride's invitation in 4:16, closing the wedding celebration. A brand-new scene begins at 5:2.

**Why do the watchmen strike the woman (5:7)?** Back in chapter 3, the watchmen let her pass peacefully; here in chapter 5, they beat her and strip away her shawl. Inside an anxiety dream, that stark shift magnifies the vulnerability, panic, and pain of losing one's beloved. Socially in the ancient world, a woman wandering the city streets alone at night also risked being misjudged by patrols and treated harshly.

**A portrait built from jewels and architecture (5:10–15).** Whereas chapter 4 describes the woman through lush, living nature imagery, chapter 5 describes the man through fine gold, ivory, sapphires, and marble pillars. Ancient poetry drew on monumental temple and royal materials to express strength, nobility, and steadfastness in the eyes of the one who loves him.
