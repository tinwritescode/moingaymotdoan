# Song of Solomon 4 — Praising the Bride and the Enclosed Garden

## Story

Chapter 4 consists almost entirely of the groom's rapturous praise of his bride, flowing straight out of the wedding procession at the close of chapter 3. Gazing from her head downward, he celebrates each feature in turn: her eyes behind her veil are gentle as doves; her dark hair cascades like a flock of goats descending Mount Gilead; her teeth are even and spotless like freshly washed ewes each bearing twins with none missing; her lips are bright as a scarlet ribbon; and her flushed cheeks behind her veil glow like halves of a pomegranate (4:1–3).

He continues admiring her proud, stately neck—like the tower of David hung with a thousand shields of mighty warriors—and her soft breasts, like twin fawns of a gazelle grazing among lilies (4:4–5). Expressing his longing to come to her—to ascend the *mountain of myrrh* and the *hill of frankincense*—and stay until dawn breaks and shadows flee, he seals his first song of praise with one simple, sweeping declaration: "You are altogether beautiful, my darling; in you there is no flaw" (4:6–7).

His voice deepens as he addresses her for the very first time as *my bride*. He invites her to come down with him from towering, rugged peaks—Lebanon, Amana, Senir, and Hermon, haunts of lions and leopards—calling her out of distant heights into closeness beside him (4:8). He confesses that she has stolen his heart completely with a single glance of her eyes and one jewel of her necklace. To him her love is sweeter than wine, her fragrance outmatches every perfume, her lips drop honeycomb with honey and milk beneath her tongue, and her garments carry the fresh scent of Lebanon's forests (4:9–11).

He calls his bride an enclosed garden, a locked spring, and a sealed fountain—emblems of purity and devotion reserved exclusively for him (4:12). That garden flourishes like paradise itself, rich with pomegranates, choice fruits, and rare spices gathered from across the world—nard, saffron, cinnamon, myrrh, and aloes—while she is a garden spring and well of living water streaming down from Lebanon (4:13–15). In the closing verse, the bride speaks at last with a graceful, decisive invitation: she summons the north wind and south wind to blow across her garden so its spices waft abroad, and invites her beloved to come into *his* garden and taste its choicest fruits (4:16).

## Key Takeaways

**Lovers gaze on each other with wholehearted admiration.** The groom pours out an entire song admiring his bride without a single complaint or comparison to anyone else, declaring that in her "there is no flaw" (4:7). True love does not pick at faults; it treasures the whole person standing before it.

**Purity gives marriage its quiet radiance.** The images of a "garden locked up," an "enclosed spring," and a "sealed fountain" (4:12) honor faithfulness and self-control kept for one's spouse. The beauty is not staying closed forever, but keeping oneself intact in order to open freely to the one beloved (4:16).

**Self-giving in love is willing and rooted in trust.** The groom never forces entry into the garden; he praises, waits, and honors her. The bride herself extends the invitation to enter her garden (4:16). When trust reaches fullness, *my garden* joyfully becomes *his garden*.

**Love awakens every human sense.** The poem brims with color, perfume, the taste of honey, milk, and wine, and the rush of wind through trees (4:10–16). Scripture never treats physical delight inside marriage as shameful, but as part of God's good gift of life.

## Deeper Meaning

**Eden's garden reawakened.** The word describing the bride's orchard in 4:13 comes from the root behind *paradise*, brimming with fruit trees and watered by living streams (4:13–15; cf. Genesis 2:8–10). Inside a world bruised by sin, faithful marital love offers a living taste of creation's original harmony.

**A private spring in Proverbs.** Calling the bride a *well of living water* and a *sealed fountain* (4:12; 4:15) walks hand in hand with the counsel in Proverbs urging a husband to drink water from his own cistern and rejoice in the wife of his youth (Proverbs 5:15–19).

**A bride without blemish in the New Testament.** The groom's praise—"in you there is no flaw" (4:7)—resounds when Paul describes Christ's love for the church: He gave Himself up and cleansed her to present her to Himself "without stain or wrinkle," holy and blameless (Ephesians 5:25–27; cf. 2 Corinthians 11:2). And the bride dressed beautifully for her husband returns at the grand close of Revelation (Revelation 21:2).

## Notes & Nuances

**Hebrew poetic metaphors work differently from modern photography.** Today's reader may smile at hair compared to a herd of goats, teeth to shorn ewes, or a neck to a shield-hung tower (4:1–4). Ancient Near Eastern poetry never aimed for a literal portrait; it conveyed *impression* and *feeling*: goats rippling down a mountain slope capture dark, flowing hair full of vitality; twin ewes rising freshly washed picture bright, even teeth with none missing; David's tower conveys a tall, regal neck glittering with layered jewelry like gleaming shields.

**Calling her "my sister, my bride" (4:9–12).** Across ancient Egyptian and Near Eastern love songs, calling a sweetheart *sister* was an affectionate endearment expressing deep closeness and kinship—not blood relation. Paired with *bride*, it unites intimate friendship with married romance.

**The poetic form of bodily praise known as a "wasf."** Scholars call this genre—praising a beloved's features sequentially from head to toe (or toe to head)—a *wasf*, a classic Middle Eastern wedding song style. The Song of Solomon contains four of them: in chapters 4 and 7 praising the woman, in chapter 5 praising the man, and a shorter one in chapter 6.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Puritan pastor, saw 4:7 as a portrait of the righteousness Christ bestows upon His church: believers carry many imperfections in themselves, yet covered by Christ's grace, He views His church as spotless. **Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English Baptist pastor, preached on 4:16 as the church's prayer inviting the Holy Spirit to move. In his exposition, the sharp *north wind* represents bracing trials or conviction that wakes the soul, while the warm *south wind* brings comfort and grace; both must blow across the heart's garden so faith's fragrance spreads and Christ delights to dwell there. *(Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit, sermon on Song of Solomon chapter 4 verse 16)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's sermon interpreting the north wind as conviction and the south wind as comfort of the Holy Spirit in chapter 4 verse 16 is widely known -->

**Gregory of Nyssa**, fourth-century Church Father, taught that the praises of the bride's beauty throughout chapter 4 portray the soul steadily growing more conformed to God's image. When the bride summons the winds into her garden in 4:16, he saw the soul throwing open its heart to the breath of the Spirit so spiritual fruit might abound. *(Homilies on the Song of Songs, homilies on chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: fits Gregory of Nyssa's theology of perpetual spiritual growth in his Song homilies, exact homily numbers not re-checked -->

**Tremper Longman III**, American Old Testament scholar, emphasized that 4:12–16 together with 5:1 form the structural centerpiece of the Song of Solomon: the wedding night uniting husband and wife in full harmony.
