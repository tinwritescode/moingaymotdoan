# Song of Solomon 8 — Love as Strong as Death and the Closing Call

## Story

The final chapter opens with a tender, deeply human wish from the woman: if only her beloved were like a brother raised in her mother's home, she could kiss him openly in the street without anyone looking down on her (8:1). She longs to lead him into her mother's house, where she would give him spiced wine to drink and sweet nectar from her pomegranates (8:2). Once more she pictures resting in his embrace—his left arm under her head and his right arm holding her—and delivers for the third and final time her solemn charge to the daughters of Jerusalem not to arouse or awaken love until it so desires (8:3–4).

An admiring chorus asks who is coming up from the wilderness leaning on her beloved (8:5). Next, one of the lovers (assigned to the man in some translations and the woman in others) recalls awakening the other beneath the apple tree tied to family roots. Then the woman speaks the most famous, profound lyric in the entire Song of Solomon: she asks him to set her as a seal over his heart and a seal upon his arm, "For love is as strong as death, jealousy as unrelenting as Sheol"—its blazing flashes burn like the very flame of the Lord (8:6). Mighty waters cannot quench love, nor can rivers sweep it away; if someone tried to buy love with all the wealth of his house, he would only earn utter scorn (8:7).

The poem flashes back briefly to her older brothers talking when she was still young and not yet grown, wondering what to do when suitors came asking for her. If she stood firm like a city wall, they would crown her with towers of silver; if she swung open easily like a door, they would barricade her with cedar planks (8:8–9). Now she replies with serene confidence and quiet dignity: she has indeed stood steady as a wall, and in her beloved's eyes she has become one who brings complete peace (8:10).

She closes her argument by contrasting King Solomon's vineyard with her own: Solomon owns a sprawling vineyard at Baal-hamon farmed out to hired tenants, each paying a thousand pieces of silver for its fruit (8:11). By contrast, *her own vineyard*—her body, her heart, and her love—remains right before her in her own keeping; Solomon is welcome to keep his thousand silver pieces, because the love she bestows on her chosen partner is freely given, never rented or sold (8:12). The final two verses leave the whole book ringing on an open, eager note: hearing companions listen for her voice in the gardens, the man pleads to hear her voice himself, and she answers by calling him to hurry to her like a gazelle or young stag leaping across hills of spices (8:13–14).

## Key Takeaways

**True love can neither be quenched nor bought.** Verses 8:6–7 stand as the summit of the Song of Solomon. Authentic love is as fierce and unstoppable as death itself, undrowned by floods of hardship, and utterly beyond purchase by money or status.

**Love belongs to permanent covenant commitment.** She asks to be pressed like a "seal over your heart" and "upon your arm" (8:6). A seal marks lasting ownership and an unbreakable pledge—the heart guarding inward devotion, the arm expressing loyal action.

**Self-respect and faithfulness yield peace and freedom.** Unlike chapter 1, where she felt self-conscious because her brothers made her tend others' vineyards while neglecting her own (1:6), at the book's close she stands tall as a solid wall, resting in full peace before her beloved (8:10–12). Self-control and fidelity produce quiet confidence.

**True love never ends with a full stop.** The Song of Solomon does not conclude with a dry moral lecture, but with an eager call for her beloved to hurry near (8:14). Real love stays alive, expectant, and renewed day after day.

## Deeper Meaning

**Song of Solomon 8:6–7 and the hymn of love in 1 Corinthians 13.** Declaring that love matches death in strength, outlasts rushing waters, and cannot be bought for silver (8:6–7) meets Paul's timeless anthem in the New Testament: love endures all things and "never fails" (1 Corinthians 13:4–8). Though one celebrates married romance and the other embraces love throughout the whole church, both flow from the same wellspring: genuine love originates in God Himself.

**Love stronger than death proven at the cross.** The Song calls love "as strong as death" (8:6), and the New Testament reveals that Christ's love proved *stronger* than death: He gave Himself up for the church to redeem her and make her spotless (Ephesians 5:25–27; Romans 8:38–39). Every faithful marriage quietly mirrors that astonishing mystery (Ephesians 5:31–32).

**The bride's final call echoes at the close of the whole Bible.** The closing plea of the Song—"Come away, my beloved" (8:14)—beats with the exact longing that ends the entire Bible in Revelation, where the Spirit and the Bride cry together for the Bridegroom, Jesus, to come swiftly for the wedding supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7; Revelation 21:2; Revelation 22:17; Revelation 22:20).

## Notes & Nuances

**The phrase "the flame of the LORD" (8:6).** This marks the single fleeting echo of God's covenant name in the entire Song of Solomon—the Hebrew attaches a shortened form of the divine name (*Yah*) directly onto the word *flame*. Some translations render it *a mighty flame* or *the fiercest blaze* (as in the Berean Standard Bible), while many scholars and versions retain *the very flame of the LORD* to stress that the fire of true love comes from God Himself.

**The meaning of "jealousy" in 8:6.** Here *jealousy* does not mean petty suspicion or spite, but fierce, passionate zeal guarding the exclusive loyalty of marriage—just as Scripture describes God Himself as a jealous God when He calls His people to undivided faithfulness (Exodus 20:5; 2 Corinthians 11:2).

**The final appearance of the refrain (8:4).** After charging the daughters of Jerusalem three times *not to awaken love* prematurely (2:7; 3:5; 8:4), the refrain sounds one last time right before the grand declaration on love's power in 8:6–7—reminding readers that a fire this fierce and sacred deserves deep reverence and patient care.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Puritan pastor, lingered deeply over 8:6–7 to celebrate Christ's love for His church: no flood of affliction could drown His love at the cross, and no human wealth or merit can buy it—it is received solely as free grace. **Tremper Longman III**, American Old Testament scholar, regarded 8:6–7 as the theological and literary pinnacle of the entire Song of Solomon.
