# 1 Samuel 26 — David Spares Saul a Second Time

## Story

After his near-bloodshed with Nabal and marriages to Abigail and Ahinoam in the previous chapter, David's fugitive days seem briefly settled. Before long, however, the Ziphites—the very people who informed on him once before—travel up to Gibeah again to tell Saul that David is hiding on the hill of Hachilah facing Jeshimon. Immediately Saul marches down to the Wilderness of Ziph with three thousand elite troops of Israel and sets up camp right beside the road on the hill of Hachilah. Out in the wilderness, David spots Saul pursuing him, sends out scouts, and confirms the king has truly arrived (26:1–4).

Instead of bolting right away, David walks over to inspect Saul's encampment firsthand. He locates Saul's exact sleeping spot in the center of the camp, ringed by army commander Abner son of Ner and all the troops bedded down around him. Turning to Ahimelech the Hittite and Abishai son of Zeruiah, brother of Joab, David asks who will sneak down into Saul's camp with him. Abishai volunteers on the spot (26:5–6).

Under cover of night, David and Abishai slip right through the sleeping lines. Right in front of them lies Saul fast asleep inside the camp, his spear stuck in the ground near his head, with Abner and the soldiers sprawled all around. Seeing the chance of a lifetime, Abishai whispers that today God has delivered David's enemy into his hands—let him pin Saul to the ground with one thrust of the spear; "I will not strike him twice!" (26:7–8).

David shuts the idea down immediately. "Do not destroy him," he tells Abishai, "for who can lift a hand against the LORD's anointed and be guiltless?" Swearing by the living LORD, David declares that God Himself will handle Saul—either the LORD will strike him, or his day will come to die, or he will go into battle and perish. As for David, may the LORD forbid that he ever lay a hand on the LORD's anointed. He instructs Abishai simply to take the spear and water jug right by Saul's head, and together they slip quietly away. Not a single soul sees them, notices, or wakes up, because "a deep sleep from the LORD had fallen on them" (26:9–12).

Crossing to the opposite side, David climbs to the top of a hill at a safe distance and shouts across to the troops and commander Abner. Startled awake, Abner yells back asking who dares disturb the king. David taunts Abner sharply: aren't you Israel's unmatched warrior—why then didn't you guard your lord the king when someone crept right in to destroy him? Pronouncing Abner worthy of death for neglecting the LORD's anointed, David tells him to look down and see where the king's spear and water jug have gone (26:13–16).

Through the dark, Saul recognizes the familiar voice and calls across, "Is that your voice, David my son?" David confirms it and pleads painfully: why does my lord keep hunting his servant—what wrong or crime is in my hands? Calmly David lays out both possibilities: if the LORD has incited Saul against him, may God accept an offering; if men have stirred him up, may they be cursed before the LORD, for they have driven him out from sharing in the LORD's inheritance, as good as telling him, "Go, serve other gods!" He begs that his blood not fall to the ground far away from the LORD's presence, asking why the king of Israel marches out to hunt "a single flea, as one hunts a partridge in the mountains" (26:17–20).

Struck to the core, Saul cries out in confession: "I have sinned. Come back, David my son!" He promises never to harm him again because David counted his life precious today, admitting aloud, "Surely I have played the fool and erred greatly." Yet David does not walk down into Saul's camp. Holding up the spear, he tells the king to send a young man over to retrieve it, adding that the LORD repays each person for his righteousness and faithfulness. Today God placed Saul into his hand, yet he refused to strike the LORD's anointed; just as he valued Saul's life today, he trusts the LORD to value his own life and rescue him from every trouble (26:21–24).

Saul pronounces a blessing over David, declaring that he will surely do great things and prevail. With that, the two go their separate ways: David continues down his path, and Saul returns home. From that night onward, the two men never lay eyes on each other again (26:25).

## Key Takeaways

**An easy opening is never automatic proof of God's approval.** Seeing Saul asleep with his spear planted inches from his head, Abishai concluded instantly that God had served David's foe on a platter (26:8). It sounded logical, pious, and tailor-made to the moment. Yet David knew the difference between an open circumstance and God's actual command. Sometimes an effortless shortcut to solve our crisis is a test of whether we will wait patiently for God's way.

**Respecting God-given office does not mean excusing the officeholder's sins.** Saul had been rejected by God and was marching three thousand troops to kill an innocent man. Even so, David refused to take removal into his own hands because Saul still bore the mark of "the LORD's anointed" (26:9; 26:11). David called out Saul's injustice openly and refused to walk blindly into danger, yet held an unshakable moral boundary: removing the king belonged to God, not to him.

**The lesson from Nabal had already sunk deep into David's heart.** Just one chapter earlier, Nabal's insults pushed David to the brink of massacring an entire household; Abigail had to restrain him from self-vengeance before the LORD Himself struck Nabal down (25:26; 25:38). Now, facing a far deadlier enemy, David needs nobody to remind him. He speaks that exact truth to Abishai himself: the LORD will strike Saul, or his day will come, or he will fall in battle (26:10). Faith matures when yesterday's hard lesson becomes today's settled conviction.

**Forgiveness never requires naive gullibility.** David spared Saul a second time, addressed him respectfully, and returned his royal spear (26:22). Yet when Saul confessed and urged "Come back" (26:21), David stayed put across the valley. He knew how fast Saul's tearful promises evaporated. Mercy and wisdom walk together: we can renounce revenge while still keeping healthy distance from someone who has not genuinely changed.

**The deepest ache of a God-fearing heart is exile from God's worship.** In his passionate plea in verse 19, what outraged David most about his slanderers was not sleeping in rocks or losing rank, but being driven away from the Promised Land—the LORD's inheritance (26:19). To someone who truly loves God, losing fellowship with God's people and sanctuary hurts deeper than any material hardship.

## Deeper Meaning

**A heaven-sent deep sleep and God's quiet orchestration.** Verse 26:12 explains why not one sentry stirred: "a deep sleep from the LORD had fallen on them." The Hebrew word (*tardemah*) denotes an extraordinary, God-sent trance—the very same word used when God put Adam into deep sleep to form Eve (Genesis 2:21) and when deep sleep fell on Abram during God's covenant ceremony (Genesis 15:12). Even without noisy miracles, God quietly clears paths and shields His servants right inside enemy camps.

**Saul's spear changes hands in silence.** Throughout 1 Samuel, Saul's spear stands as his constant companion and symbol of increasingly tyrannical rule—hurled at David inside the palace (18:10–11; 19:10) and even flung at his own son Jonathan (20:33). Tonight that very spear planted by the king's pillow rests effortlessly in David's grip, alongside the water jug sustaining life in the desert (26:11–12). Without spilling one drop of blood, the image says it all: both royal power and life itself have slipped out of Saul's grasp.

**An unwitting forecast of Mount Gilboa.** Restraining Abishai, David lists three ways the LORD might end Saul's life, including that "he will go into battle and perish" (26:10). Within just a few chapters, those exact words come true against the Philistines (31:3–4). Shortly afterward, when an Amalekite runner proudly claims to have dealt Saul the fatal blow expecting reward, David enforces the exact standard he voiced tonight, executing anyone bold enough to strike down the LORD's anointed (2 Samuel 1:14–16).

**Entrusting vengeance to the righteous Judge.** David's posture on the hill of Hachilah rings across Scripture: from Moses' law declaring vengeance belongs to God (Deuteronomy 32:35) to Paul teaching believers never to avenge themselves but to overcome evil with good (Romans 12:19–21). Higher still, claiming a throne not through violence or grasping but by patient endurance waiting on God's hour points directly to Jesus, who suffered insults without retaliating, "entrusting Himself to Him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:23).

**A wilderness cry preserved in Psalm 54.** The Ziphites betrayed David to Saul twice (23:19–20; 26:1). Psalm 54's traditional heading ties that prayer to the Ziphites reporting David's hiding place. Rather than plotting revenge or leaning on military force, David casts himself on God as his helper and righteous defender while ruthless men hunt his soul (Psalms 54:1–7).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 1 Samuel contain two remarkably similar accounts of David sparing Saul?** Compared with the cave at En-gedi (24:1–22), chapter 26 shares obvious parallels: three thousand troops, companions urging David to kill Saul, David refusing to harm the LORD's anointed, and Saul weeping and calling David "my son." Many modern scholars view them as two parallel folk traditions of a single historical episode preserved side by side. Other commentators highlight striking differences: different locations (En-gedi cave versus Hachilah camp), different tokens (a cloak corner versus spear and water jug), different behavior by David (caught by surprise and guilt-stricken over cutting the robe, versus deliberately infiltrating the camp without touching Saul's person), and clear spiritual growth in David after chapter 25's lesson with Abigail. Either way, placing these twin mercies symmetrically around chapter 25 decisively establishes David's innocence before taking the throne.

**Does 26:19 imply the LORD is merely a local deity, forcing exiles to worship foreign gods?** When David condemns foes for driving him from the LORD's inheritance as if saying "Go, serve other gods," he is not denying God's rule over the entire earth. He speaks from ancient covenant reality: public worship, tabernacle, sacrifices, and covenant community belonged strictly to the land of Israel; exile meant being cut off from God's altar and thrust into pagan surroundings (cf. Deuteronomy 4:28). Likewise, when David says "If the LORD has incited you against me, may He accept an offering," he means that if this pursuit reflects God's righteous displeasure over some hidden offense on his own part, proper Mosaic sacrifice can restore peace.

**Abishai and Abner: early sparks of a bloody future feud.** Here David publicly humiliates general Abner for failing to guard the king's spear, while David's night partner is Abishai, brother of Joab (26:6; 26:14–16). Both figures loom large in brutal chapters ahead: Abishai and Joab later ambush and murder Abner at Hebron to avenge their younger brother, bringing David's bitter curse upon Joab's house (2 Samuel 3:27–30). Right here in chapter 26, the hair-trigger ruthlessness of Zeruiah's sons stands in vivid contrast to David's restraint.

**Saul's confession sounds even clearer here—why did David still refuse to return (26:21–25)?** Compared to chapter 24, Saul states his guilt far more plainly: "I have sinned... Surely I have played the fool and erred greatly" (26:21). Yet attentive readers remember Saul already wept, confessed, and blessed David in chapter 24—only to march right back out with three thousand handpicked soldiers to hunt him again. Tellingly, even while inviting David home, Saul never mentions repenting before the LORD or surrendering his jealous obsession. David shows honor without gambling his neck on broken promises.
