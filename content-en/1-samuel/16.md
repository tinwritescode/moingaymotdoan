# 1 Samuel 16 — David Anointed and Playing the Harp for Saul

## Story

After pronouncing Saul's rejection in the previous chapter, Samuel returns home to Ramah still weighed down with grief over the king (15:35). At last the LORD speaks up to rouse the prophet: "How long will you mourn for Saul, since I have rejected him as king over Israel?" God tells Samuel to fill his horn with oil and set out for the house of Jesse in Bethlehem, because He has chosen one of Jesse's sons to be king. Alarmed, Samuel replies that if Saul hears about it, he will kill him. The LORD instructs him to take a heifer along, announce that he has come to sacrifice to the LORD, invite Jesse to the sacrifice, and wait for God to show him whom to anoint (16:1–3).

Obeying, Samuel travels to Bethlehem. Caught off guard by the prophet's sudden arrival, the town elders come out trembling and ask cautiously, "Do you come in peace?" Samuel reassures them that he comes in peace to sacrifice to the LORD, instructing them to consecrate themselves and join him for the sacrifice. He likewise consecrates Jesse and his sons and invites them to the meal (16:4–5).

When Jesse's sons arrive, Samuel takes one look at the eldest, Eliab, and thinks to himself, "Surely the LORD's anointed stands here before Him." Instantly the LORD stops him: "Do not consider his appearance or height, for I have rejected him." God speaks a timeless word to His prophet: "The LORD does not see as man does. For man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart." Next Jesse calls Abinadab and then Shammah to pass before Samuel, yet Samuel shakes his head—the LORD has not chosen either one. In this way seven of Jesse's sons pass before Samuel, and Samuel tells Jesse that the LORD has not chosen any of them (16:6–10).

Samuel asks Jesse whether these are all his sons. Only then does Jesse mention that the youngest is still left, tending the sheep out in the fields. Samuel insists they send for him immediately—nobody will sit down to eat until he gets here. Jesse sends for David. He walks in ruddy, "with handsome eyes and a striking appearance." Straightaway the LORD tells Samuel, "Rise and anoint him, for he is the one." Samuel takes the horn of oil and anoints David right in the presence of his brothers. From that day forward, "the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon David." His mission completed, Samuel rises and returns to Ramah (16:11–13).

At that very juncture, a dark turn unfolds in Saul's palace: "Now the Spirit of the LORD had departed from Saul, and a distressing spirit from the LORD tormented him." Watching the king wracked with dread, his attendants note that a distressing spirit from God is tormenting him and suggest seeking a skilled harpist to play whenever the torment strikes so the king will feel relief. Saul agrees and orders them to find someone who plays well. One attendant speaks up recommending a son of Jesse of Bethlehem he has seen—not only skilled with the harp, but "a mighty man of valor, a warrior, prudent in speech, and handsome; and the LORD is with him" (16:14–18).

Saul sends messengers to Jesse requesting David, the son who is with the sheep. Jesse loads a donkey with bread, a skin of wine, and a young goat, sending them with David as a gift to the king. When David enters Saul's service, Saul loves him deeply and appoints him as his armor-bearer. The king sends word back to Jesse asking to let David stay in his service because he has found favor in his eyes. From then on, whenever the distressing spirit from God comes upon Saul, David picks up his harp and plays; Saul finds relief and feels better, and the distressing spirit leaves him (16:19–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Human eyes measure the outside; God looks straight into the heart.** Even a seasoned prophet like Samuel—fresh off watching Saul's downfall—nearly picked wrong the instant he saw tall Eliab and assumed he must be the next king (16:6–7). Human judgment is easily dazzled by height, status, credentials, and charisma, whereas God weighs what sits inside: a heart that fears and yields to Him (13:14).

**Grief over past loss must not keep us from God's next step.** Samuel genuinely loved Saul and kept mourning as the king he once anointed threw everything away. The LORD's question—"How long will you mourn for Saul?"—does not dismiss his pain coldly, yet lifts him back to his feet to see the new work God is already starting (16:1). A leader can fail and an era can close, yet God's redemptive purpose never hits a dead end.

**God's Spirit is the true lifeline of leadership.** Separated by a single verse, the Spirit of the LORD rushes upon David and departs from Saul (16:13–14). Saul still sits on the throne holding crown, army, and court, yet without God's Spirit his life immediately sinks into darkness and dread. David remains a young shepherd without a single public title, yet because the LORD is with him, even the music of his harp brings peace to a tormented mind (16:18; 16:23).

**God shapes those He chooses through quiet seasons of service.** Being anointed did not launch David straight onto the throne. He returned to the pasture and then entered court to serve the very monarch he was destined to replace, working as musician and armor-bearer (16:19–23). In that humble post, David learned how royal government worked, practiced faithfulness in small duties, and served with respect even toward the man who would soon become his mortal enemy.

## Deeper Meaning

**God repeatedly chooses the last in line to overturn worldly standards.** Inside Jesse's own house, David was so overlooked that his father did not even bother calling him in to the prophetic feast; had Samuel not pressed him, David would have stayed out in the fields with the flock (16:11). That upside-down pattern runs through all Scripture: God chose younger Jacob over firstborn Esau (Genesis 25:23), summoned Gideon from the poorest clan (Judges 6:15), and centuries later chose tiny Bethlehem itself as birthplace of Israel's Ruler (Micah 5:2; Matthew 2:5–6). The New Testament states God's design openly: He chooses what the world considers weak and lowly to shame the strong (1 Corinthians 1:27–28).

**The horn of oil and the shadow of the Anointed One.** When anointing Saul, Samuel used a fragile "flask of oil" (10:1); this time God instructs him to fill a sturdy horn of oil (16:1; 16:13), the same vessel later used to anoint King Solomon (1 Kings 1:39). Anointing coupled with the descent of God's Spirit lays the foundation for the title Messiah—or Christ—meaning the Anointed One. Later Isaiah prophesies a shoot springing from the stump of Jesse, bearing the fullness of the LORD's Spirit of wisdom and might (Isaiah 11:1–2; 61:1). That promise reaches full bloom in Jesus—from David's line, born in Bethlehem (Luke 2:4)—when the Holy Spirit descends upon Him at His baptism to launch His ministry (Matthew 3:16; Luke 4:18).

**The shepherd becomes the template for the true King.** David's shepherd background is no quaint backdrop; it becomes one of Scripture's defining symbols. Later the LORD Himself reminds David that He took him from the pasture, from following the flock, to lead His people (2 Samuel 7:8), and the Psalms celebrate how he shepherded Israel with integrity of heart and skillful hands (Psalms 78:70–72). When later kings neglect their people, Ezekiel promises a Davidic ruler who will become the one true shepherd over God's flock (Ezekiel 34:23), and Jesus declares Himself "the good shepherd" who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11).

**A thread stretching from Ruth in Bethlehem to Jesus' teaching on the heart.** Jesse's household in Bethlehem ties this chapter directly to the book of Ruth: Jesse is the grandson of the Moabite daughter-in-law who left her homeland to cling to the God of Israel (Ruth 4:17; 4:22). Meanwhile, the truth that the LORD sees the heart (16:7) echoes across Scripture: near death, David reminds Solomon that the LORD searches every heart (1 Chronicles 28:9), and Jeremiah affirms that God tests mind and heart (Jeremiah 17:10). In the New Testament, Jesus applies the same principle when warning those who justify themselves before people and urging listeners not to judge by mere appearances (Luke 16:15; John 7:24).

**Watching the Spirit leave Saul becomes David's lifelong warning.** David witnessed firsthand the unraveling of a man once empowered by God's Spirit (10:10; 11:6) after the Spirit departed: before long, the very hand that once cherished David would hurl a spear straight at him while he played the harp (18:10–11; 19:9–10). Reading David's famous psalm of confession years later after his own grievous sin, we understand why his most desperate cry was not to keep his crown, but: "Cast me not away from Your presence; take not Your Holy Spirit from me" (Psalms 51:11).

## Notes & Nuances

**"A distressing spirit from the LORD tormented him"—does God cause evil?** Verse 16:14 is one of the toughest lines in 1 Samuel, especially alongside James's assurance that God cannot be tempted by evil nor does He tempt anyone (James 1:13). First, the Hebrew phrase often rendered "evil spirit" does not necessarily denote moral wickedness; it frequently signifies a harmful, tormenting, or gloomy spirit—much like the spirit of ill will God sent between Abimelech and Shechem or permitted to entice King Ahab (Judges 9:23; 1 Kings 22:20–23). Two complementary lenses have long helped readers here. Theologically, Old Testament authors place every ultimate reality under God's sovereignty, convinced no dark force moves outside His permitted bounds (Job 1:12); once God's Spirit withdrew from a king who rejected Him, this torment served as righteous judgment exposing Saul's inner emptiness. Psychologically, modern readers also recognize Saul's symptoms—deep gloom, paranoia, sudden violent outbursts eased temporarily by music—as resembling severe mental illness, narrated through the spiritual vocabulary of the ancient world.

**Was Samuel's sacrificial errand to Bethlehem a lie?** Some readers feel uneasy seeing God instruct Samuel to say he came to Bethlehem to sacrifice in order to avoid Saul's wrath (16:2–3). Looking closely, Samuel spoke zero falsehood: he genuinely brought a heifer to Bethlehem and genuinely offered a sacrifice with the elders and Jesse's family (16:4–5). He simply withheld his second purpose—anointing David—rather than handing himself and Jesse's household over to a murderous monarch. In biblical ethics, withholding sensitive information from someone bent on killing the innocent is prudent protection, not deceit.

**Did Jesse's family realize David was being anointed king, and why were Bethlehem's elders afraid?** Seeing Samuel arrive unexpectedly, the elders trembled and asked if he came in peace (16:4), likely knowing of his sharp break with Saul or fearing the prophet had come to pronounce judgment on some hidden sin in their town. As for Samuel anointing David "in the presence of his brothers" (16:13), the text never states that Samuel publicly announced kingship. Many commentators think either the household kept total silence to avoid execution by Saul, or the brothers assumed Samuel was inducting David into a prophetic band rather than designating him king. This helps explain why David went straight back to the sheep, why Eliab still scolded him sharply on the battlefield later (17:28), and why years passed before David was publicly anointed over Judah and then over all Israel (2 Samuel 2:4; 5:3).

**Differences between David's portrait in chapters 16 and 17, and the count of Jesse's sons.** Near the end of chapter 16, Saul's servant already praises David as "a mighty man of valor, a warrior" (16:18) and Saul makes him armor-bearer; yet in chapter 17, Saul dismisses him as a mere youth unable to fight Goliath and even asks Abner whose son he is (17:33; 17:55–58). Many scholars suggest 1 Samuel weaves together distinct narrative traditions of David's rise, or arranges David's musical service thematically right after the Spirit departs from Saul rather than in strict chronological order. Readers following a harmonized sequence note that courtly praise in 16:18 fits a brave youth who had fought lions and bears, and that David went back and forth between Saul's court and his father's flocks in Bethlehem (17:15). Similarly, chapters 16 and 17 indicate Jesse had eight sons (16:10–11; 17:12), whereas 1 Chronicles lists seven and calls David the seventh (1 Chronicles 2:13–15); the standard explanation is that one older brother died young without heirs and thus did not appear in the later tribal genealogy.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, directly addressed why Scripture calls the force tormenting Saul a spirit from the LORD. It is called this, he explained, not because God contains evil or originates sin, but because even dark spirits remain subject to His sovereign authority; once God's Spirit left Saul, God employed that spirit as an instrument of righteous judgment upon a stubbornly disobedient king. *(To Simplicianus, Book 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Augustine's direct treatment of the distressing spirit from the LORD in 1 Samuel 16 appears in To Simplicianus Book 2, though exact question number and wording are unchecked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, cited the spirit tormenting Saul to discuss God's sovereignty over evil. Scripture does not picture God watching passively from the sidelines, Calvin argued; He governs and directs even a distressing spirit toward His righteous judicial ends, while all malice and sin belong strictly to the spirit and human actors, never to God. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin cites 1 Samuel 16:14 in Book 2 of the Institutes when explaining God's governance over evil spirits without authoring sin; exact chapter and section unchecked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, explained that God instructing Samuel to bring a heifer to sacrifice in Bethlehem was no license to lie, because Samuel genuinely held the sacrifice while prudently concealing the anointing from a king poised to kill him. He also marveled at God's subtle providence: playing the harp for Saul opened palace doors to David, familiarizing the future king with court life while training him in humility and service. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 16)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's defense of Samuel's sacrifice and praise of providence bringing David into court are characteristic of his commentary on chapter 16; exact text on ccel.org unchecked -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, added a vivid detail to the Bethlehem anointing: Samuel leaned in and whispered privately into David's ear what the anointing meant so word would not leak out. Describing Saul's affliction, Josephus pictured the king suffering choking spells and torment from demons, prompting court physicians to prescribe a skilled musician singing hymns beside him to soothe his mind. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus's details of Samuel whispering to David and physicians recommending music for Saul in Antiquities Book 6 are recalled, though exact chapter and section are unchecked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, saw the entire chapter pivoting on the decisive hinge right at its center, where the Spirit of the LORD rushes upon David and departs from Saul. In his view, David's ascent and Saul's decline stem not from political maneuvering or raw human talent, but from the free, mysterious movement of God's Spirit—with supreme irony as Saul personally invites into his own palace the successor with whom the LORD abides. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: framing chapter 16 around the transfer of the Spirit and the irony of Saul welcoming David into court reflects Brueggemann's First and Second Samuel; page number unchecked -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, highlighted a sly artistic twist right inside the narrative. Immediately after God instructs Samuel not to look at outward appearance because God sees the heart, the narrator welcomes David onto the page by vividly describing his handsome looks, and Saul's attendant promptly praises his striking appearance too. Alter observed that the author deliberately keeps David's inner thoughts veiled at first; the true heart of the future king stays enigmatic, revealing itself only gradually through his actions across the rest of the story. *(The David Story, 1999)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Alter's famous literary observation contrasting 16:7 with David's attractive description in 16:12 in The David Story is well remembered; exact page unchecked -->
