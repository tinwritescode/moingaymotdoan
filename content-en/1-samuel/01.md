# 1 Samuel 1 — Hannah's Tears and the Boy Samuel

## Story

As the chaotic period of the judges draws to a close, the story of Israel's kingdom opens not in a palace, but in an ordinary household in the hill country of Ephraim. A man named Elkanah lives in Ramathaim-zophim with two wives, Hannah and Peninnah. Peninnah has children, but Hannah has none (1:1–2).

Year after year, Elkanah brings his whole family up to Shiloh, where the tabernacle has stood since the days of Joshua (Joshua 18:1), to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of Hosts. At Shiloh, the two sons of the priest Eli—Hophni and Phinehas—are serving as priests of the LORD (1:3).

Pilgrimages that should have been seasons of joy turn instead into a recurring ordeal for Hannah. Whenever Elkanah offers a sacrifice, he gives portions to Peninnah and all her sons and daughters, but to Hannah he gives "a double portion" because he loves her, even though the LORD has closed her womb. That very favor provokes Peninnah all the more. Year after year, whenever they go up to the house of the LORD, her rival taunts her mercilessly to make her miserable. Hannah weeps and refuses to eat. Seeing her grief, Elkanah tries to comfort her: "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" (1:4–8).

Once they finish eating and drinking in Shiloh, Hannah quietly rises and goes to the temple of the LORD, where Eli the priest is sitting on a chair by the doorpost. In deep bitterness of soul, she weeps bitterly, prays to the LORD, and makes a vow: if the LORD of Hosts will look upon the affliction of His maidservant, remember her, and give her a son, she will give him to the LORD for all the days of his life, "and no razor will ever touch his head"—the sign of one set apart for lifelong devotion to God (1:9–11).

Because Hannah keeps praying for a long time, Eli sits watching her mouth. She is praying in her heart; her lips move, but her voice is not heard. Assuming she is drunk, Eli rebukes her: "How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine!" Hannah does not snap back in anger. Calmly she explains that she has drunk neither wine nor strong drink, but has been "pouring out my soul before the LORD" out of great anxiety and grief. Realizing the truth, Eli blesses her: "Go in peace, and may the God of Israel grant the petition you have asked of Him." Asking to find favor in his eyes, Hannah walks away, eats something, and her face is no longer downcast (1:12–18).

Early the next morning, the family rises, worships before the LORD, and returns home to Ramah. Elkanah is intimate with his wife Hannah, and "the LORD remembered her." In due time she conceives and gives birth to a son, naming him Samuel, explaining, "Because I have asked for him from the LORD" (1:19–20).

The following year, Elkanah and his household go up to Shiloh again for the annual sacrifice, but Hannah asks to stay behind until the boy is weaned, so that she may bring him to appear before the LORD and remain there forever. Elkanah agrees. As soon as the boy is weaned—while he is "still young"—Hannah takes him up to Shiloh along with a three-year-old bull, an ephah of flour, and a skin of wine. After they slaughter the bull, she brings the child to Eli and reminds him that she is the woman who stood beside him praying in tears: the LORD granted what she asked, and now she keeps her vow completely—"So now I dedicate him to the LORD; for as long as he lives, he is dedicated to the LORD." And there they worship the LORD (1:21–28).

## Key Takeaways

**Our deepest pain can open the way to our most honest prayer.** Even a loving husband cannot fill Hannah's emptiness, and Peninnah's taunts only rub salt into the wound. Yet instead of retaliating with bitterness or blaming her husband, Hannah carries her grief straight to the doorway of God's house.

**Real prayer does not need to be loud; it only needs an open heart.** Hannah speaks no polished phrases and cannot even form audible words; she simply pours out her soul before the LORD through tears (1:15). Someone sitting right next to her, like Eli, completely misreads her, yet God hears every silent ache inside.

**Peace can arrive even before circumstances change.** Before heading home and before seeing any sign that she will conceive, Hannah leaves the sanctuary, eats, and wears a peaceful face (1:18). Once a burden is placed into God's hands, the heart grows lighter even while the answer still lies ahead.

**Keeping a promise after receiving a blessing is harder than making one in desperation.** People easily vow all sorts of things when desperate, yet once they hold a longed-for child in their arms, few have the courage to let go. Hannah waited for years, yet the moment Samuel was weaned, she took him by the hand up to Shiloh to stay there for life. In the end, children are gifts entrusted to us by God, not private possessions of their parents.

**God begins major turning points with small, overlooked people.** Amid Israel's decline and the corruption of the priesthood at Shiloh, God does not launch a new era through a celebrated warrior. He hears the quiet weeping of a barren village woman, and from her comes the prophet who will anoint Israel's first two kings.

## Deeper Meaning

**A chain of barren mothers across redemptive history.** Hannah's childlessness echoes Sarah (Genesis 11:30), Rebekah (Genesis 25:21), Rachel (Genesis 29:31), Samson's mother (Judges 13:2–5), and later Elizabeth, the mother of John the Baptist (Luke 1:7). The statement "the LORD remembered her" (1:19) closely echoes "God remembered Rachel" (Genesis 30:22). Each time, Scripture shows that the coming child is purely a gift of grace, often inaugurating a new chapter in God's saving plan.

**A lifelong Nazirite vow links Samuel with Samson and John the Baptist.** When Hannah vows that "no razor will ever touch his head" (1:11), she places her son under the Nazirite vow—one consecrated to God who neither cuts his hair nor drinks wine (Numbers 6:1–5). Normally such a vow lasted for a fixed term, yet three major figures are set apart for life from infancy: Samson to begin delivering Israel from the Philistines (Judges 13:5), Samuel to guide Israel into the monarchy, and John the Baptist to prepare the way for the Messiah (Luke 1:13–15).

**The title "LORD of Hosts" appears here for the first time in Scripture.** In 1:3, and on Hannah's lips in 1:11, the Bible first addresses God as "the LORD of Hosts"—the sovereign Commander of every army in heaven and on earth. It is deeply moving that this most majestic title is first spoken aloud in Scripture by a woman weeping quietly at the door of the sanctuary.

**Silent prayer and a poured-out heart.** Hannah's way of "pouring out my soul before the LORD" (1:15) connects directly with the call of the psalmist: "Pour out your hearts before Him" (Psalms 62:8). Centuries later, Jesus likewise teaches His followers to go into their inner room and pray to the Father who sees in secret (Matthew 6:6).

**A thread of grace continuing from the book of Ruth.** Just as the story of Ruth closes with a daughter-in-law praised as "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:15), 1 Samuel opens with a husband comforting his wife by asking if he is not better to her "than ten sons" (1:8). Both stories unfold in the twilight of the judges, where God is quietly preparing the road toward the reign of King David.

## Notes & Nuances

**Polygamy in this story is not endorsed by God.** Scripture honestly records that Elkanah had two wives according to ancient custom, just like Jacob with Leah and Rachel. Yet the narrative openly exposes its bitter fallout: jealousy, favoritism, and endless tears. Moses' law regarding a man with two wives was given only to restrain injustice (Deuteronomy 21:15–17), not to present an ideal model.

**Why is Elkanah called an Ephraimite if Samuel served in the sanctuary?** Verse 1:1 calls his family Ephraimites by region of residence, since the tribe of Levi had no tribal territory of its own and lived scattered among the other tribes. The genealogy in Chronicles reveals that Elkanah and Samuel belonged to the Kohathite branch of the tribe of Levi (1 Chronicles 6:33–38), making Samuel fully eligible to serve alongside Eli.

**Three bulls or a three-year-old bull?** In 1:24, the traditional Masoretic Hebrew text reads "three bulls," whereas the ancient Greek Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls—followed by the BSB and many modern translations—read *a three-year-old bull*, matching the singular in 1:25 ("they slaughtered the bull"). Either way, an offering of a bull, an ephah of flour (over twenty liters), and a skin of wine reflects the generous gratitude of Hannah's household.

**How old was a child when weaned?** In the ancient Near East, mothers typically nursed their children until about two or three years of age. When brought to Shiloh, the boy was "still young" (1:24), yet able to eat solid food and understand simple guidance. Elkanah's words, "Do what seems best to you" (1:23), also align with Moses' law allowing a husband to confirm his wife's vow (Numbers 30:6–8).

**The Hebrew wordplay behind "dedicate" or "lend" in 1:28.** In Hebrew, the words for *asked* (1:17; 1:20; 1:27) and *dedicated* or *lent* (1:28) come from the same root. Hannah does not mean she is merely lending Samuel to God for a while before taking him back; having requested him from the LORD, she now gives him back completely to the LORD for all his life.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople, devoted five full sermons to Hannah's story. He praised Hannah as a master teacher of prayer: she needed no loud shouting, only a heart poured out in silence, because God listens to the cry of the soul rather than measuring the volume of the voice. **The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic compilation of Jewish legal and biblical commentary, treats Hannah's prayer in 1:13 as the blueprint for silent standing prayer (*Amidah*). From her example the rabbis derived four principles: one must direct the heart, form the words silently with the lips, avoid raising the voice to distract others, and never stand to pray while intoxicated. *(Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 31a–31b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Berakhot 31a-31b deriving the laws of Amidah prayer from Hannah is a classic, widely known rabbinic passage -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, observed that Peninnah deliberately chose the most joyful feast of the year to torment Hannah. He urged readers to imitate Hannah by refusing to quarrel and bringing bitterness to God in prayer instead, trusting that whatever is given to God is never lost because He always repays abundantly. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: commentary on Peninnah spoiling the feast and Hannah's prayer and kept vow is characteristic of Matthew Henry, though not checked line by line against the original -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, noted that the transition to monarchy begins not with swords or palaces, but with the tears of a childless woman.
