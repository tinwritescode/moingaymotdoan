# 1 Samuel 18 — Jonathan's Friendship and Saul's Jealousy

## Story

The moment David finishes speaking with Saul after the Valley of Elah, Prince Jonathan's soul becomes knit to the soul of David; he "loved him as himself." From that day on, Saul keeps David at court and no longer lets him return to his father's house. Jonathan makes a covenant with David, stripping off his own royal robe and handing it to his friend along with his tunic, sword, bow, and belt. Wherever Saul sends David on campaigns, he succeeds brilliantly, so the king sets him over the fighting men; both the people and Saul's own officers admire him deeply (18:1–5).

Yet the mood shifts sharply on the victory road home. As the army returns from striking down the Philistines, women pour out from every town of Israel to greet King Saul with tambourines, singing and dancing in joyful refrain: "Saul has slain his thousands, and David his tens of thousands." That song pierces straight into Saul's deepest insecurity. He grows furious, muttering to himself: they have credited David with tens of thousands and me with mere thousands—"what more can he have but the kingdom?" From that day forward, Saul "kept a jealous eye on David" (18:6–9).

The very next day, a distressing spirit from God rushes upon Saul and he raves inside the palace. David plays the harp as usual. Clutching his spear, Saul suddenly hurls it straight at David, thinking to pin him to the wall, yet twice David dodges clear. What unsettles Saul even deeper than the young man's skill is realizing that "the LORD was with David, whereas He had departed from Saul." The king pushes David out of the palace, appointing him commander of a thousand to keep him constantly in combat. Yet in every mission David succeeds because the LORD is with him. Watching this only makes Saul fear him more, while "all Israel and Judah loved David" (18:10–16).

Unable to kill him directly, Saul resorts to plotting. He offers his eldest daughter Merab to David in marriage if only David will fight bravely in the LORD's battles—secretly telling himself, let not my hand be against him, "but let the hand of the Philistines be against him." David replies humbly that neither he nor his father's family amounts to anything worthy of becoming the king's son-in-law. Yet when the promised time arrives, Saul breaks his word and marries Merab to Adriel of Meholah instead (18:17–19).

Another opportunity opens when Saul's second daughter Michal falls in love with David. Delighted at the news, Saul schemes to turn his own daughter's love into a snare sending David to his death at Philistine hands. He has courtiers whisper privately to persuade David to become the king's son-in-law. When David hesitates because he is a poor, humble man unable to pay a royal bride-price, Saul replies that the king desires no bride-price except "a hundred Philistine foreskins," under the pretext of avenging himself on the king's foes while secretly counting on David falling in battle (18:20–25).

David accepts the terms gladly. Before the deadline even arrives, he and his men strike down two hundred Philistines and deliver the full count right before the king. Left with no way out, Saul gives Michal to David as his wife. Yet the marriage brings Saul zero peace. Seeing unmistakably that the LORD is with David and that his own daughter loves him, Saul grows even more afraid and remains "David's enemy continually." Every time Philistine commanders march out to fight, David outperforms every other officer of Saul, making his name even more celebrated across the land (18:26–30).

## Key Takeaways

**Father and son react in opposite ways to the very same David.** Jonathan stood to lose the most if David ascended the throne, yet he joyfully stripped off his royal robe and weapons to hand them to his friend (18:3–4). Saul already held the crown, yet one celebratory song plunged him into envy and attempted murder (18:7–9). When our heart clings tightly to status, anyone's success feels like a threat; when our heart rests in God's will, another's rise brings joy.

**Jealousy never stays still.** This chapter maps out envy's downward staircase step by step: starting with a suspicious glance (18:9), flaring rapidly into a spear hurled in rage (18:10–11), and hardening into cold, calculated traps willing to exploit even his own daughters' marriages as bait (18:17; 18:21). Left unchecked early, jealousy devours both sanity and family love.

**A powerful person's deepest terror is knowing God has left them.** Three times in this chapter Scripture notes that Saul "was afraid of David" (18:12; 18:15; 18:29). A king wielding armies and spears trembled before an unarmed young harpist, because he saw the one reality no throne can buy: the LORD was with David and had departed from himself (18:12).

**God's favor turns every enemy trap into a stepping-stone.** Saul shipped David off to the front lines hoping to see him killed, and the whole nation fell in love with him (18:13–16); he set a deadly bride-price of a hundred foreskins, and David doubled the tally and won higher fame (18:25–30). When God stands with someone, human schemes only put His sheltering hand on clearer display.

## Deeper Meaning

**From Cain's anger to Saul's spear.** Seeing his brother accepted while his own offering was not, Cain grew "very angry, and his countenance fell," letting unchecked fury spiral straight into murder (Genesis 4:5–8). Saul walks the exact same path: recalling Samuel's word that the kingdom would pass to a better neighbor (15:28), he aims all his bitterness at David. Proverbs calls envy "rottenness to the bones" (Proverbs 14:30), while James warns that wherever jealousy and selfish ambition reside, disorder and every evil practice follow (James 3:16).

**Jonathan surrendering his robe foreshadows John the Baptist.** The crown prince taking off his robe, sword, bow, and belt to give to David (18:4) is far more than a friendly gift—it is a voluntary transfer of royal status to God's chosen king. A millennium later, John the Baptist displays the very same noble spirit before Jesus when his disciples fret over losing crowds: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30).

**The refrain "the LORD was with him" links David to Joseph.** The chapter's repeated note that the LORD was with David, prospering everything he did until even his hostile ruler recognized it (18:12; 18:14; 18:28), closely echoes Joseph in Egypt, where the LORD was with him and prospered his work until his pagan master saw it plainly (Genesis 39:2–3).

**Victory songs and the future heartbreak of Michal.** Israel's women stepping out with tambourines to celebrate victory (18:6) carries on the ancient tradition launched by Miriam beside the Red Sea (Exodus 15:20–21). Meanwhile, the tender note that Michal loved David (18:20; 18:28) opens a poignant arc stretching into the next book: years later, peering through a palace window as King David dances before the ark, Michal will despise him in her heart, and this early love will end in bitter estrangement (2 Samuel 6:16; 6:20–23).

## Notes & Nuances

**The song pairing "thousands" and "tens of thousands" was never meant to insult Saul.** In ancient Hebrew poetry, pairing "thousand" in the first line with "ten thousand" in the second was standard intensifying parallelism praising both figures together, just like the poetic couplet "one man chase a thousand, or two put ten thousand to flight" (Deuteronomy 32:30). Saul's own bruised insecurity turned a festive victory chorus into an imagined slight against his crown.

**The friendship between Jonathan and David.** Modern readers occasionally see Jonathan loving David "as himself" (18:1) alongside David's later lament (2 Samuel 1:26) and speculate about romantic attachment. Yet the overwhelming consensus among biblical scholars points out that in the ancient Near East, the verb *love* paired with *covenant* and gifting robes and weapons is standard diplomatic language for covenant loyalty and succession transfer. It depicts profound spiritual kinship grounded in reverence for God's plan, with zero indication of sexual relationship.

**What lay behind the bride-price of "a hundred Philistine foreskins" (18:25–27)?** To modern ears, the demand sounds gruesome. Ancient warriors routinely collected battlefield tokens to prove accurate body counts. Saul specifically demanded foreskins because the Philistines were uncircumcised—guaranteeing the slain men were genuinely Philistines rather than outsiders, while hoping such a suicidal raid would get David killed by enemy swords.

**Differences between the Hebrew text and the Septuagint in chapter 18.** As in chapter 17, the ancient Greek Septuagint transmits a noticeably shorter text of chapter 18, omitting 18:1–5, 18:10–11, the broken promise of Merab (18:17–19), and recording one hundred foreskins delivered rather than two hundred—matching David's later remark (2 Samuel 3:14). Many scholars view the fuller Hebrew text as preserving a richer, more dramatic portrait highlighting Saul's escalating obsession.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth- and fifth-century archbishop of Constantinople, held up Saul's rage after the women's victory song as a stark warning about the venom of envy. David had done Saul zero wrong—on the contrary, he had just rescued the whole realm from humiliation—yet blind jealousy turned a benefactor into a mortal enemy in the king's eyes. *(Homilies on David and Saul, Homily 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Homily 1 of Chrysostom's Homilies on David and Saul famously dissects the poison of envy via Saul and David; exact wording unchecked -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, emphasized for his Greco-Roman audience how dread of losing the throne curdled Saul's initial affection into bitter hatred. Strikingly, when retelling the bride-price for Michal, Josephus replaced "foreskins" with Philistine heads to spare Gentile readers a detail they might find jarring or baffling. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus changing foreskins to heads in Antiquities Book 6 is widely cited in Samuel scholarship, though exact chapter and count are unchecked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, marveled especially at Jonathan's selflessness: by human logic the heir apparent had the sharpest reason to resent a rising star, yet he willingly laid his own cloak and weapons on David. Regarding Saul, Henry remarked that jealousy turns even praise into poison: instead of rejoicing at being lauded for slaying thousands, Saul tortured himself solely because another received more acclaim. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 18)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic Matthew Henry observations on Jonathan's nobility and Saul's reaction to the song in chapter 18; exact text on ccel.org unchecked -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, spotlighted an extraordinary verbal nuance running through chapter 18: the verb *love* carries both personal warmth and political allegiance—Jonathan loves David, all Israel and Judah love David, and Michal loves David, leaving Saul completely isolated. Yet Alter notes the narrator's total silence about David's own feelings: the text never once says whether David loved Michal back, creating an enigmatic reserve around David that lasts throughout the story. *(The David Story, 1999)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Alter's insight on the dual personal/political resonance of "love" and the narrator's silence on David's inner feelings is among the most famous observations in The David Story -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, stressed the striking paradox of power across chapter 18: Saul holds throne and spear, yet three times is described as gripped by fear. That dread flows from an undeniable reality Saul cannot escape: real authority has already shifted away from his palace because the LORD is with David. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: tracing the reversal of power and Saul's recurring fear in chapter 18 is typical of Brueggemann's commentary; exact page unchecked -->
