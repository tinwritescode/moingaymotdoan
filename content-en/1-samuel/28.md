# 1 Samuel 28 — The Dark Night at Endor

## Story

While David is still living in Philistia, the Philistines mass their armies into a huge force to attack Israel. Achish summons David and announces plainly that he and his six hundred men will march out to war alongside him. Caught in an impossible corner, David replies with a cryptic, double-edged line: "Then you will see for yourself what your servant can do." Taking this as full commitment, Achish promises to make David his personal bodyguard for life (28:1–2).

Abruptly the scene shifts to Israel's camp under a heavy cloud. The narrator recalls two crucial facts: the prophet Samuel has died and all Israel has mourned and buried him in Ramah, and earlier Saul himself had expelled all mediums and spiritists from the land. By now the Philistines have encamped at Shunem while Saul gathers all Israel opposite them on Mount Gilboa. One look at the massive Philistine host fills Saul with terror, making his heart tremble violently. He inquires of the LORD, yet heaven stays dead silent: God answers him neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets (28:3–6).

Out of options, Saul orders his attendants to find a woman who is a medium so he can consult her. Immediately they tell him there is one living in Endor. Once night falls, Saul strips off his royal robes, puts on a disguise, and slips away quietly with two companions to her house. He asks her to divine by a spirit and bring up the person he names. Guarded, the woman reminds him how Saul cut off every medium and spiritist from the land, asking why this stranger is laying a trap to get her killed. With bitter irony, the king invokes the living God to guarantee a forbidden séance: "As surely as the LORD lives, no punishment shall come upon you for this" (28:7–10).

Partly reassured, the woman asks whom she should bring up. Instantly Saul answers: "Bring up Samuel for me." Yet the moment she actually sees Samuel appear, she shrieks in terror and spins on Saul for deceiving her—suddenly realizing her disguised guest is the king himself. Hurriedly Saul calms her and asks what she sees. Trembling, she answers, "I see a spirit rising up out of the earth." Pressed to describe his form, she sees an old man coming up wrapped in a robe. Recognizing Samuel at once, Saul bows down with his face to the ground (28:11–14).

Samuel asks why Saul has disturbed him by bringing him up. Saul pours out the despair crushing him: "I am in deep distress, for the Philistines are fighting against me, and God has turned away from me." God no longer answers him by prophets or dreams, so he called Samuel up to tell him what to do. Samuel answers without softening a syllable: why consult me now when the LORD has departed from you and become your enemy? The LORD is simply carrying out what He already declared through Samuel—tearing the kingdom from Saul's hand and giving it to his neighbor David because Saul disobeyed the LORD's command regarding Amalek. Then Samuel pronounces the final sentence: the LORD will deliver both Saul and Israel into Philistine hands, and "tomorrow you and your sons will be with me" (28:15–19).

Hearing that verdict, Saul collapses full-length onto the dirt floor. Terror drains every drop of strength from him, worsened because he has eaten nothing all day and all night. Stepping over and seeing him utterly shattered, the woman speaks gently: your maidservant risked her life to listen to you—now please listen to me and let me set a bit of food before you so you gain strength for the road. At first Saul refuses flatly, yet pressed earnestly by both the woman and his two attendants, he finally pulls himself off the floor and sits on the bed. Quickly she slaughters the single fattened calf kept at her house, kneads flour, and bakes unleavened bread to serve Saul and his men. Having eaten that last supper, they rise and "went away that night" back toward their fateful battlefield (28:20–25).

## Key Takeaways

**God's silence toward Saul was the harvest of a lifetime of turning away from His word.** Watching Saul beg heaven and receive silence naturally stirs pity (28:6). Yet that silence did not drop out of thin air. Saul repeatedly dismissed clear commands from God (28:18), massacred eighty-five priests at Nob so the priest bearing the Urim fled to David (22:18–20), and never once truly repented. Keep stopping your ears to what God has clearly spoken, and a day comes when frantic cries meet silence.

**Fear without humble repentance drives people toward the very evils they once condemned.** Saul himself had enforced Moses' law banishing mediums and necromancers (28:3; Leviticus 19:31). Yet one panic-stricken night saw him creeping in disguise to consult what he had outlawed—even swearing by the living LORD to protect a forbidden séance (28:8–10). Despair unsubmitted to God quickly shreds a person's last remaining principles.

**Seeking God merely to escape trouble differs worlds from seeking God to obey Him.** Saul did not summon Samuel to confess sin or plead for the LORD's mercy; he only wanted tactical advice on beating the Philistines (28:15). Even at rock bottom, he tried sneaking around God through forbidden channels instead of turning back to God Himself—and heard nothing except the very sentence he already knew (28:17–18).

**Even in pitch-black darkness, a flicker of human kindness shines from an unlikely outcast.** Under Moses' law the woman of Endor faced the death penalty, staring right into the face of the monarch who had hunted people like her (28:9; Leviticus 20:27). Yet seeing Saul crumpled and starving on her floor, she cared for him with tender mercy, slaughtering her one prized calf to feed a doomed man without asking a coin in return (28:21–25). Saul's tragedy cuts deeper knowing the final warmth he ever tasted on earth came inside that shadowed room.

## Deeper Meaning

**Samuel's old warning returns with biting irony.** Years earlier, after Saul disobeyed over Amalek, Samuel told him bluntly that "rebellion is like the sin of divination" (15:23). That day Saul tore Samuel's cloak, and Samuel declared the LORD had torn Israel's kingdom from his hand (15:27–28). Tonight at Endor both strands snap together: rebellion has matured into literal divination, and the figure rising from the earth wears that unmistakable old robe once more (28:14).

**The collapse of all three legitimate channels of divine guidance.** Verse 6 names the three ordained means through which the LORD guided Old Testament Israel: dreams, the Urim—the sacred objects set inside the high priest's breastpiece (Exodus 28:30)—and prophets (28:6). Samuel was dead (25:1); Abiathar had carried the ephod to David after the massacre at Nob (22:18–20). Saul's spiritual isolation was no arbitrary cold shoulder from God, but the wreckage of bridges Saul burned himself. Later 1 Chronicles summarizes his death with sobering clarity: he died for his unfaithfulness to the LORD, failing to keep God's word and consulting a medium instead of inquiring of the LORD (1 Chronicles 10:13–14).

**Scripture's consistent warning against consulting the dead.** Moses' law banned mediums, necromancers, and divination outright as detestable to the LORD (Leviticus 20:6; Deuteronomy 18:10–12). Centuries later, Isaiah echoed the same sharp rebuke: shouldn't a people inquire of their own God—"why consult the dead on behalf of the living?" (Isaiah 8:19–20). Humanity has no business hunting guidance in the shadows of death when the living word of God stands open.

**A final supper and footsteps disappearing into night.** Night wraps this entire chapter like a shroud: Saul slips out at night (28:8), hears his death sentence at night, eats unleavened bread in haste, and walks back out "that night" (28:25). That darkness marks more than the clock—it mirrors the soul of a king stripped of heaven's light. A millennium later, John's Gospel strikes the exact same chilling chord as Judas takes the morsel and walks out to betray Jesus: "And it was night" (John 13:30). By morning, Samuel's word will come true in full on Mount Gilboa (31:1–6).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who actually appeared at Endor: Samuel's real spirit or a demon in disguise?** Few passages have sparked more debate across two thousand years of interpretation, falling into three main views. First, many hold it was genuinely Samuel's spirit—summoned not by the medium's occult power, but allowed unexpectedly by God Himself to deliver final judgment; supporting this, the text calls him "Samuel" five times, his prediction comes completely true, and the medium herself screams in shock at something totally outside her control (28:12; 28:15–19). Second—widely held across Reformed tradition—others believe a demonic spirit impersonated Samuel, permitted by God to speak sobering truth as judgment on Saul's disobedience, reasoning dark arts cannot disturb the righteous dead. A third view sees mere trickery by the medium, though this struggles to explain her genuine panic in verse 12 and the exact prediction of next day's deaths. Whether taking the first or second view, all interpreters agree completely: the passage utterly condemns necromancy and never endorses occult practices.

**Why did the medium say she saw "a spirit" (Hebrew *elohim*) rising from the earth (28:13)?** Besides naming the one true God, the ancient Hebrew word *elohim* serves broadly for supernatural beings, heavenly spirits, or departed shades from the realm of the dead. She is not calling Samuel Almighty God; she is gasping at an awesome otherworldly figure emerging from beneath the earth.

**Does "tomorrow you and your sons will be with me" mean Saul went to heaven (28:19)?** We should avoid reading fully developed New Testament concepts of heaven and hell straight into this phrase. In standard Old Testament terms, "with me" simply means joining Samuel in *Sheol*—the realm of the dead where every mortal descends at death. Samuel is announcing that Saul and his sons will die in battle tomorrow, not commenting on Saul's eternal state.

**Why is the Endor night wedged right in the middle of David's Philistine crisis?** Verses 1–2 open the Philistine campaign and trap David under Achish, only to cut abruptly to Saul's nightmare at Endor before returning to David in chapter 29. This deliberate cross-cutting places Saul's spiraling darkness directly beside David's predicament, tightening suspense ahead of the climactic clash on Mount Gilboa.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, paused over Endor with two unforgettable praises. First, he applauded the medium's rare generosity—harboring no grudge against the king who outlawed her craft, she slaughtered her single fattened calf to comfort a doomed monarch without hope of payback. Remarkably, Josephus also admired Saul's stoic bravery: knowing for certain that he and his sons would die the next day, he still marched back to face death beside his people like a king. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus's celebrated praise of both the Endor medium's hospitality and Saul's final courage in Book 6 is one of the best-known passages in Antiquities -->

**Origen**, brilliant third-century Alexandrian theologian of the early Church, devoted an entire homily to chapter 28 defending a straightforward reading: the figure appearing truly was the prophet Samuel, because Scripture calls him directly by name and his words proved accurate. Prior to Christ's death and resurrection opening the way into God's immediate presence, Origen argued, even righteous prophets like Samuel awaited redemption in Sheol, and his appearance that night stood wholly under God's sovereign permission. *(Homilies on 1 Samuel)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Origen's Homily on the Witch of Endor is renowned in patristic history for arguing that Samuel genuinely appeared under God's sovereignty -->

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, wrestled carefully with this difficult chapter in his replies to his old mentor Simplicianus. Leaning initially toward a demonic phantom mimicking Samuel under God's permission to judge Saul—since sorcery holds no authority over saints at rest—Augustine nevertheless conceded cautiously that if Samuel's real soul appeared, it occurred solely by an extraordinary miracle of God, never by the medium's spells. *(To Simplicianus, Book 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's careful discussion in Ad Simplicianum Book 2 Question 3 regarding Endor is widely cited in historical theology -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, underscored the grim irony of Saul swearing by the living LORD to protect a séance forbidden by God's law. Inclining toward the traditional view of a deceptive spirit mimicking Samuel while still governed by God to utter accurate doom, Henry drew a sharp warning from verse 6: those who shut their eyes to God's light while grace is offered risk meeting terrifying silence when crisis arrives. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 28)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's leaning toward a spirit impersonation and comments on Saul's ironic oath in verse 10 are recalled clearly; exact wording on ccel.org unchecked -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, hailed Endor as one of ancient literature's supreme tragic scenes. He pointed to the devastating physical image in verse 20: Saul—once introduced standing a head taller than everyone in Israel—now sprawls his entire massive frame flat on the dirt in terror and exhaustion. Amid such stark collapse, the nameless woman's quiet humanity cooking calf and unleavened bread makes Saul's silent departure into the night in the final verse unbearably poignant. *(The David Story, 1999)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Alter's literary observations on Saul collapsing full-length and the medium's humane meal in The David Story are well remembered; exact page unchecked -->
