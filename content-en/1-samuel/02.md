# 1 Samuel 2 — Hannah's Song and the Corruption at Shiloh

## Story

Right after leaving her little boy at the sanctuary in Shiloh, Hannah lifts up a bold song of prayer. She does not merely give thanks for having a child of her own; she praises the LORD as the one Holy One, the unshakable Rock, the God of knowledge who weighs all human deeds. She sings of a God who turns the world's order upside down: breaking the bows of warriors while arming the feeble with strength, filling the hungry, giving the barren woman seven children, bringing down the arrogant, and lifting the needy from the dust and the ash heap. At the close of her song, she looks far ahead to the day the LORD will judge the ends of the earth, grant strength to His king, and exalt the horn of "His anointed" (2:1–10).

Afterward, Elkanah returns home to Ramah, while the boy Samuel stays behind to minister to the LORD before Eli the priest. Yet the atmosphere at Shiloh darkens immediately. Eli's two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, are "wicked men; they had no regard for the LORD." Whenever anyone brings meat to boil as a sacrifice, their servant comes along with a three-pronged fork, plunges it into the pan or kettle, and claims whatever the fork brings up. Worse still, they demand raw meat for roasting before the fat has even been burned as an offering to God; if a worshiper protests, the servant threatens, "Hand it over right now, or I will take it by force." Their sin is very great before the LORD, because they cause the people to treat the LORD's offering with contempt (2:11–17).

Amid this ugliness, the camera turns gently back to the young boy Samuel, ministering before the LORD wearing a linen ephod. Each year when she comes up for the annual sacrifice, Hannah brings him a little robe she has made with her own hands. Eli blesses Elkanah and his wife, asking the LORD to give them more children in place of the one dedicated to Him. The LORD visits Hannah, and she bears three more sons and two daughters, while the boy Samuel continues to grow in the presence of the LORD (2:18–21).

By now Eli is very old. He hears everything his sons are doing to all Israel—including sleeping with the women who serve at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting. Eli confronts them with mild reproofs—"No, my sons, it is not a good report"—reminding them that if one person sins against another, God may mediate, but if someone sins directly against the LORD, who can intercede for them? Yet Hophni and Phinehas ignore their father's voice, "for it was the LORD's will to put them to death." Immediately after that chilling line, the story shines again with a quiet contrast: "And the boy Samuel continued to grow in stature and in favor with the LORD and with men" (2:22–26).

Then a man of God comes to Eli with a message of judgment. God reminds him that He chose Eli's ancestor back in Egypt to serve as priest, ascend His altar, burn incense, and share in Israel's offerings. Yet Eli's household has scorned His sacrifices, fattening themselves on the choicest parts of Israel's offerings, and Eli has "honored your sons more than Me." The promise that Eli's family would walk before God forever is now revoked, accompanied by a stark principle: "Those who honor Me I will honor, but those who despise Me will be disdained." The strength of Eli's house will be cut off so that no one in it reaches old age, and the opening sign will be that Hophni and Phinehas die on the very same day. In their place, God will raise up a "faithful priest" who will walk before "My anointed one" forever, while any survivors of Eli's family will come begging for a minor priestly post just to get a piece of bread (2:27–36).

## Key Takeaways

**God overturns the world's standards of power.** Hannah's song reaches far beyond her own family to declare how God acts throughout history: "It is not by strength that one prevails" (2:9). Those who grow arrogant because of power, wealth, or status can be brought low, while the weak and needy who rely on God are lifted up by His hand.

**The gravest sin of religious leaders is making others lose heart toward God.** Hophni and Phinehas worked inside the sanctuary every day, yet they "had no regard for the LORD" (2:12). Their greed and immorality did not harm themselves alone; they caused sincere worshipers to grow disgusted with bringing offerings to the LORD (2:17).

**Loving children without firm discipline ultimately harms them and dishonors God.** Eli knew full well what his sons were doing. He spoke words of rebuke, yet stopped at mild scolding and let them remain at the altar. In God's eyes, such leniency gets its true name: "You honor your sons more than Me" (2:29).

**Faithfulness can still grow inside a corrupt environment.** Samuel grew up under Eli's very roof, watching the greed and brazenness of Hophni and Phinehas day after day. Yet the boy was not swept along by the muddy current around him; he kept growing in favor with God and with people (2:26). Surroundings exert strong influence, but they do not have the final word on who we become.

**God never lets Himself be outdone in generosity.** Hannah gave her only son to the LORD, and in return the LORD gave her five more children (2:21). The principle in 2:30 works in both directions: those who wholeheartedly honor God will be honored by Him, while those who exploit sacred privilege to despise Him will surely be brought low.

## Deeper Meaning

**Hannah's song echoes in the song of Mary.** More than a thousand years later, when young Mary of Nazareth learned she was carrying the Savior, she sang a hymn mirroring Hannah's song in both tone and imagery: God looks on the humble, scatters the proud, brings down rulers from their thrones, lifts the lowly, and fills the hungry with good things (Luke 1:46–55; cf. Psalms 113:7–9; James 4:6). Two mothers at opposite ends of biblical history sing of the same God who stands with the lowly.

**The first prophecy to name "His anointed."** In 2:10, long before Israel had any human king, Hannah sings of "His king" and "His anointed." The Hebrew word for *anointed* is *Mashiach* (Messiah), rendered in Greek as *Christ*. This song establishes the central spine of 1 and 2 Samuel—connecting directly to King David's song of thanksgiving near the end of 2 Samuel, where David likewise calls the LORD his Rock and the horn of his salvation (2 Samuel 22:2–3)—and reaches even further forward to the Messiah Himself.

**Samuel's childhood foreshadows the description of young Jesus.** Verse 2:26 notes that Samuel kept growing "in stature and in favor with the LORD and with men." Luke uses almost identical wording to describe the youth of Jesus: "And Jesus grew in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52). Both grew up inside aging religious institutions to usher in a new era.

**The faithful priest points to Zadok and ultimately to Jesus.** The promise of a "faithful priest" (2:35) found an initial echo in Samuel and a clear historical fulfillment when King Solomon removed Abiathar—the last surviving priest of Eli's line—so that Zadok's descendants served before the house of David (1 Kings 2:27). Yet the New Testament shows that no merely human priest endures forever; Jesus Himself is the "merciful and faithful High Priest," holy and permanent forever (Hebrews 2:17; 7:26–28).

## Notes & Nuances

**How did Hophni and Phinehas's meat-grabbing violate the Law of Moses?** God's law explicitly assigned the priests' portion—the breast and right thigh, or shoulder, jowls, and stomach (Leviticus 7:29–34; Deuteronomy 18:3)—and above all required that the fat be burned on the altar to the LORD first (Leviticus 7:31). Eli's sons both greedily thrust their fork in for extra portions and brazenly demanded raw meat with the fat still attached before God received His share, putting their appetites ahead of God.

**Who were the women serving at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting?** Verse 2:22 mentions "the women who served at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting," a phrase already known from the days of Moses (Exodus 38:8). Scripture does not spell out their exact tasks—perhaps they assisted with upkeep, cleaning, or practical care around the sanctuary courtyard. By sleeping with them, Eli's sons dragged the sanctuary at Shiloh down to the level of Canaanite pagan shrines, where sacred prostitution was common.

**Does "for it was the LORD's will to put them to death" (2:25) mean God prevented them from repenting?** Many readers pause at this line. Read in the context of the whole chapter, Eli's sons had persisted in flagrant, brazen sin over a long period with zero desire to turn back. When people deliberately harden themselves beyond the tipping point, God's judgment takes the form of handing them over to the very hardness they have chosen, so that a father's belated warning no longer penetrates their ears.

**The number seven in "the barren woman has borne seven" (2:5) is not a literal count of Hannah's children.** Verse 2:21 records that Hannah bore six children in total (Samuel plus three sons and two daughters). In Hebrew poetry, seven symbolizes fullness and completeness—as in Psalms 113:9, the song uses vivid poetic imagery rather than writing a census ledger.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa in the fourth and fifth centuries, devoted two long chapters of his masterpiece to interpreting Hannah's song and the prophecy against Eli's house. He argued that the song reaches far beyond a mother's private triumph over a rival: Hannah's mention of "His anointed" when Israel had no king is a prophecy of Christ and His Church. In Augustine's view, the downfall of Eli's old priesthood and the promise of a faithful priest find their complete fulfillment in Christ alone. *(The City of God, Book 17, chapters 4–5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Book 17 chapters 4-5 of Augustine's City of God are well known for interpreting Hannah's song and the judgment on Eli's house christologically -->

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, pointed out the deep kinship between Mary's song in Luke and Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2. According to Luther, both songs reveal God's upside-down way of working: He always looks down into the lowest depths of human need, whereas the proud and self-satisfied lock themselves out from His grace. *(The Magnificat, 1521)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Luther's connection between Mary's Magnificat and Hannah's song regarding God looking down on the lowly is well remembered from his 1521 exposition, though exact section unchecked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, stressed that the most dreadful aspect of Hophni and Phinehas's sin was that it made ordinary worshipers despise the LORD's offerings. From Eli's failure he also drew a pointed warning for parents and leaders: when children or subordinates commit open sin and those in authority offer only mild verbal scolding instead of firm restraint, weakness becomes complicity. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasis on making people despise offerings and warning against Eli's weak restraint is characteristic of Matthew Henry; original wording not checked line by line -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, viewed Hannah's song as the theological key for reading both books of Samuel. Together with David's song in 2 Samuel 22, it forms two bookends framing the whole story: the fall of Eli's house, the rejection of King Saul, and the rise of the shepherd boy David all dramatize the God of radical reversals whom Hannah praised from the start. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: framing 1 and 2 Samuel between Hannah's song and 2 Samuel 22 as a theology of reversal is a celebrated central thesis in Brueggemann's commentary -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, praised the deliberate cross-cutting narrative technique in 2:11–26. The narrator intentionally alternates dark scenes of Eli's predatory sons with bright snapshots of little Samuel wearing his linen ephod and receiving his mother's handmade robe each year, allowing the reader to watch with their own eyes the decay of an old dynasty alongside the quiet rise of its successor. *(The David Story, 1999)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: analysis of the alternating contrast between Eli's sons and young Samuel is typical of Alter's literary commentary in The David Story; page number unchecked -->
