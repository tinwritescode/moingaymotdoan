# 1 Samuel 17 — David and Goliath

## Story

After Samuel quietly anoints David in Bethlehem and David enters Saul's court to soothe the king's distressing spirit with his harp (16:12–23), war breaks out again with the Philistines. The Philistines pitch camp on a hillside between Socoh and Azekah, while Saul and the army of Israel camp on the opposite ridge, separated by the Valley of Elah (17:1–3).

Out from the Philistine ranks strides a champion named Goliath of Gath. He stands "six cubits and a span" tall, wearing a bronze helmet, a bronze coat of scale armor weighing five thousand shekels, bronze greaves, and carrying a bronze javelin with a spear shaft "like a weaver's beam," preceded by his shield-bearer. He bellows across the valley challenging Israel to send down one man for single combat: whoever loses, his entire nation will serve the winner. Hearing this, Saul and all Israel are "dismayed and greatly afraid" (17:4–11).

Meanwhile, David's three eldest brothers—Eliab, Abinadab, and Shammah—have gone to war with Saul, while David travels back and forth to Bethlehem to tend his aging father Jesse's sheep. For forty days, morning and evening, Goliath steps out to issue his taunt. One day Jesse sends David with roasted grain and ten loaves of bread for his brothers, plus ten cheeses for their commander, telling him to check on their welfare and bring back word. Early next morning, David leaves the flock with a keeper and sets out. Arriving at the camp, he sees both armies drawn up in battle lines shouting their war cries (17:12–22).

David runs to the front lines to greet his brothers right as Goliath strides forward shouting his usual defiance. Israelite soldiers flee in terror, talking excitedly about the king's promised reward: great wealth, marriage to the king's daughter, and tax exemption for the family of whoever kills the giant. David asks around and burns with indignation: who is this uncircumcised Philistine that he should "defy the armies of the living God?" His oldest brother Eliab erupts in anger, accusing him of arrogance and abandoning a handful of sheep in the wilderness just to watch a battle. David answers mildly that he only asked a question, then turns and asks someone else (17:23–30).

Word of his remarks reaches Saul. Summoned before the king, David speaks calmly: let no one lose heart; he will go fight this Philistine. Saul brushes him off—he is just a boy, whereas Goliath has been a warrior since youth. David recounts his shepherd days: whenever a lion or bear carried off a sheep from the flock, he chased it down, pried the lamb from its jaws, and struck it dead. He declares with quiet certainty: "The LORD who delivered me from the paw of the lion and the paw of the bear will deliver me from the hand of this Philistine." Saul reluctantly agrees (17:31–37).

Saul dresses David in his own tunic, bronze helmet, coat of armor, and sword, yet David can barely walk in them because he is not used to them, so he strips them all off. Taking only his staff in hand, he selects five smooth stones from the streambed, drops them into his shepherd's pouch, grips his sling, and walks toward Goliath (17:38–40).

Seeing a ruddy youth approaching with a stick, Goliath sneers, asking if he is a dog, curses him by his gods, and vows to feed his flesh to the birds and wild beasts. David answers boldly: you come against me with sword, spear, and javelin, "but I come against you in the name of the LORD of Hosts"; today God will hand you over so that all the earth may know there is a God in Israel, and so this whole assembly will know that "it is not by sword or spear that the LORD saves; for the battle is the LORD's" (17:41–47).

As Goliath moves in, David sprints quickly toward the battle line, reaches into his bag for a stone, slings it, and strikes the Philistine square on the forehead. The stone sinks deep into his brow, and Goliath crashes facedown to the ground. Having no sword of his own, David runs up, draws Goliath's own sword from its sheath, kills him, and cuts off his head. The Philistines break and flee; Israel and Judah roar in pursuit to the gates of Gath and Ekron before returning to plunder their camp. David brings Goliath's head to Jerusalem and puts the giant's weapons in his own tent (17:48–54).

The chapter closes with a notable detail: as David walked out to face Goliath, Saul asked his army commander Abner whose son the young man was, and Abner did not know. When David returns victorious holding Goliath's head, Abner brings him before the king. Saul asks him again, and David answers simply, "I am the son of your servant Jesse of Bethlehem" (17:55–58).

## Key Takeaways

**Fear magnifies our enemies whenever we lose sight of God.** For forty straight days, Israel's entire army stared at bronze armor and towering height and trembled. David looked at the exact same giant, yet the first thing he saw was a mortal defying the living God (17:26). When God looms large in our hearts, human intimidation shrinks down to size.

**Great faith is forged in unseen places.** Long before stepping into the Valley of Elah, David practiced quiet faithfulness in remote pastures—heading back to tend his father's sheep, thoughtfully leaving the flock with a keeper before departing (17:20), and risking his life against lions and bears to rescue a single lamb (17:34–36). Steadiness in small duties prepares us for decisive tests.

**The true motive of the fight is God's honor.** The troops chattered about royal prizes, and Eliab imputed conceit to his younger brother (17:25; 17:28). Yet David's burning goal stood far higher: "All the earth will know that there is a God in Israel" (17:46). Victory was meant to exalt God's name, not polish a human reputation.

**God is never limited to the weapons the world counts formidable.** Saul's armor was the finest gear in the kingdom, yet putting it on only slowed David down (17:38–39). God used a shepherd's familiar staff, sling, and smooth stone to prove beyond doubt that the battle belongs to the LORD (17:47).

## Deeper Meaning

**Israel's tallest king stands absent at the front line.** Saul had originally stood out as "a head taller than any of the people" (9:2)—the natural choice to face an enemy giant. Instead he cowered in camp offering prizes for someone else to fight in his place (17:11; 17:25). His armor proving ill-suited to David (17:38–39) quietly signals that the coming dynasty will not rest on outward display, confirming what God told Samuel: "Man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart" (16:7).

**A thread of trust running from the Law to the Psalms.** David's declaration that the LORD does not save by sword or spear (17:47) echoes Jonathan's conviction that God saves by few as easily as by many (14:6) and Moses' assurance that God Himself marches with His people to fight for them (Deuteronomy 20:1–4). Later the Psalms sing that very same confidence: some trust in chariots and some in horses, but God's people trust in the name of the LORD (Psalms 20:7).

**The shepherd risking his life for the sheep points to the Good Shepherd.** A boy willing to snatch lambs out of the jaws of lions and bears (17:34–35) foreshadows Jesus declaring Himself the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep, unlike a hired hand who runs away at the sight of a wolf (John 10:11–12).

**One champion fighting for an entire people—and winning with the enemy's own weapon.** The rules in the Valley of Elah placed an entire nation's fate on one representative (17:8–9); trembling Israelite soldiers watched from the hillside and simply stepped forward into victory once David dispatched Goliath with the giant's own blade (17:51–52). Reading through the New Testament, many see Christ Himself here: stepping out alone for helpless humanity and using death itself on the cross to destroy the one holding the power of death, the devil (Hebrews 2:14–15).

**God chooses the weak to shame the strong.** An unarmored youth toppling a bronze-clad giant vividly embodies the principle Paul later states: God chose what the world calls weak and lowly to put the strong to shame, so that no one may boast in His presence (1 Corinthians 1:27–29).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Saul ask whose son David was when David was already playing the harp in his palace in chapter 16 (16:21–23; 17:55–58)?** This is chapter 17's most famous puzzle. Many scholars note that the ancient Greek Septuagint preserves a much shorter text of chapter 17, omitting 17:12–31 and 17:55–58 altogether, suggesting that the longer Hebrew text combined two complementary accounts of David's early rise. Readers tracing a single continuous storyline suggest Saul needed full family details to honor his pledge of tax exemption and royal marriage (17:25), or that Saul's troubled mind (16:14) combined with David's part-time palace visits (17:15) left the king unable to recall the father's name.

**Goliath's height.** The traditional Masoretic Hebrew text—followed by the BSB and many translations—lists Goliath as "six cubits and a span" tall (17:4), nearly ten feet (close to three meters). Remarkably, several very ancient witnesses—the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and Josephus—read "four cubits and a span," roughly six feet nine inches (over two meters), still towering well above average ancient stature. Joshua likewise notes Gath as a lingering refuge of the famously tall Anakim (Joshua 11:22).

**The sling was a lethal military weapon.** Far from being a child's toy, an ancient sling was a braided leather strap spun at high velocity to hurl fist-sized stones capable of fracturing skulls from afar; ancient armies maintained elite units of slingers. Recognizing this does not diminish David's faith—it shows him placing hard-won skill wholly under God's power.

**The mention of Jerusalem and the question of Elhanan.** Jerusalem still belonged to the Jebusites at this time, so 17:54 reflects a narrator looking ahead to where Goliath's head was eventually brought, while the sword was kept at Nob (21:9). Elsewhere, 2 Samuel 21:19 says Elhanan struck down Goliath the Gittite, whereas 1 Chronicles 20:5 clarifies he killed "Lahmi the brother of Goliath." Many scholars conclude the words "the brother of" dropped out of 2 Samuel in copying; others think an exploit originally credited to Elhanan was later associated with David.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, read this duel as an allegory of Christ defeating the devil: David's staff foreshadows the cross, the five smooth stones recall the five books of Moses though a single stone suffices to fell the foe, and cutting off Goliath's head with his own sword pictures Christ using death itself to conquer the lord of death. *(Sermon 32; Expositions on the Psalms)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Augustine's famous typological reading of David and Goliath in Sermon 32 and his Psalms expositions is well known, though summarized from memory without verifying every wording -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, praised David's conscientious care in returning to tend sheep and arranging a keeper before setting out. Watching David shed Saul's armor, he urged believers never to strap on borrowed weapons ill-suited to them, but to walk into trials trusting the God who has already rescued them before. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 17)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic emphases of Matthew Henry on chapter 17 are recalled clearly, though not checked line by line on ccel.org -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, highlighted the sharp artistic contrast: Goliath lumbers like a walking bronze tank through a dense inventory of armor, whereas David moves swiftly, sprinting straight toward the enemy line. Alter also pointed out that David's very first spoken words in Scripture blend burning zeal for God's honor with crisp practical interest in the king's promised reward. *(The David Story, 1999)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Alter's observations on the bronze-armor contrast and the blend of piety and pragmatism in David's first words in 17:26 are renowned highlights of The David Story -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, argued that Saul strapping his own armor onto David shows the king still thinks entirely within Goliath's categories of military power. Only when David casts that armor off does the contest become a clear theological witness that Israel belongs to the living God. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: viewing Saul and Goliath as sharing the same military paradigm is characteristic of Brueggemann's First and Second Samuel; page number unchecked -->

**Tim Keller**, New York pastor who died in 2023, cautioned against flattening this story into moralistic self-help telling readers to be brave like David and slay personal giants. Instead, Keller emphasized, we are the terrified Israelite soldiers cowering on the hillside, while David—the champion winning victory on behalf of his helpless people—points directly to Jesus Christ. *(Preaching: Communicating Faith in an Age of Skepticism, 2015)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: David and Goliath is Keller's famous flagship example of Christ-centered preaching in Preaching -->
