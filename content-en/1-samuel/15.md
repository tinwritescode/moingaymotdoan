# 1 Samuel 15 — Saul's Disobedience and Rejection

## Story

The previous chapter just summarized Saul's defensive campaigns against Israel's surrounding enemies, including the Amalekites (14:47–48). Now Samuel comes straight to the king with a specific command. He reminds Saul that the LORD sent him to anoint Saul king over His people Israel, and therefore Saul must listen closely to the LORD's words. "The LORD of Hosts" remembers how the Amalekites ambushed Israel on the road when they came up out of Egypt. God commands Saul to attack Amalek and devote everything belonging to them to complete destruction—sparing nothing, putting to death men and women, children and infants, oxen and sheep, camels and donkeys (15:1–3).

Saul immediately musters the troops at Telaim—two hundred thousand foot soldiers plus ten thousand men of Judah—and sets an ambush in the ravine near the city of Amalek. Before striking, he sends word to the Kenites living among the Amalekites urging them to withdraw so they are not swept away alongside them, since their ancestors showed kindness to Israel when coming up from Egypt. The Kenites move away. Saul defeats the Amalekites across a wide sweep from Havilah all the way to Shur, facing Egypt. He puts the people to the sword, yet captures King Agag alive. Even more, Saul and the troops spare the best sheep and cattle, the fat calves and lambs, and everything of value. Unwilling to destroy what looks good, they wipe out only whatever is despised and worthless (15:4–9).

That night the word of the LORD comes to Samuel: "I regret that I have made Saul king, because he has turned away from following Me and has not carried out My instructions." Struck with deep grief, Samuel cries out to the LORD all through the night. Rising early next morning to meet Saul, he hears that the king stopped at Carmel to "set up a monument for himself" before turning down toward Gilgal (15:10–12).

Meeting Samuel at Gilgal, Saul greets him brightly with an air of pious satisfaction: "May you be blessed by the LORD! I have carried out the LORD's instructions." Samuel punctures the boast with a single devastating question: "Then what is this bleating of sheep in my ears and the lowing of cattle that I hear?" At once Saul shifts the blame onto the soldiers: the people brought back the finest animals to sacrifice to "the LORD your God," while everything else they utterly destroyed. Samuel cuts him off and delivers what God spoke the night before. He reminds Saul how small he once was in his own eyes when God made him head of the tribes and anointed him king; why then did he disobey and pounce on the plunder to do evil in God's sight? Still Saul insists he obeyed—wiping out the Amalekites and bringing back King Agag—while the troops merely took the choicest animals from what was devoted to destruction to offer sacrifices to Samuel's God at Gilgal (15:13–21).

Confronted by such evasion, Samuel pronounces a solemn poetic verdict. Does the LORD delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices as much as in obeying His voice? "Behold, obedience is better than sacrifice, and attentiveness is better than the fat of rams," for "rebellion is like the sin of divination, and arrogance is like the wickedness of idolatry." Because Saul has rejected the word of the LORD, the LORD has rejected him as king. Only now does Saul utter a confession: "I have sinned." He admits crossing God's command and Samuel's words because "I feared the people and obeyed their voice," pleading with Samuel to pardon his sin and return with him so he may worship the LORD. Samuel refuses flatly: the sentence stands—the LORD has rejected Saul as king over Israel (15:22–26).

As Samuel turns to walk away, Saul grabs desperately at the hem of the prophet's robe, tearing it right off. Samuel pauses and turns the torn cloth into a living sign: today the LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel away from you and given it to a neighbor better than you. He adds a granite affirmation: "Moreover, the Glory of Israel does not lie or change His mind, for He is not a man, that He should change His mind." Saul confesses a second time, yet his very next plea exposes what truly preoccupies his heart: "Please honor me now before the elders of my people and before Israel; come back with me, so that I may worship the LORD your God." Reluctantly Samuel turns back with him, and Saul bows down to worship the LORD (15:27–31).

Finally, Samuel orders King Agag brought forward. Agag approaches cheerfully, thinking the bitter danger of death has passed. Yet Samuel declares that just as Agag's sword has left mothers childless, so his own mother will now be childless among women; and Samuel hacks Agag to pieces before the LORD at Gilgal. With that done, Samuel goes home to Ramah while Saul returns to his house in Gibeah. Until the day of his death, Samuel never goes to see Saul again, though he grieves deeply over him; and the LORD regrets having made Saul king over Israel (15:32–35).

## Key Takeaways

**Halfway obedience is really disobedience.** Saul raised a huge army, crushed Amalek, and destroyed most of the population and worthless goods. Yet he chose to keep the enemy king alive as a trophy and spared the prime livestock. Selecting the parts of God's command that suited him while ignoring the parts demanding surrender, he still boasted of full obedience—until the bleating sheep and lowing cattle gave everything away (15:13–14).

**Religious ritual can never replace an obedient heart.** Verse 15:22 diagnoses a timeless spiritual disease: piling generous offerings on God's altar hoping He will overlook deliberate disobedience. God is never short on sheep and cattle; what He seeks is a listening heart. Stubborn self-will, even dressed in religious language, stands alongside divination and idolatry in Scripture (15:23).

**Fearing people more than God drags a leader over the cliff.** Saul confessed he violated God's order because he feared the crowd and bowed to their voice (15:24). Even after hearing his throne was revoked, his sharpest panic was not losing fellowship with God, but losing face before the elders and the nation (15:30). Once public approval outweighs God's word, a leader forfeits both backbone and spiritual fitness.

**Saying "I sinned" is not always genuine repentance.** Saul spoke those words twice, yet each time shifted blame to the troops, distanced himself by calling God "the LORD your God," and bargained to salvage his reputation before the crowd (15:24–30). True repentance grieves over wounding God and turns around, rather than merely scrambling when caught red-handed and afraid of losing status.

## Deeper Meaning

**An ancient debt dating back to the Exodus.** The campaign against Amalek was no unprovoked aggression. More than three centuries earlier, when Israel had barely escaped slavery and trudged exhausted through the desert, the Amalekites ambushed their rear ranks, preying on the faint and straggling (Exodus 17:8–16; Deuteronomy 25:17–19). Even then God vowed to blot out the memory of Amalek, yet waited centuries before assigning that judgment to Israel's first king. Conversely, the Kenites—related to Moses through marriage and helpers of Israel after Egypt—were remembered with mercy and spared (15:6; Judges 1:16).

**"Obedience is better than sacrifice" echoes across all Scripture.** Samuel's words in 15:22 are no isolated maxim; they become a central refrain of the Psalms and prophets. David himself sings that the sacrifice God treasures is "a broken spirit," not burnt offerings (Psalms 51:16–17). Isaiah, Hosea, and Micah each remind Israel that God tires of endless sacrifices and desires mercy, justice, and humble walking with Him (Isaiah 1:11; Hosea 6:6; Micah 6:6–8). In the New Testament, Jesus applauds the scribe who recognizes that loving God and neighbor outweighs all burnt offerings (Mark 12:33), and Hebrews finds the ultimate fulfillment in Christ Himself—entering the world to do the Father's will (Hebrews 10:5–9).

**The torn robe previews the transfer of the throne.** Saul ripping the hem of Samuel's cloak (15:27–28) turns into a vivid prophetic sign of a kingdom changing hands, much like the prophet Ahijah later tearing a new cloak into twelve pieces to announce the division of Solomon's realm (1 Kings 11:29–31). Remarkably, not long afterward inside the cave at En-gedi, David secretly slices off the corner of Saul's own robe—and immediately feels conscience-stricken because he refuses to seize the crown from the LORD's anointed by his own hand (24:4–5). Saul clutches power at any cost, while his successor waits for God Himself to hand over the kingdom.

**Lingering fallout from sparing Agag and Amalek.** Saul's disobedience cast a long shadow across generations. Later, surviving Amalekite raiders burn Ziklag and capture David's family (30:1). Ironically, at the end of Saul's life, Samuel's spirit at Endor cites this very Amalek campaign as why God turned from him (28:17–18), and the messenger bringing word of Saul's death—claiming to have finished him off—turns out to be an Amalekite (2 Samuel 1:8–10). Centuries afterward in Persia, the villain plotting to annihilate all Jews is Haman "the Agagite," while the hero who foils him is Mordecai, descendant of Kish the Benjaminite—from King Saul's own family line (Esther 2:5; 3:1).

**Two confessions with identical words and opposite hearts.** Placing Saul's confession here (15:24; 15:30) beside David's confession when Nathan exposes his adultery and murder (2 Samuel 12:13) reveals a stark divide. Saul says "I have sinned" while immediately blaming the soldiers and begging for public face; David says simply, "I have sinned against the LORD," without a single excuse. Paul later draws the sharp line between "godly sorrow" that produces repentance leading to salvation and "worldly sorrow" that brings only death (2 Corinthians 7:10).

## Notes & Nuances

**The command to destroy Amalek completely is one of the hardest passages in the Bible.** Verse 15:3 ordering the death of women, children, infants, and livestock shocks and troubles thoughtful readers today. We should neither dodge nor soften the starkness of the text. Across biblical interpretation, three main approaches have emerged. First, the traditional reading stresses this as a singular judicial sentence enacted by God Himself—Sovereign over all life—after centuries of patience with Amalek's predatory cruelty (Deuteronomy 25:17–19); the Hebrew term *cherem* ("devoted to destruction") strictly barred troops from looting for personal profit, as at Jericho (Joshua 6:17–19), and never served as a license for believers today to use violence. Second, many modern Old Testament scholars note that sweeping lists from old to young reflect standard hyperbole in ancient Near Eastern war inscriptions—indeed, Amalekites reappear in force soon afterward (30:1)—meant to express dismantling an enemy power rather than literal extermination of every individual. Third, some theologians read the passage through progressive revelation: ancient Israel voiced its survival struggles in the harsh holy-war idiom of its era, before Jesus came to reveal the full face of God's love and forgiveness.

**How can God both "regret" and "not change His mind"?** Verses 15:11 and 15:35 say the LORD regretted making Saul king, while 15:29 right between them declares He "does not change His mind, for He is not a man, that He should change His mind." This deliberate pairing is no scribal slip. Verse 15:29, echoing Numbers (Numbers 23:19), affirms God's unwavering integrity and settled verdict: He never goes back on His word or bends before insincere pleading to revoke His judgment. Meanwhile, 15:11 and 15:35 employ deeply personal human language—just as God was "grieved to His heart" before the flood (Genesis 6:6) or relented over Nineveh when it repented (Jonah 3:10)—to convey His genuine sorrow over Saul's betrayal and the turning point in how He deals with him. God is neither mistaken nor caught off guard, yet neither is He cold or unfeeling.

**Why was Saul rejected in chapter 13 and then rejected all over again here?** Comparing the two sentences shows a clear escalation. In chapter 13, after Saul's impatient sacrifice, Samuel announced that "your kingdom will not endure"—meaning his dynasty would not pass down permanently to his descendants (13:13–14). Here in chapter 15, after blatant defiance against Amalek, the judgment strikes Saul personally: the LORD formally rejects him as His recognized king (15:23; 15:26), opening the door for Samuel to anoint a successor in the very next chapter.

**Verse 15:35 says Samuel never saw Saul again, yet they cross paths in chapter 19.** When Saul hunts David at Naioth, he ends up prophesying right in front of Samuel (19:24). Verse 15:35 describes the permanent end of official relationship between prophet and king: Samuel never again visits, counsels, or supports Saul on the throne; their later encounter happens only because Saul invades Naioth chasing David and is overpowered by God's Spirit. Also distinguish Carmel in 15:12, where Saul erected his monument: this is the town of Carmel in southern Judah near the Amalekite border, not Mount Carmel in the north associated centuries later with the prophet Elijah.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, closely examined God's statement that He regretted making Saul king. He explained that divine "regret" bears no resemblance to human remorse over a mistake or lack of foresight. God's nature and eternal counsel never shift; Scripture borrows the word *regret* to express a real change taking place in Saul's own standing and destiny under God's righteous rule. *(The City of God, Book 17)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Augustine's discussion of God regretting Saul's kingship in Book 17 of The City of God is recalled, though exact chapter and wording are unchecked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, likewise set the verses about God regretting and not changing His mind side by side to refute any notion that God wavers like mortal humans. God was never surprised by Saul's sin, Calvin explained; Scripture attributes grief or regret to God by accommodating Himself to familiar human language so our limited minds can grasp how deeply He abhors disobedience. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's discussion of scriptural accommodation regarding 1 Samuel 15 in Book 1 of the Institutes is recalled, though exact chapter and section are unchecked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, noted how Saul's excuses lay bare the reflex of a guilty heart: taking credit for everything admirable while instantly blaming the crowd for wrongs and hiding greed behind pious talk of sacrifices. Samuel's declaration that obedience outweighs sacrifice reminds every generation that outward ceremonies carry zero worth when detached from daily obedience. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's remarks on Saul's blame-shifting and obedience over sacrifice in chapter 15 are characteristic, though exact text on ccel.org is unchecked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, read chapter 15 as a tragic clash between a self-made king—erecting a monument to himself and harnessing religion to serve royal power—and the uncompromising word of the LORD. He drew special attention to how the narrator sandwiches Samuel's stern assertion that God never changes His mind right between two notes of God's own heartfelt grief at the beginning and end, showing that God's relationship with Saul is no icy verdict, but saturated with genuine sorrow. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: highlighting the clash of royal autonomy against God's word and framing 15:29 between 15:11 and 15:35 reflect Brueggemann's commentary; exact page unchecked -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, pointed to the masterful irony threading the dialogue: seconds after Saul solemnly boasts of obeying God's command, the bleating sheep and lowing cattle shred his claim; and three times in a row the king calls God "the LORD your God" rather than his own, unwittingly exposing the widening gulf between himself and the One who chose him.
