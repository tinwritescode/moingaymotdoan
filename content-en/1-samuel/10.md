# 1 Samuel 10 — Saul Anointed and Proclaimed King

## Story

Picking up the exact moment the two men pause at the edge of town at the end of chapter 9, Samuel takes a flask of olive oil, pours it over Saul's head, and kisses him. In the quiet early morning—with no trumpets and no royal court—the aged prophet solemnly declares, "Has not the LORD anointed you ruler over His inheritance?" (10:1).

To convince the stunned young man that this truly comes from God rather than a waking dream, Samuel foretells three signs that will unfold along his journey home. First, near Rachel's tomb on the border of Benjamin at Zelzah, Saul will meet two men announcing that the lost donkeys have been found and that his father has stopped worrying about them and is now frantic about his son (10:2). Walking on to the great tree of Tabor, he will meet three men heading up to God at Bethel—one carrying three young goats, another three loaves of bread, and the third a skin of wine. They will greet him and offer him two loaves of bread, which Samuel instructs him to accept (10:3–4).

The third sign is the strangest of all. Arriving at Gibeah of God—where a Philistine garrison stands—Saul will encounter a procession of prophets coming down from the high place, led by harps, tambourines, flutes, and lyres, prophesying as they go. Right then, "the Spirit of the LORD will rush upon you, and you will prophesy with them; and you will be turned into a different person." Samuel tells him that once these signs come true, "do whatever your hand finds to do, for God is with you." Then he adds a crucial appointment: Saul is to go down ahead of him to Gilgal and wait seven days until Samuel arrives to offer burnt offerings and peace offerings and show him what to do (10:5–8).

The moment Saul turns his back to leave Samuel, God changes his heart, and every single sign comes true that very day (10:9). Reaching Gibeah, he indeed meets a band of prophets. The Spirit of God rushes upon him, and he breaks into prophecy right in their midst. Townsfolk who have known Saul all along stare in disbelief and ask one another, "What has happened to the son of Kish? Is Saul also among the prophets?" A local bystander chimes in, "And who is their father?" From that astonishment a popular proverb is born: "Is Saul also among the prophets?" Once his prophesying ends, Saul goes up to the high place (10:10–13).

Back home, Saul's uncle asks where he and his servant have been. Saul admits they went hunting the missing donkeys and, finding none, went to consult Samuel. His uncle immediately presses him to recount what Samuel said. Saul replies briefly that Samuel told them the donkeys had been found—saying not a single word about the kingship Samuel had announced (10:14–16).

Before long, Samuel summons all Israel to appear before the LORD at Mizpah to establish the king publicly. First he delivers a sobering rebuke from God: the LORD Himself brought Israel up out of Egypt and delivered them from every oppressing kingdom, yet today they have rejected their own God to demand a king over them. Even so, Samuel directs the whole assembly to step forward before the LORD by tribes and clans (10:17–19).

The public selection narrows down round by round before God. Out of all twelve tribes, Benjamin is chosen. Within Benjamin, the clan of the Matrites is chosen. Finally, from the Matrites, the lot falls on Saul son of Kish. Yet when the crowd looks around for their new king, he is nowhere to be found. They inquire of the LORD whether the man has even arrived, and the LORD points straight to him: "Behold, he has hidden himself among the baggage" (10:20–22).

Men hurry over to the baggage and pull him out. Stepping into the middle of the crowd, Saul towers a head taller than everyone else. Samuel presents him to the assembly, pointing out that there is no one like him among all the people whom the LORD has chosen. The whole crowd erupts in a cheer: "Long live the king!" Before dismissing everyone to their homes, Samuel explains the regulations of the kingship to the people, writes them down in a scroll, and deposits it before the LORD (10:23–25).

Saul returns home to Gibeah just as before, with neither palace nor court. Valiant men whose hearts God has touched voluntarily go with him. By contrast, a few worthless men sneer, "How can this man save us?" despising him and bringing him no gifts. Hearing their contempt, Saul keeps completely silent (10:26–27).

## Key Takeaways

**God equips the people He calls.** Back in chapter 9, the young man looking for donkeys seemed timid and passive. In chapter 10, God does not push him toward a throne empty-handed. He gives three exact, detailed signs to steady his faith, sends His Spirit to rush upon him, and gives him another heart (10:6; 10:9). Whenever God hands someone a calling larger than their strength, He Himself supplies the backing to step into it.

**Israel's king never stands above the law.** A priceless detail on coronation day at Mizpah is that Samuel does not simply proclaim a monarch and walk away; he declares the regulations of the kingdom, writes them in a scroll, and places it before the LORD (10:25). Surrounding Gentile kings treated their own word as law, whereas Israel's king ruled under God and remained bound by a written covenant charter.

**Receiving a calling does not automatically erase inner fear.** Already anointed privately by Samuel, confirmed by three miraculous signs, and even moved by God's Spirit to prophesy, the tallest man in Israel still hid among the luggage when his name was called publicly at Mizpah (10:22). Earlier he could not even bring himself to tell his uncle about the kingdom (10:16). His hesitation reveals both an understandable overwhelm under an enormous weight and a lingering insecurity that will haunt him later.

**Wise restraint in the face of scorn.** Mocked openly right at the start of his reign by scoffers who withheld gifts, Saul did not explode in anger or strike back; he held his peace (10:27). A new leader who absorbs petty insults calmly instead of swinging a sword to prove authority lets time and real deeds speak instead—just as Saul will do nobly in chapter 11.

**God's mercy and God's candor walk side by side.** At Mizpah, God still chooses a king for His people and moves valiant men to stand beside Saul (10:24; 10:26). Yet beforehand He has Samuel state without sugarcoating that they are rejecting the very Savior who brought them out of Egypt (10:18–19). God granting a request never implies He approves the ingratitude behind it.

## Deeper Meaning

**From the first "anointed one" to Christ.** Samuel pouring oil over Saul's head in 10:1 is Scripture's very first account of anointing an Israelite king. In Hebrew, the anointed one is *Mashiach* (Messiah), translated into Greek as *Christ* (Psalms 2:2). The oil poured on Saul, and later on David (16:13), points forward to the perfect King whom God anointed not merely with olive oil, but with the Holy Spirit and power—Jesus Christ (Luke 4:18; Acts 10:38).

**Rachel's tomb along the path of a Benjaminite king.** The first sign happens right by Rachel's tomb, the mother who died in childbirth bringing forth Benjamin (10:2; Genesis 35:16–20). Launching Saul's royal journey from the grave of his tribe's matriarch ties Benjamin's painful past to the fresh destiny God is opening for the entire nation.

**The Spirit's empowerment: gifted for office, yet not automatic lifelong faithfulness.** The Spirit of the LORD rushes on Saul so that he is "turned into a different person" (10:6), echoing how God's Spirit came upon the judges to empower deliverance (Judges 3:10). Yet subsequent history shows that empowerment for office can be withdrawn when obedience fails: once Saul disobeys, the Spirit of the LORD departs from him and rests on David instead (16:13–14). The tragedy sharpens in chapter 19, when Saul chases David to kill him and is again overpowered by God's Spirit to lie prophesying on the ground, reviving the proverb "Is Saul also among the prophets?" in humiliation (19:23–24). Ultimately, what every human heart needs is not a passing surge of empowerment for a task, but the lasting new heart promised in the new covenant (Ezekiel 36:26).

**The Philistine outpost and the seven-day appointment at Gilgal.** Skimming 10:5 and 10:8, a reader might mistake them for casual travel details. In reality, the narrator is planting two time bombs for chapter 13. That Philistine outpost near Gibeah is the very garrison Saul's son will strike to ignite war (13:3), and the instruction to wait seven days at Gilgal for Samuel becomes the decisive obedience test Saul fails when he impatiently offers the sacrifice himself (13:8–14). Small instructions on day one decide the fate of an entire dynasty.

**Casting lots before the LORD and the charter of kingship.** Samuel narrowing the choice from tribe to clan to individual (10:20–21) follows the exact pattern Joshua used to expose Achan before the LORD (Joshua 7:14–18). In Israel's faith, the lot was no game of luck, for every decision comes from the LORD (Proverbs 16:33). Meanwhile, the scroll of royal regulations laid before God (10:25) recalls Moses' advance blueprint for Israel's future king: he must not amass warhorses, wives, or silver, and must keep a copy of the Law beside him lifelong to fear God and treat his brothers as equals (Deuteronomy 17:14–20).

## Notes & Nuances

**Does God giving Saul "another heart" (10:9) mean New Testament regeneration?** Not strictly. In Old Testament usage, "heart" embraces mind, resolve, and leadership capacity alongside moral affections. God expanded the inner horizon of a farm boy used to chasing donkeys, giving him courage and regal capacity for public leadership. It equipped him for the royal office without automatically guaranteeing lifelong obedience to God.

**Why did bystanders ask "And who is their father?" when Saul prophesied (10:11–12)?** Neighbors knew Kish's respectable, wealthy family had zero connection to prophetic bands, so seeing Saul swept up in an ecstatic musical troop stunned them. The retort "And who is their father?" carries two main readings. One is dismissive: what respectable pedigree do those wandering prophets have that Kish's son should join them? The other defends God's sovereign gift: prophecy is not inherited by family line—God Himself is the father of prophets, so He can move whomever He chooses, even Saul.

**Why hold a public lot at Mizpah after Saul had already been anointed privately (10:1; 10:20–21)?** The private anointing prepared Saul personally so he knew God's will beforehand. Yet if Samuel had simply marched out a little-known young man from tiny Benjamin and declared him king on his own word, larger tribes like Ephraim or Judah would quickly suspect favoritism. The open lot before all Israel at Mizpah proved publicly that the choice belonged to the LORD, not to Samuel's personal whim.

**Did Saul hide among the baggage out of humility or out of fear (10:22)?** Scripture states the bare fact without dissecting his motives, leaving readers divided ever since. Many classic commentators praised it as genuine modesty from a man not grasping for power. Many modern scholars read it as an early symptom of shrinking from duty—at the very instant of public calling, Israel's tallest man vanishes behind the packs. Both feelings likely mingled: real awe mixed with anxiety before a weight he felt unready to shoulder.

**Are "the regulations of the kingship" in 10:25 the same as "the rights of the king" in chapter 8 (8:11–18; 10:25)?** The Hebrew phrasing is nearly identical, leading some to think Samuel simply restated his earlier warning about royal abuses. Most commentators, however, distinguish the two: chapter 8 warned what pagan-style tyrants seize by force, whereas 10:25 sets out Israel's constitutional charter defining both the royal powers and covenant duties before God and the people, echoing Deuteronomy's law of the king (Deuteronomy 17:14–20).

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, looked kindly on Saul hiding among the baggage at Mizpah. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, carefully clarified what it meant that God gave Saul "another heart" (10:9). In Calvin's view, this was not saving regeneration of the soul, but a special gift of the Spirit granting political wisdom, courage, and royal aptitude to one about to govern. From Saul's case Calvin drew two lessons: no one possesses natural sufficiency to rule unless God supplies grace, yet possessing gifts for office never guarantees enduring fear of God. He also commended Saul's silence in 10:27 as wise self-control in a new ruler. *(Homilies on First Samuel)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: distinguishing vocational equipping from regeneration in 10:6-9 and commending Saul's patience in 10:27 reflect Calvin's Homilies on First Samuel; specific homily unchecked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, lingered over Samuel kissing Saul right after pouring oil on his head (10:1). He called it a kiss of homage and affection: even though Samuel was stepping back from the supreme leadership he had long held, he harbored zero envy and welcomed the new king generously. On 10:27, Henry remarked that anyone entering high office must learn to go "deaf" to petty jeers; winning critics by patience always beats silencing them by force. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's remarks on Samuel's kiss of homage in 10:1 and wise deafness to scorn in 10:27 are well remembered; exact wording unchecked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, highlighted the dramatic push-and-pull running all through chapter 10. In the first half, God personally anoints, confirms, and empowers Saul as future deliverer; in the second half at Mizpah, Samuel opens the public assembly with a sharp indictment that Israel is rejecting God (10:18–19). Precisely because monarchy forever risks usurping God's throne, Brueggemann noted, Samuel wrote down the royal charter and laid it before the LORD (10:25) to bind the king under covenant limits from day one. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: highlighting the chapter's tension between royal endorsement and critique alongside the charter in 10:25 is characteristic of Brueggemann's commentary; page number unchecked -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, spotlighted the subtle irony shaping Saul's portrait here. Fresh off being swept up by God's Spirit, Saul evades his uncle's questions and hides the kingship as though he still cannot quite believe or embrace it (10:14–16). Then at Mizpah the visual irony peaks: the man standing a head taller than every Israelite—physically built to tower above crowds—crouches hidden behind the luggage (10:22–23).
