# 1 Samuel 20 — Farewell in the Field and Three Arrows

## Story

Fleeing from Naioth in Ramah, David comes straight to Jonathan bursting with pent-up distress: what has he done, what wrong or sin has he committed against Saul that the king is hunting his life? At first Jonathan refuses to believe it. He insists his father never does anything great or small without confiding in him, so David surely will not die. David replies with sober clarity: Saul knows how close we are and intentionally kept it from you so you would not be grieved. He swears by the living LORD and Jonathan's own life: "There is only a step between me and death" (20:1–3).

Jonathan promises to do whatever his friend asks. David proposes a way to test the truth. Tomorrow is the New Moon festival, when he is expected to dine at the king's table, yet he will hide in the field until the third evening. If Saul asks why he is absent, Jonathan will explain that David urgently asked permission to hurry down to Bethlehem for his clan's annual sacrifice. If the king replies calmly "Very well," David is safe; if he flies into a rage, then Saul is undeniably bent on killing him. David invokes the covenant they swore before the LORD, telling Jonathan outright: if there is any guilt in me, kill me yourself rather than handing me over to your father (20:4–8).

Jonathan brushes that thought aside and invites David out into the open field away from prying ears. Beneath the wide sky, Jonathan swears by the LORD God of Israel to sound out his father and send David the honest truth—may the LORD deal severely with him if he fails. He prays that the LORD will be with David just as He used to be with Saul. Then, in a striking reversal, the crown prince begs the hunted fugitive for a solemn promise: in the future, when the LORD cuts off every enemy of David from the earth, show me "the loving devotion of the LORD" while I live, and never cut off your kindness from my household even after I die. Loving David as his own soul, Jonathan makes him renew his covenant oath once more (20:9–17).

Next they set up a secret signal. On the third day, David will hide beside the stone Ezel where he hid once before. Jonathan will shoot three arrows beside the stone as though practicing at a target, then send a young boy to fetch them. If Jonathan calls out, "Look, the arrows are on this side of you; bring them here," all is safe and David may return. If instead he shouts, "Look, the arrows are beyond you," David must flee immediately, for the LORD has sent him away. Finally, Jonathan reminds him that the LORD stands as witness between them forever (20:18–23).

When the New Moon arrives, David hides in the field. Inside the hall, Saul takes his accustomed seat against the wall, Abner sits beside him, Jonathan faces him, and David's seat stands empty. On the first day Saul says nothing, telling himself perhaps something happened making David ceremonially unclean and unable to attend. On the second day, however, the chair is still empty. Losing patience, Saul demands of Jonathan why "the son of Jesse" has missed meals two days running. Jonathan delivers the agreed explanation—that David asked leave to attend his family sacrifice in Bethlehem at his brother's urging (20:24–29).

Saul's fury explodes. In front of the whole table he lashes out at Jonathan with vicious abuse—"You son of a perverse and rebellious woman!"—insulting his own son's mother to humiliate him. He blurts out his deepest dread: as long as the son of Jesse lives on this earth, neither you nor your kingdom will ever be established, then roars for Jonathan to bring David in, "for he must surely die!" Jonathan dares ask, "Why should he be put to death? What has he done?" In answer, Saul hurls his spear straight at his own son. Instantly every shred of doubt vanishes. Jonathan leaps up from the table in fierce anger and eats nothing all that second day, deeply grieved over David because his father had shamed his friend so cruelly (20:30–34).

Next morning Jonathan goes out to the field with a small boy at the appointed time. As the lad runs ahead, Jonathan shoots an arrow clean over his head and calls out, "Is not the arrow beyond you?" He shouts for him to hurry and not linger. The boy scoops up the arrow and brings it back clueless; only Jonathan and David understand the code. Wanting one last private moment, Jonathan hands his bow and arrows to the boy and sends him back into the city ahead of him (20:35–40).

As soon as the boy disappears, David rises from the south side of the stone and bows low with his face to the ground three times. The two friends embrace and weep together, and "David wept even more." Jonathan sends his friend off with a brief, weighty blessing—"Go in peace"—repeating the covenant sworn in God's name: "The LORD shall be between me and you, and between my descendants and your descendants forever." Then David rises and departs into exile, while Jonathan walks quietly back into the city (20:41–42).

## Key Takeaways

**True friendship proves itself when loyalty carries a steep cost.** Back in chapter 19, Jonathan only had to speak a courageous word before his father. Here his loyalty costs far more: enduring public humiliation at the royal table, dodging a spear thrown by his own father (20:30–33), and letting go of the throne normally owed to him by birthright. Deep friendship shines brightest not in easy victories, but when we defend the innocent at personal loss.

**Power gripped by fear ends up tearing apart its own household.** Saul yelled at Jonathan that he was defending the crown for his son's sake (20:31), yet one honest question from that very son sent a spear flying at his chest (20:33). When people make power their reason for living, claiming "it's for the family" often masks sheer terror of losing control—and eventually wounds the ones loved most.

**Genuine faith lets us accept God's future without bitterness.** Politically, Jonathan knew Saul was completely right: if David lived, the throne would go to David. Instead of panicking or growing resentful, Jonathan calmly prayed that the LORD would be with David just as He once was with Saul (20:13). Stepping aside before God's sovereign purpose is never weakness; it is the mark of a truly free soul.

**Walking near death teaches us to lean on God's covenant faithfulness.** David enters this chapter acutely aware of human fragility—only a single step separating him from death (20:3). Once every earthly security—palace, wife, royal table—crumbles, the sole anchor he carries into years of exile is a covenant witnessed by the LORD Himself (20:42).

## Deeper Meaning

**From the oath in the field to Mephibosheth's place at the king's table.** Chapter 20 is no moving farewell left forgotten. Years later, after Saul and Jonathan have died in battle and David reigns securely over all Israel, David intentionally asks if anyone remains from Saul's house so he may show kindness for Jonathan's sake (2 Samuel 9:1–3). He brings Jonathan's crippled son Mephibosheth to court, restores all his family lands, and seats him at the royal table for life (2 Samuel 9:6–7). Later still, during a crisis of blood-guilt against Saul's house, David spares Mephibosheth explicitly because of the oath sworn before the LORD right here (2 Samuel 21:7).

**"Loving devotion" rich with covenant weight.** When Jonathan asks David to show "the loving devotion of the LORD" to himself and his descendants (20:14), the Hebrew word *chesed* goes far beyond casual niceness; it means steadfast covenant faithfulness—love that keeps promises even when the other side has nothing left to offer. That is God's signature attribute toward His people, and Jonathan invites David to mirror God's own loyalty in human bonds.

**An enduring portrait of sacrificial friendship in Scripture.** Proverbs declares that a friend loves at all times and a brother is born for adversity (Proverbs 17:17), and speaks of a friend who sticks closer than a brother (Proverbs 18:24). Watching Jonathan step between David and his father's spear also leads New Testament readers toward Jesus' words on the eve of the cross: greater love has no one than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends (John 15:13).

**The LORD stands witness across separation.** Twice in this chapter Jonathan invokes the LORD as witness between both men and both family lines (20:23; 20:42). The wording echoes the ancient parting covenant between Jacob and Laban when neither knew if they would meet again (Genesis 31:49–50). Amid an uncertain exile where no courtroom or contract can protect promises, God Himself stands watch over the faithful. After this morning, the two friends will meet only once more—briefly in the wilderness of Ziph—before death parts them forever (23:16–18).

**The New Moon feast and ritual purity at table.** The banquet where Saul expected David was the New Moon celebration, a solemn monthly sacrifice marked with trumpet blasts under Moses' law (Numbers 10:10; 28:11–15). Because sacrificial meat was holy, anyone ceremonially unclean could not join the feast (Leviticus 7:20–21). Bitterly ironic, Saul carefully ponders ritual cleanliness at the sacred meal (20:26) while nursing cold-blooded murder in his heart at the very same table.

## Notes & Nuances

**Was the fabricated Bethlehem excuse blameworthy (20:6; 20:28–29)?** David asks Jonathan to use a clan sacrifice in Bethlehem as cover for his absence, and Jonathan even adds a detail about an older brother demanding David attend. Scripture reports the episode plainly without praising or condemning it. Commentators historically take two views: many compare it to self-defense against a tyrant hunting innocent blood (like the Hebrew midwives in Egypt or Rahab in Jericho), viewing concealment to save life as understandable; others observe that Scripture never hides the flaws of its heroes, seeing in the deception a glimpse of David's fear when his trust in God flickered.

**Why arrange an elaborate arrow code if the two friends end up meeting face to face anyway (20:18–22; 20:41–42)?** The practical reason is clear: while planning ahead, neither could know whether Saul's spies would be watching the fields that morning. The arrow signal gave them a safe fallback to communicate from a distance if danger hovered nearby. Once morning arrived and Jonathan saw the field was clear, he sent the boy back to town with the weapons so the two could steal a few final moments to say goodbye.

**What does Saul's outburst in 20:30 mean?** Saul's insult—"You son of a perverse and rebellious woman!"—translates a coarse ancient Hebrew curse attacking a son's mother to humiliate the son himself. It says nothing about the character of Saul's wife; rather, it reveals how rage has stripped Saul of both royal dignity and fatherly restraint, lashing out at anyone standing in his way.

**The phrase "David wept even more" in 20:41.** The ancient Hebrew wording here is slightly unusual, prompting some modern translations to render it "until David recovered himself" or, following the Septuagint, "with intense grief." Either way, two seasoned warriors embracing and weeping openly at parting was completely normal in the ancient Near East—tears signaled not weakness, but honest grief as David lost home, wife, and dearest friend to step into years of exile.

**Verse numbering at the close of chapter 20.** In the Masoretic Hebrew text, the final sentence ("Then David rose and departed, and Jonathan went back into the city") is numbered as 21:1. Most English translations—including the BSB—keep it at the end of 20:42 to complete the parting scene, which explains why verse numbers in chapter 21 often differ by one when comparing English Bibles against Hebrew editions.
