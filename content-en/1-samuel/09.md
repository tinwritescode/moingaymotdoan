# 1 Samuel 9 — Lost Donkeys and an Unexpected Encounter

## Story

Leaving behind the tense gathering at Ramah at the end of chapter 8, the story shifts unexpectedly to an ordinary household in the tribe of Benjamin. A man named Kish, a man of standing, has a son named Saul. Scripture introduces Saul with striking outward appeal: he is "an impressive young man without equal among the Israelites—a head taller than any of the people" (9:1–2).

One day, Kish's donkeys stray and go missing. He tells Saul to take one of the servants and go search for them. The two pass through the hill country of Ephraim, the land of Shalishah, the land of Shaalim, and back across Benjamin without spotting a trace of the donkeys. Reaching the land of Zuph, Saul loses heart and suggests turning back, lest his father stop worrying about the donkeys and begin worrying about them instead (9:3–5).

The servant, however, quickly steps in with an idea. In the town right ahead lives a widely respected "man of God" whose every word comes true; perhaps they should ask him to point out the way they should go. Saul agrees, yet worries that their bread sacks are empty and they have nothing left to offer the man of God as a gift. Once again the servant rescues the plan: he happens to have a quarter-shekel of silver on him, enough to present in exchange for guidance. At this point the narrator adds a helpful note for later readers: someone now called a prophet used to be called a "seer" in earlier days (9:6–10).

As the two climb the hill toward the town, they meet young women coming out to draw water. They ask briefly if the seer is in town. The young women answer in an eager rush of detail: yes, he is right ahead of you—hurry up, for today the people are offering a sacrifice at the high place, and nobody will eat until he arrives to bless the sacrifice, so go right up and you will catch him immediately. Sure enough, as they enter the town gate, they run straight into Samuel coming out on his way to the high place (9:11–14).

Only now does the narrator draw back the curtain to reveal the invisible hand arranging everything. The day before, the LORD had quietly told Samuel that at this very hour the next day He would send a man from the land of Benjamin to be anointed ruler over His people Israel, to deliver them from the Philistines. God spoke with tender compassion: "For I have looked upon My people, because their outcry has reached Me." So the moment Samuel catches sight of Saul, the LORD tells him, "Here is the man of whom I spoke to you; he shall rule over My people" (9:15–17).

Meanwhile, Saul has no idea that Israel's most famous prophet is standing right in front of him. He walks up and asks Samuel where the seer's house is. Samuel answers calmly, "I am the seer." He invites Saul to go ahead of him to the high place for the meal, promising to send him off the next morning after telling him everything on his heart. Before Saul can even ask about the donkeys, Samuel assures him that the animals lost three days ago have already been found, then drops a cryptic question: "And upon whom is all the desire of Israel? Is it not upon you and all your father's house?" Startled, Saul protests that he belongs to Benjamin, the smallest tribe of Israel, and his clan is the least of all the clans of Benjamin—why speak to him like this? (9:18–21).

Without explaining further, Samuel brings Saul and his servant into the dining hall among about thirty guests and seats him at the place of honor. He tells the cook to bring out the special portion set aside ahead of time—the leg and the meat attached to it—sets it before Saul, and invites him to eat, saying it was kept specifically for him for this appointed hour. That day, a young man out looking for stray donkeys suddenly finds himself guest of honor to Israel's greatest prophet (9:22–24).

After the feast, they come down from the high place into the town, and Samuel speaks privately with Saul on the roof. At the break of dawn the next morning, Samuel calls Saul up to send him on his way. Reaching the edge of town, Samuel tells Saul to send the servant on ahead while he stays behind alone: "Wait here a moment, so that I may declare to you the word of God." The chapter pauses right on the brink of the anointing that opens chapter 10 (9:25–27).

## Key Takeaways

**God steers history through the most ordinary errands.** Saul did not set out looking for a crown; he tramped across hills for three days hunting his father's missing donkeys (9:3–5). From runaway livestock and a discouraged search, to a servant's quick suggestion, a spare silver coin, and the exact minute of meeting young women by the gate—every seeming coincidence aligned down to the hour with what God told Samuel the day before (9:15–16). In God's hands, no mundane detail is meaningless.

**Even amid human error, God's compassion never runs dry.** In chapter 8, Israel demanded a king out of rejection of the LORD. Yet in chapter 9, when appointing Saul, God speaks with unmistakable tenderness: "I have looked upon My people, because their outcry has reached Me" (9:16). He does not abandon His people to Philistine oppression despite their stubbornness; He still hears their misery and bends even a flawed request toward deliverance.

**An impressive exterior does not guarantee inward maturity.** Right in 9:2, Saul looks exactly like the royal figure the crowd dreamed of in chapter 8: young, handsome, standing a head taller than everyone else. Yet throughout the journey he grows discouraged quickly, waits for his servant to show initiative and supply the silver, and stands face to face with Samuel without recognizing Israel's premier prophet (9:5–8; 9:18). Chapter 9 quietly plants both appealing and troubling traits for Israel's first king.

**Early humility must be guarded throughout life.** When Samuel hints at high honor ahead, Saul's first instinct is modest: he notes that he comes from Israel's smallest tribe and humblest clan (9:21). It is an admirable beginning, yet the chapters ahead will remind us that humility before holding power is far easier than keeping that same spirit once seated on a throne.

**Honor for leaders rests under God's word.** Throughout the banquet, Samuel treats Saul with immense respect—giving him the seat of honor, the choicest cut of meat, unhurried conversation, and a personal send-off at town's edge (9:22–26). Yet in the final verse, Samuel still commands the future king to "wait here a moment, so that I may declare to you the word of God" (9:27). No matter how tall or distinguished, Israel's king always stands beneath the word of God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Echoes of Exodus resound inside the story of kingship.** God's words to Samuel in 9:16—"I have looked upon My people, because their outcry has reached Me"—echo nearly word for word what God spoke to Moses at the burning bush while Israel groaned under Egyptian bondage (Exodus 3:7; 3:9). Though Israel sinned by demanding a king in chapter 8, God still sees them crushed by the Philistines and steps in again as Deliverer. A true king of Israel is never a despot exploiting his subjects, but an instrument of rescue from oppression.

**The smallest tribe and the classic reply of the called.** Saul's protest in 9:21—that he hails from Benjamin, the tiniest tribe in Israel—recalls the haunting scar at the end of Judges, when civil war nearly wiped out Benjamin, leaving only six hundred survivors hiding at the rock of Rimmon (Judges 20:47). At the same time, his hesitant disclaimer mirrors Gideon's words when the angel of the LORD summoned him to deliver Israel: "My clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the youngest in my father's house" (Judges 6:15). God loves starting from unexpectedly small places to remind people that honor flows from His grace.

**From Saul's towering height to God's gaze on the heart in chapter 16.** Readers should keep careful note of Saul standing "a head taller than any of the people" (9:2) to compare it with David's selection a few chapters later. When Samuel sees David's tall oldest brother and assumes he must be God's choice, the LORD immediately corrects him—as though recalling the painful lesson of Saul: "Do not consider his appearance or height, for I have rejected him; the LORD does not see as man does. For man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart" (16:7).

**A living picture of providence from Proverbs.** Saul's winding search for donkeys brings the proverb vividly to life: "A man's heart plans his course, but the LORD determines his steps" (Proverbs 16:9). A young man goes hunting lost family livestock, yet God guides every footstep to the exact gate where the prophet waits and a nation's destiny turns a new page (10:1).

**Meeting young women drawing water outside town.** In Old Testament storytelling, a traveler meeting young women coming out to draw water near a town gate (9:11–13) is a classic scene signaling a pivotal twist of destiny, as when Abraham's servant sought a wife for Isaac (Genesis 24:11–13). Here, that familiar scene leads Saul not to a marriage, but to the life-altering meeting with Samuel.

## Notes & Nuances

**What does "impressive" and "handsome" in 9:2 convey?** The Hebrew words describing Saul (*bachur* and *tov*) describe a prime, striking young man of unusual attractiveness and physical stature. Standing head and shoulders above everyone else, Saul matched the exact heroic mold the people envisioned for their king.

**Was bringing silver to consult Samuel an attempt to bribe a prophet (9:7–8)?** Not at all. In ancient Near Eastern etiquette, visitors never approached an honored elder or man of God empty-handed; they brought a modest courtesy gift—food or a small piece of silver—as a token of respect. A quarter-shekel of silver was a very small sum meant purely as hospitality, not payment for a prophecy, and Samuel never mentions or asks for it.

**Why did Samuel sacrifice at a "high place" when later books condemn high places (9:12–14)?** Later, once Solomon built the Temple in Jerusalem, Scripture strictly forbade scattered sacrifices on hilltop shrines because they quickly became centers of idolatry. In Samuel's day, however, Shiloh's tabernacle had been destroyed and Jerusalem's Temple did not yet exist; 1 Kings explains clearly: "The people, however, were still sacrificing on the high places because a house had not yet been built for the name of the LORD" (1 Kings 3:2).

**What was special about the "leg" Samuel reserved for Saul (9:23–24)?** Under the peace-offering regulations in Leviticus, the right thigh or leg was the prized priestly portion reserved specifically for the officiating priest (Leviticus 7:32–33). By instructing the cook to set aside that choice cut and placing it before Saul in the seat of honor among thirty guests, Samuel signaled unmistakably that this young stranger was being elevated to foremost rank in Israel.

**The cliffhanger at the close of chapter 9.** Verse 9:27 stops right as Samuel bids Saul stand still to hear the word of God. That word and the anointing immediately follow in the opening verse of chapter 10 (10:1). Reading straight across the chapter break preserves the scene's full dramatic momentum.

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, used Kish's stray donkeys as a vivid illustration of God's hidden providence. In human eyes, losing livestock looks like trivial bad luck, yet God employed that mundane mishap to steer Saul straight to Samuel. Calvin also cautioned readers not to be dazzled by Saul's height and good looks in 9:2, since Saul's later reign proved that true leadership never resides in outward stature. *(Homilies on First Samuel)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: lessons on hidden providence through stray donkeys and caution against outward looks in 9:2 are characteristic of Calvin's Homilies on First Samuel; specific homily unchecked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, captured the chapter in a memorable line: Saul went out looking for his father's donkeys and found a kingdom. He commended Saul's filial care for his father's worry (9:5) and his modesty in calling his clan the least (9:21). Yet he shrewdly observed that Saul's failure to recognize Samuel face to face (9:18) hinted that the young man had spent little time seeking out God's prophet beforehand. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's famous quip about seeking donkeys and finding a kingdom plus his note on Saul not knowing Samuel in 9:18 are well remembered; exact lines unchecked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, drew close attention to the emotional shift in 9:16. After all the tension and indictment in chapter 8, readers might expect God to grant a king purely in wrath. Instead, 9:16 breaks into the compassionate vocabulary of Exodus: God has looked upon His people and heard their cry. **Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, praised the subtle characterization woven through chapter 9. Though introduced as standing head and shoulders above everyone else, Saul behaves passively and hesitantly all chapter—failing to find the donkeys, ready to quit early, relying on his servant for both ideas and silver (9:5–8).
