# 1 Samuel 23 — Rescuing Keilah and the Wilderness Chase

## Story

While David is still hiding in the forested hills of Judah, word reaches him that the Philistines are attacking the town of Keilah and looting the threshing floors right at harvest time. Instead of charging off on impulse, David inquires of the LORD whether he should march out against these Philistines. The LORD answers crisply: "Go, attack the Philistines, and save Keilah" (23:1–2).

David's men, however, push back in alarm. They speak candidly: right here in Judah we are already living in constant fear of Saul—how much worse if we march down to Keilah against the Philistine forces? Without berating his troops, David inquires of the LORD a second time. God answers even more reassuringly to steady them: "Arise, go down to Keilah, for I will deliver the Philistines into your hand." Strengthened by that promise, David leads his men down to Keilah, inflicts a heavy defeat on the Philistines, carries off their livestock, and saves the townspeople from plunder (23:3–5).

Here the narrator slips in a vital detail explaining how David receives such clear answers: when Abiathar son of Ahimelech escaped the massacre at Nob to join David, he managed to bring the priestly ephod down with him. Meanwhile, word that David has entered Keilah swiftly reaches Saul. Gloating, the king declares that God has delivered his foe into his hand because David has shut himself inside a town with gates and bars—trapped like a fish in a net. Immediately Saul mobilizes the whole army to march down and besiege David and his men (23:6–8).

Learning that Saul is plotting against him, David summons Abiathar the priest to bring the ephod. Before the LORD, David explains he has heard Saul intends to march down and destroy Keilah on his account, then asks two direct questions: will Saul really come down, and will the citizens of Keilah hand him and his men over to Saul? The LORD answers each clearly: Saul will come down, and the citizens of Keilah will hand them over. At once David and his men—now numbering about six hundred—leave Keilah and move from place to place, going wherever they can. Hearing David has slipped away, Saul calls off his expedition (23:9–13).

David moves out into the wilderness, hiding among rugged strongholds in the hill country of the Wilderness of Ziph. Day after day Saul hunts him relentlessly, yet "God did not deliver him into his hand." During those anxious days hiding at Horesh in the Wilderness of Ziph, knowing Saul is out seeking his life, David welcomes an extraordinary visitor: Jonathan, the king's own son (23:14–16).

Jonathan braves the journey deep into the wilderness to strengthen David's hand in God. He encourages his friend with deeply moving words: "Do not be afraid, for the hand of my father Saul will never find you. You will be king over Israel, and I will be second to you. Even my father Saul knows this." Together the two renew their covenant before the LORD. Then David remains at Horesh while Jonathan walks quietly back home—the very last time they ever see each other alive (23:16–18).

The warmth of that visit has barely faded before fresh betrayal hits. The Ziphites—David's own fellow Judahites—travel up to Gibeah to inform Saul that David is hiding among them in the strongholds at Horesh on the hill of Hachilah, south of Jeshimon. They invite the king to come down whenever he pleases, volunteering to turn David over into his hands. Overjoyed, Saul blesses them in the LORD's name for having "compassion on me." He instructs them to go scout every hiding spot carefully and learn who has seen him, since he hears David is very cunning; once they know for sure, come report back and he will track him down across every clan of Judah (23:19–23).

The Ziphites hurry back ahead of Saul, while David and his men have already slipped southward into the arid Wilderness of Maon. Saul marches hard on their trail. Alerted, David drops down to a rocky crag in the Wilderness of Maon, yet Saul keeps closing in. The chase hits fever pitch: Saul and his army advance along one side of the mountain while David and his men hurry around the opposite side. The net tightens and Saul's troops are moments from surrounding and capturing them when a breathless messenger races up to the king: "Hurry and come, because the Philistines have raided the land!" Forced to break off the hunt, Saul turns back to fight the Philistines. People name that spot the Rock of Escape (literally, Rock of Division), commemorating the hairline instant the two sides parted. Meanwhile David withdraws to the strongholds of En-gedi (23:24–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Seeking God before every move keeps a leader steady under siege.** Across this chapter David never coasts on battlefield instincts. He inquires of God before rescuing Keilah, asks again when his troops falter, and inquires once more when Saul threatens (23:2; 23:4; 23:10–12). Cornered on every side, David's composure flows not from slick cleverness, but from his habit of pausing to listen to God.

**Doing what is right does not guarantee human gratitude.** David risked his neck to rescue Keilah from Philistine raiders and save their harvest, yet moments later they stood ready to surrender their rescuer to Saul (23:5; 23:12). Soon after, the Ziphites—men of his own tribe—volunteered to betray him without even being pressured (23:19–20). Faithful people do good because God calls them to, not to collect IOUs from people.

**The richest gift of friendship is strengthening another's faith in weary hours.** Seasoned warrior though David was, months of living like hunted game wore down even his endurance. Jonathan brought neither reinforcements nor gold into the woods; the treasure he carried was reminding his friend of God's promise and strengthening his hand in God (23:16–17).

**Convenient openings never prove God approves our path.** Hearing David was inside walled Keilah, Saul exulted that God had trapped his enemy for him (23:7). When the Ziphites volunteered to inform, Saul solemnly blessed them in the LORD's name (23:21). People can wrap utterly wicked agendas in pious vocabulary and call convenient circumstances "God's will."

**God has endless ways to deliver right at the eleventh hour.** No lightning bolts split the sky in the Wilderness of Maon. Just as Saul's troops closed around both sides of the ridge, God simply timed a Philistine border raid to yank the king away (23:26–28). God's sovereign hand quietly controls every tick of the clock even when every visible exit seems sealed.

## Deeper Meaning

**Two sharply contrasting portraits of kingship.** Years earlier Israel demanded a king to lead them out and fight their battles (8:20). Ironically, by chapter 23, the sitting monarch Saul leaves the country exposed to Philistine invasion while pouring his entire army into hunting one innocent citizen (23:8; 23:27). Meanwhile David—an outlaw leading a few hundred fugitives—marches out to crush the Philistines and rescue his neighbors in Keilah (23:5). Even without a crown, David already carries the heart of Israel's true shepherd.

**Guidance through the ephod signals the transfer of divine favor.** When Abiathar escaped to David carrying the priestly ephod holding the Urim and Thummim—the sacred means of inquiring of God (Exodus 28:30)—access to God's voice departed Saul's court. Here David inquires repeatedly and receives clear, direct answers every time (23:2; 23:4; 23:11–12). Near the end of Saul's life, when crisis hits and Saul inquires of the LORD, God answers him neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets (28:6). The slaughter at Nob severed Saul from the very light he needed most.

**Betrayal by those he saved and by his own kinsmen foreshadows the Messiah.** Keilah, Ziph, and Maon all stood squarely inside Judah, David's own tribal homeland (Joshua 15:44; 15:55). Yet Keilah's rescued townsfolk would have turned him over, and the Ziphites eagerly betrayed him—and would do so a second time later (26:1). A millennium afterward, David's greater Son walked the exact same bitter road: "He came to His own, and His own did not receive Him" (John 1:11). The Anointed One's path to the throne runs straight through rejection by the very people He came to save.

**Psalm 54 rises straight out of the Ziphites' betrayal.** The ancient heading of Psalm 54 ties it directly to the Ziphites informing Saul where David hid. Strikingly, in that prayer David calls his own Judahite kinsmen "strangers" and "ruthless men" because "they do not set God before them" (Psalms 54:3). Cornered without human allies, he rejects revenge and grips one unshakable anchor: "Surely God is my helper; the Lord is the sustainer of my soul" (Psalms 54:4).

**Jonathan's self-humbling offers a rare picture of reverence for God's will.** As Saul's firstborn heir, Jonathan had every human incentive to fight David for the throne. Instead, right after meeting him he handed over royal cloak and weapons (18:3–4), and now slips into the woods affirming that David will be king while he gladly serves second (23:17). Jonathan stepped back willingly so God's chosen ruler could step forward. Though he fell in battle before that day dawned (31:2), David honored their covenant before God years later by finding Jonathan's son Mephibosheth and treating him with lifelong kindness (2 Samuel 9:1; 9:7).

**An escape route opened right within what we can bear.** That breathtaking split-second separation along opposite slopes at the Rock of Escape vividly illustrates Scripture's promise of God's faithfulness: He will not allow His people to be tested beyond what they can bear, and right inside the trial provides a way of escape (1 Corinthians 10:13). That escape rarely arrives early enough to let us stop trusting, nor ever one heartbeat too late.

## Notes & Nuances

**God said Saul "will come down" and Keilah "will hand you over," yet neither happened—is that a contradiction (23:11–13)?** Not at all; this is a fascinating insight into God's knowledge. God's answer addressed a conditional scenario: *if* David remained inside Keilah, Saul would definitely march down and the townsfolk would surrender him. Precisely because God knew what would freely unfold in that scenario, David escaped in time to avert needless bloodshed. Later theologians frequently cited this passage as clear proof that God knows not only actual past and future events, but also what free human beings would choose under hypothetical conditions.

**Why were the people of Keilah willing to surrender the man who just rescued them (23:12)?** Reading casually, Keilah looks outrageously ungrateful. Placed directly beside chapter 22, however, their terror becomes painfully understandable. Moments earlier, Saul had annihilated the entire town of Nob—priests, women, infants, and livestock—merely because Ahimelech gave David bread and a sword (22:18–19). David himself realized Saul planned to come down and "destroy the city because of me" (23:10). Keilah's citizens did not necessarily hate David; sheer dread of a ruthless tyrant simply overwhelmed gratitude.

**Why does 23:6 mention Abiathar fleeing to David after chapter 22 already reported it?** At the close of chapter 22, Abiathar escapes to David (22:20), yet mid-way through chapter 23 the narrator notes that when Abiathar fled to David at Keilah he brought the ephod along (23:6). This is standard retrospective storytelling in Hebrew prose: chapter 22 finished the Nob tragedy in one sweep by bringing Abiathar safely to David, while 23:6 circles back to add the detail of the ephod right before David uses it to inquire of God (23:9). The first two inquiries in verses 2 and 4 either used that same ephod or came through the prophet Gad (22:5).

**Was Jonathan's statement "I will be second to you" a false prophecy (23:17)?** No. Scripture never presents Jonathan as delivering a prophetic oracle from God. It is the heartfelt, humble wish of a devoted friend trusting God's promise to David and gladly surrendering royal inheritance to stand at his side. Jonathan's later death alongside his father on Mount Gilboa (31:2) makes this forest farewell all the more tender and poignant.

**David's band grew from four hundred to six hundred.** At Adullam's cave, around four hundred gathered around him (22:2); leaving Keilah, his force stands at six hundred (23:13). Even under relentless royal manhunts, David's support never dissolved—instead it grew steadily, especially after his bold rescue of Keilah.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, preaching on Psalm 54—tied directly to the Ziphites betraying David to Saul—paused over David calling them "strangers." Though they shared David's own blood and tribe, spiritually they stood as strangers because they refused to set God before their eyes. **Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, highlighted the bitter contrast at Keilah between David's chivalry and the townspeople's ingratitude. Having just rescued them from Philistine plunder, David faced betrayal simply because they feared Saul's fury. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew rich pastoral lessons from chapter 23. Hearing Saul boast that God had delivered David into a walled trap, Henry warned how easily favorable-looking circumstances fool hearts chasing wrong goals. Watching Jonathan slip into the woods to encourage his friend, Henry called strengthening another's hand in God the noblest act of true friendship, and praised God's exquisite timing at the Rock of Escape—using a Philistine raid at the exact decisive second to rescue His servant. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral comments on Saul's self-deception, Jonathan's friendship, and God's timing at the Rock of Escape are recalled clearly; exact text on ccel.org unchecked -->

**Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, highlighted the razor-sharp irony achieved by pitting Saul's dialogue against the narrator's voice. Saul constantly sprinkles God's name on his lips—declaring God has "delivered" David into his hand and blessing the Ziphites in the LORD's name—yet right mid-chapter the narrator drops one crisp sentence overturning it all: "God did not deliver him into his hand." Repeating that very verb *deliver*, Alter noted, strips bare Saul's delusion: the louder he speaks like God's favorite, the clearer his total disconnect from reality becomes. *(The David Story, 1999)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Alter's literary insight contrasting Saul's use of the verb "deliver" with the narrator's blunt denial in 23:14 fits The David Story; exact page unchecked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, read chapter 23 as a side-by-side audit of royal fitness. While Saul abandons national defense to Philistine raids to obsessively hunt one man, fugitive David listens to God's word and steps up to rescue Keilah. For Brueggemann, Jonathan's brief visit in the woods of Ziph serves as the chapter's theological anchor: ringed by betrayal from Keilah and Ziph, the crown prince's affirmation proves the throne belongs not to whoever controls troops, but to the one carried by God's promise. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: comparing royal fitness and treating Jonathan's forest visit as the theological center of chapter 23 reflects Brueggemann's commentary; exact page unchecked -->
