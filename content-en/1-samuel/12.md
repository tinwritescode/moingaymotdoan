# 1 Samuel 12 — Samuel's Farewell Address

## Story

Following the victory over the Ammonites to rescue Jabesh-gilead, all Israel gathers at Gilgal to "renew the kingdom" and formally confirm Saul as king (11:14–15). Amid that solemn celebration, Samuel—now old and gray-headed—rises to hand over the office of judge. He reminds them that he has listened to everything they asked and set a king over them; from now on the king will lead them, whereas Samuel has walked before them from his youth to this very day (12:1–2).

Standing before the LORD and before His newly anointed king, Samuel invites the entire assembly to testify regarding his lifelong record. He asks bluntly: whose ox or donkey has he taken, whom has he defrauded or oppressed, or from whose hand has he accepted a bribe to shut his eyes to injustice? If anyone has a claim, he will repay it on the spot. Together the people declare that he has never defrauded, oppressed, or taken anything from anyone's hand. Samuel calls the LORD and His anointed as witnesses to that clean record, and the people respond, "He is witness!" (12:3–5).

With his integrity established, Samuel opens a covenant courtroom before the LORD to rehearse all the righteous acts God has performed for Israel. He traces history back to Jacob entering Egypt: when their fathers cried out, God sent Moses and Aaron to bring them out and settle them in this land. Yet whenever they grew comfortable, they forgot the LORD their God, so He sold them into the hands of Sisera commander of Hazor's army, the Philistines, and the king of Moab (12:6–9).

Each time distress hit, the people cried out, confessed forsaking the LORD for Baals and Ashtoreths, and pledged to serve Him again. Mercifully God sent judges—Jerubbaal, Bedan, Jephthah, and Samuel himself—to deliver them so they could live in safety. Yet the instant they saw Nahash king of the Ammonites marching against them, they insisted on having a human king rule over them, forgetting the truth that "the LORD your God was your King" (12:10–12).

Even so, the step has been taken: the king they chose and asked for stands right before them, and the LORD has set him over them. Samuel spells out the life-or-death terms for the nation under this new era. If both people and king fear, serve, and obey the LORD's voice without rebelling against His command, all will go well. Conversely, if they stubbornly rebel, the hand of the LORD will strike against them just as it struck their ancestors (12:13–15).

To drive home the gravity of what they have done, Samuel bids them stand still and watch a mighty sign from God. It is the wheat harvest—the bone-dry season when rain almost never falls—yet when Samuel calls upon the LORD, that very day God sends thunder and pouring rain. Such an eerie storm during harvest terrifies the whole assembly into deep awe of the LORD and Samuel. Frightened, they beg Samuel to intercede so they will not die, confessing aloud that they have "added to all our sins the evil of asking for a king" (12:16–19).

Seeing their terror, Samuel softens his tone to reassure them: "Do not be afraid." He does not brush away the evil they committed, yet urges them not to turn aside from following the LORD out of guilt; instead, serve Him with all their heart and never chase empty idols that cannot profit or rescue anyone. He anchors their hope on the most solid bedrock of all: "For the sake of His great name, the LORD will not forsake His people," because it pleased Him to make them His own. As for himself, though stepping down as judge, he refuses to sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for them, and promises to keep teaching them "the good and right way." Samuel closes with a charge both warm and firm: fear the LORD and serve Him faithfully with all your heart, considering the great things He has done for you; yet if you persist in wickedness, both you and your king will be swept away (12:20–25).

## Key Takeaways

**A faithful leader leaves office with clean hands.** Before transferring authority, Samuel stood before the entire nation and invited anyone to accuse him of seizing an ox, a donkey, or a bribe (12:3). He was not chasing applause in old age; he was guarding the credibility of the word of God he was about to speak, knowing that stained hands empty a rebuke of its force.

**The sin was not merely having a king, but forgetting who was already King.** Retracing history from Moses through the judges, Samuel diagnosed a recurring spiritual ailment: whenever danger loomed, they scrambled for visible human security rather than trusting the LORD who was already their King (12:12). Demanding a king like the nations meant edging God out to the margins in the name of national safety.

**God does not abandon His people even right after a misstep.** When thunder and rain split the harvest sky, the people expected annihilation. Yet Samuel opened a fresh start right inside their imperfect situation: past choices cannot be undone, yet from today forward both king and people can choose obedience and thrive (12:14; 12:20–22). God's faithfulness rests not on human perfection, but on His own great name and gracious choice.

**Failing to pray for others is a sin against God.** Sidelined by the nation in his old age, Samuel had every excuse to walk off in hurt silence. Instead he declared that ceasing to pray for them would be to "sin against the LORD" (12:23). Stepping down from formal office never cancels the calling to love, intercede for, and guide God's people along the right path.

## Deeper Meaning

**Echoes of blameless leaders across both Testaments.** Samuel's public self-examination—taking no one's ox or donkey, wronging nobody (12:3)—closely echoes Moses' defense when Korah rebelled in the wilderness (Numbers 16:15). Over a millennium later, bidding farewell to the Ephesian elders at Miletus, the apostle Paul spoke in the exact same spirit, testifying that he had coveted no one's silver, gold, or clothing (Acts 20:33). At the same time, Samuel's questions set up a quiet, piercing contrast with his warning in chapter 8: Samuel never took anyone's donkey, whereas the king they insisted on will seize their sons, daughters, donkeys, and finest fields (8:11–17).

**A covenant speech continuing Moses and Joshua.** The layout of the Gilgal address—recounting redemption from Egypt, exposing Israel's unfaithfulness, and placing before them the two paths of obedience leading to life or rebellion leading to ruin (12:6–15; 12:24–25)—follows the exact blueprint of Joshua's farewell at Shechem (Joshua 24:14–15). Samuel places Israel's monarchy squarely underneath the Law of Moses, just as Deuteronomy required future kings to write out the Law and fear the LORD lifelong rather than rising above it (Deuteronomy 17:14–20).

**God's own name anchors His people.** When Samuel declares, "For the sake of His great name, the LORD will not forsake His people" (12:22), he touches a golden thread weaving through Old and New Testaments alike. Later, Ezekiel reminds exiled Israel that God acted to preserve them among the nations for the sake of His name (Ezekiel 20:9). In the New Testament, Paul condenses that same assurance into one sentence: even when we are faithless, He remains faithful, for He cannot deny Himself (2 Timothy 2:13).

**Empty things and the hunger for false props.** In his closing exhortation, Samuel warns Israel not to drift after "worthless things" (12:21)—the Hebrew word *tohu* meaning formless emptiness, the same word describing the barren earth before creation in Genesis 1:2. Later prophets such as Isaiah and Jeremiah pick up this exact term to expose the hollowness of idols (Isaiah 44:9; Jeremiah 2:5). Even an earthly monarch, once enthroned in God's place, quickly turns into another empty thing powerless to save.

**Unceasing intercession pointing to the perfect Mediator.** Amid a fickle nation, Samuel binds himself to lifelong prayer on their behalf (12:23). He pioneers the prophetic conscience throughout the age of kings and, beyond that, foreshadows Jesus—who does not intercede merely for one mortal lifetime, but "always lives to intercede" for all who draw near to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25).

## Notes & Nuances

**Nahash in 12:12 and the timeline with chapter 8.** In chapter 8, the elders asked for a king because Samuel was old and his sons took bribes (8:3–5); Nahash king of the Ammonites first enters the narrative in chapter 11 besieging Jabesh. Why then does 12:12 state that seeing Nahash advance prompted them to demand a king? Scholars point to a passage preserved in a Dead Sea Scrolls copy of Samuel (4QSama) and echoed by Josephus right before 11:1, reporting that Nahash had already been terrorizing Israel's eastern tribes beforehand. That background shows Nahash's menace was already brewing when the elders came to Ramah, harmonizing the two chapters seamlessly.

**Who is Bedan in 12:11?** The book of Judges mentions no judge named Bedan. Commentators offer two primary explanations. First, many scholars follow the ancient Greek Septuagint and Syriac versions, which read *Barak*—the commander who defeated Sisera alongside Deborah (Judges 4:6)—suggesting a few Hebrew letters of *Barak* were miscopied as *Bedan*. Second, classic Jewish tradition interprets *Bedan* as shorthand for *Ben-Dan* ("son of Dan"), referring to Samson (Judges 13:2). Samuel naming himself last in the list (12:11) also surprises some readers; while the Syriac and some scholars suspect a scribal slip for Samson, most retain *Samuel* as formal third-person speech suited to a solemn legal assembly.

**Why were thunder and rain during wheat harvest so terrifying?** In Israel, wheat harvest falls in May and June, squarely inside the rainless dry season; Proverbs treats "rain in harvest" as the very symbol of unnatural absurdity (Proverbs 26:1). A sudden summer thunderstorm both threatened to ruin the reaped grain in the fields and demonstrated vividly that the LORD—not the Canaanite storm god Baal—holds total command over sky and harvest.

**Why did Samuel say "my sons are with you" in 12:2?** Because his two sons had just been accused of taking bribes (8:3), the line sounds abrupt at first glance. Many commentators understand Samuel to mean: I am gray and stepping down, and my sons now stand among you as ordinary private citizens—I am not clinging to a family dynasty; if they have wronged anyone, they stand answerable under the law like everybody else.
