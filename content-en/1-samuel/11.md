# 1 Samuel 11 — Saul Rescues Jabesh-gilead

## Story

At the close of chapter 10, worthless scoffers sneered, asking how Saul could ever save anybody. In chapter 11, the new king's chance to answer with action arrives immediately. Nahash the Ammonite marches up and lays siege to Jabesh-gilead, an Israelite town east of the Jordan River. Too weak and isolated to hold out, the men of Jabesh offer terms: make a treaty with us, and we will serve you (11:1).

Nahash, however, wants more than a tributary town; he aims to humiliate the entire nation of Israel. He lays down a brutal condition: he will grant a treaty only if he can "gouge out the right eye of every one of you, and thus bring disgrace on all Israel." Cornered, the elders of Jabesh ask for a seven-day truce to send messengers throughout Israel's territory; if no one comes to save them within seven days, they will surrender. Contemptuous of Israel's fractured weakness, Nahash arrogantly agrees (11:2–3).

Messengers race across the river to Saul's hometown of Gibeah and report the terrifying news in the hearing of the crowd. All the people burst into loud weeping in helpless despair (11:4). Though already proclaimed king at Mizpah, Saul is still farming like an ordinary citizen. Walking home from the field behind his oxen, he asks in surprise, "What is wrong with the people? Why are they weeping?" They recount the desperate plea from Jabesh (11:5).

The moment he hears, the hesitant young man who once hid behind the luggage transforms completely. The Spirit of God rushes upon Saul, and his anger burns fiercely (11:6). Instantly he takes a pair of oxen, cuts them into pieces, and sends the pieces by messengers throughout every region of Israel with a thunderous summons: "This is what will be done to the oxen of anyone who does not march behind Saul and Samuel." The terror of the LORD falls upon the people, sweeping aside tribal divisions so they march out together as one man. Mustering the forces at Bezek, Saul counts three hundred thousand men of Israel and thirty thousand men of Judah (11:7–8).

Saul and the army send the messengers speeding back to Jabesh with a bold promise: "Tomorrow, by the time the sun is hot, you will be delivered." Overjoyed at the news, the men of Jabesh send word out to the Ammonites to lower their guard: "Tomorrow we will surrender to you, and you may do to us whatever seems good to you" (11:9–10).

Certain of easy victory, the Ammonites drop their alertness. Before daybreak the next morning, Saul divides his forces into three companies, crosses the river in a rapid night march, and storms the Ammonite camp during the morning watch just before dawn. Israel strikes them down until the heat of the day, smashing their ranks completely. The surviving Ammonites scatter so thoroughly that no two men are left running together (11:11).

Flushed with triumph, the people remember the scoffers from the end of chapter 10. They turn to Samuel demanding justice: "Who asked, 'Shall Saul reign over us?' Hand those men over so we can put them to death!" (11:12). Yet at the very height of popularity and power, Saul shows extraordinary magnanimity and clear-headedness. He steps in firmly: "No one shall be put to death today, for today the LORD has worked deliverance in Israel" (11:13).

Seeing the people united behind a king who has just displayed both courage and mercy, Samuel proposes, "Come, let us go to Gilgal and renew the kingdom there." The entire nation marches to Gilgal. There, before the LORD, they formally confirm Saul as king and sacrifice peace offerings of thanksgiving to God. Saul and all the people of Israel rejoice greatly—it is the brightest, purest day of Saul's entire reign (11:14–15).

## Key Takeaways

**The suffering of others awakens a true leader.** Hearing the dark report from Jabesh, all Gibeah sat down and wept, whereas Saul burned with righteous indignation and sprang straight into action (11:4–7). Real leadership never waits for a palace or standing army before stepping up; it accepts responsibility the instant neighbors are oppressed, even while walking home behind plow oxen.

**There is a righteous anger sparked by God's Spirit.** Scripture often warns against selfish rage, yet 11:6 explicitly says the Spirit of God rushed upon Saul and his anger burned fiercely. When a cruel bully threatens to gouge out the eyes of the helpless to disgrace God's people, apathy is the real sin. Anger aimed squarely at injustice—driving rescue rather than personal revenge—is anger without sin (Ephesians 4:26).

**God alone truly unites human hearts.** Sending pieces of beef across the countryside did not by itself create a vast army marching as one man; the narrator names the real cause: "The terror of the LORD fell on the people" (11:7). Days earlier at Mizpah, the tribes stood divided between supporters and cynics. Only when God acts through His chosen servant do scattered clans unite as one.

**Mercy is the truest test of a victor.** Answering the taunt "How can this man save us?" (10:27) with a decisive battlefield victory was hard enough; sparing those very mockers afterward was far harder. Saul refused to stain the nation's day of deliverance with retaliatory bloodshed (11:13). Because he gave full credit for victory to the LORD, his heart was big enough to forgive political enemies.

**Healthy leadership honors those who went before.** In his emergency call to arms, Saul did not flaunt his own name alone; he linked himself with the aged prophet: "behind Saul and Samuel" (11:7). After the triumph, he gladly let Samuel lead the nation to Gilgal to renew the kingdom before the LORD (11:14–15). Chapter 11 offers a glimpse of how bright Saul's reign might have been had he kept that humble partnership alive.

## Deeper Meaning

**Healing an ancient wound from the end of Judges.** Readers who know the Old Testament catch their breath at 11:7, recalling one of the darkest horrors in Judges: a Levite carved his murdered concubine into twelve pieces and sent them across Israel to rally a civil war that nearly annihilated Gibeah of Benjamin (Judges 19:29). Right after that bloody war, because Jabesh-gilead failed to attend the assembly, Israel slaughtered the town and gave four hundred surviving young women of Jabesh as wives to the remnant of Benjamin (Judges 21:8–14). Now history reverses itself dramatically: from Gibeah itself, a Benjaminite uses oxen instead of a human corpse to unite all Israel in rescuing Jabesh-gilead—the very town bound by blood and marriage to his tribe!

**A bond of gratitude enduring to the grave.** Saul's rescue of Jabesh in chapter 11 weaves a golden thread of loyalty stretching to the final page of 1 Samuel. Years later, when Saul falls defeated on Mount Gilboa and the Philistines nail his mutilated body to the wall of Beth-shan while Israel scatters, the valiant men of Jabesh-gilead remember today's mercy. They march all night across the Jordan, risk their lives to recover the bodies of Saul and his sons, give them honorable burial in Jabesh, and fast seven days in mourning (31:11–13). King David later sends messengers specifically to praise and bless them for that steadfast loyalty (2 Samuel 2:4–5).

**Saul acts like the last judge before becoming the first king.** Saul's victory in chapter 11 bears every hallmark of the classic judges: called straight from humble farm work by a rush of God's Spirit like Gideon (Judges 6:34), dividing his force into three companies for a pre-dawn ambush just as Gideon routed the Midianites (Judges 7:16; 7:19). God's Spirit demonstrates that Israel's new king will only truly save the people when operating in the LORD's power, not through pagan-style military machinery.

**A day of salvation leaves no room for vengeance.** Saul's generous decree in 11:13—"No one shall be put to death today, for today the LORD has worked deliverance in Israel"—will later echo on King David's own lips in a remarkably similar moment. Returning to Jerusalem after Absalom's rebellion, when Abishai urges executing a man who had cursed him, David stops him with the same spirit: "Should anyone be put to death in Israel today?" (2 Samuel 19:22). At the finest moments of both kings, God's saving grace always pairs with forgiveness.

**Gilgal: where disgrace is rolled away and new chapters begin.** Samuel's choice of Gilgal to renew the kingdom (11:14–15) is no random field. Gilgal was Israel's very first encampment after crossing the Jordan into the Promised Land, where Joshua erected twelve memorial stones (Joshua 4:19–20) and the LORD announced, "Today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from you" (Joshua 5:9). Now at that very Gilgal, Israel celebrates the day God rolls away the disgrace threatened by Nahash the Ammonite and establishes the new reign before the LORD.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Nahash demand gouging out the right eye of every man in Jabesh (11:2)?** Cruel as it sounds, ancient warfare gave this demand cold strategic logic. Soldiers held shields on their left arms, blocking vision on the left side and forcing them to sight battle and aim with their right eye. Removing every man's right eye permanently disqualified Jabesh from armed rebellion while leaving one eye intact for heavy slave labor—and branding all Israel with lifelong public shame.

**Did Nahash's threat occur before or after Israel demanded a king?** Reading straight from chapter 8 to chapter 11, the demand for a king appears first and Nahash appears afterward. Yet in chapter 12, Samuel reminds Israel that seeing Nahash king of the Ammonites marching against them is what panicked them into demanding a king (12:12). Fascinatingly, a Dead Sea Scrolls manuscript from Qumran preserves an opening paragraph right before 11:1 explaining that Nahash had already been terrorizing the tribes of Gad and Reuben east of the Jordan, gouging out right eyes until seven thousand fugitives escaped into Jabesh. Though absent from the traditional Masoretic Hebrew text followed by most standard translations, this background clarifies both why Nahash's menace was already looming in chapter 8 and why he issued that specific threat in 11:2.

**How should we understand the numbers "three hundred thousand of Israel and thirty thousand of Judah" in 11:8?** Modern readers often wonder whether eleventh-century BC Israel could mobilize 330,000 soldiers within days. Some commentators read the figures literally as an extraordinary nationwide turnout. Many scholars point out that the Hebrew word *eleph* ("thousand") can also denote a military squad or clan unit—meaning three hundred Israelite units and thirty Judahite units. Either way, the text underscores two key facts: an unprecedented nationwide response, and an early separate tally for Judah hinting at the future split of the kingdom (1 Kings 12:16–20).

**Why "renew the kingdom" at Gilgal when Saul was already chosen at Mizpah (10:24; 11:14–15)?** Across chapters 9–11, Saul ascends the throne in three deliberate stages: private anointing by Samuel (10:1), public selection by lot at Mizpah despite lingering skeptics (10:20–27), and finally—after proving the Spirit is with him by rescuing Israel—unanimous national ratification at Gilgal in full joy (11:14–15). Rather than contradictory doublets, the sequence traces the steady path from divine promise to popular confirmation.

**Was cutting up the pair of oxen in 11:7 overly violent?** In the ancient world, sending a symbolic token attached to a covenant oath was an established emergency call signaling life-or-death urgency. Tellingly, Saul threatened only the farm livestock of anyone abandoning neighbors facing mutilation—never human life, unlike the grim dismemberment in Judges (Judges 19:29). His decisive shock tactic rallied the nation in time to save thousands in Jabesh.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, gave the earliest surviving explanation for Nahash's savage condition in 11:2. Because warriors carried shields on their left arms obscuring the left eye, blinding the right eye rendered every man of Jabesh permanently unfit for battle. He insisted that not every burst of anger is sinful: when zeal flares from compassion for suffering neighbors and concern for the honor of God's people, it is a righteous indignation stirred by God Himself. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, delighted in the picture of Saul walking home behind his oxen in 11:5. He commended Saul for not lounging in royal vanity after Mizpah, but humbly returning to honest daily labor until God summoned him to act. Henry also compared Nahash's cruel treaty terms to how sin and Satan bargain with human souls: promising easy peace while demanding our eyes so we live in blindness and shame. **Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, viewed chapter 11 as the one brief shining moment when Israel's new monarchy operated exactly as hoped. **Robert Alter**, American scholar of Hebrew literature, traced the masterful literary resonance linking chapter 11 back to the haunting final chapters of Judges. Cutting up the oxen to rally the nation "as one man" (11:7) deliberately evokes the Levite sending pieces of his concubine to spark civil war against Gibeah in Judges 19–20. Yet instead of fratricide, Saul transforms that horrific memory into national solidarity to save Jabesh—the very town deeply intertwined with his own tribe of Benjamin.
