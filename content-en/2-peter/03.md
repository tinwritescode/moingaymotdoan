# 2 Peter 3 — The Promise of Christ's Return and a New Creation

## Story

Peter notes that this is now the second letter he has written to them. In both, his aim is to awaken wholesome thinking by reminding them of the words spoken beforehand by the holy prophets and the command of our Lord and Savior delivered through the apostles (3:1–2).

First of all, they must expect that in the last days scoffers will arrive, driven by their own passions and mocking: where is the promise of His coming? Ever since the ancestors fell asleep, everything has rolled along unchanged since the beginning of creation. Peter exposes what they deliberately ignore: long ago, by God's word the heavens came into being and the earth took shape out of water and through water—and by those very waters the world of that era was flooded and destroyed. By that same divine word, the present heavens and earth are preserved, stored up for fire on the day of judgment and destruction of ungodly people (3:3–7; Genesis 1:6–9; 7:11–23).

Yet do not overlook one vital truth: with the Lord, a single day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years like a single day. The Lord is not dragging His feet on His promise, as some suppose; rather, He is patient toward you, not wanting anyone to perish, but desiring everyone to come to repentance. Even so, the day of the Lord will arrive unexpectedly like a thief. On that day the heavens will pass away with a roar, the elements will dissolve in intense heat, and the earth along with every human work upon it will be laid bare before God's purifying fire (3:8–10; Psalms 90:4; 1 Thessalonians 5:2).

Since everything around us will be dissolved in this way, what kind of people ought believers to be? They should live holy and godly lives as they look forward to and eagerly hasten the coming of the day of God—when the heavens will be set ablaze and the elements melt away. Yet history does not terminate in empty ash: according to His promise, we are waiting for "a new heaven and a new earth, where righteousness dwells" (3:11–13; Isaiah 65:17).

Because they wait for that world, Peter urges them to make every effort to be found spotless, blameless, and at peace when Christ returns. Treat the Lord's patience as salvation—just as our dear brother Paul also wrote to you with the wisdom God gave him. Peter remarks that Paul writes about these matters in all his letters, containing some things hard to understand, which ignorant and unstable people twist to their own ruin, just as they distort "the other Scriptures" (3:14–16).

Forewarned, believers must guard themselves carefully so they are not swept away by the error of lawless people and knocked off their steady footing. Instead, keep growing in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. The letter ends in praise: to Him be glory both now and to the day of eternity (3:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Christ's delay is not forgetfulness, but mercy extending time for repentance.** Scoffers stare at quiet skies, conclude judgment will never arrive, and use that cynicism to justify self-indulgence. Peter flips their skepticism on its head: the waiting period is God's patient mercy giving people room to turn back (3:3–9).

**Hope for the end of the age reshapes how we live today.** Knowing that every temporary monument of this world will pass away never breeds despair or apathy; it spurs believers toward holiness, peace, and integrity as they prepare for a renewed world where justice makes its home (3:11–14).

**Steady growth in grace is the strongest defense against false teaching.** The closing verse summarizes the whole epistle: believers do not stay safe merely by dodging error, but by continually growing deeper in the grace and knowledge of Jesus Christ (3:17–18).

## Deeper Meaning

**A day like a thousand years from the prayer of Moses.** Writing that with the Lord "a day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years are like a day" (3:8), Peter echoes Psalm 90:4—Moses' meditation on God's eternity over against our brief human lifespan. The Creator's calendar never runs on human impatience.

**A new heaven and a new earth linking Isaiah to Revelation.** The promise of "a new heaven and a new earth, where righteousness dwells" (3:13) draws from Isaiah (Isaiah 65:17; 66:22) and blooms into the grand finale of Scripture in Revelation 21:1. Judgment fire does not erase creation into nothingness; it purges the world of evil to inaugurate a perfected creation.

**Paul's letters ranked side by side with Scripture.** Verses 3:15–16 mark a remarkable milestone in New Testament history: Peter speaks of "all" Paul's letters as a collection already circulating among churches and groups them directly with "the other Scriptures." Right inside the first century, the church already recognized scriptural authority in apostolic writings.

## Notes & Nuances

**Ancient manuscripts differ at the final verb of verse 3:10.** The earliest manuscripts—followed by the BSB ("will be laid bare") and most modern translations—say the earth and its works will be "found," meaning exposed and laid bare before God's scrutiny, rather than "burned up" as later manuscripts and older translations such as the KJV read. Either way, the central picture remains God's thorough purification ushering in a new heaven and new earth (3:12–13).

**"Hastening" the day of God (3:12) carries an active note.** The Greek verb means not merely waiting eagerly, but helping speed its arrival. Read beside verse 3:9—where God waits for people to repent—many commentators see the church's holy living, prayer, and gospel witness as participating in preparing for the Lord's return.

**Peter freely admits that even Paul's letters contain "some things hard to understand" (3:16).** That refreshingly honest remark reassures readers today that wrestling with difficult passages is completely normal—even the apostle Peter found parts of Paul challenging. The real danger is never encountering tough texts, but twisting them to excuse ungodly living.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fifth-century bishop of Hippo, examined verses 3:10–13 and reasoned that the fire at the end of the age will not annihilate the substance of the world into nothingness, but burn away its corruptible qualities so the old creation is transformed into a finer new heaven and new earth, much as fire purifies gold. *(City of God, Book 20, Chapter 16)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: in City of God 20.16 Augustine directly discusses 2 Peter 3 and argues for fiery renewal of creation rather than annihilation -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, admired the harmony between the two apostles in verses 3:15–16: although Paul had publicly rebuked Peter at Antioch (Galatians 2:11–14), here Peter warmly calls Paul his beloved brother and honors the wisdom of his writings. Calvin held this up as a noble pattern of humility and unity among church leaders. *(Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, on 2 Peter chapter 3 verse 15)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's contrast between Paul's rebuke in Galatians 2 and Peter's affectionate praise in 2 Peter 3:15 is a well-known point in his commentary -->

**Richard Bauckham**, British New Testament scholar, unpacks the scoffers' logic in verse 3:4: they were not merely frustrated by a wait, they were denying that God intervenes in history at all—assuming a closed, unchanging world running on autopilot. Peter counters with two seismic historical interventions: creation and the Flood. *(Jude, 2 Peter, Word Biblical Commentary, 1983, on 2 Peter chapter 3 verses 4–7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Bauckham's analysis of the scoffers' uniformitarian denial of divine intervention in 2 Peter 3:4–7 is central to his commentary on chapter 3 -->
