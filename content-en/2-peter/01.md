# 2 Peter 1 — Sharing the Divine Nature and the Sure Prophetic Word

## Story

Simon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ, addresses those who have received a faith just as precious as the apostles' own, through the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ. He prays that grace and peace may multiply to them through the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord (1:1–2).

God's divine power has already supplied everything needed for life and godliness through knowing the One who called them by His own glory and excellence. Through these He has granted His exceedingly great and precious promises, so that through them believers may escape the corruption in the world caused by evil desires and become "partakers of the divine nature" (1:3–4).

Because God has already laid down so rich a foundation, believers must make every effort to build up their faith step by step: supplying moral excellence to faith, knowledge to excellence, self-control to knowledge, perseverance to self-control, godliness to perseverance, brotherly affection to godliness, and love to brotherly affection. When these qualities are present and increasing, they keep believers from becoming idle or unfruitful in knowing Jesus Christ. By contrast, anyone lacking them is nearsighted and blind, having forgotten that he was cleansed from his past sins. Therefore they should work diligently to confirm God's calling and election; practicing these things ensures they will never stumble and will receive a rich welcome into the eternal kingdom of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ (1:5–11).

Even though his readers already know these truths and stand firmly in them, Peter feels compelled to keep reminding them. He knows he will soon lay aside his earthly tent, just as the Lord Jesus clearly revealed to him (1:12–14; John 21:18–19). For that reason he takes great care to put everything in writing so that after his departure they will always be able to recall what he taught (1:15).

What the apostles declared about the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ did not spring from cleverly invented myths; they were firsthand eyewitnesses of His majesty. On the sacred mountain, they watched Him receive honor and glory from God the Father, and heard with their own ears the voice carried from heaven: "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (1:16–18; Matthew 17:1–5).

That experience makes the prophetic word of Scripture even more certain. Peter urges them to pay close attention to it, as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until day dawns and the morning star rises in their hearts. Above all, they must understand that no prophecy of Scripture arose from anyone's private interpretation, because prophecy never originated from human will; instead, people spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit (1:19–21).

## Key Takeaways

**God's abundant grace does not breed laziness; it fuels spiritual growth.** God has already provided everything required for godly living, yet Peter still summons believers to "make every effort" to cultivate seven linked virtues climbing from faith to love. Anyone who stops growing gradually goes spiritually blind and forgets the cleansing they received (1:3–9).

**Christian faith rests on eyewitness testimony, not fabricated legends.** Before exposing false teachers in chapter 2, Peter anchors his readers to twin pillars: eyewitness testimony from the Mount of Transfiguration and the reliable prophetic word of the Old Testament. Both converge on a single certainty: Jesus Christ will return in power (1:16–19).

**Scripture comes from God through human writers carried by the Holy Spirit.** Peter portrays biblical authors neither as lifeless dictation machines nor as freelancers inventing ideas on their own; they were real people borne along and guided by the Holy Spirit to communicate God's authentic word (1:20–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**The Mount of Transfiguration previews the Lord's return.** Recalling the heavenly voice on "the holy mountain" (1:17–18), Peter looks back to the moment he, James, and John beheld Jesus' face blazing like the sun (Matthew 17:1–5; Mark 9:2–8). The Father's words fuse two Old Testament messianic texts: God's royal Son (Psalms 2:7) and the Servant in whom God delights (Isaiah 42:1).

**The imagery of a "tent" and "departure" echoes the Exodus.** Calling his body a temporary "tent" (1:13–14)—like Israel's wilderness shelters—and speaking of his coming "departure" (1:15), Peter uses the Greek word *exodos*, the exact term Moses and Elijah used on the Mount of Transfiguration when speaking with Jesus about His coming departure at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31).

**The morning star heralding the dawn.** Holding fast to Scripture like a lamp in the night until "the morning star rises in your hearts" (1:19) links back to the ancient prophecy of a star rising out of Jacob (Numbers 24:17) and forward to the title Jesus claims at the end of the Bible as the bright Morning Star (Revelation 22:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Becoming "partakers of the divine nature" (1:4) does not mean humans turn into gods.** To modern ears, that phrase might sound like Greek philosophical absorption into deity. Yet reading the immediately preceding clause clarifies Peter's meaning: sharing the divine nature stands opposite "the corruption in the world caused by evil desires." In other words, believers come to share in God's moral holiness and imperishable life without ever blurring the boundary between Creator and creature.

**Verse 1:20 ("no prophecy of Scripture comes from one's own interpretation") allows two closely aligned readings.** One emphasizes that readers cannot twist Scripture to suit private agendas as false teachers do (cf. 3:16). The other flows directly into verse 1:21: the prophets themselves did not manufacture prophecies from their own private musings, but spoke under the Spirit's impulse. Both lead to the same conclusion—Scripture carries God's own authority.

**The Greek verb rendered "carried along" in verse 1:21 pictures a sailboat propelled by the wind.** Ancient writers used this exact verb for a ship driven across the sea by gale winds (cf. Acts 27:15). The biblical prophets kept their distinct personalities and vocabulary, yet the driving power steering them safely to the destination was the Holy Spirit.

## What Commentators Say

**Athanasius**, fourth-century bishop of Alexandria, frequently cited verse 1:4 on sharing the divine nature to express the Eastern church's doctrine of sanctification: the Son of God became human so that humanity might be renewed to share in God's life. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, cautioned readers against misinterpreting verse 1:4 in a mystical way that erases the line between Creator and creature. Peter simply teaches that the gospel restores God's image within us, rescuing us from sin's decay so we reflect His holiness. *(Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, on 2 Peter chapter 1 verse 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's careful explanation of 2 Peter 1:4 guarding against pantheistic readings is well known in his commentary -->

**Richard Bauckham**, British New Testament scholar, points out that verses 1:12–15 follow the pattern of the ancient Jewish testament genre, where an aging leader anticipating death leaves farewell reminders for the next generation along with warnings about coming deceivers. *(Jude, 2 Peter, Word Biblical Commentary, 1983, on 2 Peter chapter 1 verses 12–15)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: identifying the literary testament convention in 2 Peter 1:12–15 is a landmark insight of Bauckham's WBC volume -->

**Douglas J. Moo**, American New Testament scholar, notes that the ladder of virtues in verses 1:5–7 opens with faith and culminates in love, proving that knowing Jesus is never mere head knowledge.
