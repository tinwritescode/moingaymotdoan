# Psalms 70 — Hurry, O God, to Deliver Me

## Story

Only five verses long, Psalm 70 sounds like an emergency distress call blurted out in danger with zero time for polished speeches. David opens immediately on two urgent beats: "Make haste, O God, to deliver me! Hurry, O LORD, to help me!" He asks God to turn back in shame those hunting his life and gloating over his ruin (70:1–3).

Even in danger, his vision widens to embrace everyone else seeking God: may all who seek You rejoice and be glad in You, continually magnifying God's greatness. Turning back to his own smallness—"Yet I am poor and needy"—he repeats his urgent plea: You are my help and my deliverer, "O LORD, do not delay" (70:4–5).

## Key Takeaways

**Short, breathless prayers carry full weight before God.** No eloquence or lengthy preamble is required; when crisis presses right against you, "Hurry, O LORD, to help me!" (70:1) is a complete prayer.

**Amid personal pain, believers still wish joy for others.** Even feeling poor and needy, David refuses to lock himself inside self-pity, praying that everyone seeking God will overflow with gladness and praise (70:4).

**Admitting we are "poor and needy" opens the door to God's help.** David hides none of his helplessness; owning complete neediness anchors him directly onto his sole Deliverer (70:5).

## Deeper Meaning

**The cry "Do not delay" meets God's sure promise in the New Testament (70:5).** Again and again God's people plead for Him not to linger. Hebrews quotes prophetic assurance to steady suffering Christians: "He who is coming will come and will not delay" (Hebrews 10:37; Revelation 22:20).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalm 70 reproduces the closing five verses of Psalm 40 almost word for word (Psalms 40:13–17).** The main slight variation is preferring the title *God* over *LORD*—typical of Book II of the Psalter. Setting these five verses apart as an independent psalm shows Israel prized them as a ready pocket prayer for sudden emergencies.

**The taunt "Aha! Aha!" captures cruel, gloating laughter (70:3).** Across the Psalms, that jeer sounds whenever enemies assume a believer has finally been crushed and deserted by God (Psalms 35:21).
