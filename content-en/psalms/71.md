# Psalms 71 — The Prayer of Gray Hairs

## Story

This moving prayer rises from someone well into life's twilight who still faces scheming foes. Opening in refuge, the psalmist asks the LORD to rescue him in righteousness and serve as "a rock of refuge" he can always enter. Looking back across his whole life for footing, he remembers God has been his confidence since youth—the very One who supported him from the womb. Though many regard his life as a marvel, God remains his sturdy refuge, filling his mouth with praise all day long (71:1–8).

Then surfaces the deeply human fear of growing old: "Do not cast me off in my old age; do not forsake me when my strength fails." Spotting his frailty, enemies plot together, whispering that God has abandoned him and no one will save him. He pleads with God not to stay far off, hurrying to help and putting his accusers to shame (71:9–13).

At verse 14, his tone flares into a resolve that refuses to grow old: "But I will always have hope and will praise You more and more." Even though God's righteous acts and saving deeds stretch beyond counting, his mouth will never stop telling them. Remembering how God taught him from his youth, he voices the celebrated prayer of aging believers: "Even when I am old and gray, do not forsake me, O God, until I proclaim Your power to the next generation, Your might to all who are to come" (71:14–18).

The closing section glows with hope of restoration and music. God's righteousness towers beyond compare; though He has let the psalmist endure many bitter troubles, God Himself will revive him, lift him up again from the depths of the earth, and comfort him anew. He closes pledging harp, lyre, and his whole redeemed soul to celebrate God's faithfulness and righteousness all day long (71:19–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Old age is not a shelf of irrelevance—it holds the richest story to tell about God.** Foes assume fading stamina means God has walked away (71:11), yet the psalmist prays for strength in gray hair precisely to finish a vital calling: handing down firsthand testimony of God's power to the next generation (71:18).

**Lifelong faithfulness builds rock-solid ground for today.** The writer connects three chapters of life before God: from the womb (71:6), through youthful instruction (71:17), all the way to old age and gray hair (71:18). God never alters across life's seasons.

**Advancing years can yield sweeter, deeper faith.** Resolving to "praise You more and more" (71:14) pushes back against bitterness or resignation as health declines. More years lived mean more mercies to thank Him for.

**God can revive His servants out of bitter depths.** Verse 20 refuses to sugarcoat "many bitter troubles," yet declares that trouble never gets the last word—God will revive and comfort again (71:20–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**The plea never to be forsaken in gray hair receives a direct promise in Isaiah and the New Testament.** Praying not to be cast off when old and gray (71:9; 71:18) meets God's tender answer through Isaiah: "Even to your old age, I will be the same, and I will bear you up when your hair turns gray" (Isaiah 46:4). In the New Testament, Hebrews seals that assurance: "Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5).

**Passing faith to the next generation continues across the New Testament church.** Longing to proclaim God's might to generations to come (71:18) echoes Paul's charge to Timothy to entrust sound truth to reliable people who will teach others too (2 Timothy 2:2), alongside older believers modeling mature steadiness for younger ones (Titus 2:2–5).

**Confidence of being *revived* from the depths foreshadows resurrection hope.** Being brought back up from the depths of the earth (71:20) speaks immediately of surviving near-fatal trials, yet plants a seed that blossoms fully in Christ's resurrection (Romans 8:11).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalm 71 carries no superscription in Hebrew and weaves lines from several earlier psalms.** Verses 1 through 3 closely echo Psalm 31 (71:1–3; Psalms 31:1–3), verses 5 and 6 recall Psalm 22 (71:5–6; Psalms 22:9–10), and verses 12 and 13 mirror Psalm 70 (71:12–13). Many scholars picture an elderly king or saint—tradition often thinks of aged David—so steeped in Scripture over decades that beloved earlier psalms flow naturally into his own personal prayer.
