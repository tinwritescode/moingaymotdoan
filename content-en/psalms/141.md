# Psalms 141 — Guard My Mouth Like an Evening Offering

## Story

Traditionally attributed to David, Psalm 141 is an earnest evening prayer from someone asking not only to be rescued from wicked enemies outside, but also to be kept from slipping into temptation within. The psalmist urges God to hurry and hear his voice, longing: "May my prayer be set before You like incense, my uplifted hands like the evening offering!" (141:1–2).

The heart of his petition centers on the discipline of speech and desire: "Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch at the door of my lips." He pleads with God not to let his heart incline toward anything evil, nor to join evildoers or feast on "their delicacies" (141:3–4).

Rather than craving the flattery of the wicked, the psalmist welcomes honest correction from the upright: "Let a righteous man strike me—it is a kindness; let him rebuke me—it is oil on my head; my head will not refuse it." Even while enemies keep plotting evil, he holds fast to unceasing prayer (141:5).

Next come two vivid, somewhat enigmatic verses depicting the downfall of wicked leaders and bones scattered at the mouth of Sheol—capturing how close to death his community stands (141:6–7).

At the close, the psalmist fixes his eyes straight on God, declaring that he takes full refuge in Him and asking to be kept from the snares the wicked have laid, so that "the wicked fall into their own nets, while I pass by safely" (141:8–10).

## Key Takeaways

**Sincere prayer is as precious as a temple sacrifice.** Believers could not always stand physically in the temple for the evening offering (Exodus 29:38–42). Verse 2 affirms that lifted hands and a praying heart anywhere rise to please God like fragrant incense.

**The most dangerous enemy is sometimes our own tongue and compromise.** Surrounded by hostile people, the psalmist knows that a hot-tempered outburst or an appetite for the *delicacies* of the wicked could make him just like them. That is why he asks God to station a *guard* right at the door of his lips.

**Blunt correction from a righteous friend is worth more than a banquet with the wicked.** Verse 5 displays a rare measure of spiritual maturity: treating sincere rebuke like *fragrant oil on the head* instead of brushing it off out of wounded pride.

## Deeper Meaning

**The prayers of the saints as incense before God's throne.** The picture of prayer rising like incense (141:2) becomes one of the most cherished visions in Revelation: golden bowls full of incense are the prayers of the saints ascending before God (Revelation 5:8; Revelation 8:3–4).

**Guarding the tongue in the New Testament.** The plea in verse 3 connects directly with James's piercing teaching on the destructive power of the tongue (James 3:2–10) and Paul's counsel that our speech should always be gracious, seasoned with salt (Colossians 4:6).

## Notes & Nuances

**Difficult verses in verses 5–7.** In the original Hebrew, verses 5b through 7 are extremely compressed and hard to construe word for word, which is why Bible translations render them quite differently (for instance, whose "bones" are scattered in verse 7—slain followers of the psalmist, or judged enemies). Even so, the overall flow is clear: standing at the edge of ruin, the psalmist clings all the more tightly to God in verses 8–10.

**An ancient evening hymn of the Church.** Because verse 2 mentions the *evening offering*, from the earliest centuries both Eastern and Western churches adopted Psalm 141 as a signature song for evening worship (Vespers).

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth- and fifth-century Church Father of Constantinople, noted that Psalm 141 was sung every evening in the churches of his day. He highlighted verse 2 as teaching believers how to turn their own hands and hearts into an altar offering incense to God without needing physical spices. *(Exposition of the Psalms, on Psalm 141)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Chrysostom noting the use of Psalm 141 in evening liturgy and his exposition of verse 2 -->
