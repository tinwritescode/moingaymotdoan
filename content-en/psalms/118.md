# Psalms 118 — The Rejected Stone Becomes the Cornerstone

## Story

Psalm 118 crowns the *Hallel* sequence (Psalms 113–118), opening and closing with the beloved thanksgiving refrain: "Give thanks to the LORD, for He is good; His loving devotion endures forever." Israel, the house of Aaron, and all who fear the LORD join in echoing that glad refrain together (118:1–4).

From the choir steps forward one solo voice—likely the king or festival leader—recounting a dramatic rescue. Pressed into tight distress, he cried to the LORD, who answered and set him in a spacious place. Boldly he declares: "The LORD is on my side; I will not be afraid. What can man do to me?" That rescue etched one rock-solid lesson: "It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in man"—better even than trusting princes (118:5–9).

He recalls nations swarming around him like angry bees, yet in the LORD's name they burned out as fast as a fire among thorns. Pushed hard to the brink of collapse, he felt the LORD rush to his help; the LORD became his strength, his song, and his salvation. Shouts of joy fill the tents of the righteous as God's right hand acts mightily. He proclaims, "I will not die, but I will live and proclaim what the LORD has done." Though the LORD disciplined him severely, He never handed him over to death (118:10–18).

The procession arrives before the temple gates. The leader calls for the gates of righteousness to swing open so he may enter and give thanks to the One who answered and saved him; priests throw open the gate announcing it as the LORD's gate for the righteous to enter (118:19–21).

Then rings out the psalm's most celebrated line: "The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone." Amazed, the congregation confesses this is the LORD's doing—marvelous in their eyes—and erupts in song: "This is the day that the LORD has made; we will rejoice and be glad in it" (118:22–24).

Up rises the urgent cry—in Hebrew *Hosanna*: "O LORD, save us!" and grant success. From the house of the LORD, priests pronounce blessing over the arriving worshipers: "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD!" Carrying festive branches up to the altar horns for the thank sacrifice, the assembly concludes lifting high praise to their God and repeating the opening refrain of His everlasting steadfast love (118:25–29).

## Key Takeaways

**God overturns human rejection completely.** Someone shoved nearly to death by swarming enemies, a stone tossed aside as useless by expert builders—God Himself lifts it up to anchor the entire building as its chief cornerstone (118:13; 118:17; 118:22–23).

**Taking refuge in the LORD outlasts every human prop.** Sitting near the center of Scripture, verses 8 and 9 state a practical truth: ordinary mortals and powerful princes alike remain limited and changeable; leaning on the LORD never gives way (118:8–9).

**God's day of rescue calls for public celebration.** In context, "This is the day that the LORD has made" (118:24) celebrates far more than just any sunny morning—it hails the decisive day God stepped in to rescue and set the rejected stone as cornerstone.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus riding into Jerusalem amid shouts of *Hosanna*.** On Palm Sunday as Jesus rode a donkey into the city, jubilant crowds quoted verses 25 and 26 directly: *Hosanna!* and "Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!" (118:25–26; Matthew 21:9; Mark 11:9–10). Soon afterward Jesus echoed verse 26 Himself while weeping over Jerusalem (Matthew 23:39).

**The rejected stone is Christ crucified and risen.** Days later, after telling the parable of the wicked tenants, Jesus quoted verses 22 and 23 straight to the chief priests and elders: they were the *builders* rejecting Him, yet God would make Him the cornerstone (118:22–23; Matthew 21:42). After the resurrection, Peter threw that exact verse back before the Sanhedrin (Acts 4:11) and wrote in his epistle of Jesus—the living stone rejected by humans yet chosen and precious in God's sight (1 Peter 2:4; 2:7).

**Final hymn of the Last Supper and courage against human fear.** As the closing psalm of the Passover *Hallel*, these very lines—"The stone the builders rejected" and "I will not die, but I will live"—almost certainly sounded on Jesus' lips minutes before stepping into Gethsemane (Matthew 26:30). Hebrews likewise quotes verse 6 to banish fear of human opposition (118:6; Hebrews 13:6).

## Notes & Nuances

**Where the word *Hosanna* comes from.** In Hebrew, "O LORD, save us!" in verse 25 reads *Hoshi'a-na*, carried into Greek New Testament usage as *Hosanna* (118:25). Originally an urgent plea (*Save now!*), during Tabernacles and Passover it evolved into an ecstatic shout welcoming the Deliverer.

**"With boughs in hand, join in the festal procession up to the horns of the altar" (118:27).** Traditional translations read "bind the festal sacrifice with cords to the horns of the altar," while many modern scholars translate it as worshipers holding festive branches winding up to the altar's corners. Either image captures the joyous peak of temple thanksgiving.
