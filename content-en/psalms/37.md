# Psalms 37 — Do Not Fret Over Evildoers

## Story

David writes this wisdom poem in old age (37:25) like a seasoned mentor encouraging younger believers troubled by the apparent prosperity of the wicked. Right in the opening line, he sets out the counsel running through the entire psalm: "Do not fret over those who do evil, nor be envious of those who do wrong." Their success is as brief as fresh grass that quickly gets cut down and withers (37:1–2).

Instead of burning with envy, David maps out concrete steps of trust: trust in the LORD and do good, delight yourself in Him, commit your way to Him, and wait patiently and quietly before Him. He urges, "Refrain from anger and abandon wrath; do not fret—it can only bring harm." Those who wait on the LORD and live humbly will inherit the land and delight in abundant peace (37:3–11).

The middle of the psalm sets two portraits side by side. The wicked gnash their teeth and draw sword and bow against the vulnerable, yet the Lord laughs at them because He sees their day of reckoning approaching; their swords will pierce their own hearts and they will vanish like smoke. By contrast, the little possessed by the righteous is far better than the abundance of many wicked, for the LORD upholds the righteous, knows every one of their days, and sustains them in times of famine (37:12–22).

David then shares a lifetime of observation. The LORD establishes the steps of the one who delights in Him; though they stumble, they will not fall headlong, for the LORD's hand holds them up. From youth to old age, he has never seen the righteous forsaken. The righteous lend generously and carry God's law in their hearts so their feet do not slip (37:23–31).

The closing section states its verdict calmly. The wicked may lie in wait seeking to kill the righteous and may tower like a lush tree spreading in native soil, yet pass by a moment later and they are gone. Look to the final outcome: the wicked have no future, whereas a future of peace belongs to the blameless and upright, because the LORD is their stronghold and deliverer (37:32–40).

## Key Takeaways

**Envying the wicked pulls us down into evil ourselves.** Three times David repeats *Do not fret* (37:1; 37:7; 37:8). Once resentment and fury over injustice colonize our hearts, it takes very little before we excuse ourselves for adopting the very same wrongs.

**Delighting in God transforms the desires of our hearts.** Verse 4 promises, "Delight yourself in the LORD, and He will give you the desires of your heart" (37:4). This is no blank check for selfish cravings; when God Himself becomes our deepest joy, our desires begin matching His will—and He delights to fulfill them.

**Adopt God's long horizon instead of short-term appearances.** Evildoers resemble green grass or drifting smoke (37:2; 37:20; 37:35–36), whereas the righteous receive a lasting inheritance (37:9; 37:11; 37:29). Faith requires quiet patience waiting for God's timing (37:7).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus quoted verse 11 directly in the Sermon on the Mount.** Opening the Beatitudes of heaven's kingdom, Jesus blessed the meek because they will inherit the earth (Matthew 5:5), echoing almost word for word David's promise: "But the meek will inherit the land" (37:11).

**The promised inheritance of *the land* stretches beyond Canaan toward the new heavens and new earth.** In the Old Testament, inheriting the land—repeated five times in verses 9, 11, 22, 29, and 34—centered first on the Promised Land. In the New Testament, that promise opens into an imperishable eternal inheritance in the new heavens and new earth where righteousness dwells (2 Peter 3:13; Revelation 21:1–7).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalm 37 is an intricate alphabetic acrostic.** In the Hebrew original, every two verses (occasionally one or three) begin with consecutive letters from the first to the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Because of that poetic pattern, the psalm circles one central theme from many angles like the Book of Proverbs rather than telling a linear story.

**Verse 25 is a general wisdom observation, not a mechanical rule with zero exceptions.** When David testifies that he has "never seen the righteous forsaken or their children begging bread" (37:25), he speaks of God's enduring faithfulness in his lived observation—never condemning godly people who suffer poverty or hunger like Job or Lazarus (Luke 16:20).
