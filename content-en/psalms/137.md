# Psalms 137 — By the Rivers of Babylon

## Story

This is one of the most anguished and unvarnished laments in all of Scripture, set in the exile of Judah in Babylon after Jerusalem was leveled in 586 BC. The captive community recalls sitting silently beside the canals of a foreign empire: "By the rivers of Babylon we sat and wept when we remembered Zion." They hung their harps on the willow trees, having no heart left to play (137:1–2).

Their humiliation deepens when their captors taunt them, demanding entertainment: "Sing us one of the songs of Zion!" Choking back grief, the exiles ask: "How can we sing a song of the LORD in a foreign land?" (137:3–4).

Suddenly the poem shifts from communal grief to a fierce personal vow from one of the musicians. He would rather let his right hand lose its skill on the harp, and let his tongue cling to the roof of his mouth unable to sing again, than forget Jerusalem or place any other joy above the holy city (137:5–6).

The last three verses turn abruptly into a searing cry for retribution. The psalmist asks God never to forget how the Edomites gloated on the day Jerusalem fell, shouting to tear it down to its foundations. Then the poem closes with a shocking curse aimed at Babylon: blessing whoever pays Babylon back for the atrocities it committed, even declaring, "Blessed is he who seizes your infants and dashes them against the rocks!" (137:7–9).

## Key Takeaways

**The grief of losing homeland and worship is a real wound.** The songs of Zion were not light entertainment to amuse conquerors. Keeping silent and hanging up their harps was how the captives guarded the dignity of sacred worship.

**Remembering Zion means holding fast to identity in exile.** Once everything familiar lies in ashes, the greatest peril for exiles is not merely hardship, but slowly growing comfortable in Babylon and forgetting to whom they belong. The vow in verses 5–6 sets memory of God's city above craft and personal delight.

**Scripture does not hide the raw rage of victims.** Rather than offering a polished, polite prayer, the author pours the unedited outrage of a people who had just watched their own children slaughtered straight out before God.

## Deeper Meaning

**The fall of Babylon in Revelation.** In the New Testament, *Babylon* becomes the emblem of every arrogant, brutal empire set against God's people. The call for Babylon to be repaid according to what it has done (137:8) echoes in Revelation 18:6, where a voice from heaven announces God's righteous judgment on Babylon the Great—yet that judgment belongs to God, not to His people taking private vengeance.

**The way beyond hatred at the cross.** In the light of the New Testament, Jesus never minimizes the pain of the oppressed, yet He opens a new path: love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you (Matthew 5:44), leaving all vengeance to God (Romans 12:19). Jesus Himself absorbed the world's cruelty in His own body instead of answering violence with violence.

## Notes & Nuances

**Facing the harsh curse of verses 8–9 head-on.** Verse 9 is one of the most unsettling lines in the entire Bible, and interpreters across the centuries have offered three main ways to read it without smoothing over its harshness. First, *reading it in the setting of ancient warfare and prophecy*: when Babylon took Jerusalem, Babylonian troops themselves slaughtered Judah's women and children (Lamentations 2:11; Isaiah 13:16), so the cry in verses 8–9 reflects the Old Testament principle of proportional justice (*eye for eye*) and earlier prophecies that Babylon would reap what it had sown. Second, *reading it as an honest cry handing rage over to God*: the psalmist does not tell Jews to take up swords for vigilante revenge, but lays unbearable fury at the feet of the righteous Judge—handing hatred to God keeps human beings from turning into monsters themselves, even though the emotion still falls short of the forgiveness Jesus later taught. Third, *the allegorical reading of the Church Fathers*: early writers such as Augustine and Origen found a literal violent meaning impossible for Christian prayer, so they read *Babylon's infants* as newborn impulses of sin and temptation that must be crushed immediately against the *Rock*, Christ, before they grow strong; while spiritually helpful, many modern scholars caution against letting allegory erase the real historical trauma pulsing through the poem.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine of Hippo**, fourth- and fifth-century Church Father, famously adopted an allegorical reading of verse 9: *Babylon* represents the confusion of worldly desires, its *infants* are fledgling sinful thoughts just sprouting in the heart, and the *rock* is Christ Himself. In his view, blessed is the person who smashes temptation while it is still small instead of nursing it into habit. *(Expositions on the Psalms, on Psalm 137)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's famous allegorical interpretation of Psalm 137 verse 9 is widely cited across the history of biblical interpretation -->

**C. S. Lewis**, twentieth-century British author and apologist, candidly called imprecatory lines like verse 9 expressions of terrible hatred rather than a moral standard for Christians.
