# Psalms 6 — Amid Weakness and Tears

## Story

David opens with an urgent plea from the depths of physical and spiritual exhaustion. He does not deny that he deserves correction, yet he begs the LORD not to rebuke him in anger or discipline him in wrath (6:1). His bones are shaking and his soul is deeply troubled, until all he can utter is a broken question: "How long, O LORD, how long?" (6:2–3).

He pleads for God to turn and deliver him—not on the basis of any personal merit, but because of God's loving devotion (6:4). Speaking with the blunt urgency of someone near his last breath, he argues that in the silence of death and the grave no one gives praise to God anymore (6:5). Night after night he wears himself out groaning, flooding his bed with tears, while his eyes grow weak with sorrow because of all his foes (6:6–7).

Suddenly, at verse 8, the entire tone shifts. From tearful exhaustion, David turns and orders all evildoers to depart from him, confident that the LORD has heard the sound of his weeping and accepted his prayer (6:8–9). The enemies who once pressed in on him will be put to shame, terrified, and turned back in disgrace (6:10).

## Key Takeaways

**Believers still pass through moments of deep pain and exhaustion.** This psalm refuses to sugarcoat faith: trembling bones, tear-soaked pillows, and the cry "How long?" are brought straight into prayer before God (6:2–3; 6:6).

**God's steadfast love is the sole ground of rescue.** David does not bargain with good deeds; he simply pleads, "Save me because of Your loving devotion" (6:4).

**God hears even wordless weeping.** Verse 8 says not only that God hears prayer, but that He hears "the sound of my weeping"—when we are too drained to form polished sentences, our tears still reach His ears (6:8–9).

## Deeper Meaning

**The cry "My soul is deeply troubled" echoes on the lips of Jesus Himself.** As the hour of His passion drew near, Jesus said His soul was troubled, drawing directly on the language and anguish of verse 3 as He faced the cup of suffering (6:3; John 12:27). He did not stand aloof from human pain; He stepped straight into it.

**The dismissal of evildoers near the end reappears in Jesus' warning about the final judgment.** In the Sermon on the Mount, He tells those who invoke His name while practicing lawlessness to depart from Him (Matthew 7:23; Luke 13:27), mirroring the decisive tone of verse 8 (6:8).

**The shadow of *Sheol* in verse 5 finds its fuller answer in the light of the resurrection.** Old Testament worshipers viewed death primarily as silence cut off from the praising congregation (6:5); in the New Testament, because Christ has risen, Paul affirms that to depart from the body is to be with the Lord (Philippians 1:23; 2 Corinthians 5:8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalm 6 is the first of the church's seven traditional Penitential Psalms.** The other six are Psalms 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, and 143. Although this psalm names no specific sin, its opening verse shows that the writer experiences his affliction as divine discipline.

**Saying he *floods his bed* and *drenches his couch* with tears is vivid emotional hyperbole typical of Hebrew poetry (6:6).** It captures sorrow stretching on night after night, rather than calling for literal measurement.

**The sudden turnaround between verses 7 and 8 occurs frequently in laments (6:7–8).** Many scholars suggest a priestly word of assurance may have been spoken between the two halves during worship, or simply that God granted inward certainty directly to the praying heart.
