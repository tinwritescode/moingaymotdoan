# Psalms 116 — Thanksgiving After Escaping Death's Grip

## Story

Psalm 116 brings the warmest personal voice in the entire *Hallel* group. The writer opens straight from the heart: "I love the LORD, for He has heard my voice" and cries for mercy; because God bent His ear down to listen, he will call on Him as long as he lives (116:1–2).

He replays the terrifying brush with death he survived: cords of death tangled around him, pangs of Sheol closed in, distress and anguish overwhelmed him. In sheer desperation he managed one short cry begging the LORD to save his life—and God, gracious, righteous, compassionate, and protector of the simplehearted, pulled him out at rock bottom (116:3–6).

Safely rescued, he speaks gently to his own heart: "Return to your rest, O my soul, for the LORD has been good to you." God delivered his soul from death, his eyes from tears, and his feet from stumbling so he can keep walking before Him in the land of the living. Looking back candidly, he admits feeling so battered he blurted out in dismay that all humanity is full of lies—yet right inside that anguish faith held firm: "I believed, therefore I have spoken" (116:7–11).

Next rises a question brimming with gratitude: "How can I repay the LORD for all His goodness to me?" The answer surprises everyone—you never pay God back like settling a debt; you honor Him by receiving even more grace with thanksgiving: "I will lift the cup of salvation and call on the name of the LORD," fulfilling vows publicly before all God's people (116:12–14).

He pauses on a moving reflection about life and death: "Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of His saints." Claiming himself God's servant whose bonds He has loosed, he finishes standing inside Jerusalem's temple courts offering sacrifices of thanksgiving, calling on the LORD's name, paying vows before the whole congregation, and shouting *Hallelujah* (116:15–19).

## Key Takeaways

**The truest way to thank God is lifting His cup of salvation in gratitude.** Asking how to repay the LORD (116:12), the psalmist pulls out no payment or personal achievement; instead he raises "the cup of salvation" and calls on God's name (116:13). God is honored most when we humbly receive His grace and testify openly before others (116:14; 116:18).

**Neither the life nor the death of God's people is ever cheap in His sight.** In context, verse 15 declares first that God prizes His faithful ones' lives too dearly to toss them casually to death—and even when death finally comes, it takes place beneath His tender, watchful eye (116:15).

**Genuine faith survives even amid raw turmoil.** The singer honestly admits panicking until everyone around looked untrustworthy (116:11), yet still confesses "I believed, therefore I have spoken" (116:10). God never waits for flawless poise before answering prayer.

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul quotes verse 10 speaking of resilient faith amid affliction (116:10).** Facing relentless persecution and brushes with death for the gospel, Paul cites "I believed; therefore I have spoken" to share that exact spirit of faith—convinced that the God who raised Jesus will raise believers too, keeping the apostles speaking boldly (2 Corinthians 4:13–14).

**The *cup of salvation* at the Last Supper.** Because Psalm 116 formed part of the Passover *Hallel*, lifting "the cup of salvation" in thanksgiving (116:13) echoed powerfully on the night Jesus instituted Communion—taking the cup after giving thanks and declaring it the new covenant in His blood poured out for many (Matthew 26:27–30; 1 Corinthians 10:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**The ancient Greek Septuagint divides this psalm in two.** In Catholic Bibles (following the Septuagint and Vulgate), verses 1 through 9 appear as Psalm 114 and verses 10 through 19 as Psalm 115—explaining why numbering shifts slightly when consulting older commentaries or Catholic editions.

**Thank offerings and the cup of salvation (116:13; 116:17).** Under Mosaic law, someone rescued from grave danger came to the temple bringing a thank offering alongside a poured drink offering of wine before friends and congregation (Leviticus 7:12; Numbers 15:5–10).
