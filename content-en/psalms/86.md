# Psalms 86 — Prayer for an Undivided Heart

## Story

The sole psalm in Book III bearing David's name opens with a cascade of humble pleas from someone feeling poor and needy: incline Your ear, O LORD, guard my life, be gracious, and bring joy to Your servant who lifts his soul to You all day long. Confidence rests squarely on who God is: "For You, O Lord, are kind and forgiving, rich in loving devotion to all who call on You" (86:1–7).

Right in the middle of trouble, the song lifts into grand adoration. Among all gods none compares to the Lord, nor can any deed rival His works. Every nation He has made will one day come, bow before Him, and glorify His name, for He alone is God (86:8–10).

Out of worship springs a searching prayer for the inner life: "Teach me Your way, O LORD, that I may walk in Your truth. Give me an undivided heart, that I may fear Your name." He pledges wholehearted praise forever, because God's great steadfast love has rescued his soul from the depths of Sheol (86:11–13).

At the close, David faces present danger squarely: arrogant, ruthless men who pay no regard to God are hunting his life. Immediately he quotes God's classic self-portrait from Mount Sinai—merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness—asking for strength and a tangible sign of favor so his foes see with shame that the LORD Himself has helped and comforted him (86:14–17).

## Key Takeaways

**Prayer begins with honesty about ourselves and truth about God.** David never hides his poverty and need ("I am poor and needy"), placing his full hope in God's good, forgiving nature (86:1; 86:5).

**Our fragmented hearts need God to unite them.** Praying "Give me an undivided heart, that I may fear Your name" hits humanity's deepest inward flaw: hearts pulled in conflicting directions (86:11). Walking steadily in truth requires asking God Himself to gather our scattered desires into one focused devotion.

**God's compassionate character is our safest shelter against ruthless foes.** Facing arrogant enemies, David does not chase flashy fantasies, clinging instead to God's unchanging revealed name (86:14–15).

## Deeper Meaning

**God's self-revelation at Mount Sinai returns word for word.** Verse 15—"a compassionate and gracious God, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness" (86:15)—quotes verbatim how the LORD introduced Himself to Moses after the golden calf crisis (Exodus 34:6), showing Old Testament prayer stayed anchored in Sinai's grace.

**The promise that all nations will worship echoes in Revelation.** Verses 9 and 10 declare every nation God made will come bow and glorify His name (86:9–10). In Revelation, the song of Moses and the Lamb rings out before heaven's throne with this exact chorus: all nations will come and worship before Him (Revelation 15:4).

## Notes & Nuances

**A prayer woven from earlier Scriptures.** Scholars note how Psalm 86 echoes beloved lines from earlier Davidic psalms—for instance verse 1 parallels Psalm 40 (86:1; Psalms 40:17) and verse 14 echoes Psalm 54 (86:14; Psalms 54:3)—along with the Pentateuch. Far from reducing its depth, this teaches a wonderfully practical habit: praying Scripture right back to God.

**What *for I am godly* means (86:2).** The Hebrew term (*chasid*) does not claim sinless perfection, but means *one bound in covenant loyalty to God*. David never boasts of personal merit—he appeals to the covenant relationship uniting him to his Lord.
