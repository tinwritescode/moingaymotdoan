# Psalms 22 — A Cry of Forsakenness and a Song in the Great Assembly

## Story

Psalm 22 opens with one of the most agonizing cries in all of Scripture: "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" (22:1). David cries out by day and by night without receiving an answer or finding a moment's rest (22:2). Even so, he clings to who God is: the Holy One enthroned on the praises of Israel, whom Israel's ancestors trusted and were never put to shame (22:3–5).

His present reality, however, is utter humiliation. He feels like a worm despised by everyone; passersby mock him, curl their lips, and shake their heads, taunting him that since he trusts in the LORD, let the LORD rescue him if He delights in him (22:6–8). Again he reminds himself that God drew him safely from his mother's womb and taught him to trust from infancy, pleading with God not to stay far off when no one else can help (22:9–11).

The middle section depicts physical and inner torment in harrowing detail. Enemies surround him like fierce bulls of Bashan and roaring lions (22:12–13). His body collapses: poured out like water, every bone out of joint, his heart melting like wax, his tongue clinging to the roof of his mouth as though laid in the dust of death (22:14–15). A pack of wicked men encircles him like dogs: "They have pierced my hands and feet," he can count all his bones while they stare and gloat, and "They divide my garments among them and cast lots for my clothing" (22:16–18). Gathering his final strength, he cries for rescue from sword, dog, lion's mouth, and horns of wild oxen—and right at the close of verse 21, the poem suddenly breaks into light: "You have answered me!" (22:19–21).

From verse 22 to the end, darkness dissolves into a worldwide festival of praise. The delivered sufferer vows to declare God's name to his brothers and praise Him in the assembly (22:22). He summons all Jacob's descendants to honor God, because He did not despise the affliction of the sufferer, but listened when he cried out (22:23–25). Nor does the celebration stop with Israel—it spills out to the ends of the earth: all nations will remember and turn to worship the LORD, rich and dying alike will bow before Him, and generations yet unborn will tell one another that "He has done it" (22:26–31).

## Key Takeaways

**The deepest agony for a believer is not outward loss, but feeling forsaken by God.** Yet even inside the cry "Why have You forsaken me?", the psalmist twice calls out "My God!"—faith still gripping God in the abyss (22:1–3).

**Honest prayer swings back and forth between present pain and remembered faithfulness.** The entire first half alternates rhythmically between raw lament (22:1–2; 22:6–8; 22:12–18) and deliberate reminders of who God is (22:3–5; 22:9–11; 22:19–21).

**The rescue of one sufferer unlocks blessing for all nations.** When God answers this afflicted servant, the outcome is not merely private relief, but the farthest corners of the earth turning back to worship the LORD (22:21–22; 22:27–31).

## Deeper Meaning

**Psalm 22 is the clearest prophetic portrait of Jesus' crucifixion in the Book of Psalms.** Hanging on the cross in its darkest hour, Jesus Himself cried out the opening line in Aramaic: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34).

**Detail after detail of the crucifixion in the Gospels fulfills Psalm 22 line by line.** Passersby shook their heads and mocked Jesus using the very words of verses 7 and 8—saying He trusts in God, so let God rescue Him if He wants Him (22:7–8; Matthew 27:39; 27:43). Roman soldiers divided His garments and cast lots for His seamless tunic exactly as described in verse 18, which John's Gospel explicitly cites as fulfilling Scripture (22:18; John 19:23–24; Matthew 27:35). Pierced hands and feet and parching thirst likewise mirror crucifixion—a form of execution that did not even exist in Israel during David's lifetime (22:15–16).

**The second half connects directly to the resurrection and the global church.** The letter to the Hebrews places verse 22 directly on the lips of the risen Christ, who is not ashamed to call believers His brothers and sisters as He declares the Father's name in the assembly (22:22; Hebrews 2:11–12). And the closing shout "He has done it" (22:31) resonates with Jesus' triumphant final word on the cross: "It is finished" (John 19:30).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 16 ("They have pierced my hands and feet") involves one of the most famous textual variants in the Old Testament (22:16).** The Hebrew Masoretic Text reads a form meaning *like a lion*, whereas the ancient Greek Septuagint and a Dead Sea Scroll discovered at Nahal Hever read *they have pierced*—differing by only a single stroke on the final Hebrew letter. Most modern translations, including the Berean Standard Bible, follow the *pierced* reading, which fits the sentence's grammar and context.

**When Jesus quoted verse 1 on the cross, Jewish listeners would have recalled the entire psalm, not just one isolated sentence (22:1).** In ancient Jewish practice, reciting the opening line of a psalm served as its title—calling to mind a poem that begins in the blackness of abandonment yet culminates in victory and the nations turning to worship God.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observes that while David starts from genuine personal anguish, many details—especially casting lots for his clothing (22:18) and all nations worshiping God (22:27)—far transcend David's biography to point squarely to Christ.
