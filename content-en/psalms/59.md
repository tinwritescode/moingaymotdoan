# Psalms 59 — Enemies Prowling at Night and a Song at Dawn

## Story

The traditional heading ties this poem to the night King Saul sent men to watch David's house and wait until morning to kill him (1 Samuel 19:11). David opens pleading for God to deliver and set him high above bloodthirsty evildoers lying in wait for his life. Protesting before God that he has committed no crime or offense justifying this ambush, he urges the LORD God of Hosts to rouse Himself and intervene (59:1–5).

David likens the thugs prowling the city at dusk to snarling stray dogs howling through the dark. Their mouths spew insults and swords sit on their lips while they smirk, "Who can hear us?" Yet lifting his eyes heavenward, David sees the opposite reality: the LORD laughs at their arrogance. He resolves to keep watch for God, because God is his "fortress" who will meet him with steadfast love (59:6–10).

Strikingly, when asking God to deal with these foes, David prays: "Do not kill them, or my people will forget. Scatter them by Your power, and bring them down." He wants arrogant liars humbled and turned into a lasting object lesson so people to the ends of the earth know that God rules in Jacob (59:11–13).

Once more the stray-dog refrain returns: at dusk they prowl the city again, snarling for food and wandering unsatisfied through the night. Yet over against that dark, noisy threat outside, David closes with a radiant sunrise song: "But I will sing of Your strength and proclaim Your loving devotion in the morning," for God has proven his fortress and refuge in the day of distress (59:14–17).

## Key Takeaways

**Surrounded in the night, believers can still look forward to a morning song.** The entire poem sets nighttime menace—foes howling like stray dogs (59:6; 59:14)—against bright daybreak when the believer sings aloud of God's strength and steadfast love (59:16).

**Evildoers assume nobody hears, yet God listens and reigns.** Smug muttering—"Who can hear us?" (59:7)—is the classic illusion of everyone working in the dark. Not only does God hear every word, He laughs their schemes to scorn (59:8).

**Our safest vantage point above enemy threats is God's high fortress.** Twice the refrain declares God "my fortress" (59:9; 59:17). Standing on His high rampart puts us out of reach of the snarling pack below.

**God's justice aims to make His reign known worldwide.** David does not ask for deliverance merely to settle personal scores, but so everyone knows "God rules in Jacob to the ends of the earth" (59:13).

## Deeper Meaning

**God laughing at defiant adversaries links back to the Messianic King.** Verse 8's picture of the LORD laughing at hostile nations mirrors Psalm 2 (59:8; Psalms 2:4)—the exact passage the early church quoted in prayer under persecution, realizing no plot against Jesus could ever overturn God's purpose (Acts 4:25–28).

**God acting as refuge in distress points ahead to New Testament assurance.** Surviving encirclement through God's protection (59:16–17) anticipates Paul's calm confidence that the Lord rescues His people from every evil attack and brings them safely into His heavenly kingdom (2 Timothy 4:18).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does verse 11 ask *Do not kill them* while verse 13 asks God to *consume them*?** Rather than contradicting each other, the two verses capture two phases of justice: verse 11 asks that arrogant oppressors be scattered and visibly humbled over time so God's people never forget His justice, while verse 13 looks to the final dismantling of evil's grip so it can threaten no one again (59:11; 59:13).

**Two symmetrical halves give the psalm its distinctive rhythm.** Both the first half (59:1–10) and the second (59:11–17) carry the identical refrain of enemies prowling like dogs at dusk (59:6; 59:14) and both conclude declaring God "my fortress" (59:9–10; 59:16–17). That echoing pattern shows that even when dark nights repeat, trust in God has the final word.
