# Psalms 72 — Prayer for the Ideal Righteous King

## Story

The traditional heading connects this psalm to Solomon—perhaps David's parting prayer for his son taking the throne, or a coronation prayer sung for kings of David's line. Opening immediately with justice, the psalmist asks God to endow the king with His own justice and righteousness. Thus equipped, the king will govern God's people fairly, defend the afflicted, rescue children of the needy, and crush the oppressor—causing mountains and hills alike to yield peace for the people (72:1–4).

The prayer asks that this monarch's reign endure as long as sun and moon shine, refreshing the nation like gentle rain falling on a freshly mown meadow. Under his rule the righteous will flourish and peace will abound. His dominion stretches "from sea to sea, and from the Euphrates to the ends of the earth," as desert tribes and distant rulers from Tarshish, Sheba, and Seba bring tribute and bow before him (72:5–11).

Most surprising of all is *why* the nations willingly honor this king—not from terror or brute force, but because of his compassion for the weak: "For he will deliver the needy who cry out, the afflicted who have no helper." He redeems their lives from oppression and violence, because "precious is their blood in his sight." Under such care, the people bless him, harvests ripple all the way up mountaintops, his name endures forever, and every nation blesses itself through him (72:12–17).

The closing three verses form a grand doxology sealing all of Book II (Psalms 42 through 72): blessing the LORD God of Israel who alone works wonders, praying that the whole earth be filled with His glory, and ending with the editorial line, "This concludes the prayers of David son of Jesse" (72:18–20).

## Key Takeaways

**The supreme test of righteous leadership is how it treats the vulnerable.** Four times the poem highlights *the afflicted* and *the needy* (72:2; 72:4; 72:12–13). Authority pleasing to God never exploits the weak, stepping in instead for those who have no helper and counting their lives precious (72:14).

**Lasting peace grows out of justice.** Verse 3 states clearly that mountains yield peace "through righteousness" (72:3). Durable peace cannot exist while oppression and unfairness remain unaddressed.

**Good leadership refreshes like rain on newly cut grass.** Rather than crushing citizens under taxes or whips, a righteous king arrives like "rain on a mown field" (72:6)—gentle, timely, and life-giving.

**Every good reign aims at filling the whole earth with God's glory.** Every blessing sought for the king rises ultimately to verse 19: "May the whole earth be filled with His glory!" (72:19).

## Deeper Meaning

**This portrait of the ideal king towers far past Solomon and finds full reality only in Jesus.** Even Solomon at his peak fell short, levying heavy burdens and drifting into idolatry (1 Kings 11:1–8; 12:4). No earthly monarch rules "until the moon is no more" (72:7), governs "to the ends of the earth" (72:8), and fulfills Abraham's blessing for every nation (72:17; Genesis 12:3). Every stroke fits Jesus the Messiah alone. He is *King of the poor and oppressed*, launching His ministry by announcing good news to the poor and freedom for the oppressed (Luke 4:18), embodying verses 12 through 14 (72:12–14). Distant rulers bearing *gold of Sheba* in homage (72:10–11; 72:15) recall the Magi traveling from the East to worship infant Jesus with gold, frankincense, and myrrh (Matthew 2:11). And dominion from sea to sea with an everlasting name (72:8; 72:17) reaches completion in Christ's *global eternal kingdom of peace*, where every knee bows at His name (Philippians 2:9–11; Revelation 11:15).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verses 18 and 19 serve as the closing doxology of Book II of the Psalms (72:18–19).** Arranged in five books echoing the five books of Moses, each book closes with a solemn blessing and "Amen" (Psalms 41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52; 106:48; 150:1–6).

**Why does verse 20 say "This concludes the prayers of David" when more Davidic psalms appear later?** That colophon stems from an early stage of compilation, when Books I and II (composed largely of David's psalms) stood together as an earlier completed collection before Books III, IV, and V were added alongside them.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explains that while this prayer applied first to Solomon and his successors, its sweeping majesty intentionally lifts the reader's eyes past earthly thrones to Christ's kingdom. Meanwhile, it also stands as an enduring mirror for all earthly rulers: their chief calling is defending the weak from oppression. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 72)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: accurately captures Calvin's two-level historical and messianic reading of Psalm 72; exact wording not re-verified -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, observes that David could never have prayed a nobler final petition than verse 19 ("May the whole earth be filled with His glory!"). Having asked for God's glory to fill the entire globe, Spurgeon notes, David had nothing greater left to pray for—making verse 20 a fitting close to his prayers. *(The Treasury of David, on Psalm 72)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's famous connection between verse 19 and the closing note of verse 20 is widely quoted from his Psalm 72 exposition -->
