# Psalms 135 — The Lord Is Greater Than Every Idol

## Story

Opening and closing with the shout "Hallelujah!", this song picks up right where the temple worship of Psalm 134 leaves off. The psalmist calls on the servants standing in the courts of the Lord's house to praise Him, because the Lord is good, singing to His name is pleasant, and "For the LORD has chosen Jacob for Himself, Israel as His treasured possession" (135:1–4).

From the call to praise, the song turns to confess God's absolute supremacy. He is greater than all gods, and "Whatever pleases the LORD, He does" in heaven, on earth, in the seas, and in all their depths—He summons the clouds, sends lightning with the rain, and brings out the wind from His storehouses (135:5–7).

That same power shines not only in nature but across the history of redemption. The psalmist traces the road from Egypt into the Promised Land: God struck down the firstborn of Egypt, sent terrifying signs against Pharaoh, defeated mighty rulers like Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan, and gave their land as an inheritance to Israel. Therefore His name endures forever, and He will vindicate His people (135:8–14).

With a sharp, sudden shift, the poem mocks the idols of the surrounding nations: "The idols of the nations are silver and gold, made by the hands of men." They have mouths that cannot speak, eyes that cannot see, ears that cannot hear, and no breath in their mouths—followed by a sobering verdict: "Those who make them become like them, as do all who trust in them" (135:15–18).

At the end, the song returns to vibrant liturgical praise, calling four groups in turn—the house of Israel, the house of Aaron, the house of Levi, and all who fear the Lord—to bless from Zion the God who dwells in Jerusalem (135:19–21).

## Key Takeaways

**Praising God rests on concrete reasons, not empty clichés.** The psalmist lays down three clear grounds: gracious election (135:4), sovereign rule over creation (135:5–7), and rescuing action in history (135:8–12).

**We become like what we worship.** Verse 18 delivers one of the sharpest spiritual insights in the Old Testament: whoever trusts in lifeless things crafted by human hands gradually becomes spiritually blind, deaf, and hollow like the very object they adore.

**The living God acts freely, utterly unlike motionless idols.** While silver and gold statues have to be carried around and cannot even draw breath, the Lord does whatever pleases Him, from rainstorms and lightning to the rise and fall of empires.

## Deeper Meaning

**God's treasured possession in the New Testament.** The title *treasured possession* given to Israel (135:4; Exodus 19:5) is broadened in the New Testament to all who belong to Christ: a chosen people, God's special possession, called to proclaim the excellencies of Him who called them (1 Peter 2:9; Titus 2:14).

**Transformed into the likeness of the One we behold.** If worshiping dead idols makes people numb and lifeless (135:18), the hopeful mirror image in the New Testament is that as believers behold the glory of the living Lord, they are transformed into His image with ever-increasing glory (2 Corinthians 3:18).

## Notes & Nuances

**A "mosaic" poem woven from many books of Scripture.** Several lines in Psalm 135 echo other Old Testament passages almost word for word: verses 1–2 link with Psalms 134:1; verse 7 closely matches Jeremiah 10:13; verse 14 quotes Deuteronomy 32:36; and the satire on idols plus the fourfold call to bless God in verses 15–20 nearly duplicate Psalms 115:4–11. This reflects a familiar liturgical habit in post-exilic Israel: weaving earlier words of Scripture together into a shared hymn of worship.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, lingered over verse 18 and remarked that idolatry is not limited to carving stone or wood in ancient times: anyone who elevates money, prestige, or power to the place of God will sooner or later grow as cold, unfeeling, and empty as the things they serve. *(The Treasury of David, on Psalm 135)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Spurgeon's moral application of verse 18 in his exposition of Psalm 135 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, focused on verse 6—God does whatever pleases Him—to affirm that divine sovereignty is never hemmed in by natural forces or the ambitions of earthly kings. At the same time, he stressed that this sovereign power is always joined to God's goodness toward His people in verses 3–4 and verse 14. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 135)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin's focus on divine sovereignty in verse 6 in his commentary on Psalm 135 -->

**Matthew Henry**, English Puritan pastor, noted how the summons in verses 19–20 moves from the wider nation (the house of Israel), to the sacred ministers (the houses of Aaron and Levi), and finally opens outward to all who fear the Lord (including Gentile converts).
