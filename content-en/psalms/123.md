# Psalms 123 — Lifting Our Eyes for Mercy Amid Contempt

## Story

Psalm 123 is only four verses long—a brief yet intense breath of prayer spoken in the midst of humiliation. Unlike Psalm 121, which looks up to the hills, here the psalmist lifts his gaze straight to heaven: "I lift up my eyes to You, the One enthroned in heaven" (123:1).

From the first-person singular, the prayer merges into the shared voice of the whole community through a familiar, everyday picture: "As the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master," and as the eyes of a maidservant watch the hand of her mistress, so the eyes of God's people look steadily to the LORD their God, waiting patiently until He shows them mercy (123:2).

The final two verses break into a repeated plea: "Have mercy on us, O LORD, have mercy, for we have endured much contempt." Their souls have had more than their fill of the mockery of the complacent and the scorn of the arrogant (123:3–4).

## Key Takeaways

**Looking up to God is how we keep our dignity when the world despises us.** Instead of hanging their heads in shame or glaring back at their mockers, God's people choose to lift their eyes to the One who sits enthroned in heaven (123:1; 123:3–4).

**Waiting on the LORD is like a servant watching a master's hand.** The image in verse 2 conveys three things at once: complete dependence (looking to no one else), attentive readiness to catch the master's slightest gesture, and steadfast patience that refuses to give up until God acts in mercy (123:2).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus understands the pain of contempt from the inside.** God's people cry out because they have endured more than enough scorn from the proud (123:3–4); in the New Testament, the Savior Himself was mocked and treated with contempt by soldiers and rulers as He suffered for sinners (Luke 23:11; Hebrews 12:2).

**Lifting our eyes to God and pleading for mercy.** The cry "Have mercy on us" (123:3) joins a familiar chorus throughout the Gospels—from the two blind men beside the road to the tax collector in the temple (Matthew 20:30–31; Luke 18:13)—drawing near to receive mercy and grace from God's throne (Hebrews 4:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**The historical mood fits the post-exilic era closely.** When the Jews returned to rebuild Jerusalem and its wall in Nehemiah's day, neighboring officials and peoples relentlessly ridiculed and despised them as weak (Nehemiah 2:19; Nehemiah 4:1–4). Although the psalm names no specific event, its emotional tone matches that season deeply.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, noted a subtle detail in verse 2: a servant watches his master's hand both to look for protection against oppressors and to be ready to obey the moment his master signals; in the same way, true faith looks patiently to God until He shows mercy, without chasing wrongful shortcuts. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 123)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Calvin's analysis of the servant's eyes in verse 2, though exact text has not been cross-checked -->
