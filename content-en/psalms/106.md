# Psalms 106 — Human Unfaithfulness and God's Relentless Mercy

## Story

Psalm 106 opens with a beloved refrain of praise: "Give thanks to the LORD, for He is good; His loving devotion endures forever." Wondering who can possibly recount all God's mighty deeds, the psalmist blesses those who uphold justice and begs the LORD to remember him when He brings salvation to His people so he may share their joy (106:1–5).

Right at verse 6, however, praise turns into unflinching national confession: "We have sinned like our fathers; we have done wrong and acted wickedly." The poem traces Israel's repeated rebellions across history, starting right at the Red Sea—where they doubted Him moments after witnessing Egypt's plagues. Even so, "He saved them for the sake of His name," splitting the sea for them to cross and drowning their pursuers until they finally believed and sang His praise (106:6–12).

Yet they quickly forgot His works. Out in the wilderness, greedy cravings drove them to test God; He granted what they demanded yet sent leanness wasting their souls. In camp they grew jealous of Moses and Aaron, bringing the earth splitting open to swallow Dathan and Abiram's faction while fire consumed the wicked. At Horeb they cast a golden calf, trading their glorious God for the statue of a grass-eating ox. God spoke of wiping them out—had not Moses "stood in the breach before Him" to turn away His destructive wrath (106:13–23).

Still the rebellion rolled on. They despised the pleasant Promised Land, grumbled in their tents, and disbelieved His word, prompting His oath that they would fall in the desert and later be scattered among nations. They joined in worshiping Baal of Peor and eating sacrifices offered to the dead until plague erupted—stopped only when Phinehas intervened decisively. By the waters of Meribah they provoked Moses until he lost control and spoke rashly, suffering consequences himself (106:24–33).

Even after entering Canaan, they failed to clear out pagan practices as instructed, mingling with surrounding peoples, copying their customs, and getting ensnared by idols. Worse still, they sacrificed their own sons and daughters to demons, polluting the land with innocent blood—until God's anger burned and He handed them over to foreign oppressors (106:34–42).

Yet the story never ends in extinction. Time after time God rescued them despite stubborn defiance; whenever He heard their cry in distress, He remembered His covenant, relented out of abundant loving devotion, and even moved their captors to pity them (106:43–46).

The psalm closes pleading for God to save and gather His people back from the nations to thank and praise His holy name. Verse 48 supplies the doxology sealing all of Book IV: blessing the LORD from everlasting to everlasting as the entire assembly echoes, "Amen! Hallelujah!" (106:47–48).

## Key Takeaways

**Honest praise walks hand in hand with owning our sin.** Psalm 106 refuses to airbrush national history; naming every failure from the Red Sea to Canaan reveals one undeniable truth: God's people survive solely because His steadfast love endures forever (106:1; 106:6–8).

**Sin slides steadily from ingratitude into idolatry and dehumanization.** The psalm maps that tragic slope clearly: quickly forgetting God's deeds (106:13; 106:21), spiraling into craving, grumbling, and compromise, until plunging so far as sacrificing one's own children to idols (106:35–38).

**One person standing in the gap can redirect history.** Two bright lamps shine inside this grim catalog: Moses stepping into the breach at Horeb and Phinehas acting boldly for purity at Peor (106:23; 106:30–31). God looks for intercessors willing to stand up for His people's good.

## Deeper Meaning

**Moses standing in the breach foreshadows Christ our Mediator.** Stepping between righteous wrath and guilty people (106:23; Exodus 32:11–14) points straight to Jesus, who not only intercedes in prayer, but gave Himself as a ransom absorbing the wrath our sins deserved (1 Timothy 2:5–6; Hebrews 7:25).

**Exchanging God's glory for a grass-eating ox.** Verse 20 exposes the sheer absurdity of idolatry (106:20). Paul picks up that exact thought and vocabulary describing all humanity's sin: exchanging the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal humans and four-footed beasts (Romans 1:23).

**Saving *for the sake of His name* and remembering His covenant.** Even with zero human merit to offer, God rescues His people for His name's sake and His faithful covenant (106:8; 106:45). That exact stream of grace runs straight into the New Testament: He saved us not because of righteous deeds we had done, but because of His mercy in Christ (Titus 3:5; Ephesians 2:4–8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 48 closes Book IV of the Psalter (106:48).** Each of the five books ends with a matching blessing (Psalms 41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52; 106:48). Verse 47's prayer to be gathered back from among the nations suggests completion or widespread use during the Babylonian exile (106:47).

**Sending *a wasting disease* or *leanness into their souls* (106:15).** When Israel craved meat in the desert (Numbers 11:31–34), God handed them exactly what they insisted on while letting both body and spirit waste away. Getting everything our selfish cravings demand can sometimes be a sobering form of discipline.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, notes that in verse 6 the poet never dumps blame exclusively on ancestors, folding his own generation straight in: "We have sinned like our fathers."
