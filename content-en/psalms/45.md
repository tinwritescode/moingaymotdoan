# Psalms 45 — Wedding Song for the Eternal King

## Story

A poet from the sons of Korah opens brimming with inspiration, comparing his tongue to the swift pen of a skilled scribe as he crafts a royal wedding song for the king (45:1).

First he gazes at the bridegroom—the king blessed by God forever, most handsome among the sons of men, with grace poured upon his lips. The poet hails him as a majestic warrior strapping his sword to his side, riding forth in victory for the cause of truth, humility, and righteousness, his sharp arrows subduing every adversary (45:2–5).

Suddenly the song soars far beyond any ordinary earthly monarch: "Your throne, O God, endures forever and ever, and justice is the scepter of Your kingdom. You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness; therefore God, Your God, has anointed You above Your companions with the oil of joy." The scene then returns to an opulent palace fragrant with myrrh, aloes, and cassia, alive with stringed music from ivory halls where the queen stands at the king's right hand adorned in gold of Ophir (45:6–9).

Next the poet turns tenderly to counsel the bride: listen closely, forget your people and your father's house so you may belong wholly to the king, honoring him because he is your lord. Wealthy cities like Tyre will bring gifts to seek her favor. Wearing robes woven with gold, the princess enters the king's palace escorted by joyful bridesmaids amid gladness and celebration (45:10–15).

The hymn closes looking to future generations: the king's sons will step into their ancestors' place as princes across all the earth, while nations will remember and praise the king's name forever and ever (45:16–17).

## Key Takeaways

**True authority belongs hand in hand with truth, humility, and justice.** This king rides out not from greed or cruelty, but "in the cause of truth, humility, and righteousness" (45:4), wielding an upright scepter because He loves righteousness and hates wickedness (45:6–7).

**Entering a new covenant calls for wholehearted devotion.** The advice to the bride—"Forget your people and your father's house" (45:10)—captures the essence of marriage and, more deeply, devotion to God: not erasing one's roots, yet placing this new loyalty ahead of every older bond.

## Deeper Meaning

**Hebrews quotes verses 6 and 7 verbatim to prove that Jesus is both God and the eternal Messiah (45:6–7).** Comparing the Son with the angels, the author of Hebrews cites these exact lines (Hebrews 1:8–9): God the Father addresses the Son as *God*, whose throne stands forever and who is anointed with the oil of joy above His companions. Already in the Old Testament, verses 6 and 7 open a window onto the mystery of a King who shares divine nature (*O God*) while also being anointed by God (*God, Your God*).

**The wedding of King and Queen becomes a prophetic portrait of Christ and the church.** In the New Testament, Jesus is the Bridegroom who loves the church and prepares her radiant, holy, and blameless (Ephesians 5:25–27). The bride entering the royal palace in splendor with her attendants (45:13–15) foreshadows the marriage supper of the Lamb at the end of time (Revelation 19:7–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalm 45 is the only royal wedding song in the entire Book of Psalms.** Originally composed perhaps for the marriage of a Davidic king (many think of Solomon), its language in verses 6–7 and 17 towers so high above any mortal ruler that even ancient Jewish readers understood it messianically.
