# Psalms 32 — The Joy of Being Forgiven

## Story

David opens this psalm celebrating a very specific kind of happiness—not the happiness of the wealthy or the spotless, but the joy of a sinner forgiven: "Blessed is he whose transgressions are forgiven, whose sins are covered! Blessed is the man whose iniquity the LORD does not count against him, in whose spirit there is no deceit" (32:1–2).

To help listeners feel how precious that relief is, he recounts the season he tried to hide his guilt. While he kept silent and refused to confess, his bones wasted away through all-day groaning. God's heavy hand pressed down on his conscience day and night, draining his strength like scorching summer drought (32:3–4).

The breakthrough arrived the instant he stopped hiding. He acknowledged his sin to God, quit making excuses or covering up iniquity—and found forgiveness waiting wide open: "Then I acknowledged my sin to You and did not hide my iniquity. I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,' and You forgave the guilt of my sin" (32:5).

Speaking from personal experience, David urges every godly person to pray while God may still be found, so that when surging floods of trouble rise, God will be their hiding place and surround them with songs of deliverance (32:6–7).

Next the voice shifts to God Himself (or wisdom spoken in God's name): He promises to instruct, guide, and counsel His child with a watchful eye. He warns believers not to act stubborn like a senseless horse or mule that must be steered with bit and bridle just to bring them near (32:8–9).

The psalm finishes with a crisp contrast: many sorrows await the wicked, whereas steadfast love surrounds the one who trusts the LORD. Therefore let the righteous and upright in heart rejoice and shout for joy in Him (32:10–11).

## Key Takeaways

**Hiding sin drains the soul, while confessing sin opens the way to healing.** David vividly paints the emotional and physical toll of a guilty conscience kept locked inside (32:3–4). People hesitate to confess out of fear of punishment, yet before God silence itself becomes the heavier torment, while honest confession leads straight to pardon (32:5).

**God does more than cancel the penalty—He teaches a new way to walk.** Right after granting forgiveness (32:5), God pledges, "I will instruct you and teach you the way you should go" (32:8). Grace never invites carelessness; it invites a responsive heart willing to follow a Father's gentle eye rather than needing bit and bridle like a stubborn mule (32:9).

## Deeper Meaning

**The apostle Paul quotes Psalm 32 directly to explain the heart of justification by faith.** In his letter to the Romans, Paul cites David's opening two verses verbatim (32:1–2) to demonstrate that the blessing of being counted righteous flows not from earning merit through works, but from God's grace forgiving and refusing to count sin against the believer (Romans 4:6–8).

**The pardon celebrated in verses 1 and 2 was paid for in full at the cross.** When David speaks of sin being *lifted*, *covered*, and *not counted*, he relies on God's mercy. In the New Testament, the curtain lifts completely: God does not count believers' trespasses against them because He placed those sins on Christ—the One who knew no sin yet became sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:19; 5:21).

## Notes & Nuances

**Tradition closely links Psalm 32 with Psalm 51.** If Psalm 51 captures David's heartbroken repentance right after the prophet Nathan exposed his sin with Bathsheba (2 Samuel 12), Psalm 32 reads like his testimony afterward—fulfilling his vow that once restored he would teach transgressors God's ways (Psalms 51:13).

**The opening two verses pair three terms for sin with three pictures of forgiveness.** Sin is called *transgression* (rebellion), *sin* (missing the mark), and *iniquity* (crookedness); correspondingly, God *forgives* (lifts away the burden), *covers* (hides out of sight), and *does not count* (cancels from the ledger). Hebrew parallelism layers these terms to assure us that God's pardon is total.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine of Hippo**, fourth- and fifth-century church father, famously loved Psalm 32 so dearly that in his final days he had the Penitential Psalms copied onto the walls of his room so he could read them and weep from his bed.
