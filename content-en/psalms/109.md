# Psalms 109 — Appealing to God Amid Slander and Curses

## Story

Bearing David's name, Psalm 109 rises out of bitter false accusation. He begs the God of his praise not to remain silent, because wicked and deceitful mouths have opened against him, surrounding him with words of hatred and attacking him without cause. Worse still is the sting of betrayal: "They repay me evil for good, and hatred for my love." In answer, David offers one crisp reply: "But I am in prayer" (109:1–5).

Verses 6 through 19 deliver the heaviest volley of curses in the entire Psalter, abruptly switching from plural (*they*) to singular (*him*): appoint a wicked accuser at his right hand, find him guilty in court, and count even his prayer as sin; let his days be few and "may another take his position." The curse reaches family and estate alike: widow and orphans left begging, creditors seizing everything he owns, his line blotted out, his parents' sins left unforgotten. Verses 16 through 19 explain why: because he never thought to show kindness, hounding the poor and brokenhearted to death, loving curses until curses soak into his body like water, into his bones like oil, wrapping round him like a cloak and belt (109:6–19).

Verse 20 seals that stream: may this be the LORD's repayment to my accusers and those speaking evil against me—shifting back to plural (109:20).

From verse 21 onward, the tone softens into fragile, earnest pleading. David begs the Lord to intervene for His name's sake and out of His good steadfast love. He portrays his own exhausted state: poor and needy, wounded at heart, fading like a lengthening evening shadow, shaken off like a locust, knees weak from fasting, body gaunt until foes wag their heads in scorn at the sight of him (109:21–25).

He pleads for God's merciful help so everyone recognizes God's own hand at work, resting on one bright certainty: "Let them curse, but You will bless." Let accusers be clothed in shame while David gives abundant thanks and praises the LORD amid the throng, "For He stands at the right hand of the needy" to defend and rescue them from those who condemn their soul (109:26–31).

## Key Takeaways

**God is the sole safe place to pour out anguish over repaid cruelty.** Raw as the emotion runs in this psalm, David never grabs a blade for personal retaliation, placing every ounce of outrage and betrayal before God in prayer (109:4; 109:20–21).

**Whoever chooses cruelty and cursing reaps what they sowed.** Verses 16 through 18 show biblical justice is not random malice, but moral reaping: someone who mercilessly hounds the poor and delights in cursing ends up wrapped in the very curses they loved (109:16–18).

**God Himself stands at the right hand of the vulnerable.** In direct contrast to a hostile prosecutor standing at the defendant's right hand (109:6), verse 31 declares the LORD Himself stands at the needy person's right hand as Advocate (109:31). Human curses cannot overturn God's blessing (109:28).

## Deeper Meaning

**Prophetic fulfillment in Judas Iscariot.** In the opening days of the church, when the apostles met to replace Judas—who betrayed Jesus and took his own life—Peter quoted verse 8 directly ("May another take his position") alongside Psalm 69 (109:8; Psalms 69:25) to apply to Judas and appoint Matthias to apostolic ministry (Acts 1:20).

**Jesus endured repaid hatred and mocking head-shaking.** A righteous sufferer attacked without cause, repaid evil for good, and jeered by onlookers shaking their heads (109:3–5; 109:25) mirrors Jesus' agony on the cross, where passersby wagged their heads insulting Him (Matthew 27:39; John 15:25).

**The cross lifts forgiveness higher without discarding justice.** Where Old Testament faith handed vengeance over to the righteous God, Jesus on the cross both entrusted Himself to Him who judges justly (1 Peter 2:23) and prayed for the Father to forgive His executioners (Luke 23:34), calling disciples to bless those who curse them (Luke 6:28; Romans 12:14; 12:19).

## Notes & Nuances

**Two major ways interpreters read the curses in verses 6 through 19 (109:6–19).** Because this passage unsettles Christian readers, commentators highlight two thoughtful approaches. In the *traditional reading*, verses 6 through 19 are David's own prayer calling God to judge the ringleader slandering him; in ancient society judicial ruin touched whole households, yet crucially David surrenders judgment to God rather than retaliating himself, and the New Testament cites verse 8 as Scripture addressing the traitor (Acts 1:20). In the *enemy-quotation reading*, several modern scholars point out how verses 2–5 and 20 speak of foes in the plural (*they*) while verses 6–19 suddenly switch to singular (*him*), suggesting David quotes the venomous curses his enemies are shouting against him before begging God in verses 20 and 28 to turn their curse back and bless him instead. Both readings carry serious grammatical and theological weight—and under either view, the psalm never teaches believers to nurse personal hatred or curse neighbors, guiding us instead to lay every wrong before the righteous Judge.
