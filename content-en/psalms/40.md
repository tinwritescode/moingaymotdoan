# Psalms 40 — Obedience Over Sacrifice

## Story

David opens with a vibrant thanksgiving song. He waited patiently for the LORD, and God inclined His ear to hear his cry. God lifted him out of a desolate pit and miry clay, set his feet solidly upon a rock, and placed a new song in his mouth so that many will see, revere, and trust the LORD (40:1–3).

He celebrates the blessing of trusting the LORD rather than turning toward the proud or deceptive. God's wondrous deeds and thoughtful plans for His people are too countless even to list or declare (40:4–5).

Next comes the psalm's deepest passage: David recognizes that what God truly desires from humanity is never bare external ritual, but an open ear and joyful obedience. "Sacrifice and offering You did not desire, but my ears You have opened. Burnt offerings and sin offerings You did not require. Then I said, 'Here I am, I have come—it is written about me in the scroll: I delight to do Your will, O my God; Your law is within my heart'" (40:6–8).

Because God's law lives inside his heart, he refuses to stay quiet about God's grace, boldly proclaiming His righteousness, faithfulness, steadfast love, and salvation before the great assembly (40:9–10).

Unexpectedly in the second half, the tone swings from thanksgiving to urgent distress amid fresh crisis. Troubles beyond number surround him, and his own iniquities outnumber the hairs on his head until his heart fails. He pleads with God not to withhold mercy, begs Him to hurry to the rescue, put his attackers to shame, and gladden everyone who seeks the LORD. The poem closes in humble dependence: "Yet I am poor and needy; may the Lord think of me" (40:11–17).

## Key Takeaways

**God looks for an obedient heart over outward ceremony.** Verses 6 through 8 echo a steady biblical refrain: sacrifices mean nothing without a heart glad to do God's will (1 Samuel 15:22; Hosea 6:6). Once God's law moves from paper into the heart, obedience stops feeling like drudgery and turns into delight (40:8).

**Remembering past rescue fuels prayer in fresh trouble.** The first half of Psalm 40 gives thanks for being pulled from miry clay (40:1–3); the second half cries out from brand-new danger (40:11–17). That movement matches real life: believers thank God for what He has done and lean on that same faithfulness to seek help today.

## Deeper Meaning

**Hebrews quotes verses 6 through 8 directly on the lips of Jesus as He enters the world (40:6–8).** The author of Hebrews cites these exact lines (Hebrews 10:5–7) to show that the blood of bulls and goats could never remove sin. Therefore Christ came to fulfill God's will by offering His own body as a sacrifice once for all (Hebrews 10:8–10).

**Both *ears You have opened* in Hebrew and *a body You have prepared for Me* in Hebrews point to total obedience (Hebrews 10:5).** In Hebrew, verse 6 literally reads "ears You have dug/pierced for me"—meaning either unstopping the ear to listen or recalling the bondservant who voluntarily had his ear pierced to serve his master for life (Exodus 21:6). The ancient Greek Septuagint quoted by Hebrews paraphrases the idea as *a body You have prepared for me*, spotlighting how Christ took on human flesh to obey the Father all the way to death on a cross (Philippians 2:7–8).

## Notes & Nuances

**The final five verses of Psalm 40 appear almost word for word as the entire Psalm 70 (40:13–17).** This reveals how flexibly Israel used the Psalter in worship: a brief urgent plea from a longer composition could stand alone as a crisp prayer for emergencies.

**In verse 12, David confesses *my iniquities* outnumber the hairs on his head—an important reminder for reading Messianic psalms (40:12).** When Hebrews applies verses 6 through 8 to Jesus (Hebrews 10:5–7), that does not mean every surrounding verse (like David's confession of personal guilt in verse 12) attributes sin to Christ; Christ was spotless, fulfilling completely what David only reached toward imperfectly.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, clarifies on verse 6 that God was not abolishing the sacrifices He Himself had ordained under Moses at that time, but confronting hypocrites who treated offerings like bribes replacing obedience.
