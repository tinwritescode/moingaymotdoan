# Psalms 20 — Not in Chariots or Horses, but in the Name of the LORD

## Story

Psalm 20 is the congregation's prayer of blessing over their king on the eve of battle. In the opening five verses, the people address the king directly, praying that the LORD will answer him in the day of distress, send help from the sanctuary on Mount Zion, accept the burnt offerings he has just presented, and grant his heart's desire and every plan (20:1–4). They look ahead in faith to victory's celebration, promising to shout for joy over his triumph and lift up banners in the name of their God (20:5).

At verse 6, the voice switches to a single speaker—perhaps the king himself or a priest announcing assurance: "Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed" with the saving power of His right hand from His holy heaven (20:6).

The final three verses voice the united confession of the entire community, containing the psalm's most famous line: "Some trust in chariots and others in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (20:7). Those who lean on military hardware stumble and fall, whereas God's people rise and stand firm (20:8). The poem closes with a brief cry lifted heavenward: asking the LORD to grant victory and praying that the King answer them on the day they call (20:9).

## Key Takeaways

**True victory comes not from superior weaponry, but from the name of the LORD.** In the ancient world, war chariots represented cutting-edge military might, yet God's people choose to place their trust in God's character and power rather than hardware (20:7–8).

**Prayer is the essential preparation before action.** Before marching out, both king and people offer sacrifices and seek help from God's sanctuary on Zion (20:1–3).

**Faith can celebrate before the battle is even decided.** Verses 5 and 6 show them ready to raise victory banners and declare, "Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed," before combat has begun (20:5–6).

## Deeper Meaning

**The title *His anointed* (*Messiah*) in verse 6 links David directly to Jesus (20:6).** Israel's king going out to fight for his people foreshadows Christ, who stepped into the decisive battle against sin and death and was rescued out of death by God through His resurrection (Hebrews 5:7).

**Refusing to rely on chariots or horses echoes God's teaching across both Testaments (20:7).** Mosaic law forbade Israel's kings from stockpiling warhorses (Deuteronomy 17:16), and Paul reminds the church that the weapons of our warfare are not worldly, but carry divine power (2 Corinthians 10:4).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalms 20 and 21 form a deliberate pair.** Psalm 20 is the prayer offered before the king marches into battle; Psalm 21 is the thanksgiving song after he returns victorious.

**Verse 9 can be punctuated two ways in Hebrew (20:9).** One reading translates "O LORD, save the king! Answer us on the day we call" (referring to the earthly monarch, as in the Berean Standard Bible); another addresses the LORD Himself as King—"O LORD, save! May the King answer us when we call."
