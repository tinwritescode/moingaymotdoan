# Psalms 140 — A Cry for Rescue From Venomous Tongues and Traps

## Story

Attributed to David, Psalm 140 is an urgent cry for rescue from someone surrounded by scheming, violent enemies. The psalmist begs God to shield him from those who stir up conflict every day, turning sharp words into weapons: "They sharpen their tongues like snakes; the venom of vipers is on their lips" (140:1–3).

He repeats his plea, this time picturing the tactics of the arrogant through the imagery of a hunter: they hide snares, spread cords, and set traps along his path to trip him up (140:4–5).

Hemmed in on every side, the tone shifts to a steadfast confession of faith. The psalmist reminds himself who the Lord is: "You are my God," "the strength of my salvation," the One who has already covered his head like a helmet on the day of battle (140:6–7).

Standing on that foundation, he asks God not to let the wicked succeed in their schemes lest they grow even more proud, and prays that the malice and trouble they meant for others would rebound on their own heads, so that slanderers may not remain established in the land (140:8–11).

The poem closes not in bitterness, but in quiet assurance of God's justice: "I know that the LORD upholds justice for the poor and defends the cause of the needy," and the upright will dwell in God's presence giving thanks to His name (140:12–13).

## Key Takeaways

**Slander and malicious plotting are just as brutal as swords.** The picture of snakes' tongues and viper venom (140:3) shows that violence is not limited to physical blows; it also works through carefully sharpened gossip designed to ruin an innocent person's name and life.

**Remembering past deliverance steadies us in new battles.** Surrounded by hidden snares, the psalmist does not stare only at his enemies; he looks back and recalls how God shielded his head on the day of battle (140:7).

**God stands up for the vulnerable.** No matter how clever the schemes of the wicked may be, verse 12 declares that the moral order of the world rests in God's hands—He will champion the afflicted and bring justice to the needy.

## Deeper Meaning

**A diagnosis of universal human sin in Romans.** Paul quotes verse 3 directly—"the venom of vipers is on their lips"—inside his chain of Old Testament texts demonstrating that Jews and Gentiles alike stand under sin's power, with no one righteous on their own before God (Romans 3:13). The portrait of the psalmist's enemies becomes in the New Testament a mirror revealing the common condition of the human heart apart from God.

**God as our shield in spiritual conflict.** The picture of God shielding the believer's head like a helmet on the day of battle (140:7) anticipates the *helmet of salvation* in the armor of God that Paul urges believers to put on (Ephesians 6:17).

## Notes & Nuances

**The word "Selah" shaping the rhythm of the poem.** In the Hebrew text, three *Selah* markers (usually understood as musical interludes or pauses for reflection) appear at the end of verses 3, 5, and 8, clearly framing the psalm's emotional flow: a cry against venomous tongues (140:1–3), a cry against hidden traps (140:4–5), a confession of trust and plea to thwart the wicked (140:6–8), and finally judgment coupled with settled confidence (140:9–13).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, placed this psalm in the period when David was slandered and hunted by Saul along with flatterers such as Doeg and the Ziphites. Calvin stressed that when David asks God not to let the wicked's plans succeed in verse 8, his deepest reason is not merely personal safety, but *lest they be exalted*—so that evildoers will not grow arrogant and despise God. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 140)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin's historical setting under Saul and his observation on verse 8 -->
