# Psalms 124 — If the LORD Had Not Been on Our Side

## Story

Psalm 124 bears the heading of a Song of Ascents of David—brief yet dramatic, like the retelling of a hair's-breadth escape from death. The psalmist invites the whole nation to repeat a chilling *what-if*: "If the LORD had not been on our side" when enemies rose up in fury against them—he repeats the supposition a second time to let its weight sink in—they would have swallowed them alive, and the raging floodwaters and torrent would have engulfed and swept away their very lives (124:1–5).

From that shuddering glance back at the abyss, the song erupts in grateful praise: blessed be the LORD, who did not give His people as prey to the teeth of wild beasts! The imagery turns to a vivid picture of deliverance: "We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowler; the snare is broken, and we have escaped" (124:6–7).

The closing verse seals the entire poem with a confession of faith cherished across the history of the Church: "Our help is in the name of the LORD, the Maker of heaven and earth" (124:8).

## Key Takeaways

**Looking back at past dangers shows clearly that we survived solely because of God.** The question *what if the LORD had not been on our side?* (124:1–2) shatters the illusion that we carried ourselves through the storm. The enemy's force is like a predator, a flash flood, and a hunter's trap; the one thing that changed the ending was the presence of the LORD (124:3–7).

**A trapped bird cannot break its own snare—God breaks the snare.** In verse 7, God's people picture themselves as a tiny, helpless bird already caught in the fowler's net; yet the snare snaps open and they fly free (124:7). It is one of Scripture's most concise pictures of saving grace.

## Deeper Meaning

**If God is for us, who can be against us?** The opening line, "If the LORD had not been on our side" (124:1), finds its eternal answer in the letter to the Romans: because God did not spare His own Son, but gave Him up for us all, if God is for us, who can be against us (Romans 8:31–32)?

**Rescued from the devil's snare.** The image of the soul escaping the fowler's snare (124:7) echoes in the New Testament where the apostle Paul speaks of God rescuing people from the trap of the devil to bring them into the freedom of Christ (2 Timothy 2:26; Galatians 5:1).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 8 became a classic opening call in Reformed liturgy.** Beginning in sixteenth-century Geneva, John Calvin adopted Psalm 124:8 ("Our help is in the name of the LORD, the Maker of heaven and earth") as the opening votum for public worship, a tradition that many Protestant churches still cherish today.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, treasured verse 8 so deeply that he made it the opening declaration of worship in Geneva.
