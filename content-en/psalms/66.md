# Psalms 66 — Through Fire and Water into a Place of Abundance

## Story

This vibrant thanksgiving hymn moves through two distinct voices: the first half rings with the whole congregation's praise, and the second shares an individual's personal testimony. Opening with a global summons, all the earth is invited to shout joyfully, sing the glory of God's name, and gaze at His awesome deeds. God's people look back to two foundational miracles of their history: turning the Red Sea into dry land and letting them cross the Jordan River on foot (Exodus 14:21–22; Joshua 3:14–17). He rules forever over all nations, keeping the rebellious from exalting themselves (66:1–7).

The call to bless God swells again among the peoples, now grounded in a recent ordeal Israel survived. They acknowledge openly that God tested and refined them like silver in a furnace, letting them fall into a net, bear crushing loads on their backs, endure foes riding over their heads, and pass "through fire and water." Yet His goal was never ruin: "You brought us into a place of abundance" (66:8–12).

At verse 13, the voice shifts cleanly to the singular *I*. A rescued worshiper enters God's temple bringing burnt offerings to keep the exact vows made while distressed (66:13–15).

Turning to everyone who fears God, he invites them to hear his story: "Come and listen... and I will declare what He has done for my soul." He shares one penetrating spiritual lesson: had he cherished sin in his heart, the Lord would not have listened. Yet God truly did listen and attend to the voice of his prayer—so he concludes blessing the God who neither rejected his prayer nor withheld His steadfast love (66:16–20).

## Key Takeaways

**God allows fiery trials to purify, never to destroy.** Being "refined like silver" (66:10) reveals that the crucible burns away impurities. Passing through fire and water ultimately leads out into "a place of abundance"—spacious, overflowing grace (66:12).

**Promises made in crisis should be honored in peace.** Plenty of people make earnest vows to God when panicked, only to forget them once calm returns. This worshiper models integrity: stepping into God's house to fulfill every pledge his lips spoke in trouble (66:13–14).

**Harboring sin clogs the channel of prayer.** Verse 18 poses an honest gut check: "If I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened" (66:18). God warmly hears repentant sinners, yet refuses to indulge a heart stubbornly hugging sin.

**Experiencing God's rescue invites sharing your story to lift others.** Saying "Come and listen... and I will declare what He has done for my soul" (66:16) reminds us that personal testimony enriches corporate worship.

## Deeper Meaning

**Trials refining faith like silver reappear in 1 Peter.** Peter comforts suffering Christians by telling them their faith—far more precious than perishable gold—is tested and proven genuine through fire (1 Peter 1:6–7). Across both Testaments, testing refines faith and honors God.

**Passing through fire and water into abundance echoes God's enduring pledge.** Going "through fire and water" (66:12) links directly with Isaiah's promise that when God's people pass through waters or walk through fire, He will be with them so neither floods overwhelm nor flames consume (Isaiah 43:2). In the New Testament, God weaves every trial together for the good of those who love Him (Romans 8:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does the psalm switch cleanly from *we* (66:1–12) to *I* (66:13–20)?** This pattern reflects authentic Israelite temple liturgy: first the choir or whole congregation praises God for national deliverances, and then the king or an individual worshiper steps forward to present a thank offering and testify publicly before the assembly.

**Verse 18 does not mean only sinless people may pray (66:18).** The verb *cherish* means nursing, treasuring, and clinging stubbornly to sin without wanting to let go. Scripture consistently promises God hears the broken cry of the repentant (Psalms 51:17), while rejecting hypocritical prayer paired with willful wickedness (Isaiah 1:15).

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, pauses carefully on verse 18. *Cherishing iniquity*, he explains, means entertaining sin like a welcome guest inside the heart's private chamber—impossible to combine with expecting God to grant our requests.
