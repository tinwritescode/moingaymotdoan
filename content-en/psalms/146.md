# Psalms 146 — Trusting the Defender of the Vulnerable

## Story

Psalm 146 launches the five final *Hallel* psalms that bring the book of Psalms to its grand close (Psalms 146–150), each one beginning and ending with the shout "Hallelujah!" The psalmist speaks straight to his own heart—"Praise the LORD, O my soul!"—vowing to praise God all his life, as long as he has breath (146:1–2).

Immediately after that personal vow comes a sober, practical warning against misplacing trust: "Put not your trust in princes, in mortal men, who cannot save." The reason is stark and undeniable: "When their spirit departs, they return to the ground; on that very day their plans perish" (146:3–4).

In sharp contrast to fleeting human rulers, the psalmist pronounces blessed the person whose help is "the God of Jacob." This God is not only the Maker of heaven, earth, and sea who keeps faith forever; He actively champions the most vulnerable members of society: "He executes justice for the oppressed and gives food to the hungry. The LORD sets the prisoners free, the LORD opens the eyes of the blind, the LORD lifts those who are weighed down, the LORD loves the righteous." He watches over foreigners, sustains the fatherless and the widow, and frustrates the ways of the wicked (146:5–9).

The song closes with a declaration rising far above every short-lived earthly dynasty: "The LORD reigns forever," King over Zion to all generations (146:10).

## Key Takeaways

**No matter how imposing human power looks, it lasts only as long as one breath.** Verses 3–4 do not tell us to despise leaders or civic order; they warn us never to hang our ultimate hope on them. Every human promise and blueprint halts the instant breath runs out.

**The Maker of the cosmos sides with those who have no voice.** What is astonishing in verses 6–9 is the seamless bridge: the very God who crafted heaven, earth, and sea introduces Himself as the One who feeds the hungry, opens blind eyes, and protects the stranger, orphan, and widow.

**Lifelong praise is the natural answer to a King who never dies.** Earthly monarchs pass away and their plans collapse with them, yet the Lord reigns forever (146:10)—which is why believers can safely devote their entire lives to praising Him.

## Deeper Meaning

**The credentials of the Messiah in Jesus.** When John the Baptist sent disciples from prison asking if Jesus was truly the Coming One, Jesus answered with the very deeds celebrated in Psalms 146:7–8 and Isaiah 61:1—the blind receive sight, the lame walk, and the poor hear good news (Matthew 11:4–5; Luke 4:18). Jesus literally gave bread to thousands of hungry people and opened the eyes of a man born blind (John 6:11; John 9:6–7).

**An unshakable kingdom.** The confession "The LORD reigns forever" (146:10) becomes the anchor of the New Testament church, receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken so we may worship God acceptably with reverence and awe (Hebrews 12:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**Opening the final Hallel sequence (Psalms 146–150).** The last five psalms of the Psalter build a rising symphony of praise: moving from personal devotion and compassion for the weak (Psalm 146), to the restoration of Jerusalem and the rhythms of nature (Psalm 147), expanding out to all creation in heaven and on earth (Psalm 148), to the victorious song of the faithful assembly (Psalm 149), and finally exploding into the universal chorus of everything that has breath (Psalm 150).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, pointed out that the human heart's deepest temptation is constantly grasping for visible props—rulers, wealth, or political clout. Verses 3–4 pull away that glittering curtain to remind us that God alone deserves our wholehearted trust. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 146)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin's exposition on the fragility of human support in verses 3–4 -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, especially loved verse 8—*the LORD opens the eyes of the blind*—and remarked that the nine acts listed in verses 6–9 read like a complete sketch of Jesus' earthly ministry. *(The Treasury of David, on Psalm 146)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Spurgeon connecting verses 6–9 of Psalm 146 directly to the ministry of Christ -->

**Matthew Henry**, English Puritan pastor, noted that in verse 1 the psalmist does not start by lecturing others, but addresses his *own soul* first.
