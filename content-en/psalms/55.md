# Psalms 55 — Betrayed by a Close Friend, Casting Your Burden on God

## Story

The poem opens with an urgent cry from a turbulent heart. David pleads with God to listen and not hide Himself, because he feels restless and overwhelmed by the clamor and pressure of the wicked. Anguish writhes inside his chest, the terrors of death crash over him, and trembling grips his whole body. He longs for wings like a dove to fly far out into the wilderness and find quiet shelter from the raging wind and storm (55:1–8).

From inner dread, he turns to describe the chaos gripping his city. Violence, strife, malice, oppression, and fraud prowl day and night along its walls and through its marketplaces. He begs God to confuse the tongues and divide the schemes of those corrupting the city (55:9–11).

Then the deepest wound comes into view. Had an open enemy insulted him, he could have endured it or hidden out of sight. Instead, the traitor is his equal, his companion, his trusted friend—someone with whom he once shared sweet fellowship while walking together through the crowd into the house of God. Pierced by such treachery, David bursts out praying that sudden ruin overtake these ruthless plotters (55:12–15).

Right inside the emotional storm, he steadies himself through regular prayer: "Evening, morning, and noon, I cry out in distress, and He hears my voice." He trusts that the eternal God will redeem his soul in peace from the battle against him and humble those who refuse to change or fear God—people whose words glide smooth as butter and oil while hiding drawn swords inside, breaking covenant with their own friends (55:16–21).

The final two verses ring out as counsel both to himself and to every reader: "Cast your burden upon the LORD and He will sustain you; He will never let the righteous be shaken." Bloodthirsty and deceitful men will not live out half their days before plunging into the pit, while David chooses a quiet, unshakable stand: "But I will trust in You" (55:22–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Betrayal by a close companion wounds deeper than any strike from an enemy.** You can brace yourself against an open adversary; watching someone who walked beside you into worship turn around and stab your back shatters the heart (55:12–14). Scripture refuses to hide the very human urge to flee from that hurt (55:6–8).

**Instead of escaping to the desert, David escapes into steady prayer.** Wishing for dove's wings is understandable, yet the true lifeline appears in verses 16 and 17: crying out to God morning, noon, and evening (55:16–17). Running away only changes scenery; prayer changes what holds you up.

**Smooth words never guarantee an honest heart.** Mouths slicker than butter and softer than oil concealing drawn swords warn us sharply against hypocrisy (55:21). True loyalty shows up in keeping covenant, never in polished flattery (55:20).

**Casting your burden on God keeps you from being shaken.** God may not remove every enemy overnight, yet He promises to sustain the person carrying the load (55:22). The believer's steadiness comes from God's upholding arm.

## Deeper Meaning

**Being betrayed by a trusted companion anticipates Judas's betrayal of Jesus.** The friend who once walked in warm fellowship to God's house turning traitor (55:12–14) foreshadows Judas Iscariot—one of the Twelve who ate at Jesus' table and then handed Him over with a kiss (Matthew 26:47–50; John 13:18).

**The call to cast your burden on the Lord is echoed directly by the apostle Peter.** Verse 22 stands directly behind Peter's beloved encouragement to suffering believers: cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you (55:22; 1 Peter 5:7). Jesus extends that same invitation to everyone weary and burdened (Matthew 11:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**Many commentators connect this psalm with Absalom's rebellion and Ahithophel's defection.** In 2 Samuel 15–17, Ahithophel—David's most trusted counselor—joined Absalom to plot the king's murder. Although the psalm's heading names no specific event, the turmoil inside Jerusalem and the agony of betrayal by an intimate advisor match that crisis remarkably closely.

**The plea that foes go down *alive to Sheol* echoes Korah's rebellion (55:15).** In Numbers, when Korah and his faction revolted against Moses, the earth opened and swallowed them alive into the grave (Numbers 16:30–33). David borrows that memory to appeal for God's decisive judgment on brazen treason rather than taking personal vengeance.

**Praying *evening, morning, and noon* reflects the Jewish reckoning of the day (55:17).** In Jewish tradition, a new day begins at sunset (Genesis 1:5), placing evening first. That daily rhythm of praying three times a day later characterizes the prophet Daniel as well (Daniel 6:10).

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, notes on verse 22 that the Hebrew word for *burden* can also mean *what God has allotted to you*.
