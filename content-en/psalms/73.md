# Psalms 73 — Envying the Wicked and the Turning Point in the Sanctuary

## Story

Psalm 73 opens Book III of the Psalter (Psalms 73 through 89), bearing the name of worship leader Asaph. The poem begins with a hard-won conclusion: "Surely God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart." Yet immediately he confesses his feet had nearly slipped when he saw the wicked thriving effortlessly (73:1–3).

He lays bare his crisis of faith with total candor. Arrogant people seem immune to normal human pain and struggles; pride hangs around their necks like a necklace, violence clothes them like a robe, their bodies grow sleek with wealth, and they sneer toward heaven daring, "How can God know?" Carefree and comfortable, they just pile up more riches. Looking at himself, Asaph grows bitter, wondering whether keeping his heart clean has been completely pointless since affliction and discipline strike him every morning. He barely holds back from voicing those doubts aloud lest he betray the next generation of God's children, yet brooding over the puzzle alone only sinks him deeper into exhaustion (73:4–16).

Right at the center comes the great turning point: "until I entered the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end." Inside God's presence, his entire perspective snaps into focus. Those wicked people who looked rock-solid are actually standing on "slippery ground": in a single instant they are swept away in terror, vanishing like a dream the moment someone wakes up (73:17–20).

Looking back on his bitterness, Asaph feels ashamed at how senseless and beastlike he acted before God. Yet God never let him go: "Yet I am always with You; You hold my right hand. You guide me with Your counsel, and later receive me in glory." From a healed heart bursts one of the deepest confessions in all Scripture: whom have I in heaven but You, and on earth there is nothing I desire besides You; even when flesh and heart fail, "God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever." He ends in serene gladness: "But as for me, it is good to be near God" (73:21–28).

## Key Takeaways

**Measuring life on a short-term yardstick makes faith slip easily.** Comparing immediate bank accounts and comfort makes the wicked look like big winners while the upright appear shortchanged (73:3–14). Only viewing "their end" in eternity's light reveals that prosperity without God is a fleeting dream perched on slippery ice (73:17–20).

**God's sanctuary realigns distorted sight.** Asaph could not untangle the riddle by brooding in isolation (73:16). Stepping into worship and God's presence broke the spell of bitterness (73:17).

**Even when we are foolish and doubting, God keeps holding our right hand.** Verses 22 and 23 shine with tender grace: Asaph stayed safe not because he was clever enough to grip God tightly, but because God held his right hand and refused to let him tumble over the edge (73:22–23).

**The believer's supreme reward is God Himself.** Early on Asaph envied the wicked for wealth and ease; by the close he discovers nothing in heaven or on earth compares to being near God—"my portion forever" (73:25–28).

## Deeper Meaning

**Being guided by God and afterward *received into glory* opens a window onto eternal life.** In the Old Testament, verse 24 shines as one of the clearest glimmers of life beyond death: fellowship with God runs so deep that even when "my flesh and my heart fail" (73:26), He receives His own into glory (73:24). The New Testament brings that hope into full daylight through Jesus, who goes ahead to prepare a place and promises to take His people to Himself (John 14:2–3; 2 Corinthians 4:16–18).

**The folly of godless wealth reappears in Jesus' parable.** Picturing the affluent wicked swept away in an instant (73:12; 73:19) mirrors the story of the rich man and Lazarus, where eternity's great reversal exposes the fragility of earthly luxury (Luke 16:19–25).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 15 models wise stewardship during spiritual doubt (73:15).** Even while inwardly wondering whether purity was wasted effort (73:13), Asaph refused to broadcast unprocessed cynicism across the congregation lest he "betray the generation of Your children." Instead, he carried his doubts straight into the sanctuary before God and told his story only after finding God's answer.

**The word *good* opens (73:1) and closes (73:28) the poem like a sturdy frame.** It starts with a broad confession that God is good to His people and lands on a deeply personal discovery: drawing near to God is the highest good of all.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, regularly pointed to Psalm 73 as medicine for a classic temptation of believers: envying the wicked when everything goes their way.
