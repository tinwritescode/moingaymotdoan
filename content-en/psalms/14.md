# Psalms 14 — Living as Though God Does Not Exist

## Story

David begins with a blunt verdict: "The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God.'" The fallout of that posture shows up at once in daily life: they are corrupt, their deeds are vile, and there is no one who does good (14:1).

From heaven, the LORD Himself looks down on humanity to see if any understand and seek after God. The verdict is bleak: all have turned aside, together they have become corrupt, and there is no one who does good—"not even one" (14:2–3).

Next the poem confronts evildoers directly: have they no knowledge, devouring God's people like bread and never calling upon the LORD? (14:4). Yet dread will overtake them, for God stands with the generation of the righteous; they may frustrate the plans of the poor, yet the LORD remains their refuge (14:5–6). The psalm closes with an ardent longing: that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion, so that when the LORD restores His captive people, Jacob will rejoice and Israel will be glad (14:7).

## Key Takeaways

**Biblical *folly* is a moral stance, not merely an intellectual flaw.** The fool described here does not lack cleverness, but banishes God from the heart in order to indulge corruption and prey on the weak (14:1; 14:4).

**Sin is the universal condition of humanity.** When the LORD scans the earth from heaven, He finds not a single exception who is righteous on their own—"not even one" (14:2–3).

**The only hope for God's people comes from God Himself.** In a broken world, salvation does not arise from human effort, but comes out of Zion—the place where the LORD dwells (14:6–7).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul quotes verses 1 through 3 as the backbone of his universal indictment of human sin in Romans.** He cites "There is no one who does good, not even one" (14:3; cf. Romans 3:10–12) to prove that both Jews and Gentiles alike need redemption through faith in Christ, justified freely by His grace (Romans 3:23–24).

**The longing "Oh, that the salvation of Israel would come out of Zion!" finds its fulfillment in Christ the Savior (14:7).** Paul echoes this hope when writing that the Deliverer will come from Zion and remove godlessness from Jacob (Romans 11:26).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalm 14 is nearly identical to Psalm 53.** The most noticeable difference is that Psalm 14 favors the covenant name *LORD* (*YHWH*), whereas Psalm 53 uses *God* (*Elohim*) and alters verses 5 and 6 slightly. Having the same poem appear twice across different collections within the Psalter shows how widely ancient congregations sang it.

**The Hebrew word for *fool* is *nabal*—also the name of the stubborn man Nabal in 1 Samuel 25.** It describes someone hard-hearted, ungrateful, and contemptuous toward both God and neighbor.
