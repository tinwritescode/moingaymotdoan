# Psalms 69 — Sinking in Deep Waters and Scorned for God's Sake

## Story

The poem opens with the frantic cry of a drowning man. Waters rise right up to his neck, his feet sink into deep mire with no foothold, and rushing floods sweep overhead. His throat grows raw from calling out and his eyes fail waiting for God. People hating him without cause outnumber the hairs on his head, demanding he pay back what he never stole (69:1–4).

Even while candidly confessing he is not sinless before God—"You know my folly, O God, and my guilt is not hidden from You"—he worries lest his downfall discourage others hoping in the Lord. The real reason for his public disgrace is loyalty to God: for God's sake he bears scorn, treated as a stranger by his own brothers, because "zeal for Your house has consumed me, and the insults of those who insult You have fallen on me." When he weeps, fasts, and wears sackcloth, elders gossip about him at the city gate and drunkards turn him into mocking songs (69:5–12).

From the pit of despair, David appeals again to God's boundless mercy, pleading to be pulled from the mire and deep waters before they swallow him whole. Worst of all is crushing loneliness: scorn has broken his heart—"I looked for sympathy, but there was none; for comforters, but I found no one." Instead of comfort, adversaries offer him "poison for food" and "for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink" (69:13–21).

Out of raw agony bursts a fierce torrent of judgment prayers aimed at bullies trampling someone already wounded. He prays that their own table turn into a snare, their eyes grow dim, their dwelling lie deserted without an inhabitant, and their names be blotted out of the Book of Life (69:22–28).

Yet the psalm does not stop in anger—it climbs into ringing praise and hope. Still afflicted and aching, David trusts God's salvation will lift him high; he vows to praise God's name in song and thanksgiving—pleasing the LORD far more than sacrificing an ox. The humble and seekers of God will see and take heart, for the LORD hears the needy and never despises His captives. Summoning heaven, earth, and seas to join the chorus, he ends certain that God will save Zion and rebuild Judah's cities for the descendants of those who love His name (69:29–36).

## Key Takeaways

**Devotion to God can bring loneliness and ridicule.** David suffers hostility not for harming neighbors, but because "zeal for Your house has consumed me" (69:9). Standing with God can turn someone into a stranger even inside their own family (69:8).

**Scripture never minimizes deep loneliness.** Looking around for sympathy and comfort and finding "no one" (69:20) shows how honestly the Bible names those bleak seasons when everyone seems gone, inviting us to lay that exact ache before God.

**Heartfelt thanksgiving pleases God more than outward sacrifice.** Echoing Psalm 51 (Psalms 51:16–17), David affirms that singing praise with gratitude delights the LORD more than presenting a prized bull on the altar (69:30–31).

**God never looks down on the needy or imprisoned.** Even while trampled by human cruelty, the believer's sure anchor remains God's attentive, redeeming heart (69:33).

## Deeper Meaning

**Psalm 69 is one of the Old Testament passages most frequently quoted in the New Testament regarding Jesus' suffering.** Detail after detail of David's anguish becomes a prophetic mirror of Christ. When Jesus drove merchants from the Jerusalem temple, His disciples immediately remembered verse 9—"Zeal for Your house will consume Me" (69:9; John 2:17)—and Paul later cited the second half of that verse to show how Christ absorbed insults aimed at God (Romans 15:3). "Those who hate me without cause" (69:4) was quoted directly by Jesus about the world's hostility toward Him (John 15:25), while receiving sour wine in thirst (69:21) was fulfilled literally on the cross (Matthew 27:34; John 19:28–29). Finally, Peter cited verse 25 about a deserted habitation regarding Judas Iscariot's fate (69:25; Acts 1:20), and Paul cited verses 22 and 23 when discussing temporary spiritual hardening among parts of Israel rejecting the gospel (69:22–23; Romans 11:9–10).

## Notes & Nuances

**How can we read the stark judgment prayers of verses 22 through 28 fairly (69:22–28)?** Asking for foes to be "blotted out of the Book of Life" hits hard (69:28). Three complementary lenses help read this passage faithfully. First, it is an *appeal for covenant justice*: a slandered, isolated victim beaten down while already suffering (69:26) calls on the righteous Judge to halt unchecked cruelty rather than grabbing a weapon for private revenge. Second, it serves as a *sober warning about hardened rebellion*: in the New Testament, Peter and Paul quote these lines not as personal curses, but as solemn confirmations of the tragic consequences awaiting deliberate rejection of the Messiah (Acts 1:20; Romans 11:9–10). Third, place it *beside Jesus' own prayer from the cross*: although Jesus fulfilled line after line of Psalm 69, on Calvary He never prayed verses 22–28 over His executioners, crying instead, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34)—teaching Christians in gospel light to hate sin while pleading mercy for people.

**How can verse 5's confession of *my guilt* fit the sinless Jesus (69:5)?** This illustrates a key rule for Messianic psalms: first and foremost, this poem is David's authentic prayer—a real sinner who honestly owns his folly (69:5)—whose experiences of suffering for righteousness then serve as prophetic foreshadowings of Christ. Not every line describes Jesus' personal nature; rather, the New Testament spotlights the specific strands of righteous suffering that culminate in Him.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth-century bishop in North Africa, reads Psalm 69 as the voice of the *whole Christ* (*Christus Totus*)—Christ the Head united with His Body, the church. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explains that the poem arose first out of David's genuine affliction, and because David stood as a prophetic type of Christ, the apostles rightly applied its words to Jesus.
