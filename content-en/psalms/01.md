# Psalms 1 — Two Ways

## Story

The opening poem of the entire Book of Psalms does not name God immediately, but speaks of a blessed person. This person knows how to say no to a three-step downward slide: not walking in the counsel of the wicked, not standing in the path of sinners, and not sitting in the seat of mockers (1:1).

Instead of finding pleasure in those places, the blessed person delights in the law of the LORD and meditates on His word day and night (1:2). As a result, this life is like a tree planted beside streams of water: it bears fruit in its season, its leaf never withers, and whatever the person does prospers (1:3).

Then the poem's tone turns sharply to the other side. Not so the wicked; they are merely like chaff that the wind blows away (1:4). On the day of judgment, the wicked will not stand, nor will sinners enter the assembly of the righteous (1:5). The poem closes with a crisp summary: the LORD knows the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked will perish (1:6).

## Key Takeaways

**We become like what we root ourselves in.** The blessed person chooses to stay close to God's word like a tree sending its roots down by a stream, gaining enduring vitality even when dry seasons arrive (1:2–3). The wicked have no roots and are therefore weightless, like chaff caught by the wind (1:4).

**True happiness begins with delight in God's word.** The law here is not a suffocating burden, but something that brings joy and draws the reader to ponder it day and night (1:2).

**Moral decline usually happens in small steps.** The first verse traces a clear downward progression: first walking according to bad advice, then lingering and growing comfortable on that path, and finally settling down among mockers (1:1).

**The final outcome of both paths rests in God's hands.** For a time the wicked may appear secure, yet the poem fixes its gaze on the judgment and on the LORD's personal knowledge of the righteous (1:5–6).

## Deeper Meaning

**Psalm 1 serves as the gateway into the entire Psalter.** Before the reader enters songs of exuberant praise or cries of lament in distress, the very first psalm poses a foundational question: which path are you walking?

**The image of the tree beside streams of water links directly to the prophets and to Jesus' own words.** Jeremiah uses the very same picture for the one who trusts in the LORD (Jeremiah 17:7–8). In the New Testament, Jesus likewise speaks of two ways—the narrow gate leading to life and the wide road leading to destruction (Matthew 7:13–14)—and calls Himself the true vine who gives life to the branches (John 15:5).

**Jesus is the Blessed Man in the fullest sense.** No human being has ever kept every word of verses 1 and 2 perfectly (1:1–2). Reading this poem in the light of the New Testament, believers recognize Christ as the only One who always delighted completely in the Father's will, and all who are united to Him share in that life.

## Notes & Nuances

**Hebrew poetry rhymes thoughts rather than sounds.** Here we see clear antithetical parallelism: a tree rooted by water stands over against chaff blown by the wind; the way of the righteous contrasts with the way of the wicked (1:3–6). Placing opposite images side by side is how Hebrew verse sharpens its core point.

**The word *meditate* in verse 2 originally means to murmur or speak softly aloud.** Ancient readers rarely read silently with their eyes alone; they recited God's word quietly under their breath so it would sink deep into the heart, just as the LORD instructed Joshua (Joshua 1:8).

**The promise that *whatever he does will prosper* is not a formula for material wealth.** Read within the whole Book of Psalms—where the righteous also face suffering and injustice—verse 3 speaks of a fruitful, resilient life that fulfills God's purpose, not an exemption from hardship (1:3).

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth-century bishop in North Africa, reads Psalm 1 first of all as speaking of Jesus Himself, the One who never stepped onto Adam's path of sin. In Augustine's view, all who belong to Him by grace share in the blessedness of this Righteous Man. *(Expositions on the Psalms, on Psalm 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's Christological reading at the start of Expositions on the Psalms is famous and widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, suggests that whoever compiled the Book of Psalms deliberately placed Psalm 1 at the front as a general preface to the whole collection. He emphasizes that right prayer and worship must begin with loving and diligently studying God's word. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: treating Psalm 1 as a preface commending devotion to God's word is familiar in Calvin; exact wording not re-verified -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, compares Psalm 1 to a prologue introducing readers to the treasures of the entire book. He highlights the three verbs *walk*, *stand*, and *sit* in verse 1 as three downward stages of sin: people start by taking bad counsel, grow accustomed to wrongdoing, and end up scoffing at what is holy. *(The Treasury of David, on Psalm 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the three-stage progression of walk, stand, and sit is a very well-known point in Spurgeon's exposition of Psalm 1 -->

**C. S. Lewis**, twentieth-century British writer and literary scholar, observes that modern readers often hear the word *law* as dry or restrictive, and are therefore surprised to find the psalmist delighting in God's law.
