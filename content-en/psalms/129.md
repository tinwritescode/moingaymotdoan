# Psalms 129 — Oppressed Yet Never Conquered

## Story

Continuing the sequence of *Songs of Ascents*, the whole nation of Israel speaks with one voice to tell its own painful history. From the nation's earliest youth, enemies have oppressed them time and again, yet the song repeats a resilient refrain: "Many a time they have persecuted me from my youth, yet they have not prevailed against me" (129:1–2).

The imagery of suffering turns stark and harrowing as the poet compares the lash marks and yoke of bondage laid upon his people to farming: "The plowmen plowed over my back; they made their furrows long." Yet the story turns sharply through God's intervention: "The LORD is righteous; He has cut the cords of the wicked" (129:3–4).

From past deliverance, the song turns to a prayer against present foes who hate Zion. The psalmist asks that their schemes wither quickly "like grass on the rooftops, which withers before it can grow"—too sparse to fill a reaper's hand or gather into sheaves, so barren that passersby never even utter the customary harvest blessing over them (129:5–8).

## Key Takeaways

**A history of suffering becomes proof of endurance through God.** Israel does not hide the scars left from slavery in Egypt through centuries of later invasions. What matters is not that they escaped being struck, but that no enemy ever managed to wipe them out.

**God rescues by severing the cords of oppression.** The picture of the LORD cutting the ropes of the wicked (129:4) shows that His righteousness is never merely theoretical. He acts decisively so His people are not tied forever to a yoke of bondage.

**Plots against God's people have no deep roots.** Grass sprouting on ancient packed-earth rooftops shoots up fast after rain, yet has no soil beneath it to put down roots and shrivels the moment the hot sun hits. The psalmist uses that sight to declare that the success of oppressors is fleeting and leaves no lasting harvest.

## Deeper Meaning

**Furrows on the back and the scourged body of Christ.** The picture of a back plowed with long furrows (129:3) has led generations of Christians straight to the lash marks across Jesus' back before His crucifixion (Matthew 27:26; Isaiah 50:6). Just as Israel bore deep wounds without being destroyed, Christ suffered death, yet death could not hold Him (Acts 2:24).

**The Church persecuted yet never defeated.** The declaration that enemies have attacked repeatedly yet never prevailed (129:2) echoes in Jesus' promise that the gates of Hades will not prevail against His Church (Matthew 16:18) and in Paul's testimony that believers are hard-pressed on every side yet not crushed, struck down yet not destroyed (2 Corinthians 4:8–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**The harvest greeting in verse 8.** In Ruth 2:4, when Boaz walks out to visit his reapers in the field, he greets them with "The LORD be with you," and they reply with a blessing in return. In Psalm 129:8, the silence of passersby who withhold that warm harvest greeting paints a vivid picture: Zion's haters will have no real harvest worth celebrating.

**The step-like opening typical of the Songs of Ascents.** Verse 1 opens a line, inserts the invitation "let Israel now say," and then verse 2 repeats the entire line before pushing the thought forward. This stair-step repetition creates a stately rhythm, like call-and-response between a song leader and the pilgrim crowd.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, observed that the plowed furrows on the back in verse 3 depict the extreme agony of God's people down through the ages, from Egypt to the Roman emperors. He pointed to verse 4 as the turning point: no matter how sharp the plowshare, it becomes useless the instant God slices through the wicked's harness ropes. *(The Treasury of David, on Psalm 129)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Spurgeon's comments on the plowed-back image and God cutting the cords of the wicked in Psalm 129 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, found in this psalm a word of comfort for the Church in every age: facing relentless attacks from the very beginning is no sign that God has abandoned His people, but the ordinary experience of the covenant community. In his view, verses 6–8 remind believers that hostile powers may sprout thickly for a moment, yet possess no lasting foundation before God. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 129)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin's application of Psalm 129 to the persecuted Church and the rooftop-grass imagery -->

**Matthew Henry**, English Puritan pastor, noted that God's people look back on their suffering not to nurse resentment, but to strengthen their faith. He linked verse 8 to the friendly harvest greeting between passersby and reapers in the book of Ruth, observing that the absence of God's blessing is the heaviest judgment upon those who hate Zion. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Psalm 129)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Matthew Henry's connection between verse 8 and Ruth chapter 2 verse 4 -->
