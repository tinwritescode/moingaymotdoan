# Psalms 131 — Quiet Like a Child With Its Mother

## Story

Packed into just three verses, this poem traditionally attributed to David is a hushed and deeply honest prayer to God. The psalmist examines himself before the Lord: "My heart is not proud, O LORD, my eyes are not haughty," insisting that he does not chase ambitions beyond his strength or mysteries too great for him to grasp (131:1).

Instead of the restlessness that demands to know everything and control everything, the author describes an inward discipline he has practiced: "Surely I have stilled and quieted my soul; like a weaned child with his mother, like a weaned child is my soul within me." The image of a weaned child resting peacefully in its mother's arms is repeated to underline a calm that no longer makes frantic demands (131:2).

Just like Psalm 130 right before it, this personal confession closes with a call to the whole nation to look toward the Lord: "O Israel, put your hope in the LORD, both now and forevermore" (131:3).

## Key Takeaways

**Humility means knowing your own limits.** The psalmist is not rejecting wisdom or healthy aspiration; he is refusing the arrogance that claims the right to master every secret of heaven and earth. Recognizing what lies beyond your reach is the first step toward peace.

**Inner peace does not happen automatically—it is learned by quieting the soul.** The phrase *stilled and quieted my soul* shows that the psalmist had to wrestle with his own impatience. Weaning a child involves tears and struggle, yet once that season passes, the child learns to rest quietly simply to be near its mother, not merely to demand food.

## Deeper Meaning

**Becoming like a little child to enter the kingdom of heaven.** The humble, wholehearted trust of Psalm 131 echoes directly in the words of Jesus when He placed a child among disciples arguing over who was greatest: unless you humble yourselves like this little child, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 18:1–4).

**Casting all anxiety on God.** The call for all God's people to hope in the Lord forevermore (131:3) flows in the same current as Peter's counsel: humble yourselves under God's mighty hand and cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you (1 Peter 5:6–7).

## Notes & Nuances

**A weaned child.** In biblical times, children were typically nursed until around two or three years old before being weaned. So a *weaned child* in verse 2 is not a newborn crying hungrily for milk, but a child who has moved past fussing and is content to lean against its mother because her presence alone is enough.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, observed that Psalm 131 is one of the shortest psalms to read yet one of the longest lessons to practice over a lifetime. He highlighted the image of the weaned child: a mature soul is one that no longer insists that God bend to its wishes, but rests contentedly in God Himself. *(The Treasury of David, on Psalm 131)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Spurgeon's well-known remark on this being among the shortest psalms to read yet hardest to learn in his exposition of Psalm 131 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, suggested that David composed this psalm to clear himself of slanderous accusations that he was plotting to seize Saul's throne. According to Calvin, refusing to pursue *great matters* does not mean laziness, but waiting for God's leading rather than climbing ahead by human scheming. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 131)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls the historical setting of David under Saul noted by Calvin in his commentary on Psalm 131 -->

**Matthew Henry**, English Puritan pastor, pointed out that true humility appears in all three areas named in verse 1: the heart within, the eyes without, and a person's conduct in daily affairs.
