# Psalms 5 — A Morning Prayer

## Story

David steps into the new day with an earnest plea. He asks the LORD to hear not only his spoken words but also the silent groaning of his heart, addressing Him as both his King and his God (5:1–2). Right in the morning, he lays his case before God and waits watchfully in expectation (5:3).

Next he meditates on the holy character of the One to whom he prays. God takes no pleasure in wickedness; evil cannot dwell with Him, the boastful cannot stand in His sight, and He abhors and destroys liars, violent bloodshedders, and deceivers (5:4–6).

In sharp contrast to them, David knows he enters God's house not because he imagines himself spotless, but "by the abundance of Your loving devotion" (5:7). Because enemies lie in wait for him, he asks the LORD to lead him in righteousness and make His path straight before him (5:8).

He then exposes the true nature of his opponents: not a word from their mouths can be trusted, their hearts are filled with destruction, their throats are an open grave, and their tongues drip with flattery (5:9). He asks God to declare them guilty and let them fall by their own schemes, for they have rebelled not merely against him but against God Himself (5:10).

In the final verses, the poem brightens into joy. All who take refuge in God and love His name will know His protection and sing forever (5:11). The LORD will bless the righteous and surround them with favor as with a large shield (5:12).

## Key Takeaways

**Starting the day with God sets the direction for everything that follows.** In the morning, before stepping out into a world of lies and traps, David lays his concerns before the LORD and waits attentively on Him (5:3).

**We draw near to God through His mercy, never our own merit.** After describing God's uncompromising holiness against evil, David does not boast of his purity; he relies purely on God's abundant loving devotion to enter worship (5:4–7).

**Deceitful speech harms just as deeply as swords.** David pictures wicked throats as open graves and tongues smooth with flattery—sweet-sounding lies can swallow people whole (5:9).

**God's protection completely surrounds those who trust Him.** The picture of a full-length shield in the closing verse points to all-around defense, not merely partial cover (5:12).

## Deeper Meaning

**The apostle Paul quotes verse 9 to sketch the universal condition of human sin.** In Romans, while proving that both Jews and Gentiles stand under sin, Paul weaves together several passages from the Psalms, including verse 9—"Their throat is an open grave; with their tongues they practice deceit" (cf. Romans 3:13). What David saw in enemies around him, the New Testament reveals as a deeper malady in every human heart apart from grace.

**David's habit of morning prayer echoes the rhythm of Jesus Himself.** Mark's Gospel records that while it was still dark before dawn, Jesus rose, went out to a solitary place, and prayed (Mark 1:35). Beginning the day in fellowship with the Father marks the life of those who belong to Him.

**Access to God's house by grace opens fully in Christ (5:7).** The letter to the Hebrews invites believers to approach the throne of grace with confidence so they may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need (Hebrews 4:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**The verb *lay before* in verse 3 was originally used for priests arranging wood and sacrifices on the altar each morning.** Just as a priest laid out the morning offering in order (Leviticus 6:12), David orders his prayer before God at daybreak and stands on the lookout for His answer (5:3).

**The statement that God *hates all workers of iniquity* can sound stark to modern ears (5:5).** In the blunt idiom of Hebrew poetry, this insists that God never compromises with or tolerates wickedness; it does not deny His mercy toward the repentant—indeed, verse 7 immediately celebrates the abundance of His loving devotion (5:7).

**The *shield* in verse 12 differs from the small handheld buckler in Psalm 3 (Psalms 3:3).** The Hebrew term here refers to a large, body-length shield protecting the soldier on every side, which is why the verse says God *surrounds* the righteous with favor (5:12).

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, focuses on the two verbs in verse 3: *lay out* (or direct) and *watch*. He compares prayer to shooting an arrow straight toward heaven and lifting your eyes to watch where it goes; many people pray and promptly forget, never bothering to look for God's answer. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Psalm 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's arrow-of-prayer image on Psalm 5:3 is one of his most widely quoted remarks -->
