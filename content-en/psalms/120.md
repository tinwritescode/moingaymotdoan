# Psalms 120 — Setting Out from a Land of Lies Toward Peace

## Story

Psalm 120 opens the fifteen *Songs of Ascents* (Psalms 120–134)—starting not inside Jerusalem's temple, but far away amid exhaustion and deceit. The pilgrim recalls answered prayer: "In my distress I cried to the LORD, and He answered me," immediately pleading for rescue from "lying lips" and a deceitful tongue (120:1–2).

Turning straight to that lying tongue, he warns what fitting payoff awaits it: sharp arrows of a warrior and glowing coals of the broom tree—vivid images of piercing, long-burning judgment (120:3–4).

The final three verses sigh with the weariness of living too long inside hostile territory. He laments sojourning in Meshech and dwelling among the tents of Kedar alongside people who despise peace, closing on an aching, honest note: "I am in favor of peace; but when I speak, they want war" (120:5–7).

## Key Takeaways

**The journey toward God often starts with growing sick of lies and strife.** Launching a pilgrimage toward Jerusalem begins not with instant euphoria, but with realizing we do not belong in a culture soaked in slander and hostility (120:2; 120:5–7).

**Deceit inflicts real wounds—and faces real judgment.** Pairing a lying tongue with warrior's arrows and lingering broom-tree embers shows how seriously Scripture treats slander and falsehood (120:4).

## Deeper Meaning

**Believers live as strangers on earth pursuing peace.** Feeling out of place in Meshech and Kedar (120:5–6) mirrors New Testament discipleship: living as foreigners and exiles on earth (1 Peter 2:11), called to be peacemakers (Matthew 5:9) who strive to live peaceably with all even when the world answers back with hostility (Romans 12:18; John 15:19).

**A small tongue capable of igniting a blaze.** Linking lying lips to glowing coals (120:2–4) echoes James's warning that the untamed tongue is a fire packed with deadly poison (James 3:5–8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Opening the *Songs of Ascents* (Psalms 120–134).** All fifteen psalms carry the title *A Song of Ascents* (literally *going up*). Traditionally, Israelite pilgrims sang them while climbing the mountain roads *up* to Jerusalem for the three great annual feasts: Passover, Weeks (Pentecost), and Tabernacles (Deuteronomy 16:16).

**Meshech and Kedar as symbolic borders of exile.** Meshech lay far north near the Black Sea while Kedar belonged to nomadic Arab tribes in the southern desert—no single person could live in both simultaneously. Combining opposite distant extremes poetically expresses feeling stranded far from home among people alien to faith and peace.
