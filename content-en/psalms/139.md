# Psalms 139 — Known Completely and Surrounded Everywhere

## Story

Traditionally attributed to David, Psalm 139 is one of Scripture's deepest meditations on the relationship between the infinite God and one individual human being. The poem unfolds in four balanced stanzas of six verses each.

The first stanza marvels at God's all-knowing gaze. The psalmist begins: "O LORD, You have searched me and known me." From sitting down to rising up, from thoughts perceived from afar to words not yet formed on his tongue, God knows him inside and out. God hems him in behind and before and lays His hand upon him—knowledge so wonderful and lofty that the human mind cannot reach it (139:1–6).

The second stanza turns to God's presence everywhere. The psalmist asks: "Where can I go to escape Your Spirit? Where can I flee from Your presence?" Whether he ascends to heaven or makes his bed in Sheol, or rides the wings of the dawn to the far side of the sea, God's right hand still holds him fast. Even the thickest night cannot hide him, "for darkness is as light to You" (139:7–12).

The third stanza looks inward to the mystery of life in the womb. God Himself knitted the psalmist's inward parts and frame together in the most hidden place, drawing forth a burst of praise: "I praise You, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made." God's eyes saw his unformed body and wrote all his ordained days in His book before a single one came to be. Reflecting on God's thoughts toward him—outnumbering the grains of sand—fills him with peace each morning when he wakes (139:13–18).

The final stanza turns sharply fierce before settling back into humility. Standing in such deep intimacy with God, the psalmist flares with anger against bloodthirsty people who blaspheme and misuse God's name, aligning himself wholly with God in rejecting them. Yet at once he turns the searchlight back onto his own heart in a famous closing prayer: "Search me, O God, and know my heart; test me and know my anxious thoughts. See if there is any offensive way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting" (139:19–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Being fully known by God is not surveillance—it is being loved and guarded.** Eyes that see all our secrets usually frighten us, yet in this poem the hand of the One who knows everything is a hand that *hems in*, *leads*, and *holds fast* (139:5; 139:10).

**Every human life is intentionally crafted by God in the womb.** The picture of God *knitting* a person together in secret (139:13–16) affirms that human dignity comes neither from chance nor from achievement, but from the careful handiwork of the Creator.

**Zeal against evil must be paired with honest self-examination.** Verses 23–24 provide a remarkably wise turn after the anger of verses 19–22: rather than assuming his own motives are spotless, the psalmist invites God Himself to inspect whether any *offensive way* lurks inside him.

## Deeper Meaning

**Nowhere can separate us from God's love.** Traveling from highest heaven to Sheol, from east to west, and remaining in God's grip (139:7–10) meets Paul's triumphant chorus in Romans: neither death nor life, neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:38–39).

**Everything lies uncovered before the One to whom we must give account.** God's all-seeing knowledge piercing even the dark (139:1–4; 139:11–12) echoes in Hebrews 4:13—nothing in all creation is hidden from God's sight, but everything is uncovered and exposed before His eyes—and immediately afterward Hebrews directs readers to Jesus, our merciful Great High Priest (Hebrews 4:14–16).

## Notes & Nuances

**The abrupt shift in verses 19–22.** Many readers cherish the lyrical first eighteen verses and are startled by the fiery anger of verses 19–22. Numerous scholars suggest that the psalmist was facing false accusations of treason or idolatry, making the whole psalm an appeal to the all-knowing Judge to clear his name: *Lord, You know my heart; I have no fellowship with Your enemies—yet if any hidden wrong remains in me, search me Yourself and guide me onto the right path* (139:23–24).

**The image of "the depths of the earth" in verse 15.** This phrase does not mean humans are literally formed underground; it is poetic imagery for a mother's womb—hidden and dark like the earth's interior—while also echoing Genesis 2:7, where humanity was shaped from the dust of the ground.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, called Psalm 139 one of the brightest jewels in the entire Psalter.
