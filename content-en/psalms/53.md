# Psalms 53 — The Fool Says There Is No God

## Story

This brief twin of Psalm 14 opens with a candid diagnosis of the human heart. The writer states plainly, "The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God.'" The moral fallout shows immediately: they are corrupt, their deeds are vile, and no one does good (53:1).

The scene shifts up to heaven. God looks down on humanity to see whether anyone understands and seeks after Him. The search comes up grimly empty: everyone has turned away, together they have become corrupt—"there is no one who does good, not even one" (53:2–3).

God then confronts evildoers directly: have they no knowledge at all? They exploit and devour God's people as casually as eating bread, and never call upon Him. Yet their smug confidence shatters without warning: right where there seemed nothing to fear, overwhelming dread grips them, because God scatters the bones of those besieging His people and puts them to shame (53:4–5).

The psalm ends with a hopeful sigh. The writer longs for Israel's salvation to break forth from Zion. When God restores the fortunes of His captive or oppressed people, Jacob will rejoice and Israel will be glad (53:6).

## Key Takeaways

**Biblical folly is a matter of the heart, not intellect.** The fool of verse 1 is not necessarily uneducated or debating philosophical theory (53:1). Rather, he lives practically as though no God watches or holds him accountable, leaving him free to do harm.

**Sin reaches across all humanity without exception.** God scanning the earth from heaven and finding not a single naturally good exception shatters the myth that only a handful of bad apples spoil the world (53:2–3). Every human heart tilts away from God.

**Practical atheism routinely leads to exploiting the vulnerable.** Once reverence for God vanishes, people quickly treat neighbors as disposable prey, "devouring my people as though eating bread" (53:4). Losing the fear of God goes hand in hand with losing compassion for people.

**Final rescue can come only from God.** Looking across this dark portrait of human nature, the writer puts no trust in self-made moral reform, lifting his eyes instead to Zion for salvation coming straight from God (53:6).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul uses this exact passage to prove that all humanity needs the gospel.** Building his case in Romans that Jews and Gentiles alike stand under sin, Paul quotes verses 1 through 3 alongside their twin in Psalm 14 to declare that no one is righteous (53:1–3; Psalms 14:1–3; Romans 3:10–12). That bleak verdict clears the ground for the good news of righteousness received through faith in Jesus Christ (Romans 3:21–24).

**The longing for salvation out of Zion is fulfilled in the Savior.** Ancient Israel prayed for redemption from Zion to bring home the afflicted (53:6). Paul later echoes the prophecy that the Deliverer will come from Zion and banish godlessness from Jacob (Romans 11:26; Isaiah 59:20).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why is Psalm 53 nearly identical to Psalm 14?** The Book of Psalms grew out of several earlier hymnbooks. Psalm 14 belongs to Book I (which favors the covenant name *LORD*), whereas Psalm 53 sits in Book II (where editors favored the title *God*). Preserving both versions shows how frequently Israel reused this song across different eras and collections.

**The sharpest divergence appears in verse 5 (53:5).** Compared with Psalm 14 (Psalms 14:5–6), verse 5 vividly pictures besiegers seized by panic with their bones scattered. Many scholars suspect Psalm 53 was slightly adapted to commemorate a specific military deliverance—perhaps the sudden overnight rout of the Assyrian army outside Jerusalem (2 Kings 19:35).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, highlights "says in his heart" as pointing to hidden practical atheism: many people pay lip service to God's existence while quietly convincing themselves He pays no mind to their actions so they can indulge freely.
