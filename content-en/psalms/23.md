# Psalms 23 — The LORD Is My Shepherd

## Story

Just six short verses long, Psalm 23 is the best-loved and most widely memorized poem in the entire Bible. David—who grew up tending flocks across Judean hills—offers a simple yet complete confession: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (23:1).

The opening verses picture a shepherd guiding his flock through both tranquil days and dark ravines. He lets the sheep rest in green pastures, leads them beside still waters, restores weary souls, and guides them along right paths for His name's sake (23:2–3). Yet the trail is not always sunlit; sometimes it cuts through "the valley of the shadow of death." Right at verse 4, the pronoun shifts tenderly from *He* to *You*—no longer talking about God, but speaking straight to Him: "I will fear no evil, for You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me" (23:4).

The final two verses trade the pasture for a gracious banquet hall. Right in full sight of David's enemies, God calmly sets a generous table before him, anoints his head with fragrant oil, and fills his cup to overflowing (23:5). David closes in serene certainty for every remaining day: surely goodness and mercy will pursue him all the days of his life, and he will dwell in the house of the LORD forever (23:6).

## Key Takeaways

**True sufficiency comes not from owning everything, but from belonging to the Shepherd.** The pledge "I shall not want" follows directly on "The LORD is my shepherd"—because He Himself supplies every genuine need of the soul (23:1–3).

**Peace in dark valleys comes not from the absence of danger, but from God's presence.** The psalm never promises believers will bypass the "valley of the shadow of death"; it promises something better: "for You are with me" (23:4).

**God's grace not only leads ahead, but follows right behind.** In verse 6, the Hebrew verb rendered *follow* literally means *pursue*—God's goodness and mercy steadily chase after His people every single day of life (23:6).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus claims the entire picture of Psalm 23 for Himself when He declares, "I am the good shepherd" (John 10:11; 10:14).** Unlike ordinary shepherds who merely guard the flock as best they can, the Good Shepherd lays down His own life for the sheep, knows each one personally, and leads them into abundant life (John 10:10–11).

**The Shepherd accompanies His people all the way into eternity.** The New Testament calls Jesus the Great Shepherd of the sheep (Hebrews 13:20) and the Chief Shepherd (1 Peter 5:4). In Revelation, the Lamb at the center of the throne will shepherd His people, guide them to springs of living water, and wipe away every tear from their eyes (Revelation 7:17).

## Notes & Nuances

**The *rod* and the *staff* were two distinct tools carried by Middle Eastern shepherds (23:4).** The rod was a short, sturdy club used to fend off predators and protect the flock; the staff was a long crooked pole used to nudge, guide, and rescue sheep that wandered off or slipped down a ledge.

**The shift in pronoun at verse 4 is the poem's finest artistic stroke (23:4).** Out in green pastures (23:1–3), David speaks of *Him*; entering the valley of the shadow of death (23:4–5), he turns directly to *You*. In the darkest stretch of the road, fellowship with God grows closer than ever.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, calls Psalm 23 *the Pearl of Psalms*.
