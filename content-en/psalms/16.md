# Psalms 16 — A Delightful Inheritance and Hope Beyond Death

## Story

David opens with a brief plea for God's safekeeping because he has taken refuge in Him (16:1). Immediately he voices a lifelong confession: "You are my Lord; apart from You I have no good thing" (16:2). His delight is in the saints on earth, while for those who rush after other gods—only multiplying their own sorrows—he flatly refuses to pour out their drink offerings or take their names on his lips (16:3–4).

The poem shifts into deep contentment. The LORD Himself is David's chosen portion and his cup, holding his lot secure in pleasant places (16:5–6). He blesses the LORD who counsels him even in the quiet of night, keeping God continually before him so he will never be shaken (16:7–8).

The climax arrives in the final three verses: his heart is glad, his tongue rejoices, and even his body will rest secure, because of an extraordinary conviction: "For You will not abandon my soul to Sheol, nor will You let Your Holy One see decay" (16:9–10). God will make known to him the path of life, for in God's presence is fullness of joy, and at His right hand are pleasures forevermore (16:11).

## Key Takeaways

**God Himself is the ultimate reward, not merely the giver of rewards.** David does not ask only for blessings from God; he confesses that God Himself is his portion, his cup, and his whole inheritance (16:2; 16:5–6).

**Living consciously in God's presence brings steadiness amid upheaval.** Setting the LORD always before us keeps circumstances from knocking us off balance (16:8).

**Fellowship with God is stronger than death.** True fullness of joy does not halt at the edge of the grave, but opens onto the path of life in His presence forevermore (16:9–11).

## Deeper Meaning

**In his Pentecost sermon, the apostle Peter quotes verses 8 through 11 directly to prove Jesus' resurrection (16:8–11; Acts 2:25–31).** Peter points to an undeniable fact: King David died, was buried, and his tomb remained right there to that very day, so his body clearly saw decay. Because David was a prophet who knew God had sworn to set the Messiah on his throne, when he wrote "You will not abandon my soul to Sheol, nor will You let Your Holy One see decay," he looked ahead and spoke of the resurrection of the Christ (Acts 2:29–31).

**The apostle Paul uses the very same argument when preaching in Pisidian Antioch.** Quoting verse 10, he contrasts David—who served his generation, fell asleep, and saw decay—with Jesus, whom God raised up so that He never saw decay (16:10; Acts 13:35–37).

**The imagery of a *portion* and *inheritance* recalls the special calling of the tribe of Levi (16:5–6).** When Canaan was divided among the tribes, the Levites received no land allotment because the LORD Himself was their inheritance (Numbers 18:20); in the New Testament, every believer in Christ shares an imperishable inheritance in Him (1 Peter 1:4).

## Notes & Nuances

**The word *Sheol* in verse 10 refers in the Old Testament to the realm of the dead in general, not necessarily to hell as a place of torment (16:10).** Verse 10 uses synonymous parallelism: not being abandoned to the grave corresponds directly to not letting the body undergo decay.

**The traditional superscription calls Psalm 16 a *Miktam* of David.** The exact meaning of this ancient Hebrew term is uncertain—some suggest *golden poem* or *inscription*—and it also appears over Psalms 56 through 60.
