# Psalms 78 — Lessons from Israel's Unfaithful History

## Story

Asaph's sweeping historical poem opens like a father or teacher gathering the whole nation to listen. He announces he will open his mouth in parables and utter hidden lessons from ancient days passed down by their ancestors. His aim is crystal clear: never hide the LORD's marvelous deeds from the next generation, so children yet unborn put their trust in God, obey His commands, and refuse to repeat the failures of an earlier stubborn, rebellious generation (78:1–8).

Immediately he offers a sobering warning: the warriors of Ephraim, fully armed with bows, turned tail on the day of battle because they broke God's covenant and forgot His wonders (78:9–11). From there he traces history from the plains of Zoan in Egypt straight into the desert. God split the sea, led them with a cloud by day and fire by night, and split rocks open to gush water like rivers. Still the people tested Him, sneering over whether God could spread a table and furnish bread and meat out in the wilderness (78:12–20).

Fierce anger flared against their unbelief, yet God still opened heaven's doors to rain down manna and blew quail into camp like sand. They ate until stuffed, yet while meat stayed between their teeth, judgment struck the greedy and their years melted away in terror (78:21–33). Whenever discipline hit, they scrambled back to seek God, remembering Him as their Rock and Redeemer—yet only flattering Him with their lips while their hearts stayed disloyal. Even then, God proved deeply compassionate, forgiving again and again instead of wiping them out, remembering they were mere flesh—a passing breeze that never returns (78:34–39).

Next Asaph revisits the plagues God unleashed on Egypt—rivers turned to blood, flies, frogs, locusts, hail, pestilence, and the death of every firstborn—to guide His flock safely into the Promised Land and apportion their inheritance (78:40–55). Once settled, however, they rebelled again like a warped bow, provoking Him with high places and carved idols. This time God forsook the tabernacle at Shiloh, handed the ark of the covenant into enemy capture, and let young men and priests fall by the sword (78:56–64).

At last, the Lord awoke as a mighty warrior shaking off sleep and beat back His foes. Rejecting Joseph's tent and the tribe of Ephraim, He chose the tribe of Judah and Mount Zion to establish His lasting sanctuary. And He chose David, calling him straight from the sheepfolds to shepherd Israel "with integrity of heart" and guide them "with skillful hands" (78:65–72).

## Key Takeaways

**Spiritual amnesia breeds unfaithfulness.** A tragic refrain runs throughout the poem: God's people kept forgetting His miracles, causing them to doubt and rebel whenever trouble struck (78:11; 78:42). Passing living faith to children starts with parents telling God's story honestly and often (78:4–7).

**Crisis-driven repentance without a changed heart is mere flattery.** Under discipline Israel rushed back calling God all the right names, yet their hearts stayed unfaithful to His covenant (78:34–37). True repentance runs deeper than fearing consequences.

**God's compassion outpaces human stubbornness.** The real marvel of Israel's history is not human loyalty, but divine patience. Remembering how fragile humanity is—just a passing breath—God restrained His wrath over and over (78:38–39).

**Godly leadership unites an upright heart with capable hands.** Closing with David moving from sheepfolds to throne, the psalm joins two vital leadership qualities: pure integrity within and wise skill in action (78:70–72).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus fulfilled the psalm's opening line through His parables.** Matthew quotes verse 2 directly—"I will open My mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden since the foundation of the world" (78:2)—to explain why Jesus unveiled kingdom mysteries through stories (Matthew 13:35). In New Testament light, Old Testament history was never just moral advice, but a tapestry holding Christ's redemptive plan.

**Both manna and water from the rock point straight to Christ.** When crowds cited Moses' bread from heaven, Jesus replied that He Himself is the true bread from heaven giving life to the world (John 6:31–35). Paul likewise recalls Israel's wilderness rebellions to warn the church against craving evil and testing the Lord, declaring that the spiritual Rock accompanying them was Christ (1 Corinthians 10:1–11).

**Choosing Judah, Zion, and David opens the road to the Messiah.** Even after Shiloh's collapse (1 Samuel 4:11; Jeremiah 7:12), God never cast off His people forever, launching a fresh chapter through David's house. David shepherding with an upright heart foreshadows Jesus—Son of Judah and David—the Good Shepherd who tends God's people forever (John 10:11; Revelation 5:5).

## Notes & Nuances

**The count and sequence of the Egyptian plagues.** Verses 44 through 51 list seven plagues in an order slightly different from Exodus 7–12 (78:44–51). This selective poetic summary aims not to rewrite a chronological record, but to spotlight God's saving power.

**Picturing God waking "like a warrior shouting from wine" (78:65).** This bold anthropomorphic metaphor conveys God erupting suddenly in irresistible might after seeming silent while foes ran wild. The writer is by no means claiming God literally sleeps or drinks wine (cf. Psalms 121:4).

**Ephraim's role in the poem.** Ephraim was the leading tribe of the northern kingdom and home to old Shiloh. Recounting Ephraim's failure alongside God's choice of Judah and Zion likely appealed to northern tribes after the kingdom split, inviting them back to Jerusalem's temple and David's dynasty.
