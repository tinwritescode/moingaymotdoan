# Psalms 147 — The One Who Counts the Stars and Binds Up Broken Hearts

## Story

Psalm 147 unfolds in three clear stanzas, each opening with a fresh summons to praise (verses 1, 7, and 12). The first stanza celebrates how good, pleasant, and fitting it is to sing to God. Its setting reflects the return from exile: the Lord rebuilds Jerusalem, gathers the scattered outcasts of Israel, and "heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds." Immediately alongside that tenderness stands breathtaking cosmic power: "He determines the number of the stars; He calls them each by name." He lifts up the humble and casts the wicked to the ground (147:1–6).

The second stanza calls for music on the harp thanking God for His quiet provision throughout creation: He covers the sky with clouds, supplies rain for the earth, makes grass sprout on the hills, and gives food to livestock and to "the young ravens that cry." From there the psalmist states a surprising measure of what truly pleases God: "He does not delight in the strength of the horse; He takes no pleasure in the legs of a man. The LORD is pleased with those who fear Him, who hope in His loving devotion" (147:7–11).

The third stanza directly invites Jerusalem and Zion to praise their God, because He has strengthened the bars of their gates, blessed their borders with peace, and filled them with the finest wheat. His spoken word runs swiftly across the earth—spreading snow like wool, scattering frost like ashes, hurling down hail so cold no one can bear it, and then sending a single word to make His warm wind blow until the ice melts into flowing streams. Yet His greatest privilege, towering above every wonder of nature, is that He has revealed His word, His statutes, and His judgments to Israel (147:12–20).

## Key Takeaways

**God's limitless power never puts Him out of reach of broken hearts.** Placing verse 3 next to verse 4 is one of the finest brushstrokes in all of Scripture: the very hand that names billions of stars across the cosmos stoops down to bandage the inner wounds of returning exiles.

**God is not dazzled by military might or human bravado.** *The strength of the horse* and *the legs of a man* (147:10) picture ancient cavalry and infantry. God is never impressed by muscle or weaponry; what delights Him is a heart that reveres Him and hopes in His steadfast love (147:11).

**God's word governs both the seasons of nature and the life of His people.** In the final stanza, God's *word* sends winter frost and spring thaw (147:15–18) while also being entrusted to Israel as light to guide their steps (147:19–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus came to heal the brokenhearted.** The portrait of the One who binds up broken hearts (147:3) and gathers the scattered (147:2) comes vividly to life in Jesus—who announced He was sent to heal the brokenhearted (Luke 4:18) and who acts as the Good Shepherd gathering scattered sheep into one flock (John 10:16).

**God feeds the ravens and cares for His children.** The detail that God provides food for *the young ravens that cry* (147:9) echoes directly in Jesus' teaching in Luke 12:24: consider the ravens—they neither sow nor reap, yet God feeds them, and how much more valuable are you than birds!

## Notes & Nuances

**The return from exile in Nehemiah's day.** Details such as *rebuilding Jerusalem*, *gathering the exiles* (147:2), and *strengthening the bars of your gates* (147:13) match closely the joyful dedication of Jerusalem's rebuilt walls and gates under Nehemiah (Nehemiah 12:27–43).

**Numbering in the ancient Greek version.** The ancient Greek Septuagint and the Latin Vulgate split Psalm 147 into two separate psalms (verses 1–11 and verses 12–20), offsetting the psalm numbering through much of the Psalter by one compared to the Hebrew text and most English Bibles before realigning at Psalm 148.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, marveled at the pairing of verses 3 and 4: if God only numbered the stars without binding up broken hearts, He would feel too distant for suffering people; if He only felt pity without power to govern the heavens, He could not save us. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, emphasized verses 19–20 as the climax of the entire psalm: after celebrating peace, wheat, and creation, the psalmist places receiving *God's Word* above every material blessing, because without Scripture's light people remain in the dark no matter how full their barns are. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 147)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin's emphasis on verses 19–20 regarding the privilege of receiving God's Word -->

**Matthew Henry**, English Puritan pastor, highlighted the healthy balance in verse 11: those who please God hold together two attitudes that might seem opposite—*fearing Him* (which guards against complacency and irreverence) and *hoping in His loving devotion* (which guards against despair and paralyzing dread). *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Psalm 147)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Matthew Henry's remarks on the twin attitudes of fear and hope in verse 11 -->
