# Psalms 42 — As the Deer Pants for Streams of Water

## Story

According to the heading, this poem comes from the sons of Korah—Levites devoted to singing in the temple. The writer finds himself exiled far up north near the headwaters of the Jordan and Mount Hermon, cut off from the temple in Jerusalem. He opens with scorching thirst: "As the deer pants for streams of water, so my soul longs after You, O God." He thirsts for the living God, eating tears day and night while mockers keep asking, "Where is your God?" (42:1–3).

Memories of happier days only sharpen the ache. He remembers leading joyful throngs up to God's house amid festive shouts and songs of praise. Right in the depths of grief, he suddenly turns to address his own soul in a courageous refrain: "Why are you downcast, O my soul? Why the unease within me? Put your hope in God, for I will yet praise Him, my Savior and my God" (42:4–5).

Still, heaviness does not vanish overnight. From the distant Jordan highlands and Mount Hermon, he feels buried beneath roaring waterfalls and crashing waves sweeping over him. Yet faith glimmers through the dark: by day the LORD directs His loving devotion, and by night His song and prayer stay beside him (42:6–8).

He keeps pouring out his heart to God—his Rock—asking why God seems to have forgotten him while enemies taunt him daily like a stabbing pain in his bones. The poem closes by summoning his soul with the same resolute refrain once more: put your hope in God, for He remains his salvation and his God (42:9–11).

## Key Takeaways

**Do not merely listen to your feelings—learn to preach truth to your own soul.** Rather than letting gloom carry him away, the psalmist talks straight back to his heart twice (42:5; 42:11): he questions his turmoil and deliberately redirects his mind to God's promises.

**Thirsting for God proves a soul is truly alive.** Cut off from public worship and mocked by skeptics, what he aches for most is not simply going home, but meeting "the living God" Himself (42:2). That thirst hurts, yet it keeps him tethered tightly to God.

**Even crushing waves remain *Your waves*.** In verse 7, the writer calls the torrents pounding him "Your waterfalls" and "Your breakers and waves" (42:7). Seeing God's sovereign hand even inside the storm enables him to keep a prayer song alive in the night (42:8).

## Deeper Meaning

**Inward sorrow and the taunt *Where is your God?* echo in Gethsemane and at Calvary.** On the night before His death, Jesus confessed His soul was overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death (Matthew 26:38)—closely mirroring the sigh of verses 5 and 11. And beneath the cross, mockers jeered that He trusted in God, so let God rescue Him now if He wants Him (Matthew 27:43).

**The soul's thirst in verses 1 and 2 finds everlasting satisfaction in Christ.** The deer panting for water pictures humanity's longing for life in God. Standing amid a Jerusalem festival, Jesus cried out that anyone who is thirsty should come to Him and drink (John 7:37), promising living water that satisfies forever (John 4:14).

## Notes & Nuances

**Psalm 42 opens Book II of the Psalter.** All 150 psalms are arranged into five books—Book I covers Psalms 1–41, Book II covers 42–72, Book III covers 73–89, Book IV covers 90–106, and Book V covers 107–150—echoing the five books of Moses' Law, with each book ending in a solemn doxology. Throughout Book II, the title *God* (*Elohim*) appears far more frequently than the personal name *LORD* (*YHWH*).

**Psalms 42 and 43 originally formed one connected pair.** Many ancient Hebrew manuscripts write them as a single poem: both share exile from the temple, nearly identical laments (42:9; Psalms 43:2), the same refrain repeated three times (42:5; 42:11; Psalms 43:5), and Psalm 43 carries no separate heading.
