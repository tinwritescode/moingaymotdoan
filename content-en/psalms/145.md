# Psalms 145 — An Acrostic Song to the Eternal King

## Story

This is the final poem in the entire Psalter bearing David's name, and the only psalm explicitly titled a *song of praise*—a magnificent gateway leading straight into the five closing *Hallel* psalms (Psalms 146–150). There is no lament here and no shadow of an enemy; from first line to last, pure adoration rings out.

The psalmist opens with warm intimacy and deep reverence: "I will exalt You, my God and King; I will bless Your name forever and ever." He pledges to praise God every single day, for "Great is the LORD and greatly to be praised; His greatness is unsearchable" (145:1–3).

The next lines describe praise flowing down through the generations: "One generation will commend Your works to the next." Together they tell of God's awesome deeds and abundant goodness, breaking into the core confession of the Old Testament: "The LORD is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and rich in loving devotion. The LORD is good to all; His compassion rests on all He has made" (145:4–9).

From mercy, the song lifts its gaze to royal majesty: all God's works give thanks to Him, and His faithful people make known the splendor of His reign to all humanity, for "Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and Your dominion endures through all generations" (145:10–13).

Yet this eternal King does not rule with an iron fist, but with tender care: "The LORD upholds all who fall and lifts up all who are bowed down." Every creature looks expectantly to Him, and "You open Your hand and satisfy the desire of every living thing." He is righteous in all His ways, "near to all who call on Him" in truth, hearing their cry, saving those who fear Him, watching over those who love Him, and destroying the wicked (145:14–20).

The final verse completes the personal pledge of verse 1 while widening it to all humanity: "My mouth will declare the praise of the LORD; let every creature bless His holy name forever and ever" (145:21).

## Key Takeaways

**God's boundless greatness goes hand in hand with compassion for every creature.** A King too magnificent to fathom (145:3) still bends down to catch those who stumble, straighten those bowed low, and open His hand to feed every living thing (145:14–16).

**Faith stays alive as one generation tells the next.** Verses 4–7 show that praise is never meant to stay locked inside a private room; every generation bears the joyful calling of telling what God has done so the next generation learns to trust and celebrate Him.

**God stays near to every sincere heart.** Across an infinite kingdom, the throne room is never farther away than an honest cry: the Lord stays close to everyone who calls on Him in truth (145:18–19).

## Deeper Meaning

**An everlasting kingdom in Christ.** The declaration "Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and Your dominion endures through all generations" (145:13) echoes in the angel's announcement about Jesus—of His kingdom there will be no end (Luke 1:33)—and in heaven's anthem in Revelation 11:15 declaring that the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of His Christ.

**An open hand feeding the world.** The image of God opening His hand to feed every living creature in due season (145:15–16) comes alive when Jesus breaks five loaves and two fish to feed the crowd in the wilderness (Matthew 14:19–20), and when He reveals Himself as the bread of life for the world (John 6:35).

## Notes & Nuances

**An alphabetic acrostic poem.** In the original Hebrew, Psalm 145 is an *acrostic*: each successive verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet (from *Aleph* to *Tav*), offering praise to God *from A to Z*—complete and ordered. One fascinating textual detail is that the traditional Hebrew Masoretic text contains 21 verses, missing the *Nun* line between verses 13 and 14; meanwhile the ancient Greek Septuagint, the Syriac version, and a Dead Sea Scroll from Qumran preserve that line (*The LORD is faithful in all His words and kind in all His actions*), which is why many modern translations—including the Berean Standard Bible—include it as the second half of verse 13.

**A cherished place in Jewish prayer.** Psalm 145—paired with two opening lines from Psalms 84:4 and Psalms 144:15, known as the *Ashrei* prayer—is recited by observant Jews three times every day. The word *praise* (*Tehillah*) in this psalm's superscription is the very root of the Hebrew title for the entire book of Psalms (*Tehillim*—"Praises").

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, highlighted the union of justice and grace across the whole psalm: in verses 8–9, the psalmist echoes God's own self-revelation to Moses on Mount Sinai (Exodus 34:6), underscoring that mercy is the foremost attribute God wants His people to know about Him. *(Commentary on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 145)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin connecting verse 8 with Exodus 34 verse 6 in his commentary on Psalm 145 -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, called Psalm 145 *David's crown of praise*.
