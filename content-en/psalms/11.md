# Psalms 11 — When the Foundations Shake

## Story

David faces anxious advice from people around him: they urge him to flee like a bird to the mountains, because the wicked have bent their bows and fitted arrows to shoot in the dark at the upright in heart; if the very foundations of order are destroyed, what can the righteous do? (11:1–3).

David rejects that counsel to run away right from the first sentence, because he has already taken refuge in the LORD (11:1). He lifts his gaze above every earthly upheaval: the LORD is still in His holy temple, His throne remains firm in heaven, and His eyes watch and examine every human being (11:4).

Times of testing do not mean God has lost control. He tests the righteous to refine them, while His soul hates the wicked and those who love violence; fiery coals, burning sulfur, and a scorching wind will be their allotted cup (11:5–6). This brief poem closes on a radiant hope: because the LORD is righteous and loves justice, "the upright will see His face" (11:7).

## Key Takeaways

**God's throne never collapses along with earthly foundations.** When justice, social order, or familiar securities crumble (11:3), the first place believers must look is upward to the LORD's unshakable throne in heaven (11:4).

**Within the very same crisis, God acts differently toward two kinds of people.** For the righteous, He uses hardship to test and refine; for lovers of violence, He reserves just retribution (11:5–6).

**The highest reward of the upright is beholding God's face.** Their ultimate destiny is not merely escaping arrows in the dark, but entering the direct presence of God Himself (11:7).

## Deeper Meaning

**The promise that "the upright will see His face" resounds in the Sermon on the Mount and in Revelation (11:7).** Jesus blesses the pure in heart because they will see God (Matthew 5:8), and Revelation pictures God's servants gazing on His face in the New Jerusalem (Revelation 22:4).

**The imagery of fire and sulfur recalls the judgment of Sodom and Gomorrah (11:6; Genesis 19:24).** It confirms that God's patience toward evil has a limit and that His justice is real.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verses 1 through 3 embed a spoken dialogue inside the poem (11:1–3).** Many interpreters read verses 2 and 3 as continuing the fearful advice of David's friends urging him to escape, to which verse 4 delivers faith's bold reply (11:4).

**The *foundations* in verse 3 refer to the bedrock pillars of civic order, law, and moral stability (11:3).** When those collapse, panic feels natural; the psalm voices that question squarely so it can answer with God's supreme sovereignty.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, remarks that the hardest temptation in a crisis often comes not from open enemies, but from well-meaning friends offering fearful counsel.
