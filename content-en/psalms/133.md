# Psalms 133 — The Beauty of Brothers Living in Harmony

## Story

In just three brief verses, this poem traditionally linked with David opens with an exclamation of wonder: "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is when brothers live together in harmony!" (133:1).

To capture a beauty that plain words can hardly contain, the psalmist paints two vivid pictures. The first carries a sacred fragrance: unity is like precious anointing oil poured on the head of Aaron the high priest, running down onto his beard and flowing all the way to the collar of his robes (133:2).

The second carries the coolness of the natural world: harmony is also like the morning dew of Mount Hermon falling down to refresh the dry hills of Zion. And there, the psalmist says, the Lord has bestowed His greatest blessing—"even life forevermore" (133:3).

## Key Takeaways

**Unity is a sacred blessing, not merely polite social manners.** The picture of Aaron's consecration oil (Exodus 30:22–30) shows that brotherly harmony is not just emotionally *pleasant*; it carries a holy aroma that makes a community fit to worship God.

**Harmony brings fresh life to the whole community.** Across the dry summer months of Palestine, night dew was a vital source of moisture keeping plants alive. When brothers and sisters dwell in one accord, the entire community is refreshed and receives *life* from God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus' prayer for unity.** The joy of seeing Israel's tribes gather from every region to dwell peacefully together in Jerusalem foreshadows Jesus' heartfelt prayer on the night before His suffering: that all who believe in Him would be one so that the world might believe (John 17:20–21).

**Unity in one Spirit.** Paul urges believers to make every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace (Ephesians 4:3). Just as the anointing oil flowed down from Aaron's head to his garments, grace and unity flow down to every member of the church from Christ the Head.

## Notes & Nuances

**Oil on Aaron's head and dew on Mount Hermon.** Literally speaking, Mount Hermon rises in the far north of Israel, more than a hundred miles from Zion (Jerusalem), so Hermon's dew could never naturally fall on Zion's hills. The psalmist deliberately uses poetic exaggeration to say that unity accomplishes something wondrous: it brings the richest moisture of the northern peaks to refresh the parched slopes of the south.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, admired how both images in the psalm flow downward from above—oil from head to robe, dew from high mountain to hills—showing that genuine unity always descends as a gift from God on high rather than being forced by human pressure. *(The Treasury of David, on Psalm 133)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Spurgeon's emphasis on the downward-flowing imagery in Psalm 133 -->

**Matthew Henry**, English Puritan pastor, connected the setting of the psalm with the tribes of Israel ending their division to unite behind David as king, as well as the pilgrim festivals when the nation gathered in Jerusalem. He noticed that the poem does not issue a dry command *be united*, but celebrates unity's beauty so that hearers long for it themselves. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Psalm 133)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Matthew Henry's historical link to David's accession and his comment on the exclamatory tone of Psalm 133 -->

**Augustine of Hippo**, fourth- and fifth-century Church Father, famously applied verse 1 of Psalm 133 to monastic community life and the unity of the Church. He stressed that the anointing oil flows only over the *body* joined to the *head*, Christ, so anyone who breaks away from the fellowship of the Church cuts himself off from that stream of grace. *(Expositions on the Psalms, on Psalm 133)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Augustine's well-known application of Psalm 133 to communal life and the head-and-body image -->
