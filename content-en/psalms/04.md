# Psalms 4 — Peace as Night Falls

## Story

David begins with an urgent appeal to the God of his righteousness. He recalls past grace: whenever distress hemmed him in, God gave him relief and brought him into an open space; now he asks God to be gracious again and hear his prayer (4:1).

Turning from God to the people slandering him, David addresses his accusers directly. How long will they turn his honor into shame, love worthless illusions, and chase after lies? (4:2). He reminds them of a settled truth: the LORD has set apart the godly for Himself, so when David calls, the LORD will surely listen (4:3).

He then counsels them to tremble and not sin; lying on their beds at night, let them search their own hearts and be still (4:4). Instead of scheming, let them offer right sacrifices and place their trust in the LORD (4:5).

In the closing verses, David mentions the discouraged mood around him, as many ask who will show them any good. He prays that the LORD will lift the light of His face upon them (4:6). As for his own heart, God has filled it with greater joy than people know when their grain and new wine abound (4:7). He closes with a serene surrender before bedtime: he will lie down and sleep in peace, because the LORD alone keeps him dwelling in safety (4:8).

## Key Takeaways

**Past deliverance anchors today's prayer.** When hemmed in by trouble, David looks back to how God previously led him into a wide-open place, drawing fresh courage to cry out again (4:1).

**Strong emotion needs boundaries and silence.** The counsel to search one's heart on the bed and be silent calls us to pause before letting agitation or anger spill over into sin (4:4).

**The deepest joy does not depend on harvests or wealth.** Abundant grain and new wine symbolize material prosperity, yet knowing God's shining favor over your life brings a far richer joy (4:6–7).

**Peaceful sleep comes from trusting the Lord alone.** David does not rely on fortress walls or armed guards in order to rest; his security rests wholly in the LORD (4:8).

## Deeper Meaning

**The apostle Paul quotes verse 4 when teaching the church how to handle anger.** In Ephesians, he uses the Greek translation of this verse—"Be angry, yet do not sin"—and urges believers not to let the sun go down on their anger (Ephesians 4:26). Mastering anger before nightfall is a shared spiritual discipline across both Testaments.

**The prayer "Let the light of Your face shine upon us" echoes the priestly blessing (4:6).** In Numbers, God instructed Aaron to bless Israel with the words, "The LORD make His face shine upon you and be gracious to you" (Numbers 6:25). David's gladness flows from knowing he lives beneath that gracious gaze.

**The peace of verse 8 anticipates Jesus' own promise of peace (4:8).** Before His suffering, Jesus gave His disciples a peace unlike anything the world can offer (John 14:27). Those who trust Him can close their eyes in rest even when much around them remains unresolved.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 4 has two well-known renderings.** The Hebrew verb can be translated *tremble*—"Tremble and do not sin"—highlighting reverent awe before God. The ancient Greek Septuagint translates the same verb as *be angry* (followed by the Berean Standard Bible: "Be angry, yet do not sin"), which Paul quotes in Ephesians (Ephesians 4:26). Both meanings focus on governing intense inner feelings so they do not lead into sin.

**The *sacrifices of righteousness* in verse 5 do not reject temple worship.** Rather, they mean that outward offerings to God must be matched by an upright heart and genuine trust in Him, never used as ritual cover for deceit (4:5).

**Augustine**, fourth-century bishop in North Africa, lingers over verses 6 and 7 about the light of God's face and joy surpassing grain and wine. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explains verse 7 as a remedy against envy. **Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, calls Psalm 4 the evening hymn paired with the morning hymn of Psalm 3.
