# Psalms 60 — A Prayer After Military Defeat

## Story

The traditional heading locates this psalm during David's military campaigns against Aram and Edom (2 Samuel 8:3–14). Far from a victory march, the poem opens with the heavy lament of a whole nation reeling from defeat: God seems to have rejected and scattered them, cracking open and shaking the earth in anger. His people have endured severe hardship, staggering as if given dizzying wine to drink. Yet amid the wreckage they cling to one spark of hope: God has raised a banner for those who fear Him to rally around for truth's sake, so His beloved ones may be rescued (60:1–5).

Suddenly God's own voice breaks through from His sanctuary in an authoritative oracle. He asserts total sovereignty across the Promised Land—from Shechem to the Valley of Succoth, claiming Gilead and Manasseh, naming Ephraim His helmet and Judah His royal scepter. Meanwhile hostile neighbors like Moab, Edom, and Philistia all stand under His sweeping rule (60:6–8).

Hearing that promise, the king asks a blunt, practical question: who will lead his troops into Edom's fortified stronghold if God Himself has rejected them and no longer marches out with their armies? Yet that very question leads to the psalm's humblest and sharpest plea: "Give us aid against the enemy, for the help of man is worthless." The song closes in steady faith: "With God we will perform with valor, and He will trample our foes" (60:9–12).

## Key Takeaways

**Even in defeat, God's people recognize everything stays in His hands.** David blames neither bad luck nor rival gods; he acknowledges that God Himself allowed the setback to discipline His people (60:1–3). The One who shook the earth open is the only One who can mend its fractures (60:2).

**God's sovereignty towers over temporary setbacks.** The divine oracle in verses 6 through 8 reminds Israel that despite one painful loss, the entire land and every surrounding nation still belong completely to God (60:6–8).

**Human help alone is empty.** Verse 11 distills the psalm's central lesson: massive armies, impressive alliances, and clever tactics avail nothing without God (60:11). Only when we admit the limits of human strength do we truly lean on God's power to act valiantly (60:12).

## Deeper Meaning

**Calling Judah God's *scepter* continues the ancient Messianic promise.** The line "Judah is My scepter" (60:7) reaches straight back to Jacob's blessing that the scepter would never depart from Judah until the rightful Ruler comes (Genesis 49:10). The New Testament reveals that royal authority fulfilled completely in Jesus, the Lion of the tribe of Judah (Revelation 5:5).

**Triumphing *with God* echoes across New Testament faith.** The closing certainty that with God we perform valiantly while He tramples our foes (60:12) connects to Paul's pledge that the God of peace will soon crush Satan under believers' feet (Romans 16:20) and his confession: "I can do all things through Christ who gives me strength" (Philippians 4:13).

## Notes & Nuances

**Calling Moab a *washbasin* and tossing a sandal over Edom use ancient idioms of ownership (60:8).** In the ancient Near East, a foot-washing basin belonged to lowly servants, while throwing or handing over a sandal legally signaled claiming property (Ruth 4:7). The verse declares that Israel's proud neighbors stand entirely subject to God's lordship.

**The second half of Psalm 60 reappears almost word for word in Psalm 108 (60:5–12; Psalms 108:6–13).** Just like Psalm 57, this prayer was later woven together with Psalm 57's praise to create a fresh congregational song.

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, stresses verses 11 and 12.
