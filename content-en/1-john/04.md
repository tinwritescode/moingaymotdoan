# 1 John 4 — Testing the Spirits and God Is Love

## Story

John opens with a clear-headed warning: do not automatically believe every spirit or spiritual claim, but test the spirits to see whether they genuinely come from God, because many false prophets have gone out into the world. The test for recognizing God's Spirit is plain: every spirit that confesses "Jesus Christ has come in the flesh" is from God; any spirit that refuses to confess Jesus in this way does not come from God, but carries the spirit of the antichrist already at work in the world (4:1–3).

Yet John steadies their hearts against fear: they belong to God and have already overcome those false prophets, because "He who is in you is greater than he who is in the world." False prophets belong to the world, so they speak the world's language and the world eagerly listens. The apostles, however, belong to God: whoever knows God listens to them, while whoever does not belong to God refuses to listen. By this we recognize "the Spirit of truth and the spirit of falsehood" (4:4–6).

Starting at verse 7, John composes one of the loveliest passages on love in all of Scripture. Let us love one another, for love comes from God; everyone who loves has been born of God and knows Him. Whoever fails to love does not know God, because "God is love." God displayed His love unmistakably by sending His one and only Son into the world so that we might live through Him. Real love does not consist in our loving God first, but in His loving us and sending His Son as the atoning sacrifice for our sins. Since God has loved us so deeply, we also ought to love one another (4:7–11).

No one has ever seen God, yet when we love one another, God abides in us and His love reaches its full expression among us. We know we abide in Him and He in us because He has given us His Spirit. The apostles have personally witnessed and testify that the Father sent the Son to be the Savior of the world. Whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God has God dwelling in him, and he dwells in God (4:12–15).

We have come to know and trust the love God holds for us. John repeats his bold declaration a second time: "God is love," and whoever abides in love abides in God, and God in him. As love matures in us, we gain bold confidence for the day of judgment, because in this world we stand in the same favored standing as Christ. There is no fear in love; instead, "perfect love drives out fear," because fear involves dread of punishment, and anyone who remains afraid has not yet been brought to maturity in love (4:16–18).

He seals the chapter with an airtight truth: "We love because He first loved us." Anyone who declares "I love God" while hating a fellow believer is a liar: if you cannot love a brother standing right in front of your eyes, how can you claim to love the God you have never seen? His command leaves zero ambiguity: whoever loves God must love his brother as well (4:19–21).

## Key Takeaways

**The decisive test of every spiritual message is what it says about Jesus.** Moving rhetoric, large crowds, or loud claims of inspiration never prove a message comes from God. The foundational test is whether it confesses that Jesus Christ truly came in real human flesh to atone for sin, and whether it aligns with the apostles' witness (4:1–6).

**God does not merely possess love; He is the very source and essence of love.** Yet John never detaches "God is love" from the cross to turn it into hazy sentimentality. God's love wears a concrete historical shape: the Father sending His one and only Son to be an atoning sacrifice for people who had not yet loved Him (4:8–10; 4:19).

**Loving the neighbor we can see is the only proof that we love the unseen God.** It is effortless to whisper "I love God" in a quiet room, where God never interrupts our schedule or rubs us the wrong way. John strips away that illusion: how we treat the brother or sister right beside us is the real test of our love for God (4:12; 4:20–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**No one has ever seen God, yet love makes Him visible.** Verse 4:12 ("No one has ever seen God") echoes the closing line of John's Gospel prologue word for word (John 1:18). In the Gospel, the unique Son made the unseen Father known; in 1 John, after the Son has ascended, a community loving one another becomes the place where the world sees God's living presence (4:12; cf. John 13:35).

**"Savior of the world" echoes the Samaritans' confession.** That exact title in verse 4:14 appears only one other time in the entire New Testament—on the lips of the Samaritans after meeting Jesus by Jacob's well (John 4:42). God's love is never fenced inside an elite circle, but reaches across the whole human family.

**Love for God and love for neighbor can never be torn apart.** Verse 4:21 weaves together the two greatest Old Testament commands that Jesus joined as one: loving the LORD with all your heart (Deuteronomy 6:5) and loving your neighbor as yourself (Leviticus 19:18; Mark 12:29–31).

## Notes & Nuances

**"God is love" (4:8; 4:16) cannot be flipped backward into "love is God."** In everyday speech today, people often take romantic feelings or vague affection as the benchmark and project them onto God. John argues in the exact opposite direction: human beings do not invent a definition of love and attach God's name to it; God's concrete act of sending His Son as an atoning sacrifice (4:10) defines what real love means.

**The fear cast out by love in verse 4:18 is dread of judgment and punishment.** This does not conflict with the healthy reverent fear of God commended elsewhere in Scripture (1 Peter 1:17). Reverent fear is deep awe before a holy Father; the fear love drives away is the guilty terror of a condemned defendant waiting for a judge's sentence.

**Verse 4:2 targets an early heresy denying Jesus' true human flesh.** Late in the first century, some teachers (later called Docetists) argued that physical matter was tainted, so God's Son could never genuinely take on a real human body, only appearing to be human. John labels that teaching the spirit of the antichrist: if Jesus never truly became human, then His atoning death was never real either.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fifth-century bishop of Hippo, delivered his famous maxim while preaching on chapter 4: "Love, and do what you will." He immediately explained that when genuine divine love forms the root inside us, only good can spring from that root—whether we stay silent, speak out, correct someone, or forgive. *(Ten Homilies on the First Epistle of John, Homily 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's famous maxim "love and do what you will" anchored in godly love appears in Homily 7 on 1 John 4:4–12 -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observed on verse 4:1 that the church must avoid twin extremes: gullibly accepting everyone who claims to have the Spirit, or retreating into cynical skepticism that shuts out all teaching. Verses 4:2–3 hand every believer a clear measure to test all claims against the gospel of the incarnate Christ. *(Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, on 1 John chapter 4 verses 1–3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's balance on discerning spirits in 1 John 4:1–3—avoiding both credulity and cynicism—is clear in his commentary -->

**John Stott**, the twentieth-century British pastor and commentator, stressed that verses 4:8 and 4:10 bind God's love tightly to the cross. Anyone searching for the true meaning of love should look not to philosophical abstractions, but to Calvary, where God took the initiative to love us before we ever sought Him. *(The Letters of John, Tyndale New Testament Commentaries, 1988, on 1 John chapter 4 verses 7–10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: emphasizing the cross as the objective definition of God's love in 1 John 4:9–10 is central to Stott's TNTC exposition -->
