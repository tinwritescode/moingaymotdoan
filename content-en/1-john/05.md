# 1 John 5 — The Victory of Faith and the Assurance of Eternal Life

## Story

John links faith and love into a seamless circle: everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God, and everyone who loves the Father also loves the children born of Him. We know we genuinely love God's children whenever we love God and carry out His commands. Loving God means obeying His commands—and His commands are not burdensome, because everyone born of God overcomes the world. What secures that victory over the world is our faith: who overcomes the world except the one who believes that Jesus is the Son of God (5:1–5)?

Next, John points to two inseparable historical milestones in Jesus' life: He is the One who came by water and blood—not by water alone, but by both water and blood. And the Spirit bears witness, because the Spirit is truth. Thus three witnesses testify in complete harmony—the Spirit, the water, and the blood—and the three agree as one. If we routinely accept human testimony, God's testimony carries far greater weight, for it is the witness God Himself has given concerning His Son. Whoever believes in the Son of God holds that testimony within his own heart; whoever refuses to believe calls God a liar. That testimony comes down to one crisp, unmistakable message: God has granted us eternal life, and this life is in His Son. "Whoever has the Son has life; whoever does not have the Son of God does not have life" (5:6–12).

Verse 13 states the purpose of the whole letter openly: John writes to those who believe in the name of the Son of God "so that you may know that you have eternal life." That steady assurance opens bold confidence in prayer: whenever we ask anything according to His will, He hears us; and knowing that He hears whatever we ask, we know we already hold what we have asked of Him (5:13–15).

From prayer in general, John turns straight to interceding for a sinning fellow believer. If you see your brother committing a sin that does not lead to death, pray, and God will grant him life. Yet John also mentions "a sin that leads to death," noting he is not telling them to pray about that. All wrongdoing is sin, yet there is sin that does not lead to death (5:16–17).

The letter closes with three ringing declarations beginning with "we know." First, we know that anyone born of God does not keep on sinning; the One born of God keeps him safe, and the evil one cannot lay hold of him. Second, we know we belong to God, while the entire world lies under the sway of the evil one. Third, we know that the Son of God has come and given us understanding so that we may know Him who is true, and we stand in Him who is true—in His Son Jesus Christ, who is the true God and eternal life. Then comes a sudden, memorable final warning: "Little children, keep yourselves from idols!" (5:18–21).

## Key Takeaways

**Obeying God ceases to feel crushing once we genuinely love Him and share His new life.** To outsiders, God's commands look like a bleak list of restrictions. John says His commands are not heavy—not because God lowered the standard, but because those born of God receive fresh inward power and love that turn obedience into joy (5:3–4).

**Eternal life is not something we have to wait until death to discover whether we possess.** The stated goal of the entire letter stands in verse 5:13: so believers may know with calm certainty right now that they have eternal life. That confidence rests not on fluctuating feelings, but on God's trustworthy testimony and living relationship with His Son (5:11–13).

**Guarding against idols sums up an entire letter devoted to the truth.** After five chapters exposing distorted portraits of Jesus, verse 5:21 leaves believers with one sharp charge: stay clear of every idol—not only pagan statues of wood or stone, but any fabricated mental picture of God substituted for the true God revealed in Jesus Christ (5:20–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**1 John 5:13 forms a perfect pair with John 20:31.** At the end of his Gospel, John explains why he wrote it: so that readers may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life in His name (John 20:31). At the close of 1 John, he writes so that those who already believe may know for certain they possess eternal life (5:13). The Gospel brings readers to faith; the letter anchors believers in assurance.

**"Water and blood" recall two defining events in John's Gospel.** "Water" points to Jesus' baptism in the Jordan, where the Father affirmed Him as His beloved Son (John 1:29–34), while "blood" points to His atoning death on the cross. Remarkably, in John's Gospel, when the Roman soldier pierced Jesus' side on the cross, blood and water flowed out immediately (John 19:34–35)—unmistakable evidence that He bore genuine flesh and truly died.

**Jesus is the conqueror of the world.** The phrase "overcomes the world" (5:4–5) echoes Jesus' parting encouragement in the upper room before heading to Gethsemane: in this world you will face tribulation, yet take heart, for I have overcome the world (John 16:33). Believers overcome the world by clinging in faith to His victory.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does verse 5:6 emphasize "not by water alone, but by water and blood"?** Certain late first-century false teachers (such as Cerinthus, remembered by early church fathers) claimed that the divine Spirit of Christ descended onto the man Jesus at His water baptism, yet departed before the crucifixion because deity could not suffer death. John dismantles that split: the very same Jesus Christ came both in the water of baptism and in the shed blood of the cross.

**What is "a sin that leads to death" in verse 5:16?** This puzzling phrase has worried many sensitive readers. Within the letter's context, it does not mean an ordinary moral stumble, but deliberate, hardened apostasy—rejecting Jesus as the Son of God and walking permanently away from the truth just as the secessionist teachers had done (2:19; 2:22; 4:3), akin to blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (Mark 3:28–30). John is certainly not discouraging prayer for stumbling believers; on the contrary, he actively urges intercession to restore struggling brothers and sisters.

**Verses 5:7–8 in the BSB and modern translations differ from the older King James Version.** In the KJV and a few older English versions, verses 5:7–8 include an extra phrase naming three heavenly witnesses—the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit. Known as the *Comma Johanneum*, that line does not appear in any Greek manuscript earlier than the fourteenth century; it began as a Latin marginal explanation that accidentally slipped into later copies. Like other modern translations grounded in the earliest Greek manuscripts, the BSB omits that later insertion.
