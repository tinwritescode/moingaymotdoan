# Ruth 4 — Redeeming the Inheritance and the Line of King David

## Story

Just as Naomi predicted, Boaz wastes not a single minute. The very next morning he goes straight to the city gate—where ancient towns handled public business and legal disputes—and takes a seat to wait. Sure enough, the nearer kinsman-redeemer he spoke of the night before walks right past. Boaz calls him over to sit down, then gathers ten of the city's elders to sit alongside them as official witnesses (4:1–2).

Boaz lays out the matter plainly. Naomi, who returned from Moab, is selling the parcel of land that belonged to their late relative Elimelech. By law, this man holds first right to buy it back, and Boaz stands second in line. So right here in front of the elders and townspeople, does he want to redeem it? Hearing a chance to add good farmland to his family's holdings, the relative agrees without hesitation: "I will redeem it" (4:3–4).

Only then does Boaz bring out the second half of the arrangement: on the day he buys the field from Naomi, he must also marry Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of the deceased, to raise up an heir who will carry on the dead man's name on his inheritance. At that, the man reverses course immediately. He worries that if he marries Ruth and fathers a son who bears Elimelech's family name, the newly bought land will eventually pass to that child, draining and complicating his own children's inheritance. He backs out and transfers his right of redemption to Boaz (4:5–6).

The author pauses to explain an old Israelite custom: in earlier days, whenever property was redeemed or a right transferred, a man would slip off his sandal and hand it to the other party to make the agreement legally binding. So the nearer relative tells Boaz, "Buy it for yourself," and takes off his sandal (4:7–8).

Boaz rises and makes a formal declaration before the ten elders and everyone gathered at the gate: you are witnesses today that I am buying from Naomi everything that belonged to Elimelech, Chilion, and Mahlon, and at the same time I am taking Ruth the Moabitess as my wife to preserve the name of the deceased, so his name will not vanish from his brothers or from his hometown gate. The elders and all the people respond in chorus as witnesses and pour out a warm blessing: may the LORD make the woman entering your house like Rachel and Leah—the two mothers who built up the whole house of Israel—and may your family flourish like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah (4:9–12).

Boaz marries Ruth. The LORD enables her to conceive, and she gives birth to a son. Now the women of Bethlehem—the very neighbors who had once gasped when Naomi called herself *Mara*, the bitter one, back in chapter 1—gather around Naomi again in celebration. They praise the LORD for not leaving her without a kinsman-redeemer, and speak a tender tribute to Ruth: this child will renew her life and sustain her in old age, because his mother is the daughter-in-law who loves her and "is better to you than seven sons" (4:13–15).

Naomi takes the baby boy into her lap and lovingly cares for him. The neighborhood women announce with joy, "A son has been born to Naomi," and name him Obed, meaning *servant*. Then the author drops a single sentence that floods the entire book with light: "He became the father of Jesse, the father of David." The book closes with a crisp ten-generation genealogy running from Perez through Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmon, Boaz, Obed, and Jesse, coming to rest on one unforgettable name: David (4:16–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Those who cling only to protecting their own name lose it; those who sacrifice for others are remembered forever.** Chapter 4 carries a razor-sharp irony: the nearer kinsman refuses to marry Ruth out of fear it will jeopardize his own estate (4:6), and the author does not even bother recording his name—in the original Hebrew, Boaz literally addresses him as *so-and-so* (4:1), politely rendered "my friend." Boaz, by contrast, gladly spends his own money to redeem the land and marry Ruth so a dead relative's name will live on—and as a result, Boaz's own name stands forever in the royal line of Israel's kings and of the Savior.

**God heals our emptiness with a story more beautiful than we dared hope for.** At the start of the book, Naomi loses her husband and both sons, comes home empty-handed, and tastes only bitterness. By the end, she cradles a grandson in her lap, is cherished by a daughter-in-law "better to you than seven sons" (4:15), and hears her bitterness turned into the whole town's song of praise. God does not merely hand back what was lost; He turns loss into the doorway to a far richer blessing.

**Quiet faithfulness in small things paves the way for great history.** Across all four chapters, Ruth and Boaz have no clue what role they are playing in their nation's future. They are simply caring faithfully for an aging widow, keeping their promises, and practicing everyday goodness in a chaotic age. Yet right out of that small barley field in Bethlehem, God prepares the lineage of Israel's greatest king.

**God's grace turns an outsider into family.** From beginning to end, Ruth is repeatedly labeled *the Moabitess*—an outsider from a nation Israel viewed with suspicion. Yet on her wedding day, the elders bless her right alongside Rachel and Leah, the founding mothers of Israel (4:11). In God's family, loyalty and faith matter far more than pedigree.

## Deeper Meaning

**The genealogy at the end of Ruth connects directly to the opening page of the New Testament.** The ten names closing the book of Ruth (4:18–22) are copied verbatim right into the opening chapter of Matthew's Gospel introducing the origins of Jesus (Matthew 1:3–6). Remarkably, alongside Ruth the Moabitess, the Savior's family tree also includes Tamar (4:12) and Rahab, the woman of Jericho who became Boaz's own mother (Matthew 1:5). The very bloodline of the Messiah embraces both Jew and Gentile alike.

**Boaz's public redemption at the city gate foreshadows Jesus' public redemption on the cross.** Boaz does not act in secret; he pays the price openly before the elders to redeem the inheritance and take Ruth as his bride. In the New Testament, Jesus likewise pays the redemption price openly on the cross—not with silver or gold, but with His own precious blood—to redeem the Church as His bride (1 Peter 1:18–19; Ephesians 5:25–27).

**The answer to the dark era of the Judges.** The book of Judges ends with a weary sigh: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). The book of Ruth, set during that very era, closes with a single word answering that longing: *David*—announcing that a king after God's own heart is on the way, and pointing further still to the King of kings, the Son of David (Luke 1:32–33).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why was the nearer relative afraid he would "jeopardize my own inheritance"?** Had he simply bought Naomi's land, his money would have folded that field into his own children's estate. Once the purchase came paired with marrying Ruth in the spirit of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5–6), the firstborn son would carry Elimelech's family name and inherit that field. In the relative's eyes, he would pay the purchase price and raise the child, only to watch the property pass out of his own family name.

**Why did the women say "A son has been born to Naomi" (4:17)?** Biologically the baby is Boaz and Ruth's son, yet legally and relationally he continues the family line of Naomi's late husband and sons, keeping her household from dying out. The neighbors' exclamation honors Ruth and Boaz's selflessness while marking Naomi's complete restoration.

**Does the genealogy in 4:18–22 skip several generations?** More than eight hundred years separate Perez (son of Judah, around the nineteenth or eighteenth century BC) from King David (around 1000 BC), yet the list records only ten names. Biblical genealogies commonly select key representative figures to form a memorable symmetrical structure—here exactly ten generations, mirroring the ten generations from Adam to Noah and from Shem to Abraham—where "was the father of" can carry the broader sense of "was the ancestor of."

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, smiled at the irony surrounding the unnamed relative in 4:6. He observed that the man was so calculating about protecting his own estate and family name that he refused an act of mercy—only to have Scripture forget his name completely, while the name of generous Boaz lives on for all generations. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ruth chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the ironic observation that the self-protective relative loses his very name in Scripture is a very famous comment by Matthew Henry on Ruth 4 -->

**Kirsten Nielsen**, the Danish Old Testament scholar, stresses the indispensable role of the genealogy in 4:18–22. Rather than dismissing it as a dry appendix tacked on later, she argues that the genealogy unlocks the political and theological purpose of the entire book: it demonstrates that David's dynasty is not merely legitimate, but built on a foundation of loyal kindness, integrity, and gracious welcome toward foreigners. *(Ruth: A Commentary, Old Testament Library, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading the closing genealogy as the theological centerpiece rather than a late appendix is a prominent argument in Nielsen's OTL commentary; exact page not verified -->

**Daniel Block**, the American Old Testament scholar, observes that across chapter 4, God is directly mentioned as acting only once, in 4:13 ("the LORD enabled her to conceive"), matching the single direct mention in 1:6 (where God provides food for His people). In Block's view, the entire book teaches a profound lesson: God rarely puts on noisy miraculous displays, choosing instead to work quietly behind ordinary people who live faithfully day by day. *(Judges, Ruth, New American Commentary, 1999)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting that God acts directly only twice in Ruth (1:6 and 4:13) is a well-known structural observation in commentaries like Block; exact page not verified -->
