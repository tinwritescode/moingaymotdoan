# Ruth 3 — A Night at the Threshing Floor and a Promise to Redeem

## Story

Both the barley and wheat harvests have ended. The two women now have enough food to get by, yet Ruth's future still hangs in the air. Naomi cannot sit back and watch her devoted daughter-in-law remain a widow forever. She tells Ruth it is time to seek "a resting place" for her—a home of security and happiness where life will go well for her (3:1).

Naomi knows that tonight Boaz will be down at the threshing floor winnowing barley, using the cool evening breeze to blow away the chaff. She lays out a daring plan for Ruth: wash up, put on perfume, dress in her best clothes (also signaling that her season of mourning is over), and go down to the threshing floor. She is to stay out of sight until Boaz finishes eating and drinking, note the spot where he lies down beside the grain pile, slip in quietly, uncover his feet, and lie down there—and Boaz himself will tell her what to do next. Trusting her mother-in-law completely, Ruth replies, "I will do everything you say" (3:2–5).

Everything unfolds just as Naomi expected. After eating and drinking, in good spirits over a bountiful harvest, Boaz lies down at the end of a heap of barley. Ruth slips in silently, uncovers his feet, and lies down. At midnight, Boaz wakes with a start, rolls over, and is stunned to find a woman lying right at his feet (3:6–8).

"Who are you?" Boaz asks. Rather than simply waiting passively for his instructions as Naomi suggested, Ruth takes the lead with a marriage proposal that is both delicate and unmistakable: "I am your servant Ruth. Spread the corner of your garment over me, for you are a kinsman-redeemer." In the culture of that day, asking a man to draw the corner of his cloak or blanket over you was a clear request for him to take you as his wife and shelter you for life (3:9).

Boaz's response is deeply moving. Far from being offended or dismissive, he blesses her on the spot: "You have shown more kindness now than before, because you have not run after the younger men, whether rich or poor." Instead of pursuing a younger husband for herself, Ruth has chosen to appeal to an older kinsman-redeemer like Boaz in order to preserve her late husband's family line and care for Naomi. Boaz reassures her not to be afraid—he will do everything she asks, because everyone in Bethlehem knows she is "a woman of noble character" (3:10–11).

Yet Boaz is a man of integrity who honors the law. He tells Ruth honestly that while he is indeed a kinsman-redeemer, there is another relative in the clan even nearer than he is. He tells her to stay safely where she is until morning; first thing tomorrow he will approach the other man. If that man chooses to redeem her, well and good; if not, Boaz swears by the living LORD that he himself will surely redeem her (3:12–13).

Ruth lies at his feet until just before dawn, rising while it is still too dark for anyone to recognize another's face, because Boaz is determined to protect her reputation: "Do not let it be known that a woman came to the threshing floor." Before she leaves, he tells her to hold out her shawl and shovels six heavy measures of barley into it for her to carry home. When she arrives back at the house, Naomi asks straightaway how things went. Ruth recounts everything Boaz did and shows her the six measures of barley, repeating his thoughtful message: "Do not go back to your mother-in-law empty-handed." Hearing that, Naomi tells Ruth to sit back and wait in peace, because she knows what kind of man Boaz is: "he will not rest unless he has resolved the matter today" (3:14–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Loyal love actively looks for ways to build someone else's happiness.** Back in chapter 1, Naomi could see nothing but her own bitterness. Now her thoughts turn entirely toward her daughter-in-law's future, seeking "a resting place" for her (3:1). Ruth, in turn, does not chase after younger men for her own sake, but thinks of the honor and security of her late husband's family (3:10). Two widowed women are living for each other first.

**True nobility practices self-control in the dark and guards the honor of the vulnerable.** Late at night on a quiet threshing floor, with nobody watching and a foreign widow lying at his feet, taking advantage of the situation would have been all too easy. Instead, Boaz treats Ruth with unwavering respect: he keeps his hands off her, shields her good name before sunrise, and honors the prior legal claim of the nearer relative even though his affection for her is unmistakable.

**Answering our own prayers with our own actions.** In chapter 2, Boaz had blessed Ruth for coming to take refuge "under whose wings" the LORD shelters His own (2:12). Here in chapter 3, Ruth uses the very same Hebrew word when she says, "Spread the corner of your garment over me" (3:9)—for the Hebrew word for the *corner* or *edge* of a garment is the exact same word as *wings*. Gently and boldly, she is reminding him: you once prayed that I would be sheltered under God's wings; now please become those wings!

**Fullness begins replacing emptiness.** At the end of chapter 1, Naomi lamented coming home "empty" (1:21). By the end of chapter 3, Boaz sends six measures of barley along with the words, "Do not go back to your mother-in-law empty-handed" (3:17). That gift is a tangible promise that God is about to fill Naomi's emptiness to the brim.

## Deeper Meaning

**Spreading the corner of a garment over someone pictures God's marriage covenant.** In the book of Ezekiel, God Himself uses this exact gesture to describe taking poor, forsaken Israel as His bride: He spreads the corner of His garment over her, enters into covenant with her, and makes her His own (Ezekiel 16:8). Ruth's plea on the threshing floor mirrors the covenant of grace between God and His people.

**A woman of noble character—the bridge linking Ruth to Proverbs 31.** Boaz calls Ruth "a woman of noble character" (3:11)—the exact same Hebrew expression used for the "wife of noble character" worth far more than rubies at the close of Proverbs (Proverbs 31:10). In the traditional Hebrew ordering of Scripture, the book of Ruth often sits right after Proverbs, as if Ruth herself is the flesh-and-blood portrait of that famous poem.

**A Redeemer who will not rest until redemption is complete.** Naomi's confidence in 3:18—"he will not rest unless he has resolved the matter today"—rings like a radiant foreshadowing of Jesus. He never settles for a half-finished rescue of His Church, but presses steadily all the way to the cross until He can declare, "It is finished" (John 19:30).

## Notes & Nuances

**What really happened on the threshing floor?** Many modern readers, hearing of a woman uncovering a man's feet in the dark, immediately suspect a sexual encounter—especially because the Hebrew word for *feet* can occasionally serve as a euphemism. Yet reading the chapter carefully shows the author goes out of his way to affirm their complete moral purity: Boaz wakes up startled in the middle of the night (3:8), praises Ruth as "a woman of noble character" (3:11), keeps her sleeping *at his feet* for safety until dawn (3:14), and immediately settles the marriage legally before the city elders the very next morning (4:1–13). Any illicit affair would completely unravel the book's celebration of Boaz's and Ruth's integrity.

**Why did Naomi's plan seem so risky?** Had Ruth proposed marriage to Boaz out in the open fields in broad daylight in front of all the harvesters, both of them would have faced intense embarrassment if Boaz declined or if the nearer kinsman stepped in. A private meeting at the threshing floor gave Boaz room to respond discreetly without anyone losing face. Even so, it was a bold gamble—one that banked completely on Boaz's upright character.

**How much were the "six measures of barley" in 3:15?** The original Hebrew literally says just *six of barley*, without naming a unit of measure (English translations supply *measures* for clarity). If measured in the common *seah* of that era, six measures of barley would weigh roughly 25 to 35 kilograms (around 55 to 75 pounds)—a heavy load, yet still manageable for a strong woman to carry home on her shoulder or head. The number six may also hint at a gift almost complete, waiting for the seventh—the coming marriage itself—to make it full.
