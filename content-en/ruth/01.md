# Ruth 1 — A Bitter Return and a Vow of Devotion

## Story

The story opens in a turbulent age: "In the days when the judges ruled," a time when Israel had no king and everyone did what was right in his own eyes (cf. Judges 21:25). A famine strikes the land of Judah. A man named Elimelech lives in Bethlehem—a town whose very name means *house of bread*, yet now has no bread to eat. Taking his wife Naomi and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, he crosses the Dead Sea to the land of Moab to survive the famine (1:1–2).

What begins as a temporary move turns into a decade-long tragedy. Elimelech dies in this foreign land, leaving Naomi with her two sons. The young men marry Moabite women named Orpah and Ruth. Then both Mahlon and Chilion die as well, leaving no children behind. Naomi is left stranded in a foreign country, "without her two sons and without her husband" (1:3–5).

One day, Naomi hears news that the LORD has attended to His people by providing food in Judah once again. She packs up and sets out with her two daughters-in-law on the road back home. After walking a stretch together, Naomi stops and urges both women to return to their mothers' homes. She prays that the LORD will show them the same loving devotion they have shown to their late husbands and to her, granting each of them rest in the home of a new husband. She kisses them goodbye. Both women weep aloud and insist they want to go back with her to her people (1:6–10).

Naomi will not hear of it. She lays out the harsh reality: she is far too old to marry again and have children; even if she had a husband tonight and bore sons, would the two of them really wait around for those boys to grow up so they could marry them? She sighs that her grief is far more bitter than theirs, because "the hand of the LORD has gone out against me" (1:11–13).

The two daughters-in-law break down weeping again. Finally, Orpah kisses her mother-in-law goodbye and turns back. But Ruth refuses to leave her. Pointing after Orpah, Naomi urges Ruth one last time: your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and her gods; follow her home. Ruth answers with one of the most moving vows in all of Scripture: "Do not urge me to leave you or to turn from following you. For wherever you go, I will go, and wherever you live, I will live; your people will be my people, and your God will be my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried." She seals it with a solemn oath that nothing but death itself will separate her from her mother-in-law. Seeing how determined Ruth is, Naomi stops trying to persuade her (1:14–18).

The two widows make the journey on foot back to Bethlehem. The whole little town buzzes at their arrival. The women recognize an old neighbor and exclaim in surprise, "Can this be Naomi?" The name *Naomi* means *pleasant* or *sweet*. Sorrowfully she answers, "Do not call me Naomi. Call me Mara, because the Almighty has dealt quite bitterly with me." (*Mara* means *bitter*.) She tells them she went away full, and the LORD has brought her back empty. Yet the closing line of the chapter quietly plants a small gleam of hope: the two women arrive in Bethlehem right "at the beginning of the barley harvest" (1:19–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Even in the darkest eras, quiet faithfulness still shines.** The book of Ruth unfolds right in the middle of the violent, chaotic period of the Judges. There are no earth-shattering miracles here and no battlefield heroes—just a foreign young woman choosing to stay and care for her widowed mother-in-law. God's light often radiates from the humblest acts inside a poor family.

**Loyal love defies every calculation of self-interest.** Orpah's decision to return to her mother's house is completely reasonable, and Scripture never criticizes her for it. Ruth, however, chooses something beyond ordinary sense: leaving behind her homeland, her family, and her best chance to remarry in order to cling to an aging widow with nothing to offer—even embracing Naomi's people and Naomi's God as her own.

**Genuine faith does not force people to fake cheerfulness while they are hurting.** Naomi makes no attempt to disguise her bitterness. She says openly before the whole town that her life right now is *Mara*—bitter—and that she feels God's hand striking against her (1:20–21). What stands out, though, is that she still recognizes God as sovereign and still turns her steps back toward the land of God's people rather than walking away altogether.

**When we feel we are returning empty-handed, God has already placed blessing right beside us.** Naomi laments that "the LORD has brought me back empty" (1:21), even while standing right next to her is a daughter-in-law who will prove "better to you than seven sons" (4:15), and stretching out before them are barley fields just entering the harvest (1:22). Grief has a way of clouding our eyes so we miss the seeds of hope God has already quietly planted.

## Deeper Meaning

**A foreigner enters God's people through faith.** The Law of Moses had barred Moabites from entering the assembly of the LORD even to the tenth generation (Deuteronomy 23:3). Yet Ruth, a Moabite woman, chooses the God of Israel with all her heart. Her vow—"your God will be my God" (1:16)—opens up a great current running through the entire Bible: God's grace is never boxed in by bloodline or borders, but welcomes everyone who trusts in Him (Acts 10:34–35; Galatians 3:28).

**Walking in the footsteps of Abraham.** When Ruth leaves her father, her mother, and her native land to live among a people she has never known (2:11), she is retracing the very step of faith Abraham took long before when he left Ur to follow God's call (Genesis 12:1).

**A small seed in Bethlehem leads to the Savior.** The little town of Bethlehem where Naomi and Ruth arrive empty-handed today will become the birthplace of King David a few generations later—and a thousand years after that, the birthplace of Jesus (Matthew 1:5; 2:1). The name of Ruth, this widowed Moabite woman, is written with honor right into the genealogy of the Savior.

## Notes & Nuances

**The word "clung" in 1:14 carries more weight than it first appears.** When Scripture says "Ruth clung to her," the underlying Hebrew verb means to stick fast or cleave tightly—the exact same verb used when a man leaves his father and mother and is "united to his wife" (Genesis 2:24) and when Israel is told to "hold fast to" the LORD (Deuteronomy 10:20). Ruth's commitment carries the weight of a lifelong covenant.

**The meaning behind the names in chapter 1.** In Hebrew storytelling, names often hint at the entire plot: *Elimelech* means *My God is King* (sharply ironic as he leaves God's land for Moab); *Naomi* means *pleasant* or *sweet*, while *Mara* means *bitter*; *Mahlon* evokes *sickness* and *Chilion* evokes *wasting away*. Scholars debate whether these were their original birth names or wordplay chosen by the author to underscore the family's tragedy.

**Should we blame Elimelech for moving to Moab or Orpah for turning back?** The text narrates events with remarkable restraint, offering no condemnation of either Elimelech or Orpah. Many older readers assumed the deaths of the three men were divine punishment for leaving the promised land for Moab, yet Scripture never says so. As for Orpah, she simply accepts her mother-in-law's sincere counsel; her ordinary, sensible choice serves to throw Ruth's extraordinary devotion into sharper relief.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, treats Ruth's declaration in 1:16–17 as the finest model of wholehearted commitment to God.
