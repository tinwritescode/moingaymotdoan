# Ruth 2 — Gleaning in the Fields and Finding a Benefactor

## Story

Right as chapter 2 opens, the narrator lets the reader in on a crucial fact that neither Naomi nor Ruth has even considered yet: in Bethlehem, Naomi has a relative on her late husband's side named Boaz, a wealthy and widely respected man of noble character from the clan of Elimelech (2:1).

Two penniless widows still need food for the day. Ruth takes the initiative and asks her mother-in-law's permission to go out into the fields and glean fallen heads of grain behind the harvesters, hoping to find a landowner generous enough to allow it. Naomi gives her blessing. Ruth sets off, walks into a field, and quietly gathers stalks behind the reapers. Scripture drops a sentence that sounds completely casual yet brims with quiet irony: "And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was from the clan of Elimelech" (2:2–3).

Before long, Boaz arrives from Bethlehem to check on the harvest. The greeting exchanged between owner and workers immediately signals that the atmosphere here stands apart from the surrounding lawlessness: Boaz calls out, "The LORD be with you," and the harvesters answer, "The LORD bless you." Spotting an unfamiliar face, Boaz asks his foreman which family the young woman belongs to. The foreman explains that she is the Moabite daughter-in-law who came back with Naomi; she politely asked to glean and has worked steadily from early morning until now, pausing only briefly in the shelter (2:4–7).

Boaz immediately calls Ruth over and speaks to her with deep warmth. Addressing her as "my daughter," he tells her not to go glean in any other field, but to stay right here close to his servant girls. He lets her know he has given strict orders to the young men not to touch or bother her, and whenever she gets thirsty, she is welcome to drink freely from the water jars they have filled (2:8–9).

Overwhelmed, Ruth bows her face low to the ground before him and asks why a foreigner like her should receive such kindness and notice. Boaz answers that he has heard the full story of everything she has done for her mother-in-law since her husband died—how she left her father, her mother, and the land of her birth to live among a people she had never known. Then he pronounces a tender, unforgettable blessing over her: "May the LORD repay your work, and may you receive a rich reward from the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have taken refuge." Ruth thanks him humbly for comforting her and speaking so kindly, even though she does not even rank alongside one of his servant girls (2:10–13).

When mealtime comes, Boaz invites Ruth to sit with the harvesters and dip her bread in the cooling vinegar sauce. With his own hand he passes her roasted grain; she eats until she is full and still has leftovers to wrap up and carry home. As Ruth gets up to go back to gleaning, Boaz quietly instructs his workers to let her gather right among the sheaves without embarrassing her—and more than that, "pull out for her some stalks from the bundles and leave them for her to gather. Do not rebuke her" (2:14–16).

Ruth keeps gleaning tirelessly until evening. When she beats out what she has collected, she has about an ephah of barley—an astonishing haul, enough to feed both women for weeks. She hoists the grain and carries it back into town to show her mother-in-law, pulling out the leftovers from lunch to give her as well. Seeing such a pile of barley, Naomi lights up and immediately asks whose field she worked in today and blesses the man who took notice of her. The moment Ruth speaks the name *Boaz*, Naomi's dark clouds break open. She blesses Boaz and exclaims that the LORD "has not withdrawn His kindness from the living or the dead!" Then she reveals a vital truth to Ruth: Boaz is a close relative—one of their "kinsman-redeemers." Ruth adds that Boaz told her to stay with his workers until the entire harvest is finished. Naomi agrees that staying close to his young women will keep her safe. So Ruth gleans alongside Boaz's servant girls right through both the barley and the wheat harvests, continuing to live with and care for her mother-in-law (2:17–23).

## Key Takeaways

**God often guides us through what looks like pure coincidence.** The line "she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz" (2:3) carries the narrator's knowing smile. Ruth has no idea who Boaz is or which patch of farmland belongs to him; she simply steps out in faithfulness to do the work in front of her. God's unseen providence quietly steers her footsteps to the exact right place.

**True kindness protects the dignity of the person receiving it.** The Law of Moses required landowners to leave fallen grain for the poor and the foreigner (Leviticus 19:9–10). Boaz goes far beyond the legal minimum: he shields Ruth from harassment, invites her to share the table, and quietly tells his harvesters to drop extra stalks in her path so she never has to feel like a beggar. The loveliest generosity knows how to preserve the dignity of the poor.

**Taking refuge under God's wings—and receiving His care through human hands.** Boaz prays that Ruth will be richly rewarded by the God "under whose wings you have taken refuge" (2:12)—and then, in the very next breath, becomes those sheltering wings himself. God often answers our prayers by using the very person who prayed them.

**One gracious act can rekindle hope in a heart that had gone cold.** At the close of chapter 1, a bitter Naomi assumed God had turned against her and afflicted her. After a single day of gleaning and the mention of Boaz's name, her tone transforms completely: once again she praises the LORD, "who has not withdrawn His kindness from the living or the dead" (2:20).

## Deeper Meaning

**The gleaning laws reveal God's heart for the vulnerable.** Old Testament law instructed farmers never to reap all the way to the corners of their fields or go back for dropped stalks, leaving them instead for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the foreigner (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19). The story of Ruth shows how alive and humane that law becomes when a God-fearing person like Boaz actually puts it into practice in everyday life.

**The image of taking refuge under God's wings.** Boaz's blessing in 2:12 echoes the tender language of the Psalms, which picture safety and shelter beneath the shadow of God's wings (Psalms 36:7; 91:4). In the New Testament, Jesus Himself uses the image of a mother hen gathering her chicks under her wings to express His compassion for Jerusalem (Matthew 23:37).

**The kinsman-redeemer points ahead to our Redeemer.** For the first time in the book, Naomi introduces the idea of a *kinsman-redeemer* (2:20). Under Israelite law, when someone fell into poverty and had to sell family land, or died without an heir, a close relative had the right to step in, buy back the property, and uphold the family (Leviticus 25:25). Boaz—wealthy, bound by blood, and willing to pay the price to rescue poor relatives who cannot save themselves—stands as one of the clearest Old Testament portraits of Jesus Christ, the Redeemer of humanity (Hebrews 2:11–15).

## Notes & Nuances

**How much is an "ephah" of barley?** Scholars estimate an ephah at roughly 20 to 22 liters, weighing around 13 to 15 kilograms (about 30 pounds) of threshed grain. For a lone woman gleaning fallen stalks in a single day, that is an extraordinary amount—roughly half a month's wages for a day laborer at the time. No wonder Naomi gasped the instant she saw it.

**Why did Boaz have to warn the young men not to touch Ruth?** Ruth was doubly vulnerable—a widow with no male protector and a foreign woman from Moab—living during the violent era of the Judges (cf. Judges 19–21). Gleaning alone in open fields exposed her to a very real threat of humiliation or assault. Both Boaz's warning in 2:9 and Naomi's advice in 2:22 respond to that concrete danger.

**How long did the barley and wheat harvests last?** In Israel, the barley harvest began around April, and the wheat harvest wrapped up in late May or early June. That means verse 2:23 covers roughly six to eight weeks of Ruth working faithfully in Boaz's fields before the events of chapter 3 unfold.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, delighted in the detail that Ruth "happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz" (2:3). He explains that what appears purely accidental to human eyes sits squarely inside God's wise orchestration; even the smallest daily steps of those who trust the LORD are guided by His hand. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ruth chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the remark on divine providence hidden behind seeming chance in Ruth 2:3 is one of Matthew Henry's most famous comments on the book of Ruth -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the nineteenth-century English preacher, drew on Boaz's instruction to pull extra stalks from the bundles and leave them for Ruth (2:16) as a picture of God's abundant grace toward everyone who seeks Him. He urged preachers to follow Boaz's example: never preach aloofly or stingily, but drop plenty of *handfuls of grace* within easy reach for poor, trembling souls to gather up. *(Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon preached a famous sermon titled Handfuls of Purpose based directly on Ruth 2:16 -->

**Daniel Block**, the American Old Testament scholar, observes that Boaz comes like a breath of fresh air against the dark backdrop of the Judges period. Amid widespread moral decay, the mutual greeting in the LORD's name between Boaz and his harvesters (2:4) reveals a landowner who genuinely weaves his faith into compassionate treatment of his workers and the poor. *(Judges, Ruth, New American Commentary, 1999)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block emphasizes Boaz's role as a sharp contrast to the Judges era in his NAC volume; exact page not verified -->
