# Philippians 2 — The Mind of Christ

## Story

Paul begins with four "ifs": if there is any encouragement in Christ, if any comfort from His love, if any fellowship with the Spirit, if any affection and compassion, then make his joy complete by being like-minded, having the same love, being one in spirit and purpose. Do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit, but in humility consider others more important than yourselves. Each person should look not only to their own interests, but also to the interests of others (2:1–4). Quiet cracks of friction seem to have surfaced inside Philippi; by chapter 4, Paul will name two individuals directly.

"Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus." Paul then quotes lines widely recognized as an early Christian hymn. Existing in the form of God, He did not consider equality with God something to be grasped, but emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in human likeness. And being found in appearance as a man, He humbled Himself and became obedient to death—even death on a cross (2:5–8). That is the downward arc: from the throne of God to the posture of a slave, from life to death, and down to the most degrading execution Rome reserved for slaves.

Then comes the upward arc. Therefore God exalted Him to the highest place and gave Him the name above all names, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow—in heaven and on earth and under the earth—and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father (2:9–11; Isaiah 45:23). Isaiah declared that every knee would bow before the LORD; Paul places Jesus right in that throne room. Citizens of Philippi knew lofty imperial hymns glorifying Caesar; this hymn glorifies a crucified servant.

Therefore, beloved friends, just as they have always obeyed—not only in his presence, but now much more in his absence—they must work out their salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in them both to will and to act according to His good purpose (2:12–13). They work precisely *because* God is at work. This verse never suggests earning salvation by human effort, but living out fully the salvation God has already planted inside them.

Do everything without grumbling or arguing, so that they may become blameless and pure, children of God without fault in a crooked and depraved generation, shining among them like stars in the universe as they hold firmly to the word of life. Then Paul will be able to boast on the day of Christ that he did not run or labor in vain. Even if his life is poured out like a drink offering on the sacrifice and service of their faith, he rejoices and shares his joy with all of them—and they too should rejoice and share their joy with him (2:14–18; Deuteronomy 32:5; Numbers 28:7). Grumbling was Israel's classic sin in the wilderness. A drink offering was wine poured atop the main altar sacrifice; Paul views their faith as the main offering, and his possible martyrdom as simply the wine poured on top.

Next he writes about two friends he plans to send. He hopes in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy soon so he himself can be cheered by news of them. He has no one else like-minded who genuinely cares for their welfare, for everyone else looks out for their own interests rather than those of Jesus Christ. They already know Timothy's proven worth, how as a son with his father he served beside Paul in the gospel. Paul plans to send him as soon as he sees how his own case turns out, trusting in the Lord that he himself will come soon as well (2:19–24). Timothy embodies in flesh and blood the opening charge to look out for others' interests.

Meanwhile, Paul considers it necessary to send back Epaphroditus—his brother, fellow worker, and fellow soldier, whom the Philippians had dispatched to care for his needs. Epaphroditus has been longing for all of them and was deeply distressed because they heard he had fallen ill. Indeed, he fell so sick he nearly died, yet God had mercy on him—and not on him alone, but also on Paul, to spare Paul sorrow upon sorrow. Paul is therefore eager to send him home so they can rejoice upon seeing him again and Paul's own anxiety will ease. Welcome him in the Lord with great joy, and honor people like him, because he nearly died for the work of Christ, risking his life to make up for the help the Philippians could not personally deliver (2:25–30). Paul makes no effort to hide his grief: apostle or not, he never pretends it did not hurt when his friend almost died.

## Key Takeaways

**Unity begins with humility.** Paul appeals for harmony not by barking orders, but by reminding them of the grace they already share in Christ. Healing division requires laying down rivalry and vanity and choosing to "consider others more important than yourselves," actively caring for one another's good (2:1–4).

**Christ's path descends before it rises.** The centerpiece hymn tells Jesus' whole story in two sweeping movements: refusing to cling selfishly to His divine status, He emptied Himself, took a slave's place, and obeyed all the way to a cross—and for that very reason God lifted Him above every name (2:5–11).

**We work out our salvation because God is already working inside us.** The call to "work out your salvation with fear and trembling" stands anchored to the promise that God Himself energizes both our desires and our actions. Living without grumbling or quarreling lets the church shine like bright stars in a dark world (2:12–16).

**Christ's mindset takes shape in ordinary, visible people.** Paul never leaves humility suspended in midair; he points directly to three living examples—himself ready to be poured out like a drink offering, Timothy caring genuinely for the church instead of chasing self-interest, and Epaphroditus nearly giving his life to serve Christ (2:17–30).

## Deeper Meaning

**Adam grasped; Christ let go.** In Eden, the serpent lured humanity with the promise that eating forbidden fruit would make them "like God" (Genesis 3:5), and humanity reached out to seize a rank not theirs. Christ reversed that tragedy completely: already equal with God, He refused to clutch His privilege and stooped all the way down (2:6–8).

**Isaiah's Suffering Servant.** Christ "taking the form of a servant" and humbling Himself "to death" (2:7–8) directly echoes Isaiah's great Servant Song: the Servant who "poured out His life unto death" bearing the sins of many, and who consequently "will be raised and lifted up and highly exalted" (Isaiah 52:13; Isaiah 53:11–12).

**An oath reserved for the LORD alone now belongs to Jesus.** In the Old Testament, God declared exclusively of Himself, "Before Me every knee will bow; by Me every tongue will swear" (Isaiah 45:23). When the Philippian hymn applies that pledge to the name of Jesus and confesses Him as Lord (2:10–11), the earliest church unambiguously recognized Jesus as sharing the divine name and sovereignty of Israel's God.

**The foot-washing upper room puts this hymn in motion.** On the night before His death, fully aware that He had come from God and was returning to God, Jesus rose from supper, laid aside His outer garment, wrapped a towel around His waist, and washed His disciples' feet (John 13:3–5). Performing a household slave's task is the living picture of "emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant" (2:7).

**Refusing Israel's wilderness mistake.** Urging believers to avoid grumbling and live blamelessly amid a "crooked and perverse generation" (2:14–15) echoes Moses indicting rebellious Israel as "a warped and crooked generation" (Deuteronomy 32:5), while shining like lights recalls Daniel's promise that the wise will "shine like the stars forever and ever" (Daniel 12:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**"Emptied Himself" in 2:7 does not mean Jesus gave up His deity.** Some modern theologians imagined Jesus shedding divine attributes like omnipotence or omniscience during the incarnation. Yet the hymn itself immediately explains *how* He emptied Himself: not by subtracting divinity, but by adding humanity—"taking the form of a servant, being made in human likeness" (2:7). He surrendered status and privilege to embrace humble servanthood.

**"Did not consider equality with God something to be grasped" (2:6).** Because the Greek noun here (*harpagmos*) is exceedingly rare, scholars highlight two closely related nuances: either He already possessed equality with God and refused to cling selfishly to its privileges, or He refused to exploit His equality with God for personal advantage, using it instead to serve. Both point to the same truth: real power never grabs for itself.

**Does "work out your salvation with fear and trembling" (2:12) conflict with salvation by grace?** Torn away from 2:13, verse 12 can sound like earning heaven by sweat, contradicting Paul's clear teaching elsewhere (Ephesians 2:8–9). Yet Paul addresses people already saved; "working out" means putting the salvation they already possess into active communal practice with earnest reverence—powered entirely by the next verse's promise: God Himself is working inside them (2:13).

**Epaphroditus's near-fatal illness (2:25–30).** Strikingly, Paul—an apostle through whom God performed extraordinary healings—did not simply snap his fingers to heal his dear coworker, but thanked God for showing mercy and pulling him back from the brink. The episode shows that miraculous healing is never at a Christian's automatic command, and even the most devoted servants fall gravely ill and feel sharp human grief.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century church father in Constantinople, pairs the two parallel phrases in 2:6–7 to refute those denying Christ's deity. If "form of a servant" means Jesus genuinely became human rather than merely wearing a mask, then "form of God" equally means He genuinely shares the divine essence. *(Homilies on Philippians, on chapter 2 verses 5–8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's parallel contrast of "form of God" and "form of a servant" against Arianism is famous in his Philippians homilies -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, uses Philippians 2:6–7 as the living template for Christian liberty and love. Just as Christ was Lord of all yet freely became servant of all, a believer is through faith a free lord subject to none, and through love a willing servant subject to all. *(The Freedom of a Christian, 1520)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther quotes Philippians 2:6–7 directly in this famous treatise to anchor his twofold thesis of Christian freedom -->

**Ralph P. Martin**, twentieth-century British New Testament scholar, wrote a classic monograph on 2:6–11 as an early Christian worship hymn quoted by Paul. He argued that 2:5 does not merely hold up Jesus as an external moral hero to copy, but summons believers to live out the mindset that belongs organically to life *in Christ*. *(Carmen Christi, 1967, on chapter 2 verses 5–11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this is the central thesis of Martin's landmark monograph Carmen Christi -->

**N. T. Wright**, British New Testament scholar, builds on linguistic studies of the rare Greek word in 2:6 to show that Christ refused to treat equality with God as an advantage to exploit for Himself. The hymn declares not that Christ became a servant *in spite of* being God, but *precisely because* He is God: self-giving love is the very heart of who God is. *(The Climax of the Covenant, 1991, chapter on Philippians chapter 2 verses 5–11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading 2:6 as "because He was in the form of God" and "not something to exploit" is Wright's renowned contribution in this volume -->

**The Aleinu prayer**, an ancient Jewish prayer rooted in early rabbinic liturgy and recited at the close of daily synagogue services, quotes Isaiah 45:23 directly to proclaim the coming day when every knee bows before the one God, King of the universe.
