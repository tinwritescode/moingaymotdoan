# Revelation 17 — Babylon the Great and the Scarlet Beast

## Story

One of the seven bowl angels approaches John and invites him to witness the verdict on "the great prostitute" seated upon many waters—the one with whom earth's kings committed immorality, intoxicating earth's inhabitants with the wine of her adulteries (17:1–2).

Carried in the Spirit into the wilderness, John sees an unsettling sight: a woman perched astride a scarlet beast covered in blasphemous names, sporting seven heads and ten horns. Dressed in regal purple and scarlet and dripping with gold, precious stones, and pearls, she clutches a golden cup brimming with abominations and filth. Across her forehead stretches an enigmatic title: "Mystery: Babylon the Great, the Mother of Prostitutes and of the Abominations of the Earth." More chilling still, John sees her reeling drunk on the blood of the saints and the blood of Jesus' faithful witnesses (17:3–6).

Noticing John's astonishment, the angel unpacks each symbol. The beast that once was, now is not, is poised to rise from the abyss and march straight to destruction; earth's dwellers whose names are absent from the book of life will marvel and follow it. Here a mind with wisdom is called for: the seven heads represent both seven hills where the woman sits and seven kings—five have fallen, one currently reigns, the other has not yet arrived and will stay only briefly. And the beast itself that was and is not acts as an eighth king—yet belongs to the seven and goes straight to destruction (17:7–11).

The angel continues: the ten horns represent ten kings who have not yet received a kingdom, yet will unite in handing their authority over to the beast to rule alongside it for one brief hour. Together they will wage war against the Lamb—and the Lamb will crush them decisively, because He is "Lord of lords and King of kings," accompanied by His called, chosen, and faithful followers. The waters where the woman sits represent peoples, crowds, nations, and languages (17:12–15).

Then comes a stunning twist: the ten horns and the beast suddenly turn in hatred against the great prostitute—stripping her naked, devouring her flesh, and burning her to ash. The angel reveals that God Himself planted this intent in their hearts so His word would be fulfilled. The closing verse leaves zero doubt about her identity: "the great city that rules over the kings of the earth" (17:16–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Beneath the dazzling polish of a seductive civilization may hide cruelty and innocent blood.** Cloaked in purple silk, sparkling with gold and pearls, holding a golden goblet—yet inside the cup lurks filth, and she staggers drunk on martyrs' blood (17:4–6). Revelation trains believers to look clean through the glittering veneer of power and wealth.

**Evil invariably ends up turning inward and devouring itself.** The beast and ten kings propped the woman up, yet in the end they rip her apart and torch her (17:16–17). God rules so supremely that even evil's internal treachery serves His righteous justice.

**Every hostile coalition proves brittle before the "Lord of lords and King of kings."** Even when ten kings pool their power with the beast, their dominance lasts barely "one hour" before shattering against the Lamb and His loyal people (17:12–14).

## Deeper Meaning

**Babylon enthroned on many waters with a golden cup directly echoes Jeremiah's oracle against ancient Babylon.** Jeremiah called old Babylon the city dwelling by many waters and a golden cup making the whole earth drunk (Jeremiah 51:7; 51:13). By New Testament times, "Babylon" served as a recognized code name for Rome, the new imperial power dominating God's people (1 Peter 5:13).

**The prostitute stripped and burned by her former lovers mirrors Ezekiel and Nahum.** Ezekiel pictured unfaithful cities stripped of finery and torched by the very foreign allies they had courted (Ezekiel 16:37–41; 23:25–29), while Nahum branded bloodthirsty Nineveh a seductive harlot ensnaring nations through sorcery (Nahum 3:4).

**Babylon the prostitute stands as the deliberate mirror opposite of the Bride—New Jerusalem—in chapter 21.** One of the very same bowl angels escorts John to view both women and both cities (17:1; 21:9): one sits in a wasteland draped in scarlet loot and drunk on martyrs' blood; the other descends from heaven atop a high mountain dressed in radiant fine linen—the righteous deeds of the saints (19:8; 21:10–11).

## Notes & Nuances

**Babylon's identity (17:5; 17:18) clearly shows how first-century history meets the major interpretive schools.** To first-century ears, two clues—seated on "seven hills" (17:9) and "the great city that rules over the kings of the earth" (17:18)—pointed unmistakably to Rome, famous across antiquity as the City of Seven Hills. Preterists equate Babylon with Rome (while one preterist branch links her to apostate pre-AD 70 Jerusalem). Reformation historicists identified Babylon with the corrupt medieval Roman papacy. Futurists often separate chapter 17's *religious* Babylon (a global apostate religion crushed by the beast) from chapter 18's *economic-political* Babylon, or anticipate a rebuilt literal Babylon in Iraq. Idealists see Rome as Babylon's first historical embodiment, while Babylon endures as a timeless emblem of every seductive imperial-consumer metropolis pulling humanity away from God.

**Counting the seven kings (17:10) ranks among Revelation's knottiest puzzles.** Attempts to tally specific Roman emperors—debating whether to begin with Julius Caesar or Augustus, and whether to count the three fleeting emperors of AD 69—yield rival candidates for the "sixth" reigning king (Nero, Vespasian, or Domitian). Many contemporary scholars (including Bauckham, Beale, and Koester) suggest avoiding rigid emperor arithmetic and instead reading seven symbolically for the complete chain of God-opposing empires across history (Egypt, Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome, and a final realm), with the eighth king embodying the climactic resurgence of beastly power right before its doom.

**The Lamb's victory in verse 17:14 does not hinge on military might from the saints.** Those sharing His triumph are described by three quiet marks: "called," "chosen," and "faithful"—people who overcome through steadfast loyalty even while Babylon drinks their blood.

**Richard Bauckham** stresses that verses 17:9 and 17:18 left zero ambiguity for first-century Asian readers: Babylon was Rome perched on its seven hills. He highlights a biting piece of historical irony: Roman coins minted under Vespasian depicted the goddess Roma seated majestically atop seven hills holding a sword; John flips imperial propaganda inside out into a blood-drunk prostitute astride a monster. *(The Climax of Prophecy, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: citing Vespasian's Dea Roma coin seated on seven hills alongside Revelation 17:9 is a famous historical parallel highlighted by Bauckham -->
