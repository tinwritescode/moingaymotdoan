# Revelation 11 — The Two Witnesses and the Seventh Trumpet

## Story

John is handed a measuring rod like a staff and told to rise and measure God's temple, the altar, and those worshiping inside it. Yet he is instructed to leave out the outer court and not measure it, because it has been given over to the nations, who will trample the holy city for forty-two months (11:1–2).

During that exact same span—1,260 days—God commissions His two witnesses dressed in sackcloth to prophesy. They are called "the two olive trees and the two lampstands standing before the Lord of the earth." Anyone attempting to harm them is consumed by fire pouring from their mouths. They hold authority to shut up the sky so no rain falls during their days of prophecy, to turn water into blood, and to strike the earth with every kind of plague as often as they wish (11:3–6).

Once their testimony is complete, however, the beast rising from the abyss wages war against them, conquers them, and kills them. Their bodies lie unburied in the main street of the great city—figuratively called Sodom and Egypt, where also their Lord was crucified. For three and a half days people from every tribe, language, people, and nation stare at their corpses and refuse to allow them burial. Earth's inhabitants gloat, celebrate, and exchange gifts because these two prophets had tormented their consciences (11:7–10).

Yet after precisely three and a half days, the breath of life from God enters them; they rise to their feet, and sheer terror seizes everyone watching. A booming voice from heaven calls out, "Come up here!" They ascend to heaven in a cloud right before their enemies' eyes. At that very hour a massive earthquake strikes, collapsing a tenth of the city and killing seven thousand people; the terrified survivors give glory to the God of heaven. The second woe has passed, and the third woe comes swiftly (11:11–14).

Finally, the seventh angel blows his trumpet. Loud voices erupt across heaven: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of His Christ, and He will reign forever and ever!" The twenty-four elders fall face down in worship, thanking the Lord God Almighty for taking up His great power and beginning His reign: the nations raged, yet now His wrath has arrived—the appointed hour for judging the dead, rewarding His servants the prophets and saints and all who fear His name, small and great, and destroying those who destroy the earth. Then God's temple in heaven swings open, revealing the Ark of His Covenant inside, accompanied by lightning, rumblings, thunder, an earthquake, and heavy hail (11:15–19).

## Key Takeaways

**God's people may suffer outward trampling while remaining measured and protected within.** Measuring the inner sanctuary and its worshipers while leaving the outer courtyard to be trampled (11:1–2) portrays the dual reality of Christian life: bodies may face persecution or even martyrdom, yet believers' spiritual life stands guarded by God where no foe can reach.

**The church's faithful witness walks in the exact footprints of Jesus' cross and resurrection.** Invincible until their assignment finishes, the two witnesses are slain by the beast and mocked by the world for three and a half days—only to be raised alive and lifted into heaven by God (11:7–12). And marvelously, unlike chapter 9 where sheer terror produced zero repentance, witnessing the martyrs' resurrection moves survivors to tremble and "give glory to the God of heaven" (11:13).

**The seventh trumpet announces history's certain destination: the world's kingdom belongs to God and His Christ.** Every ruthless regime will fall, and God will "destroy those who destroy the earth" (11:18), keeping His covenant in full.

## Deeper Meaning

**The two witnesses unite Israel's two towering Old Testament prophets—Moses and Elijah—with Zechariah's vision.** Shutting the skies against rain and calling fire from heaven vividly recall Elijah (1 Kings 17:1; 2 Kings 1:10); turning water to blood and summoning plagues vividly recall Moses (Exodus 7:17–20). Meanwhile, "the two olive trees and the two lampstands" come directly from Zechariah 4, where two olive trees feed continuous oil into a golden lampstand sustained not by human might, but by God's Spirit (Zechariah 4:2–6; 4:11–14).

**Measuring the temple and breath restoring life echo Ezekiel.** Ezekiel watched a figure with a measuring reed survey God's sanctuary to guarantee His holy presence (Ezekiel 40:3–5), and saw God's breath enter a valley of dry bones so they stood up on their feet (Ezekiel 37:5–10). John draws on that exact imagery to portray the two witnesses rising alive (11:11).

**The Ark of the Covenant appearing inside heaven's temple (11:19) guarantees God's unfailing faithfulness.** The earthly Ark vanished when Babylon sacked Jerusalem in 586 BC. John reveals the true heavenly Ark standing untouched before God—proving that not a single covenant promise has been lost or revoked.

## Notes & Nuances

**Interpretive schools differ dramatically over the temple (11:1–2) and two witnesses (11:3–12).** Futurists expect a rebuilt physical temple in Jerusalem during the tribulation and two literal individual prophets (often suggested as Moses and Elijah, or Enoch and Elijah) ministering in Jerusalem for 1,260 days. Preterists connect the temple to Herod's temple prior to AD 70 and see the two witnesses as early apostolic and prophetic testimony silenced before Jerusalem fell. Symbolic readers point out that Revelation itself explicitly defines lampstands as churches (1:20), making "two lampstands" a portrait of the entire witnessing church—with two being the minimum legal number required to validate testimony (Deuteronomy 19:15).

**Forty-two months (11:2), 1,260 days (11:3), and three and a half years (12:14) all represent the same span derived from Daniel.** In Daniel 7:25 and 12:7, three and a half years designates the bounded season when a hostile power oppresses the saints before being overthrown. Futurists take it literally as the final three and a half years of the tribulation; idealists treat it as a symbol for the entire age of the church's witnessing amid trial until Christ returns; classical historicists applied a day-for-a-year formula to calculate 1,260 years of church history.

**The "great city" (11:8) wears three overlapping identities: Sodom, Egypt, and the place where the Lord was crucified.** Mentioning Jesus' crucifixion leads preterists and futurists to identify literal Jerusalem. Yet layering on "Sodom" (moral corruption) and "Egypt" (imperial oppression) leads symbolic interpreters to see a wider emblem: any religious or political center that rejects Jesus and murders His witnesses takes on that city's character.

## What Commentators Say

**Richard Bauckham** highlights verse 11:13: only a tenth of the city collapses and seven thousand perish, while the remaining nine-tenths tremble and "give glory to the God of heaven." Throughout Revelation, giving glory to God consistently signals genuine repentance and worship (14:7; 16:9). Thus what terrifying plagues failed to achieve in chapter 9, the church's faithful, resurrected witness accomplishes in chapter 11—turning the nations back toward God. *(The Climax of Prophecy, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading the survivors giving glory to God in 11:13 as genuine conversion won through faithful witness is Bauckham's most celebrated thesis on chapter 11 -->

**John Walvoord**, from a futurist vantage point, defends a literal understanding of two historical prophets serving 1,260 days in Jerusalem during the second half of the tribulation, noting that worldwide onlookers viewing their corpses for three and a half days (11:9) makes complete sense in an era of instant global broadcasting. *(The Revelation of Jesus Christ, 1966)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading the two witnesses literally and citing global media for 11:9 is characteristic of Walvoord's futurist commentary -->

**G. K. Beale** builds a strong case for a symbolic reading: verse 11:4 explicitly identifies the two witnesses as "two lampstands," and Jesus already defined lampstands as churches in 1:20. Showing two rather than seven does not erase five churches, but mirrors the exact two blameless churches from chapters 2–3 (Smyrna and Philadelphia)—representing the faithful, uncompromised witnessing church across the world. *(The Book of Revelation, NIGTC, 1999)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking the two lampstands in 11:4 with the two unrebuked churches of chapters 2–3 is a distinctive and well-known insight in Beale's NIGTC volume -->
