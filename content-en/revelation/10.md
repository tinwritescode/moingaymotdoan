# Revelation 10 — The Mighty Angel and the Little Scroll

## Story

Just as chapter 7 paused the action between the sixth and seventh seals, chapter 10 and the first half of chapter 11 slow the tempo before the seventh trumpet blows.

John watches a colossal, majestic angel descend from heaven, wrapped in a cloud with a rainbow arching above his head, his face blazing like the sun and his legs glowing like pillars of fire. Held open in his hand is a little scroll. Setting his right foot upon the sea and his left foot upon the land—straddling both realms—he lets out a tremendous shout like a lion's roar. The instant he roars, seven thunders boom back with distinct voices. John reaches for his pen to write down what the seven thunders declared, yet a voice from heaven stops him immediately: "Seal up what the seven thunders have spoken, and do not write it down" (10:1–4).

Standing astride sea and land, the angel lifts his right hand toward heaven and swears by the One who lives forever and ever—Creator of heaven, earth, sea, and everything within them—that there will be no more delay. In the days when the seventh angel is about to sound his trumpet, "the mystery of God will be fulfilled," exactly as He announced to His servants the prophets (10:5–7).

The heavenly voice directs John once more: go and take the open little scroll from the hand of the angel standing on sea and land. John walks over and asks for it. The angel hands it to him with a warning: "Take it and eat it. It will turn your stomach bitter, though in your mouth it will taste as sweet as honey." John obeys: as he chews, it tastes sweet as honey in his mouth, yet the moment he swallows, his stomach turns sour and bitter. Then comes a renewed commission: "You must prophesy again about many peoples, nations, languages, and kings" (10:8–11).

## Key Takeaways

**God has not chosen to reveal every secret to human curiosity.** The command sealing up the seven thunders (10:4) injects a humbling pause into a book bursting with visions. Some mysteries belong solely to the LORD (Deuteronomy 29:29); faithful readers focus not on cracking every undisclosed riddle, but on obeying what God has clearly opened.

**God's word tastes both sweet and bitter inside the life of anyone who carries it.** The little scroll tastes sweet as honey because it announces God's sure victory and nearness, yet turns bitter in the gut because it involves sobering judgment and the cost of suffering borne by God's people (10:9–10). Every faithful messenger tastes both flavors.

**John's calling—and the church's—is never to sit back as passive spectators, but to keep bearing witness before the nations.** Wedged between the dark plagues of chapter 9 and the fierce conflict erupting in chapters 12–13, John receives fresh orders to prophesy concerning peoples, nations, tongues, and kings (10:11). History continues because God's witness must still ring out across the earth.

## Deeper Meaning

**Swallowing the little scroll replays Ezekiel's prophetic commissioning word for word.** God once handed Ezekiel a scroll covered in lamentation and told him to eat it; inside Ezekiel's mouth it tasted sweet as honey before he went out to confront a stubborn house (Ezekiel 2:8–10; 3:1–4). John stands squarely in that prophetic lineage: God's word is not merely glanced at on a page, it must be digested into the very bones of the messenger.

**The mighty angel's oath directly completes the closing chapter of Daniel.** In Daniel 12, an angel standing above the river raised his hands to heaven, swore by the Eternal One concerning the final appointed span, and instructed Daniel to seal his scroll until the end (Daniel 12:7–9). In Revelation 10, the angel plants his feet over both ocean and earth, swears that all delay has ended (10:5–6), and holds a scroll that now lies *open*—signaling that what Daniel sealed has reached its hour of fulfillment.

**Seven thunders echo Psalm 29, where the LORD's voice thunders seven times over mighty waters.** The rainbow crown and fiery pillars for legs (10:1) recall both the glory surrounding heaven's throne in chapter 4 and the pillar of fire guiding Israel through the wilderness (Exodus 13:21). Representing the Creator in full majesty, the angel demonstrates that ocean and land alike lie beneath God's feet.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is the mighty angel of verse 10:1? Two leading views emerge.** Classic interpreters (including Matthew Henry and several Reformation-era commentators) identify him as Christ Himself, citing features shared with chapters 1 and 4: sun-bright face, fiery pillars, rainbow halo, and robe of cloud. Most modern scholars note that verse 10:1 calls him "another mighty angel," matching an earlier angel (5:2), and in verse 10:6 he swears by the Creator—something Christ Himself would never need to do—pointing instead to an exalted angel clothed in reflected divine glory as God's envoy.

**Is the little scroll of verse 10:2 identical to the seven-sealed scroll of chapter 5?** Two perspectives prevail. Some believe it is the very same scroll from chapter 5 now completely unsealed and lying open, or a focused excerpt zooming in on the church's mission across chapters 11–14. Others point out that John shifts to a slightly different Greek diminutive noun for a smaller scroll, distinguishing the sweeping cosmic decree of chapter 5 from the specific prophetic charge handed to John in chapter 10.

**Older translations reading "there should be time no longer" (10:6) are often misread as saying time itself vanishes.** The Greek idiom accurately means "there will be no more delay" (as rendered in the BSB), directly answering the martyrs' plea "How long?" in verse 6:10. Waiting is over; when the seventh trumpet sounds, God's purpose reaches its climax.

**John Calvin** and sixteenth-century Reformers—though Calvin never wrote a commentary on Revelation—championed the exact theological discipline illustrated by verse 10:4: wherever God sets a boundary on revelation (as in sealing the seven thunders), human speculation must stop. Later commentators such as **Matthew Henry** applied this lesson directly to verse 10:4, urging readers of Revelation to stay humble and never pry beyond what Scripture reveals. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Revelation chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: drawing a lesson in humble restraint from the sealed seven thunders in 10:4 is a well-known emphasis in Matthew Henry's commentary -->

**Grant Osborne** highlights that announcing the completion of "the mystery of God" in verse 10:7 ties every Old Testament prophetic thread into New Testament redemption.
