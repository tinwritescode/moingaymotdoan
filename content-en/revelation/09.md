# Revelation 9 — The Fifth and Sixth Trumpets

## Story

The next two trumpets unleash the first two of the three terrifying woes announced by the soaring eagle at the end of chapter 8.

When the fifth angel blows his trumpet, John sees a star fallen from heaven to earth, handed the key to the shaft of the bottomless abyss. Opening the abyss unleashes thick smoke billowing like a gigantic furnace, blacking out sun and sky. Out of the smoke swarm locusts across the earth, endowed with power like scorpions. Forbidden to touch green grass or trees, they may harm only people who lack God's seal on their foreheads. Nor are they allowed to kill—only to torment victims for five months with pain like a scorpion's sting, until people desperately crave death while death keeps fleeing from them (9:1–6).

The locusts look bizarre and nightmarish: shaped like warhorses armored for combat, wearing what look like golden crowns, with human faces, long flowing hair like women's hair, teeth like lions, iron breastplates, wings roaring like charging chariots, and tails armed with venomous scorpion stingers. Ruling over them is the angel of the abyss—named Abaddon in Hebrew and Apollyon in Greek, both meaning "Destroyer." Thus the first woe passes, with two woes still ahead (9:7–12).

The sixth angel blows his trumpet. A voice resonates from the four horns of the golden altar before God, ordering the release of four angels bound beside the great Euphrates River. Those four angels have been held ready for that precise hour, day, month, and year to wipe out a third of humanity. Behind them rides a staggering cavalry numbering two hundred million. John describes riders clad in fiery red, deep blue, and sulfur-yellow breastplates; horses with lion heads breathing fire, smoke, and sulfur from their mouths, and serpent-like tails with heads inflicting harm. A full third of humanity perishes from that fire, smoke, and sulfur (9:13–19).

Yet the bleakest tragedy waits in the closing two verses: the rest of humanity who survive these devastating plagues still refuse to repent. They cling to worshiping demons and idols of gold, silver, bronze, stone, and wood—lifeless statues that cannot see, hear, or walk—refusing to abandon their murders, sorceries, sexual immorality, or thefts (9:20–21).

## Key Takeaways

**Evil carries inner torment and ruin within itself, yet God still holds the leash limiting its reach.** Twice the text uses passive verbs of divine permission—"was given" and "they were allowed" (9:1; 9:5): even the abyss opens only when granted a key, and the four Euphrates angels remain bound until the exact appointed hour (9:14–15). Darkness never stands on equal footing with God.

**The keenest agony in the fifth trumpet springs not from nature, but from dark spiritual powers.** Those who reject God's protective seal expose themselves to torment from the very evil forces they serve—an anguish of soul so deep that sufferers long for death yet cannot find it (9:4–6).

**Terror and catastrophe alone can never transform human hearts.** The final two verses of chapter 9 rank among Scripture's saddest diagnoses: even after watching a third of humanity fall, survivors harden their hearts around dead idols and entrenched sin (9:20–21). Only grace and inner renewal can turn people home.

## Deeper Meaning

**Locusts from the abyss fuse Egypt's eighth plague with the invading locust army of Joel.** God once sent locusts blanketing Egypt (Exodus 10:12–15), and the prophet Joel pictured a locust horde rushing like warhorses, with lion's teeth and chariot thunder darkening the sun (Joel 1:6; 2:2–10). John elevates the image even higher: these locusts ignore vegetation and afflict only unsealed people (9:4), turning a natural plague into a haunting portrait of demonic assault.

**The Euphrates River stood as a classic frontier of dreaded invasion in both Old Testament and Roman times.** For ancient Israel, Assyrian and Babylonian armies marched in from beyond the Euphrates (Isaiah 7:20; Jeremiah 46:10). For first-century Asian readers, the Euphrates formed Rome's eastern frontier where feared Parthian horsemen loomed. John taps that vivid border dread to depict an unstoppable tidal wave of judgment.

**The indictment of idols in verse 9:20 echoes Psalm 115 and Daniel almost line for line.** Old Testament writers mocked images of gold, silver, bronze, stone, and wood that have eyes without seeing, ears without hearing, and feet without walking (Psalms 115:4–7; Daniel 5:23). Even after terrible blows, rebellious humanity hugs lifeless idols—just as Pharaoh kept hardening his heart plague after plague (Exodus 8:15; 9:34).

## Notes & Nuances

**Avoid forcing the locusts (9:7–10) or the two-hundred-million cavalry (9:16) to match modern weapons or nations.** During the twentieth century, popular futurist books frequently turned armored, roaring locusts into attack helicopters, or assigned the two-hundred-million army to a specific modern Asian power. Careful scholars—including respected futurists—reject newspaper-style prophecy guessing: John uses supernatural prophetic imagery rooted in Joel and the Old Testament to describe demonic forces emerging from the abyss (9:1–3; 9:11), not twentieth-century military hardware.

**Interpretive traditions frame chapter 9 across distinct horizons.** Futurists anticipate literal demonic plagues and global conflict in the closing years before Christ returns. Preterists connect the visions with chaos and Rome's siege of Jerusalem in the first century. Older historicists linked the two trumpets with the rise of medieval Islamic and Ottoman empires pressing against Europe. Idealists view them as vivid pictures of self-inflicted spiritual torment and ruin suffered whenever humanity rejects God across any age.

**The name "Apollyon" (9:11) may carry sharp irony aimed at Rome's emperor.** Apollo was a favored patron deity of Emperor Domitian, and locusts were even linked with Apollo's cult. Playing on the sound from *Apollo* to *Apollyon* ("Destroyer"), John quietly unmasks the empire's prized deity as an envoy of the abyss.
