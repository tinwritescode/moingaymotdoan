# Ecclesiastes 5 — Keeping Your Word Before God and the Hunger for Money

## Story

Chapter 5 opens with sober counsel for anyone going to worship. Guard your steps when you enter the house of God; draw near to listen rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools, who go through religious motions without realizing they are doing wrong (5:1). Do not rush to open your mouth or let your heart make hasty promises before God, "for God is in heaven and you are on earth. So let your words be few" (5:2). Too many cares breed endless dreams, and too many words expose folly (5:3).

He presses on to vows. Whenever you make a vow to God, do not delay in fulfilling it, because God takes no pleasure in fools: "It is better not to vow than to make a vow and not fulfill it" (5:4–5). Do not let your mouth drag you into sin, only to excuse yourself later before God's messenger by claiming it was a slip of the tongue—why provoke God to anger and watch Him undo the work of your hands? Amid swirling dreams and empty talk, the core duty remains clear: "Therefore fear God" (5:6–7).

Stepping out from the temple, *the Teacher* observes the machinery of government across the province. If you see the poor oppressed and justice and righteousness trampled, do not be shocked, because inside any hierarchy one high official watches over another, and above them all stand still higher powers (5:8). Even so, a ruler who attends to the fields brings advantage to the land (5:9).

The second half tackles money head-on. Whoever loves silver never has enough silver, and whoever loves wealth is never satisfied with his income; this too is futility (5:10). As goods multiply, so do the mouths that consume them—what extra benefit does the owner get except watching them with his eyes? (5:11). Sweet is the sleep of the laborer, whether he eats little or much; by contrast, the abundance of the rich keeps him tossing awake through the night (5:12).

He points out another grievous evil: riches hoarded by an owner to his own hurt, or wiped out overnight in a bad venture; when he fathers a son, nothing remains in his hand to pass along (5:13–14). Naked a person came from his mother's womb, and naked he will depart, carrying away nothing from his lifelong toil. Yet countless people spend all their days eating in darkness, weighed down with grief, sickness, and resentment simply chasing the wind (5:15–17).

At the chapter's close, the sky clears again with the book's recurring refrain. He sees what is truly good and fitting for life: to eat, drink, and find satisfaction in all one's toil during the days God grants, for that is our portion (5:18). Whenever God grants someone wealth and possessions *and* empowers him to enjoy them and accept his lot in his labor, that itself is "the gift of God." Such a person seldom broods heavily over the brevity of his life, because God keeps his heart filled with gladness (5:19–20).

## Key Takeaways

**Approaching God calls for listening and reverence more than flashy talk.** The vast distance between the One in heaven and mortals on earth calls for humility (5:2). True worship is never measured by long-winded prayers or piles of offerings, but by a listening heart that honors what it promises God (5:1; 5:4–5).

**Money carries a built-in trap: the more you hold, the emptier you feel.** The appetite for wealth never turns off when the account fills up (5:10). It brings along sleepless anxiety over guarding it, crowds of hangers-on, and the dread of losing everything (5:11–14). A simple worker sleeping soundly often owns more real peace than a mogul with overflowing vaults (5:12).

**Nobody takes a fortune past the door of death.** Arriving naked and leaving empty-handed (5:15) resets every hoarding ambition to its proper scale. Remembering we are temporary stewards loosens money's grip on our hearts.

**Owning wealth is one thing; enjoying it is a separate gift.** Some wealthy people spend their entire lives dining in darkness and irritation (5:17). Only when God grants a grateful heart to savor our portion does daily labor turn into genuine joy (5:18–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus echoes the exact spirit of restraint and integrity found in 5:1–7.** Teaching on prayer, He warns against babbling on with empty repetition as though sheer word count wins a hearing (Matthew 6:7). Teaching on oaths, He calls disciples to simple, truthful speech—let your "Yes" be "Yes" and your "No" be "No"—instead of tossing reckless vows before God (Matthew 5:33–37).

**Paul nearly quotes 5:10–15 in substance when warning against the love of money.** We brought nothing into the world and can take nothing out; with food and clothing we should be content, because the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil that pierces people with many griefs (1 Timothy 6:6–10). And in Luke 12:15, Jesus says outright that a person's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 5:8 admits two readings of the "higher ones" watching above.** The first reads it as dry satire on layers of bureaucracy: lower officials fleece the poor, higher officials watch to take their cut from the lower ones, leaving the weak with nowhere to appeal. The second sees in the highest authority a reminder of God Himself, standing over every earthly power and seeing every abuse. Both layers fit *the Teacher*'s blend of sharp realism and reverence for God.

**Verse 5:9 is notoriously compressed in Hebrew, yielding distinct translations.** Some traditional renderings read it positively: a king committed to cultivating the land brings real benefit to the country. Other modern translations—including the Berean Standard Bible—read it as continuing the satire of verse 8: everyone takes a share of the land's produce, and even the king lives off the fields.
