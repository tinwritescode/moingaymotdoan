# Ecclesiastes 6 — Having Everything Yet Enjoying Nothing

## Story

Chapter 6 picks up straight from the end of chapter 5, flipping the coin to its dark side. *The Teacher* describes another heavy tragedy under the sun: God gives a man everything—wealth, possessions, and honor, so that he lacks nothing his heart could wish for—yet God does not grant him the power to enjoy any of it; instead, a stranger steps in to enjoy it. He calls this "futility and a grievous affliction" (6:1–2).

He pushes the example to an extreme to make his point unforgettable. Suppose a man fathers a hundred children and lives many long years—two of the highest blessings imaginable in the ancient world—yet his soul never tastes satisfaction from life's good things, and in the end he does not even receive a proper burial. In that case, *the Teacher* says bluntly: "a stillborn child is better off than he" (6:3). The stillborn arrives in vapor, slips away into darkness, its name covered in shadow, never having seen the sun or known anything of the world; yet it finds more rest than that long-lived man (6:4–5). Even if someone lived two thousand years without ever tasting good, do not all people go to the exact same place in the end? (6:6).

From that stark case, he distills a crisp observation on human nature: all a person's toil is for his mouth, yet his appetite is never filled (6:7). What real edge, then, does the wise person hold over the fool? What extra gain does the poor person have for knowing how to conduct himself skillfully before others? (6:8). Better is what the eyes can see right here than the endless wandering of craving—yet even that remains fleeting, like chasing the wind (6:9).

The final three verses close the first half of the book by naming humanity's limits. Whatever exists has already been named long ago; what a human being is—and how fragile we are—is already known, so no one can argue in court against the One stronger than himself (6:10). Multiplying words only multiplies emptiness; what profit does all that debating bring? (6:11). During the few vapor-like days of life that pass like a shadow, who truly knows what is good for a person? And who can tell anyone what will take place under the sun after they are gone? (6:12).

## Key Takeaways

**Owning plenty is not the same as living joyfully.** The scenario in 6:1–2 happens all around us: people possess assets, status, longevity, and large families, yet anxiety, emptiness, or illness leaves them unable to savor what sits right in front of them. A long, hollow existence holds no edge over a brief one (6:3–6).

**The human heart's hunger runs deeper than material things can satisfy.** Verse 6:7 hits a universal nerve: people work to feed the mouth, yet behind the mouth lies a soul that bread and wealth can never fill. Contentment with what rests before our eyes outweighs chasing boundless desires (6:9).

**Humans are in no position to sue or cross-examine their Creator.** Saying that all things have already been named and that we cannot contend with One stronger than ourselves (6:10) returns us to our rightful place as creatures. Letting go of our demand to control the future opens the door to humility.

## Deeper Meaning

**The unquenchable appetite of 6:7 prepares the way for Jesus' invitation.** All our labor for daily sustenance still leaves the soul hungry. In John's Gospel, Jesus tells the Samaritan woman that everyone who drinks from the well will thirst again, whereas whoever drinks the water He gives will never thirst (John 4:13–14). He likewise declares Himself the Bread of Life: whoever comes to Him will never hunger, and whoever believes in Him will never thirst (John 6:35).

**The reminder of human limits before God echoes in Romans and James.** Just as *the Teacher* states that no mortal can dispute with the One mightier than himself (6:10), Paul later asks, "Who are you, O man, to talk back to God?" (Romans 9:20). And the image of life passing like a shadow in 6:12 matches James's reminder that our lives are a mist appearing for a little while and vanishing, so every plan for tomorrow belongs under the Lord's will (James 4:14–15).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verses 6:1–2 form the exact mirror opposite of 5:19.** Just sentences earlier in 5:19, *the Teacher* said that receiving wealth *together with* the capacity to enjoy it is God's gift. Here in 6:2, strip away only that second piece—the capacity to enjoy—and every ounce of wealth and prestige turns tragic. Placing both portraits side by side proves that true joy is always God's gift, never an automatic side effect of possessions.

**Verses 6:10–12 act as the hinge between the two halves of Ecclesiastes.** After six chapters testing every path to meaning under the sun, *the Teacher* poses two summary questions in 6:12: *who knows what is good for a person in this life?* and *who knows what will happen after them?* The second half of the book (chapters 7–12) systematically wrestles with those very two questions.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, remarked on 6:2 that possessing riches without a heart to enjoy them is like holding the keys to a treasure vault without permission to unlock it—serving merely as a caretaker guarding wealth for someone else to spend.
