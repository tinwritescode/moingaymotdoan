# Jeremiah 42 — Ten Days of Silence and an Empty Oath

## Story

Having abandoned Mizpah after Gedaliah's massacre and halted at Geruth Chimham near Bethlehem with their bags already packed for the journey south (41:17–18), the Judean military leaders—headed by Johanan son of Kareah and Jezaniah son of Hoshaiah—along with all the people from the least to the greatest approached the prophet Jeremiah. They spoke with utmost humility, begging him to receive their plea and pray to "the LORD your God" on behalf of this surviving remnant. They reminded him that out of many, "only a few of us are left, as you can see with your own eyes," and asked that God show them "the way we should walk and the thing we should do" (42:1–3).

Jeremiah agreed at once. "I have heard you," he replied. He promised to pray to "the LORD your God" just as they had asked, and whatever answer the LORD gave, he would report completely, holding nothing back (42:4).

Hearing this, the commanders and people swore a solemnly chilling oath. They called on the LORD himself to be "a true and faithful witness" against them if they failed to carry out everything he commanded through Jeremiah. They declared with absolute certainty: "Whether it is pleasant or unpleasant, we will obey the voice of the LORD our God," convinced that obeying him would bring them blessing (42:5–6).

Yet the answer did not come right away. Only "after ten days" did the word of the LORD come to Jeremiah. Immediately he summoned Johanan, all the commanders, and all the people from the least to the greatest to proclaim the message from the LORD, the God of Israel (42:7–9).

The first half of the message was a warm, grace-filled promise. The LORD declared that if they remained in the land of Judah, he would "build you up and not tear you down; I will plant you and not uproot you," because he had relented concerning the disaster he had brought upon them. Again and again he urged them not to fear the king of Babylon, for he himself was with them "to save you and deliver you from his hand." He would show them compassion, moving even the king of Babylon to treat them with mercy and let them settle back safely on their own land (42:10–12).

The second half, however, delivered a bone-chilling warning. If they stubbornly refused to stay, disobeyed the voice of God, and insisted on going down to Egypt—imagining that there they would "not see war or hear the sound of the trumpet or hunger for bread"—the exact opposite would overtake them. The very sword they dreaded and the famine they feared would catch up with them right in Egypt. Everyone determined to go and reside in Egypt would die there "by sword, famine, and plague," with no survivor escaping the disaster. The LORD's anger that had poured out on Jerusalem would pour out on them in a foreign land; they would become an object of cursing, horror, reproach, and scorn, and they would never see their homeland again (42:13–18).

Finally, Jeremiah looked straight into the faces of the crowd standing before him—no doubt seeing displeasure and resistance already written across their expressions—and spoke bluntly without mincing words. He repeated God's decisive command, "Do not go to Egypt," declaring that he had testified plainly to them that very day. Then he exposed the hypocrisy in their hearts: "you have deceived yourselves at the cost of your lives." They had sent him to inquire of God and sworn to follow every word he spoke, yet the moment Jeremiah finished delivering it, they refused to obey a single instruction. Therefore, they could be certain they would perish by sword, famine, and plague in the very place they longed to go and settle (42:19–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Asking for God's guidance when your mind is already made up is pure self-deception.** Before ever approaching Jeremiah, Johanan and the crowd had already moved camp down to Bethlehem, poised on the road to Egypt (41:17). They were not genuinely seeking God's path; they wanted to borrow the prophet's prestige and God's name to stamp approval on their own plan. The moment God's word cut across their intention, their oath—"whether pleasant or unpleasant" (42:6)—vanished like mist. Jeremiah named it plainly as deceiving themselves at the cost of their own lives (42:20): people imagine they can maneuver around God, when in truth they are walking themselves into ruin.

**Ten days of waiting test both the inquirer and the messenger.** God made Jeremiah and the entire crowd wait ten full days before speaking (42:7). Jeremiah did not invent a quick, soothing answer, nor did he substitute his own political judgment for God's voice. For the panicked people, ten days of silence should have been a precious pause to let their terror subside and their hearts be searched; sadly, the longer they waited, the harder their self-will set.

**True safety lies not in where we run, but in where God promises to stay with us.** To human eyes at that moment, staying in Judah looked like handing their necks to Babylon's fury, while Egypt looked like a quiet shelter free from war trumpets and hunger (42:14). Yet God unveiled a sobering paradox: fleeing the place God assigns drives us straight into the very catastrophe we fear most (42:16). Conversely, remaining in what seems the most exposed spot—held by the promise "I am with you" (42:11)—is the only secure refuge.

**God's grace stands ready to rebuild even in the wake of his fiercest judgment.** Jerusalem had barely stopped smoldering and the temple lay in ashes, yet God opened a brand-new door for this tiny remnant. He declared that his hand of discipline had stayed, and he was ready to build, plant, and even turn the heart of a ruthless Babylonian king toward mercy (42:10–12). The tragedy of chapter 42 is not that God ran out of compassion for his people, but that his people refused to believe in it.

## Deeper Meaning

**Uprooting and tearing down reversed into building and planting.** On the very first day God called Jeremiah to be a prophet, he entrusted him with a six-verb commission: "to uproot and tear down, to destroy and overthrow, to build and to plant" (1:10). For decades, most of Jeremiah's message consisted of uprooting and tearing down, culminating in Jerusalem's collapse. In chapter 42, God returns to the constructive half of that original call—"I will build you up and not tear you down; I will plant you and not uproot you" (42:10)—echoing the promise he also gave the exiles in Babylon (24:6). Judgment is never God's final word; wherever people turn back in obedience, the very hand that tore down is eager to rebuild (18:7–10).

**Walking backward out of the Exodus.** For Israel, Egypt was never merely a wealthy neighbor to the south. It was the house of bondage from which God had redeemed their ancestors by his mighty arm to make them his own people. Accordingly, the law of Moses strictly forbade leading the people back to Egypt (Deuteronomy 17:16), even portraying a return to Egypt as the ultimate climax of the covenant curses (Deuteronomy 28:68). The remnant's longing for bread and eagerness to head for Egypt (42:14) repeats word for word the grumbling of the wilderness generation—those who pined for the meat pots and endless bread of Egypt and plotted to choose a leader to take them back (Exodus 16:3; Numbers 14:2–4). Voluntarily trading the promised land for Egypt meant tearing up their own story of redemption.

**The deceitful shadow of Egypt across the prophets.** A full century before Jeremiah, Isaiah had issued the same warning to Judah when terror of Assyria drove them to seek Pharaoh's protection: they "set out to go down to Egypt without consulting Me, to seek shelter under Pharaoh's protection and take refuge in Egypt's shadow" (Isaiah 30:1–2). Isaiah foretold that Pharaoh's protection would bring them nothing but shame and disgrace (Isaiah 30:3). A hundred years later, the northern empire had changed from Assyria to Babylon, yet the temptation to lean on human power instead of trusting the LORD replayed unchanged.

**A God glad to relent from disaster.** Verse 10 states that the LORD has relented of the disaster he brought upon them (42:10). This deeply tender language connects with great moments of mercy throughout the Old Testament—as when Moses interceded on Mount Sinai and the LORD relented from the calamity he had threatened (Exodus 32:14), or when Nineveh repented and God relented from destroying the city (Jonah 3:10). It reveals the heart of a God who takes zero pleasure in punishing sin, and longs for discipline to end so he can bless once more.

**Hearing without doing—an age-old self-deception.** Jeremiah's contemporary in Babylon, the prophet Ezekiel, encountered the exact same irony: the people crowded in to sit before him, listening intently to God's word and speaking words of affection, while their hearts refused to practice what they heard (Ezekiel 33:31–32). Centuries later in the New Testament, James distilled the whole lesson of Jeremiah 42 into one razor-sharp sentence: "Be doers of the word, and not hearers only. Otherwise, you are deceiving yourselves" (James 1:22).

## Notes & Nuances

**Does God "relenting" or "repenting" in verse 10 mean he admitted a mistake?** Not at all. Elsewhere Scripture explicitly declares that God is not a human being that he should change his mind or repent of wrongdoing (1 Samuel 15:29)—he never errs, never compromises his holiness, and never breaks a promise. The Hebrew verb *nacham* used in verse 10—rendered "relent" in modern versions like the BSB and "repent" in older English Bibles—carries the sense of deep grief, compassion, and willingness to stay the hand of judgment. Like a father who aches when he must discipline his child and longs for the punishment to end so he can embrace the child again, God uses human language to reveal profound mercy: Judah's punishment is enough, and now he desires to rebuild rather than go on tearing down (cf. 18:7–10).

**Why did God keep Jeremiah waiting for ten days?** The text never explains the ten-day silence (42:7), yet that brief detail speaks volumes. First, it proves Jeremiah was a true prophet, not a fortune-teller able to conjure oracles on demand; he had to wait until the LORD actually spoke. Second, those ten days show he did not fire off a hasty opinion in the heat of crisis. Tragically, instead of letting those ten days calm their panic and deepen their trust in God, the Judean leaders probably spent the delay talking among themselves and hardening their resolve to leave for Egypt.

**Does "not one of them will survive or escape" (42:17) contradict chapter 44?** Turning to chapter 44, we hear God say that "only a small number who escape the sword will return from the land of Egypt to the land of Judah" (44:28; cf. 44:14). This is no contradiction, but familiar prophetic hyperbole describing near-total annihilation: the refugee community fleeing to Egypt would be shattered as a viable group with no future there, even if a handful of fugitives survived and straggled back as living proof that the prophet's word had come true.

**A subtle shift in pronouns across verses 2–6.** Reading slowly reveals a striking psychological touch. When they first came asking for help, the leaders twice spoke of "the LORD your God" (42:2–3), as though God were Jeremiah's private concern rather than theirs. Jeremiah gently corrected them in his reply, promising to pray to "the LORD your God"—meaning their own God (42:4)—reminding them that they still belonged to him. Only when swearing their oath did they finally say "the LORD our God" (42:6)—yet by the very start of the next chapter, the instant God's answer crossed their wishes, they rejected his word outright (43:2–3).

**Jezaniah and Azariah are the same man.** Chapter 42 names the commander "Jezaniah son of Hoshaiah" (42:1), whereas chapter 43 calls him "Azariah son of Hoshaiah" (43:2). Biblical scholars widely recognize them as the same individual: in ancient Hebrew script the two names look remarkably similar and represent either an alternate form of the name or a minor scribal variation (the ancient Greek Septuagint reads Azariah in both places).

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, recounts the episode of chapter 42 as the most tragic turning point for the surviving Judean remnant. He describes how Johanan and the commanders pleaded with Jeremiah to inquire of God, even binding themselves by oath to obey every decree completely. Yet when God appeared after ten days instructing Jeremiah to tell them to remain safely in the land without fearing Babylon—and warning of destruction if they fled to Egypt—they chose their own fear and suspicion over the prophetic word. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Josephus recounting Johanan's request to Jeremiah, the ten-day wait, and the command to remain in Judah in Book 10 of Antiquities; exact section number not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, unmasks the hypocrisy behind the solemn oath of verses 5–6. He observes that the Judean leaders felt bold enough to promise obedience "whether it is pleasant or unpleasant" only because they assumed Jeremiah would tell them what they wanted to hear; the moment God's word collided with their prearranged plan, their true face appeared. Calvin also pauses on the ten-day silence (42:7) to teach that even the greatest prophets do not control revelation, but must wait patiently on God's timing; and he explains God's "relenting" (42:10) as God stooping to human speech to express his mercy in halting judgment. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 42)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's exposition of the hypocritical oath, the ten-day wait, and the anthropomorphic language of God relenting in chapter 42; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, treats chapter 42 as a mirror of a very common human habit: people act eager to seek God's direction when in truth they only want God to endorse a decision already locked in their hearts. Reflecting on verse 16, Henry offers a penetrating insight: when we flee our duty out of fear, the very calamity we tried to dodge hunts us down on the road of disobedience; staying on the path of obedience, however dangerous it appears, remains the safest ground because God guards it. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah chapter 42)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: observations on seeking divine rubber-stamping for pre-made plans and the paradox of feared disaster overtaking fugitives in 42:16 are characteristic of Matthew Henry; original text not checked -->

**Derek Kidner**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, highlights the ten-day wait (42:7) as a stamp of prophetic authenticity: Jeremiah did not manufacture oracles on cue, but waited steadily until God's word genuinely arrived. Kidner also points to the irony embedded in the very setting of the conversation: the entire community had already marched down to Bethlehem on the road toward Egypt before turning to ask Jeremiah whether they should go—a classic portrait of bringing a pre-signed verdict to God for approval. *(The Message of Jeremiah, 1987, on chapter 42)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Kidner's comments on the authenticity of the ten-day wait and the irony of the remnant already camped on the road to Egypt; exact page number not checked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 42 as a stark theological crossroads set before the surviving community. Staying in Judah meant accepting a humbled existence under Babylon while placing complete trust in the LORD who promised to "build" and "plant" anew out of the rubble (42:10); going down to Egypt meant grabbing a self-engineered illusion of safety and reversing the entire Exodus. When Jeremiah charges them with deceiving themselves at the cost of their own lives (42:20), he exposes how fear had stripped them of the capacity to hear a fresh promise from God. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 42)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Brueggemann framing the choice between Judah and Egypt as a reversal of the Exodus and a clash between faith and fear in chapter 42; exact page number not checked -->
