# Jeremiah 20 — Locked in the Stocks and Fire in the Bones

## Story

Jeremiah's thunderous announcement right inside the temple courtyard at the close of chapter 19 sets off an immediate explosion. Pashhur son of Immer—a priest serving as chief officer overseeing order in the house of the LORD—hears those words with his own ears and strikes back. He has Jeremiah beaten and locked in the stocks at the Upper Gate of Benjamin in the temple precinct overnight (20:1–2).

The next morning, Pashhur releases Jeremiah from the stocks. Expecting a flogging and a night of public humiliation to break the prophet's nerve, he instead gets the shock of his life: fresh out of the stocks, Jeremiah looks the chief officer straight in the eye and renames him by the LORD's decree. God no longer calls you Pashhur, but *Magor-missabib*—meaning *Terror on Every Side* (20:3). Jeremiah spells out the sentence clearly: you will become a terror to yourself and to all your friends, forced to watch them cut down by enemy swords. God will hand all Judah, every treasure of Jerusalem, and the royal storehouses over to the king of Babylon—the very first time in the book the northern foe is called by name. Pashhur himself, his entire household, and every friend to whom he prophesied lies will be hauled off to Babylon, to die and be buried on foreign soil (20:4–6).

Yet immediately after that breathtaking display of courage before power, the scene closes into the private chamber of a shattered soul. Alone before the LORD, Jeremiah pours out one of the rawest accusations in all of Scripture: "You have deceived me, O LORD, and I was deceived. You have overcome me and prevailed." All day long he has become a public laughingstock; every time he opens his mouth he has to cry out "Violence and destruction!" turning the word of the LORD into nonstop insult and reproach day after day (20:7–8).

At times he resolved to quit—never to mention God again, never to speak another word in His name. Yet silence hurt even worse than speaking. Every time he tried to keep quiet, God's word inside his heart became "like a burning fire, shut up in my bones," leaving him exhausted from holding it back until he simply could not contain it (20:9). Stepping outdoors, he hears mocking whispers on every side—echoing the very nickname "Terror on Every Side" he had just pinned on Pashhur—while even his closest friends watch for him to stumble, plotting to denounce him so they can overpower him and get revenge (20:10).

Out of that suffocating corner, a sudden flash of light breaks through. Jeremiah reminds himself that the LORD still stands beside him "like a mighty warrior," ensuring his persecutors will trip and bear everlasting disgrace. He entrusts his case to the One who tests the righteous and sees heart and mind, then abruptly breaks into psalm-like praise: "Sing to the LORD! Praise the LORD!" because He rescues the life of the needy from the hands of evildoers (20:11–13).

Yet that light flickers out almost as fast as it flared. Right on the heels of praise, Jeremiah plunges into the darkest abyss of his entire life and curses the day he was born: "Cursed be the day I was born!" He wishes the day his mother bore him may never be blessed, cursing even the messenger who ran to bring his father glad news of a baby boy—wishing that man the fate of cities God overthrew without pity, hearing war cries from dawn until noon (20:14–16). He wishes he had died inside the womb so his mother's belly might have remained his grave forever. The chapter closes on a bone-chilling question left hanging without an answer: "Why did I come out of the womb to see only trouble and sorrow, and to end my days in shame?" (20:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Courage in public does not mean we never break apart in private.** Facing the temple officer who had just beaten and locked him up, Jeremiah stood tall without a tremor in his voice (20:3–6). Yet moments later, alone with God, he collapsed so low he wished he had never been born (20:14–18). Godly strength is not an unfeeling armor of iron; it is pressing on in faithfulness even while bleeding inside.

**God's true word is a fire no wooden stocks can lock away.** Pashhur clamped Jeremiah in the stocks overnight hoping to shut him up (20:2), yet even Jeremiah himself could not shut himself up (20:9). Once God's word burns inside a person's bones, neither beatings nor mockery can outweigh the inner compulsion to speak truth.

**Sellers of smooth lies reach for the whip against truth-tellers.** Verse 20:6 slips in a telling detail: Pashhur was not just a security officer—he also "prophesied lies" to his friends. People whose careers depend on peddling comforting fantasies cannot tolerate anyone bursting their bubble; once arguments fail, they reach for power and chains.

**Real faith can pass through dark valleys without tidy happy endings.** Many imagine trusting God means smiling serenity around the clock. Jeremiah 20 shatters that illusion: separated by a single verse, he drops straight from joyful praise (20:13) into cursing his birthday (20:14), and the chapter ends right inside unanswered pain (20:18). Scripture leaves that darkness untouched so despairing believers know they are not outcasts before God.

## Deeper Meaning

**The darkness of 20:17 confronting the call from the womb.** At the start of the book, the LORD told Jeremiah He knew him and set him apart before he left the womb, promising to stay with him and deliver him (1:5; 1:8; 1:19). In this chapter those two sides of his calling collide violently: in 20:11 he still clings to the mighty Warrior standing beside him, yet by 20:17–18 he wishes the very womb God knew had become his tomb. His obedient life proves God rescues His servants *through* suffering rather than exempting them from it.

**Jeremiah's lament harmonizing with Job's cry.** Cursing his birth and wishing he had died in the womb (20:14–18) echoes beat for beat Job's famous lament sitting in ashes after losing children and wealth (Job 3:1–3; 3:11; cf. 15:10). Meanwhile, cities God overthrew without pity in 20:16 recall Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis 19:24–25). Two of the Old Testament's most faithful saints touched rock-bottom despair, and God never edited their cries out of Scripture.

**Fire in the bones resounding through the New Testament apostles.** The inability to keep silent in 20:9 echoes the prophet Amos asking: the Lord GOD has spoken—who can but prophesy? (Amos 3:8). In the New Testament, when Peter and John are threatened and flogged by temple authorities just as Pashhur treated Jeremiah (cf. 1 Kings 22:24–25), they reply flatly, "We cannot stop speaking about what we have seen and heard," walking out of the council to keep teaching without pause (Acts 4:19–20; 5:40–42). The apostle Paul felt that exact inner fire when writing, "Woe to me if I do not preach the gospel!" (1 Corinthians 9:16).

**Betrayed by close friends and walking ahead of the Suffering Messiah.** Jeremiah's loneliness as even his "closest friends" wait for him to trip and report him (20:10) echoes the psalmist's pain over a close companion who shared his bread yet lifted a heel against him (Psalms 41:9). Deeper still, the path of a faithful servant beaten, mocked by his own people, and crying out in darkness before God foreshadows Jesus—who confessed in Gethsemane, "My soul is consumed with sorrow to the point of death," and cried aloud from the cross amid midday darkness (Matthew 26:38; Mark 15:34).

## Notes & Nuances

**The verb "deceived" in 20:7 packs far more force than polite translations hint.** The Hebrew verb (*patah*) can mean *entice*, *seduce*, *deceive*, and in Old Testament legal texts even describes overpowering someone weaker. Paired with "You have overcome me and prevailed," Jeremiah's outcry sounds like the raw protest of someone feeling overpowered by an irresistible God into a burden heavier than he can bear. That Scripture preserves this startlingly bold line shows God welcomes unfiltered honesty from hurting hearts.

**What were "the stocks" in 20:2?** The Hebrew term denotes a wooden frame designed to twist and bend a prisoner's body—clamping neck, wrists, and ankles into a cramped, agonizing hunch right beside a busy temple gate for maximum public shame. Pashhur inflicted a punishment reserved for madmen and false prophets to wreck Jeremiah's credibility in public view.

**Why does cursing his birthday (20:14–18) come immediately after singing praise (20:13)?** That jarring plunge startles almost every reader: soaring trust in 20:11–13 drops instantaneously into despair in 20:14–18. Some scholars believe the book's editor placed two independent poems of Jeremiah side by side; others note how naturally such emotional whiplash mirrors deep exhaustion under relentless hostility. Either way, the sequence preserved in Scripture speaks an honest truth: a triumph of faith today does not guarantee tomorrow will be free of turmoil.

**How does Jeremiah's lament in 20:14–18 differ from self-destruction?** Even at his lowest ebb, Jeremiah never attempts suicide, never curses God, and never walks off his post. He aims his anguish at a past calendar date and an anonymous messenger, then carries his unanswered question directly before God—turning deep depression itself into a prayer.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, the third-century Alexandrian scholar, devotes his entire twentieth homily on Jeremiah to tackling the shocking line in 20:7—rendered bluntly in the ancient Greek Bible as *You deceived me*. He explains that God never lies maliciously; rather, just as a wise father or caring physician guides a child or patient toward something vital without spelling out every painful detail upfront, God drew Jeremiah into his mission with gentle wisdom exceeding the young prophet's narrow foresight. *(Homilies on Jeremiah, Homily 20)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Origen's Homily 20 explaining the verb "deceived" in 20:7 via the father and physician analogies is the most celebrated homily in his Homilies on Jeremiah -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, refuses to gloss over Jeremiah's meltdown in 20:14–18, naming it frankly as genuine human frailty and impatience buckling under unbearable humiliation. Turning to 20:9 and the fire trapped in his bones, however, Calvin highlights that anyone truly commissioned by God cannot trade faithfulness for comfort; that inward fire kept the prophet standing even when natural instinct screamed to run. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah and the Lamentations, on chapter 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin candidly acknowledging Jeremiah's weakness at the end of chapter 20 while highlighting the irresistible fire of God's word in verse 9, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, spotlights the intensely human contrast dividing chapter 20: out in the temple court Jeremiah faced down the officer who flogged and shackled him without flinching, yet back in private he crumbled so deeply he cursed his birth. Henry cautions believers never to grow overconfident after a moment of triumph, because the distance between a song of praise (20:13) and the pit of despair (20:14) is razor-thin in human hearts. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: contrasting public boldness with private collapse and noting the sudden drop from verse 13 to 14 reflect Matthew Henry's familiar exposition, though not verified verbatim -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, regards 20:7–18 as the climax of Jeremiah's laments, placing two seemingly incompatible realities side by side without smoothing over the tension: blazing confidence in a mighty Warrior (20:11–13) and unresolved gloom at the close (20:14–18). Leaving the chapter suspended in an unanswered question rather than stitching on a tidy happy ending testifies honestly to the real cost of prophetic faithfulness. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: highlighting the unresolved clash between trust in 20:11–13 and darkness in 20:14–18 is a standout signature insight of Brueggemann on chapter 20 -->
