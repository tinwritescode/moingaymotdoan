# Jeremiah 23 — Bad Shepherds, the Righteous Branch, and False Prophets

## Story

Right after sentencing Judah's kings in chapter 22, the LORD rebukes the nation's rulers through the metaphor of shepherds. God declares: "Woe to the shepherds who destroy and scatter the sheep of My pasture!" Instead of caring for His people, they scattered and drove away the flock without ever attending to them. Therefore God Himself will punish them for their evil deeds. Yet He will not abandon the sheep: He Himself will gather the remnant from all the countries where He drove them, bring them back to their own pasture to be fruitful and multiply, and set new shepherds over them to tend them so the flock will never again be afraid or missing a single sheep (23:1–4).

From the broader promise of faithful shepherds, the oracle climbs to a far greater promise: "Behold, the days are coming, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch." That future King will reign wisely and carry out justice and righteousness in the land; in His days Judah will be saved, Israel will dwell securely, and He will be called by the name "The LORD Our Righteousness!" His deliverance will be so monumental that people will no longer swear by the LORD who brought Israel up out of Egypt, but by the One who brought the descendants of the house of Israel back from the north and from every country where He scattered them, settling them peacefully on their own soil (23:5–8).

Starting at verse 9, the chapter opens a new section titled "Concerning the prophets." Before delivering God's indictment, Jeremiah himself cries out in shuddering distress: "My heart is broken within me; all my bones tremble. I have become like a drunken man, like a man overcome by wine, because of the LORD, because of His holy words." He looks out across a land overrun with adulterers, pastures parched under a curse, and people rushing down evil roads. Most terrifying of all, prophets and priests alike are ungodly; God sees their wickedness spilling right inside His own temple. Consequently their path will turn into slippery ground in pitch darkness, pushing them headlong to fall in the year of their punishment (23:9–12).

The LORD compares Jerusalem's prophets with the prophets of Samaria in earlier days. In Samaria He saw repulsive folly as prophets prophesied by Baal and led Israel astray. Yet among Jerusalem's prophets He sees something even more horrifying: they commit adultery, walk in lies, and "strengthen the hands of evildoers, so that no one turns his back on wickedness." To God's eyes, all of them have become like Sodom and the city's citizens like Gomorrah. Because ungodliness has radiated out from Jerusalem's prophets across the whole country, God declares He will feed them wormwood and give them poisoned water to drink (23:13–15).

Therefore the LORD of Hosts warns the public not to listen to what these prophets say. They fill people with false hopes, speaking visions spun from their own minds rather than from the mouth of the LORD. They keep assuring those who despise God that they will have peace, and telling stubborn hearts that no harm will touch them. God fires a piercing question to expose them: who among them has actually "stood in the council of the LORD" to see and hear His word? Already the storm of the LORD's wrath has burst out like a whirling tempest spinning down over the heads of the wicked, and it will not subside until it accomplishes His full purpose. God declares flatly He never sent them, yet they ran; He never spoke to them, yet they prophesied. Had they truly stood in His council, they would have proclaimed His words to His people and turned them back from their evil ways and deeds (23:16–22).

God reminds those frauds exactly who He is: "Am I only a God nearby, and not a God far away?" Nobody can squeeze into a hiding spot out of His sight, for He fills heaven and earth. He hears every word false prophets boast in His name: "I had a dream! I had a dream!" Whispering dream-stories to one another, they conspire to make His people forget His name just as their ancestors forgot Him for Baal. Let any prophet who has a dream tell his dream, God says, while the one who has My word speaks My word faithfully—for "what does straw have in common with grain?" God's real word packs power totally unlike hollow dreams: "Is not My word like fire, and like a hammer that smashes a rock?" (23:23–29).

Next, the LORD announces a triple "I am against" exposing three tricks of false prophets. He is against prophets who steal words from one another and pass them off as fresh revelations. He is against prophets who wag their own tongues and slap "He declares!" onto their inventions. And He is against prophets peddling lying dreams and reckless boasts to mislead His people—God never sent or appointed them, and they do this people no good whatsoever (23:30–32).

The chapter closes with a sharp clash over the phrase "the burden of the LORD"—in Hebrew the term for a prophetic oracle sounds identical to the word for "burden," so crowds, prophets, and priests sling it sarcastically at Jeremiah. God instructs him that if they sneer, "What is the burden of the LORD?" he must answer straight back: God will cast them off! From now on, anyone tossing that term around mockingly will be punished along with his household. When sincerely inquiring of God, people should simply ask one another, "What has the LORD answered?" or "What has the LORD spoken?" They must stop uttering "the burden of the LORD," for they have twisted the words of the living God, turning their own words into a crushing burden on themselves. If they stubbornly keep parroting that phrase in defiance of His command, God will forget them altogether and fling both them and Jerusalem out of His sight into everlasting disgrace and unforgettable shame (23:33–40).

## Key Takeaways

**When human leaders betray their trust, God steps in personally to rescue His flock.** Judah's kings held authority to shepherd the people, yet they neglected and scattered them instead (23:1–2). The chapter never stops at condemning human corruption; it promises God Himself will regather His flock and raise up a truly righteous King for David's house (23:3–6).

**True righteousness is God's gift, not a badge humans pin on themselves.** Calling the coming King "The LORD Our Righteousness" reveals that hope rests not in royal dynasties or political maneuvers, but in God's own righteousness (23:5–6). He both establishes justice on earth and becomes the righteousness by which His people dwell safely.

**The deadliest false prophecy does not openly attack God—it uses God's name to soothe sin.** Jerusalem's prophets did not invoke Baal as Samaria's did; they smoothly kept saying "The LORD declares" (23:13–17). Yet they promised safety to people despising God and emboldened evildoers so nobody repented. Religion tailored to please crowds and dodge truth is far more toxic to God than blatant paganism.

**God's genuine word is proved by moral fruit and power to break hard hearts.** God provides two clear touchstones distinguishing real messengers from frauds: those truly sent by Him live uprightly and urge people off evil paths (23:14; 23:22). God's word is never harmless fluff like straw or idle dreams; it burns away dross like fire and shatters stone-hard hearts like a hammer (23:28–29).

**Never mock or twist the word of God.** Citizens wielded the pun on "burden" to ridicule Jeremiah's solemn warnings (23:33–36). Whenever people treat God's word lightly and turn it into a punchline, their own contempt boomerangs back as a crushing burden (23:38–40).

## Deeper Meaning

**From bad shepherds to the Good Shepherd.** God's rebuke against shepherds scattering the flock and His pledge to hunt down and gather His sheep Himself (23:1–4) was expanded richly just years later by Ezekiel among the exiles in Babylon (Ezekiel 34:2–4; 34:11–12). That thread runs straight into the New Testament when Jesus declares, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep," promising to gather sheep from other folds into one flock (John 10:11; 10:14–16).

**The "righteous Branch" and Christ our righteousness.** The vision of a fresh shoot sprouting from David's felled stump runs as a scarlet thread across the prophets—from Isaiah to Zechariah and again later in Jeremiah (Isaiah 11:1; Zechariah 3:8; 6:12; Jeremiah 33:14–16). The apostle Paul catches the exact resonance of "The LORD Our Righteousness" in 23:6 when writing that Christ Jesus has become for us "wisdom from God—our righteousness, holiness, and redemption," and in Him we become the righteousness of God (1 Corinthians 1:30; 2 Corinthians 5:21).

**A new Exodus eclipsing the old.** Rescue from Egyptian slavery stood as Israel's defining national memory of who God was. Yet in 23:7–8—repeating the promise of 16:14–15—God vows that regathering from the north and every nation will tower so high it overshadows Egypt itself. That promise began dawning as Jewish exiles returned from Babylon, yet gathering from "all the countries" without one sheep lost reaches full scale only in the worldwide salvation opened by Jesus.

**Standing in God's council and the living power of His word.** The picture of a true prophet standing in "the council of the LORD"—the heavenly assembly around God's throne—vividly recalls the prophet Micaiah facing four hundred court prophets under King Ahab (1 Kings 22:19). Likewise, God's omnipresence in 23:23–24 echoes Psalm 139 celebrating that nobody can hide from His presence in heaven or the depths (Psalms 139:7–10). And God's word as fire and rock-shattering hammer in 23:29 finds its New Testament echo in Hebrews describing God's word as "living and active, sharper than any double-edged sword," piercing straight into the thoughts and intentions of the heart (Hebrews 4:12).

**Tests separating true from false prophets across Scripture.** The Law of Moses laid down two primary checks for anyone claiming prophetic authority: does their message pull people away from the true God, and do their predictions actually come true? (Deuteronomy 13:1–3; 18:20–22). Jeremiah 23 adds the crucial test of personal character and stance toward sin (23:14; 23:17; 23:22). Centuries later, Jesus distilled that very standard when warning followers about false prophets wearing sheep's clothing: "By their fruit you will recognize them" (Matthew 7:15–16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Hidden irony linking "The LORD Our Righteousness" with King Zedekiah's name.** Judah's final king—ruling during the closing years before the fall—was named Zedekiah, which in Hebrew literally means "The LORD is my righteousness." His name sounded noble, yet his reign was spineless, faithless, and led the kingdom to ruin. Naming the coming King "The LORD Our Righteousness" (23:6) speaks pointedly: the hollow monarch wearing a grand name will pass away, and God will raise up a true King who actually embodies everything that name promises.

**Verse 23:23 is easily read backward when lifted out of context.** Hearing "Am I only a God nearby, and not a God far away?" many readers take it as a cozy reassurance that God stays close. Right in the flow of 23:23–24, however, it is a stern warning aimed straight at false prophets. They imagined God stayed localized—confined to heaven or the temple—leaving them free to cook up fake dreams in dark corners. God retorts that He is both near and far—nothing anywhere escapes His gaze, because He fills heaven and earth.

**The wordplay on "burden of the LORD" at the chapter's close.** In Hebrew, *massa* served both as a standard prophetic label for a solemn oracle and literally meant "a heavy load." Because Jeremiah constantly delivered grim warnings, citizens taunted him: "What heavy 'burden' from the LORD do you have for us today?" At 23:33, the ancient Greek Septuagint and many modern English versions—by splitting the Hebrew letters slightly differently—read God's retort as "You are the burden, and I will cast you off!" rather than "What burden? I will cast you off." Catching that sarcastic pun clarifies why God responds so severely to a seemingly casual question (23:33–40).

**Scripture does not reject every dream—it rejects using dreams to peddle lies.** God Himself spoke through dreams to Jacob, Joseph, and Daniel. In 23:28 God does not claim all dreams are wicked; He says, "Let the prophet who has a dream retell his dream"—meaning call a dream a dream, never pass off subjective imagination as the authoritative word of God, just as no farmer mixes straw into wheat.

**Harsh judgments like "wormwood and poisoned water" and "everlasting disgrace" target deliberate spiritual fraud.** Comparing Jerusalem's prophets to Sodom and Gomorrah (23:14) and swearing to fling the city away (23:39–40) sounds fierce. Yet read in context, these were trusted spiritual leaders actively enabling wickedness and lulling an entire nation to sleep on the eve of catastrophe. God's severity against frauds exploiting His name stands right beside His tenderness toward the scattered sheep opening the chapter (23:1–4).

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, reads the promised "righteous Branch" named "The LORD Our Righteousness" in 23:5–6 as a direct prophecy of Jesus, whom the New Testament proclaims as believers' righteousness. Expounding the close of the chapter on "the burden of the LORD," he alerts readers to the double meaning of Hebrew *massa*—both oracle and heavy load—explaining why the people's mockery amounted to blasphemy against God's word. *(Commentary on Jeremiah, on chapter 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: christological reading of 23:5–6 and explaining the double meaning of massa at the end of chapter 23 are classic points in Jerome's commentary, though not checked verbatim in Latin -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, ranks 23:6 ("The LORD Our Righteousness") among the Old Testament's supreme testimonies to Christ. In his famous debate with theologian Andreas Osiander, Calvin rejected the notion that God infuses divine essence into believers to make them righteous; instead, he argued the verse means believers are counted righteous through Christ's obedience and saving work rather than personal merit. Calvin also cited applying the divine name "LORD" to David's Branch as clear witness to the Savior's deity. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 3, Chapter 11; Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 23)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's controversy with Osiander over Jeremiah 23:6 in Institutes 3.11 is one of his most famous and widely cited passages -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, unpacks the vivid contrast between straw and grain alongside fire and hammer in 23:28–29. Human fabrications and counterfeit dreams are pure straw—weightless, hollow, unable to nourish a soul—whereas God's authentic word acts like grain feeding life, like fire scorching away rubbish and melting cold hearts, and like a hammer strong enough to crack the hardest stone into repentance. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's pastoral exposition of straw, wheat, fire, and hammer on 23:28–29, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the nineteenth-century British preacher, delivered a celebrated sermon on the title "The LORD Our Righteousness" from Jeremiah 23:6. He stressed that self-made human righteousness amounts only to tattered rags incapable of covering us before a holy God; only by resting completely in Christ—who bore our sin and clothes us in His flawless righteousness—do believers find unshakable peace free from condemnation. *(Sermon on "The Lord Our Righteousness")* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's sermon on "The Lord Our Righteousness" (Jehovah Tsidkenu) from Jeremiah 23:6 is well known, though exact sermon number on spurgeongems.org and wording were not verified -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic treasury of Jewish law and commentary, preserves a famous teaching from the school of Rabbi Ishmael on God's word being "like a hammer that smashes a rock" in 23:29. Just as one hammer blow against stone sends multiple sparks flying, the rabbis taught that a single verse of Scripture can radiate multiple valid shades of meaning under careful study. Talmudic tradition likewise cites 23:6 when discussing exalted names bestowed on the Messiah. *(Babylonian Talmud, Tractates Sanhedrin and Bava Batra)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Rabbi Ishmael's teaching on 23:29 (hammer splitting rock into many sparks in Sanhedrin) and citing 23:6 for messianic names (in Bava Batra) are famous Talmudic citations -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, sees in Jeremiah 23 a fierce collision between two rival voices inside Judah. On one side stands establishment religion serving the royal court, where false prophets sell God's name to manufacture reassuring "peace" so elites can keep profiting from injustice undisturbed. On the other stands genuine prophecy emerging from God's own presence—uncomfortable to swallow, yet indispensable for breaking the old regime's illusions and clearing ground for the righteous kingdom God Himself will raise up. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: framing Jeremiah 23 as conflict between status-quo court prophecy and illusion-shattering true prophecy is a hallmark of Brueggemann, though exact pages were not verified -->
