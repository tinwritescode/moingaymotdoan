# Jeremiah 48 — Wine Left on Its Dregs and Tears for Moab

## Story

Leaving the Philistine coast of chapter 47, the oracles against the nations swing across the Dead Sea to the east, targeting Moab—a prosperous neighboring kingdom renowned for its flocks and vineyards on the fertile highlands. This is the longest prophecy among all the oracles directed against Judah's smaller neighbors. "The LORD of Hosts, the God of Israel" opens with cries over its leading cities: Nebo lies devastated, Kiriathaim is put to shame, and the fortress is smashed down. In Heshbon enemies plot to wipe Moab off the map of nations; Madmen too will be silenced as the sword pursues it (48:1–2).

Heartrending cries ring out across the hillsides. From Horonaim up the ascent of Luhith, lines of refugees climb weeping as they go, while even little children wail amid the wreckage. The oracle urges the people to flee for their lives, stranded alone "like a bush in the desert." Because Moab trusted in its own achievements and hoarded treasures, even its patron god Chemosh—together with his priests and officials—will be marched off into exile. The destroyer sweeps into every city, valley, and plain. This opening section closes with a chilling curse directed at those carrying out the sentence: "Cursed is the one who is remiss in doing the work of the LORD, and cursed is he who holds back his sword from bloodshed!" (48:3–10).

After that initial tempest, the oracle slows down to diagnose Moab's deep-seated disease through an everyday winemaker's image. From its youth, Moab lived at ease, never experiencing exile, "like wine left on its dregs, not poured from jar to jar," so its original taste and aroma never changed. When wine sits undisturbed too long on its sediment, it turns syrupy and stale. Therefore, the LORD announces the day he will send pourers to tip out Moab's jars and smash its pitchers to pieces. Moab will stand ashamed of Chemosh, just as the house of Israel once stood ashamed of the golden calf at Bethel. Boasts of battlefield heroism fall quiet; every neighbor is summoned to mourn: "How the mighty scepter is broken, the glorious staff!" (48:11–17).

The map of ruin spreads across the entire plateau. Daughter Dibon must step down from her place of honor to sit on parched ground as the destroyer shatters her fortresses. Residents of Aroer stand anxiously by the roadside, questioning fleeing men and women about what happened; word of Moab's defeat echoes along the Arnon River. Judgment rolls off a long roster of highland towns—from Holon, Jahzah, Mephaath, Dibon, and Nebo to Kiriathaim, Kerioth, and Bozrah—sparing neither near nor far. The kingdom's military strength snaps in two: "The horn of Moab has been cut off, and his arm is broken" (48:18–25).

The LORD then names the exact reason Moab must drink itself drunk until it staggers, wallows in its own vomit, and becomes a laughingstock: it "magnified itself against the LORD." Back when Israel suffered disaster, Moab stood by shaking its head in mockery, treating Israel like a thief caught red-handed. Now it is Moab's turn to abandon its towns and hide in rock crevices, trembling "like a dove that nests in the sides of the mouth of a gorge." The oracle heaps up every sharp word for arrogance: "We have heard of Moab's pride—how exceedingly proud he is!"—his loftiness, haughtiness, and boasts; yet God declares that all his bluster and idle talk amount to nothing (48:26–30).

Then, right at the height of judgment, the chapter's tone suddenly drops into a tear-drenched elegy. A voice speaking for the LORD and the prophet breaks out weeping for Moab, mourning the people of Kir-heres and the lush vineyards of Sibmah and Jazer. The grape harvest that should ring with joyous singing falls silent as the destroyer raids the summer fruit; wine stops flowing from the presses, and the shouting heard now is battle cries—"their shouting will not be shouts of joy." Wailing sweeps from Heshbon to Zoar and Horonaim; the waters of Nimrim dry up. God will put an end to those offering sacrifices on idolatrous high places, yet his own heart aches: "my heart moans for Moab like a flute." Every corner of the land wears signs of total mourning: shaved heads, clipped beards, gashed hands, and sackcloth around the waist; on every rooftop and town square nothing is heard but weeping, for Moab has been smashed "like a jar that no one wants" (48:31–39).

The storm of judgment closes with an inescapable trap. The invader "soars like an eagle and spreads his wings against Moab"; Kerioth and every stronghold fall, leaving the hearts of the bravest warriors trembling like a woman in labor. Moab will be destroyed as a nation because it defied the LORD in arrogance. Three layers of doom await Moab's people: "terror, pit, and snare"—flee the terror and fall into the pit; climb out of the pit and step straight into the snare. Exhausted fugitives seeking shade beneath Heshbon's walls meet fire bursting out from Heshbon and Sihon's ancient citadel to consume them; the people of Chemosh are undone, their sons and daughters marched into exile (48:40–46).

Yet the chapter does not end in ashes. One brief closing sentence cracks open an astonishing shaft of light in the dark: "Yet in the latter days I will restore Moab from captivity, declares the LORD" (48:47).

## Key Takeaways

**Unbroken ease slowly turns the human heart stale and arrogant.** The image of wine sitting undisturbed on its dregs, never poured from vessel to vessel (48:11), is a razor-sharp portrait of false security. When a nation or an individual sails along for years untouched by upheaval, they easily assume their wealth and safety are guaranteed—earned by their own skill and strength (48:7). Comfort detached from the fear of God never clarifies the soul; it only lets pride settle thicker at the bottom.

**Arrogance and gloating over another's suffering are sins before God.** Again and again this chapter diagnoses Moab's root failure: towering pride, boasting, and shaking its head in derision when neighboring Israel fell into trouble (48:26–29). Standing on the sidelines mocking someone in distress is no small matter to the LORD. Whoever exalts himself and sneers at another's pain will one day taste the very humiliation he laughed at.

**God judges righteously, yet never takes cold pleasure in human ruin.** Right in the middle of one of Scripture's longest and fiercest verdicts against a pagan nation, we hear sobbing like a mournful flute over Moab's ruined vineyards and fallen cities (48:31–36). God's justice is uncompromising, yet his heart is never callous. He punishes sin, while still grieving deeply over human devastation.

**No idol or human monument survives the day of testing.** Moab leaned completely on its treasuries, fortresses, and the god Chemosh. Yet when crisis hit, Chemosh himself was hauled away into exile alongside his priests (48:7; 48:13). Everything human beings build to replace God—money, military power, or false religion—proves utterly helpless at the exact hour we need it most.

**God's final word even to a proud Gentile nation still leaves room for grace.** After forty-six verses of sword, fire, and weeping, verse 47 promises that in the latter days the LORD will restore Moab from captivity (48:47). God's judgment is dead serious, yet judgment never extinguishes his mercy.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jeremiah weaves earlier prophecies and ancient songs to show the unity of God's word.** Look closely at chapter 48 and you hear echoes rising almost phrase for phrase from Isaiah's oracle on Moab a century earlier: climbing the ascent of Luhith weeping, the drying waters of Nimrim, the withered vines of Sibmah, Moab's overweening pride, and the heart moaning for Kir-heres (Isaiah 15:2–6; 16:6–11). The closing verses also echo the ancient victory song about fire blazing from Heshbon in Sihon's day (Numbers 21:27–29), Balaam's oracle about crushing the forehead of Moab (Numbers 24:17), and Isaiah's triad of "terror, pit, and snare" (Isaiah 24:17–18). Jeremiah is not copying mechanically; he shows every word God spoke across centuries converging into one unified verdict.

**Wine left on its dregs warns God's own people as much as outsiders.** Moab was hardly alone in growing stale through untroubled ease. Jeremiah's near-contemporary Zephaniah aimed the exact same metaphor straight at Jerusalem's citizens—those "complacent on their dregs," murmuring to themselves that the LORD will do neither good nor harm (Zephaniah 1:12). Even the picture of Moab smashed "like a jar that no one wants" (48:38) repeats the exact words Jeremiah had spoken over Judah's own King Jehoiachin (22:28). Complacency respects no borders; covenant people who grow smug look just like Moab.

**Chemosh's disgrace mirrors Israel's old sin at Bethel.** Verse 13 warns that Moab will be ashamed of Chemosh just as Israel was ashamed of Bethel (48:13). Bethel was where King Jeroboam had erected a golden calf for northern Israel to worship, and the prophet Hosea foretold the day that idol would be hauled off to Assyria as tribute, leaving Israel in disgrace (1 Kings 12:28–29; Hosea 10:5–6). Even more painfully, King Solomon himself had once built a high place for Chemosh on the hill directly facing Jerusalem (1 Kings 11:7). Judging Chemosh sounds a sharp alarm against the idol-making instincts of God's own people.

**A nation born in shadow still enters the Messiah's family tree.** Moab's ancestor was born out of the tragic, incestuous aftermath of Sodom's destruction between Lot and his elder daughter (Genesis 19:36–37), and Moab's history with Israel was scarred by bitter clashes, leading prophets like Amos and Ezekiel to denounce Moab's cruelty and scorn (Amos 2:1–3; Ezekiel 25:8–11). Yet Scripture devotes an entire book to Ruth—a faithful Moabite woman who became King David's great-grandmother and holds an honored place in the genealogy of Jesus (Ruth 4:13–17; Matthew 1:5). Setting Ruth's story beside the promise of restoration in verse 47 shows that God's grace reaches far past every historical prejudice.

**Tears over a doomed city point ahead to Jesus weeping over Jerusalem.** The flute-like lament over Moab in verses 31–36 opens a window into God's own heart before a city collapses. Centuries later, approaching Jerusalem and seeing ahead to the day enemies would surround it and leave not one stone upon another, Jesus himself stopped and wept over the city (Luke 19:41–44). The Sovereign Judge of the universe is also the One who sheds tears for those who refuse the peace he offers.

## Notes & Nuances

**The curse in verse 10 is hard to read and easily misused.** The line "Cursed is the one who is remiss in doing the work of the LORD, and cursed is he who holds back his sword from bloodshed!" strikes modern readers harshly, sounding as though it urges merciless violence. It must be kept strictly in its literary and historical context: this is a rhetorical battle charge within a war poem, directed at Babylon's armies as the divinely appointed executors of Moab's judgment, much like chapter 47 calling the invader "the sword of the LORD." It is never a warrant for believers today to use violence or shed blood in the name of religion. Right inside chapter 48 itself, barely twenty verses later, the tone breaks into raw weeping for the victims of that very war (48:31–36), proving Scripture never celebrates callous cruelty.

**When was Moab devastated in history?** Scripture does not pin down the exact date this oracle was fulfilled, though the Jewish historian Josephus records that Nebuchadnezzar campaigned east of the Jordan—subduing Moab and Ammon—about five years after Jerusalem fell, around 582 BC. Even then, Moab's decline unfolded gradually through further incursions by eastern Arabian tribes (cf. Ezekiel 25:10), until Moab ceased to exist as an independent kingdom.

**Who is weeping in verses 31–36—Jeremiah or God?** Commentators have long discussed this point. Within the flow of the oracle, the first-person "I" of verses 30 and 35 is unmistakably the LORD, yet right between them (48:31–32; 48:36) rises the broken cry, "my heart moans for Moab like a flute." Some hear the prophet Jeremiah's own deep empathy (echoing Isaiah's lament); others hear the anguished sorrow of God himself. Many modern interpreters recognize that the boundary intentionally blends into one: the prophet takes God's own grief into his chest, so his weeping reveals God's compassionate heart.

**How should we understand the promise "I will restore Moab from captivity" (48:47)?** Alert readers notice an intriguing textual detail: verse 47 appears in the traditional Hebrew text (followed by English Bibles like the BSB), yet is absent from the ancient Greek Septuagint. As for its meaning, because Moab eventually merged with surrounding populations and never re-established an independent state, many commentators read "in the latter days" not merely as a modest return under Persia, but as looking further ahead to the day of God's universal grace, when people from every nation—even former enemies—are gathered into his kingdom.

**A textual detail in verse 18 ("the destroyer of Moab").** In some older printed editions of traditional Bible translations, a minor typographical slip occasionally distorted the name "Moab" in verse 18; in the Hebrew text, however, it unmistakably reads "the destroyer of Moab has come up against you"—the conqueror climbing from the lowlands to shatter Dibon's fortresses, matching the surrounding verses cleanly.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pays special attention to the image of wine sitting on its dregs in verse 11. He explains that prolonged, untested comfort often sinks people deeper into complacency and vice, like wine left undisturbed on its sediment so its harsh flavor stays locked in; when God shakes up that tranquil life, he exposes the hidden corruption within. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, reads verse 11 as a searching warning against the spiritual hazards of long prosperity and ease. Just as wine left untouched on its dregs grows thick and dull, a human soul untouched by trial easily hardens into pride; sometimes God must "pour" his people from vessel to vessel through unsettling changes so they do not spoil in complacency. **Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, observes that Jeremiah 48 functions like a poetic chorus weaving together older Israelite traditions—especially Isaiah 15–16 and the Heshbon song in Numbers—to dramatize the fall of a smug, self-satisfied power. What Brueggemann highlights most is the stunning paradox between fierce judgment and broken weeping in the chapter's center (48:31–36): the LORD is no tyrant relishing destruction, but One who bears tearing grief even while executing justice—and that very heart makes room for the surprise promise at the end (48:47). *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 48)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Brueggemann's analysis of chapter 48 recycling earlier traditions, divine pathos in 48:31–36, and the surprise turn in 48:47; exact page number not checked -->
