# Jeremiah 8 — The Lying Pen and the Unhealed Wound

## Story

The horrifying sentence at the end of chapter 7 about corpses lying unburied at Topheth has barely faded when chapter 8 pushes the disgrace one step further. The LORD declares that in that day of calamity, enemies will dig up the bones of every rank in the city—kings of Judah, officials, priests, prophets, and ordinary citizens of Jerusalem—dragging them straight out of their graves. Their bones will be spread bare beneath the sun, the moon, and all the starry "host of heaven," the very objects they loved, served, chased after, consulted, and worshiped while alive. No one will gather or rebury them; those bones will lie scattered "like dung on the face of the earth." Meanwhile, the survivors whom God drives into distant lands will sink into such misery that they will "choose death rather than life" (8:1–3).

Shifting into sorrowful appeal, the LORD tells Jeremiah to set plain common-sense questions before the people: when someone falls, do they not get back up? When someone turns off the road, do they not turn back? Yet Jerusalem persists in "perpetual apostasy," clinging tightly to deceit and flatly refusing to return. God Himself has leaned in and listened closely, yet nobody speaks honestly, and not a single soul pauses in repentance to ask, "What have I done?" The whole nation charges headlong like a battle horse plunging blindly into war. God holds up the birds to shame human stubbornness: the stork in the sky knows her appointed seasons, and the turtledove, swift, and thrush keep their exact time of migration. Instinct-driven creatures obey their Creator's design, "but My people do not know the requirements of the LORD!" (8:4–7).

Hearing that charge, Jerusalem's learned elite might well retort: how can we not know the law when we hold the sacred scrolls right in our hands? The LORD cuts off that boast immediately: how dare you claim to be wise and boast that the law of the LORD is with you, when "the lying pen of the scribes" has twisted it into falsehood? The self-styled wise will be shamed, dismayed, and snared, for they have rejected the word of the LORD—so what kind of wisdom is left in them? Therefore God pronounces that their wives will be given to others and their fields to new owners. The indictment spares no one: from the least to the greatest, everyone chases unjust gain; from prophet to priest, everyone deals deceitfully. Facing the gaping wound of the nation, they dress "the wound of the daughter of My people with very little care," chanting "Peace, peace!" when there is no peace at all. They commit abominations without shame, not even knowing how to blush, so when God punishes them they will tumble alongside the fallen (8:8–12).

The oracle swings swiftly from indictment to unfolding disaster, braiding several voices together. The LORD vows to strip them bare like a barren orchard: no grapes left on the vine, no figs on the fig tree, even the leaves withered, and every good gift He gave them taken away (8:13). Right then, the panicked voices of the people break in, urging one another: why are we sitting still? Let us huddle inside the fortified towns and perish there in silence, for the LORD our God has doomed us to silence and given us "poisoned water" to drink because we sinned against Him. They watch their illusions shatter before their eyes: "We hoped for peace, but no good has come, for a time of healing, but there was only terror!" (8:14–15).

From the distance, war news booms closer by the minute. Up at the northern frontier in Dan, the snorting of enemy warhorses is already heard; the neighing of stallions shakes the entire land as invaders sweep down to devour the fields, the cities, and everyone inside them. Immediately the LORD confirms that He Himself is letting this unstoppable scourge loose: He will send venomous serpents and vipers among them—snakes no charmer can soothe—and they will bite them (8:16–17).

The closing section of chapter 8 softens into a sobbing lament where the prophet's tears, the exiled people's cries, and God's own sigh interweave. Jeremiah cries out that his grief has no cure and his heart is faint inside him. He hears the wail of "the daughter of My people" echoing from a faraway land, asking in despair, "Is the LORD no longer in Zion? Is her King no longer there?" Mid-verse, God's own pained voice cuts in: why did they provoke Me with their carved images and worthless foreign idols? Then the people's cry rises again, brief and bone-chilling: "The harvest has passed, the summer has ended, but we have not been saved!" Jeremiah does not watch his people's punishment from a safe distance; because the daughter of his people is crushed, he too is crushed, mourning and seized with horror. Chapter 8 ends on an open, heartrending question: "Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there? Why then has the health of the daughter of My people not been restored?" (8:18–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Stubbornness in sin runs against nature itself.** The chapter opens with simple, everyday comparisons: stumble and you get back up, take a wrong turn and you turn around; even wild birds that cannot read or write remember the season to fly home (8:4–7). Only human beings—even those bearing God's name—charge bullheadedly into destruction like warhorses rushing into battle. Rebelling against God is not freedom; it is losing our most basic survival instinct.

**Holding Scripture in our hands is not the same as living under God's word.** The scribes prided themselves on possessing the LORD's law, yet their lying pens bent God's word to serve the greed and appetite of the age (8:8–10). The gravest threat is not only people who ignore Scripture altogether, but those who weaponize sacred words to excuse fraud and numb other people's consciences.

**Cheap comfort heals no one—it lets the wound turn gangrenous.** False prophets and priests dressed "the wound of the daughter of My people with very little care," repeating the soothing refrain "Peace, peace!" (8:11). They dodged naming the disease of sin for fear of offending listeners and losing their perks. The moment real disaster struck, that painted-on peace crumbled straight into terror (8:15).

**Losing the capacity to blush signals a seared conscience.** Jeremiah spotlights a chilling symptom of social decay: committing detestable acts while not even knowing how to blush (8:12). So long as people still feel embarrassment over wrongdoing, a road home remains open; once brazen evil plays out in broad daylight without anyone flinching, a society is racing toward the cliff.

**A faithful messenger never delights when judgment comes true.** Crowds hated Jeremiah for announcing grim news, yet the final verses of chapter 8 lay his true heart bare: he collapses in agony watching his people shatter (8:18; 8:21). Precisely because he loved them genuinely, he could never lie to them with hollow chants of "Peace, peace!" like the false prophets.

## Deeper Meaning

**Creatures that know their master while God's people do not.** Comparing Judah unfavorably to the stork, turtledove, swift, and thrush knowing their migration seasons (8:7) connects straight back to Isaiah's famous opening line over a century earlier: the ox knows its owner and the donkey its master's manger, yet Israel does not understand (Isaiah 1:3). While the rest of creation keeps the rhythm set by the Creator, rational humans entrusted with His law live upside down against their own design.

**The lying pen of the scribes and Jesus' confrontations.** The charge in 8:8 against guardians of the law whose pens twist it into lies anticipates the sharp clashes in the Gospels centuries later. Jesus likewise rebuked the scribes and Pharisees of His day because they "disregard the commandment of God to keep the tradition of men" (Mark 7:8–9). Later, the apostle Paul echoes the principle of 8:9 when he asks, "Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe?"—for every brand of wisdom that turns its back on God's word ends up exposed as foolishness (1 Corinthians 1:19–20).

**The fruitless fig tree and the warning over Jerusalem.** The picture of God inspecting His people only to find "no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree" (8:13) is a recurring Old Testament portrait of a nation lush with religious foliage yet empty of righteous fruit. In the New Testament, right before His crucifixion, Jesus walked up to a leafy fig tree bearing no fruit and cursed it to wither—a living acted parable of the Jerusalem temple's fate (Mark 11:13–14).

**Jeremiah's tears and the tears of Jesus.** At the end of chapter 8, Jeremiah weeps bitterly over "the daughter of My people" teetering on destruction without realizing it (8:21–22), an outpouring that spills right into the next chapter as he wishes his eyes were a fountain of tears day and night (9:1). Centuries later, standing on the hillside overlooking that very same Jerusalem, Jesus wept because the city did not recognize "what would bring you peace" or the hour of her visitation, leaving her to be surrounded and leveled without one stone left upon another (Luke 19:41–44). Both times, the tears of God's messenger fell over a city locking its own gates against rescue.

**Balm in Gilead and the True Physician.** The region of Gilead east of the Jordan had been famed since ancient times for a healing resin known as balm—as far back as Joseph's story, caravans carried balm from Gilead down to Egypt (Genesis 37:25). Elsewhere in Jeremiah, Gilead's balm reappears when speaking of the incurable wounds of Egypt and Babylon (46:11; 51:8). The aching question in 8:22 hangs in despair: medicine exists and doctors exist, so why is the people's wound still unhealed? Later in the book, Jeremiah reveals the answer human effort can never supply: though his people's wound is "incurable," the LORD Himself promises one day to restore their health and reveal "an abundance of peace and truth" (30:12–17; 33:6). Reading onward into the New Testament, Christians recognize that fulfillment in Jesus—the One who calls Himself the Physician come for the sick (Mark 2:17), by whose wounds humanity is healed (Isaiah 53:5; 1 Peter 2:24).

**The living longing for death.** Verse 8:3 describes exiled survivors so crushed that they "choose death rather than life," a depth of despair once voiced by Job in his agony (Job 3:21) and later mirrored in Revelation's terrifying judgment scene where people seek death and cannot find it (Revelation 9:6). Scripture never glamorizes life estranged from God; living stripped of all hope weighs heavier than death itself.

## Notes & Nuances

**What did digging up bones from graves (8:1–2) signify in the ancient world?** To modern ears, exhuming human remains and scattering them under open sky sounds gruesome and cruel. In the ancient Near East, desecrating tombs and exposing bones was the ultimate humiliation victorious armies inflicted on conquered enemies—both to loot valuables buried with the wealthy and to blot out the honor of their lineage. King Josiah of Judah himself had once dug up the bones of pagan priests at Bethel to defile a false altar (2 Kings 23:16). Here Jeremiah employs the image with biting irony: those who defied the Law of Moses (Deuteronomy 4:19) by erecting altars right inside the temple courts to worship sun, moon, and stars under King Manasseh (2 Kings 21:3–5) will now spend eternity "close" to those lifeless deities—as dry bones bleached in sun and wind while sun and moon lift not a finger to save them.

**How should we read "I will give their wives to other men, and their fields to new owners" (8:10)?** This verse reads harshly because it appears to group women alongside real estate as spoils changing hands. It should be read as a stark prediction of ancient wartime devastation: when a city falls and its men are killed or deported, their property and households fall into the hands of conquerors. Jeremiah also repeats 8:10–12 nearly verbatim from his earlier sermon in chapter 6 (6:12–15). Scripture here is not endorsing the seizure of women or goods, but announcing the bitter real-world fallout that greedy leaders brought down on their own families—those who plundered others will lose everything they clutched.

**Does "the lying pen of the scribes" (8:8) mean Scripture was miswritten?** This line often sparks debate. Some scholars suggest Jeremiah is criticizing scribes who altered or tampered with legal texts—even linking it to Josiah's book of the law if reduced to mere bureaucracy. Most commentators, however, point straight to the very next verse (8:9), where Jeremiah identifies the real issue: they "have rejected the word of the LORD." In other words, God's law itself was not defective; rather, the scribes were churning out skewed rulings, interpretations, and traditions in the law's name to cater to greed and reassure the public that all was fine.

**Who is speaking in 8:18–22, and notice that the passage does not stop at verse 22.** Reading the close of chapter 8 carefully, voices shift rapidly without quotation marks: Jeremiah speaks (8:18; 8:21–22), the exiled crowd cries out in the opening part of 8:19 and again in 8:20, and unexpectedly the LORD Himself breaks into the second half of 8:19 ("Why have they provoked Me with their carved images, with their worthless foreign idols?"). Many scholars even observe that in 8:18 and 8:21, the prophet's grief and God's own grief fuse so closely that they can hardly be separated. Moreover, this lament flows straight across the chapter break into 9:1—in fact, in the original Hebrew text, what English Bibles number as 9:1 is verse 8:23, sitting right inside chapter 8.

**Where was Dan in 8:16?** Dan marked the northernmost tip of ancient Israel (4:15). Because Babylonian forces attacking Judah had to sweep down from the north along the Fertile Crescent, hearing warhorses snorting from Dan meant the enemy had already crossed the border gate; invasion was no longer a distant rumor, but right on top of them.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Bible, lingers thoughtfully over the migrating birds of 8:7 and the question about Gilead's balm in 8:22. He observes that birds know when to move as seasons shift, whereas humans cling to the cold winter of sin instead of turning toward God's light. On "the lying pen of the scribes" (8:8), Jerome warns against teachers who use verbal cleverness to bend Scripture to their own preferences; and on 8:22, he reads the balm and physician as pointers to the spiritual remedy God holds ready for everyone willing to admit they are sick. *(Commentary on Jeremiah, on chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Jerome's characteristic comments on chapter 8 regarding migrating birds, scribes, and the spiritual physician, though without checking the Latin or English text verbatim -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, focuses closely on the scribes' smugness in 8:8–9. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, places special weight on the two famous lines closing this chapter. On 8:20—"The harvest has passed, the summer has ended, but we have not been saved"—he hears a solemn warning for all who keep postponing repentance, letting season after season of grace slip away until opportunity runs out. And on 8:22, he highlights a truth both comforting and searching: Gilead lacks neither balm nor physician; if the sick remain unhealed, the fault lies neither with the Savior nor His cure, but with the patient who refuses to come for treatment. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall both familiar pastoral applications by Matthew Henry on 8:20 and 8:22, though without checking verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Polish-born American Jewish thinker and scholar, draws on laments like the end of chapter 8 in his classic work on the prophets to illuminate what he calls divine pathos. For Heschel, the prophet is never a detached announcer relaying sentences from an unfeeling deity on high; rather, Jeremiah's crushing agony over his people's wounds (8:18–21) mirrors God's own heartbroken anguish over the ruin of His people. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: this is Heschel's central thesis in his Jeremiah chapter of The Prophets, though exact verses analyzed within 8:18–22 were not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 8 as a sharp collision between two postures in crisis: on one side stand royal scribes and court prophets clinging to the slogan "Peace, peace!" in denial of a crumbling reality (8:8–11); on the other stands the prophet's honest weeping, willing to stare straight at the community's unhealed wound (8:18–22).
