# Jeremiah 14 — Drought, False Prophets, and Refused Intercession

## Story

The word of the LORD comes to Jeremiah during a blistering drought scorching all of Judah. Mourning blankets the countryside: city gates languish in silence, citizens "sit on the ground in mourning clothes," and Jerusalem's cry of distress rises toward heaven. Nobles send servants for water, yet they reach the cisterns to find not a drop and "return with empty jars," so shamed and bewildered they cover their heads. The ground splits open with cracks because no rain has fallen on the land, leaving plowmen humiliated and covering their heads as well. Even wild animals are pushed to the brink: "Even the doe in the field deserts her newborn fawn because there is no grass." Wild donkeys stand on barren hills, gasping for air like jackals, their eyes failing because every pasture has burned brown (14:1–6).

Amid that desperation, a fervent prayer rises to the LORD. The speaker makes no pretense of innocence: though our sins testify against us and our rebellions are many, "act for the sake of Your name!" The prayer calls God "the Hope of Israel, its Savior in times of distress," then voices an aching question: why do You seem like a stranger visiting the land, "like a traveler who stays but a night," or like a warrior caught off guard who cannot rescue? Still, the prayer clings to the one cord left holding: You are still in our midst, "and we are called by Your name; do not forsake us!" (14:7–9).

Yet the LORD responds with an icy refusal. This people "loves to wander; they have not restrained their feet"—chasing relentlessly after foreign gods—so He no longer accepts them; now is the hour He remembers their wickedness and punishes their sins. God turns straight to Jeremiah and forbids him: "Do not pray for the well-being of this people." Even if they fast, He will not listen to their cries; even if they present burnt offerings and grain offerings, He will not accept them, but will finish them off with "sword, famine, and plague" (14:10–12).

Hearing that severe decree, Jeremiah immediately steps forward to plead in defense of his people. He tells God that the other prophets keep promising the nation they will see neither sword nor famine, assuring them God will grant them "lasting peace" in this place. At once the LORD tears off their mask: those prophets are prophesying lies in His name. He never sent them, never commanded them, never spoke to them; what they hawk is only "a false vision, worthless divination, and the deceit of their own minds." Consequently, the very prophets declaring there will be no sword or famine will themselves perish by sword and famine. And the crowds who swallow their message will be tossed out into the streets of Jerusalem alongside their wives and children with no one to bury them, "for I will pour out on them the evil they deserve!" (14:13–16).

The LORD then gives Jeremiah a tear-drenched word to speak over them: "Let my eyes overflow with tears night and day without ceasing, for the virgin daughter of my people has been shattered by a crushing blow, a severely grievous wound." Step out into the open country, and bodies slain by the sword lie there; enter the city, and people waste away from famine. Even prophets and priests wander dazed across the land without comprehending what is happening (14:17–18).

Despite the earlier ban, a second prayer breaks out, more anguished than the first: have You rejected Judah completely, does Your soul detest Zion, have You struck us beyond all cure? "We hoped for peace, but no good has come; for a time of healing, but there was only terror." The prayer confesses both this generation's wickedness and the guilt of their ancestors, pleading with God for His name's sake not to despise them or "disgrace Your glorious throne! Remember Your covenant with us and do not break it." The chapter closes with a clear-eyed confession under parched skies: among "the worthless idols of the nations," can any of them bring rain? Or can the heavens send showers by themselves? Only the LORD our God can do that. "So we put our hope in You, for You have done all these things" (14:19–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Crisis forces people to see who they are really leaning on.** The drought in 14:1–6 exposes every human limitation: wealthy nobles cannot buy water, farmers cannot plow cracked soil, and pagan deities stay dead silent. Once the cisterns go dry, the foundational question stands stripped bare in 14:22: who actually makes rain fall and sustains life?

**Outward acts of repentance cannot substitute for feet willing to turn back.** The people knew how to wear mourning clothes, fast, offer sacrifices, and recite eloquent prayers (14:2; 14:7–9; 14:12). Yet God looked straight at their daily walk: they still "love to wander; they have not restrained their feet" (14:10). Solemn ceremonies do not move God when people only want the drought ended without leaving their wrong path.

**Counterfeit promises of peace are deadlier than painful warnings.** False prophets painted a rosy future with no sword, no famine, only guaranteed peace (14:13). Their words sounded comforting, yet that fake painkiller sedated an entire nation into walking unprepared straight into catastrophe (14:14–16).

**Genuine prayer rests not on our merits, but on God's name and faithfulness.** In both prayers (14:7; 14:21), the speakers freely admit their sins leave them zero bargaining power. Their sole anchor is God's own name and the covenant He established—and even before a single drop of rain falls from brassy skies, real faith chooses to stand and wait on Him alone (14:22).

## Deeper Meaning

**Covenant drought and the impotence of Baal.** The Law of Moses had long warned that if Israel deserted the LORD for other gods, the sky overhead would turn to bronze, the ground underfoot to iron, and powder and dust would fall instead of rain (Deuteronomy 28:23–24). Because Canaanite religion hailed Baal as the storm god commanding thunder and rain, droughts constantly tempted Judah toward Baal. Two centuries before Jeremiah, the prophet Elijah declared neither dew nor rain would fall except at the LORD's word, before God Himself sent torrential rain after the showdown on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 17:1; 18:41–45). Even in the New Testament, Paul reminded Gentile listeners that God sending rain from heaven and fruitful seasons stands as His ongoing witness to humanity (Acts 14:17).

**All creation groaning when humanity rebels.** The heartbreaking image of a mother deer abandoning her fawn and wild donkeys going blind on parched ridges (14:5–6) continues the ecological sorrow introduced in chapter 12 (12:4) and echoes the prophet Joel watching cattle groan and wild beasts pant as pastures burn and streams dry up (Joel 1:18–20). Scripture views creation as an interconnected whole: human rebellion never stays neatly inside human borders, but pulls innocent creatures into the drought too.

**The third time God forbids Jeremiah to intercede.** The command "Do not pray for the well-being of this people" in 14:11 is not the first: twice already the LORD had barred Jeremiah from interceding (7:16; 11:14). Reading all three bans together shows Judah's hardening deepening step by step—while simultaneously revealing Jeremiah's heart: even under prohibition, he cannot bear to look away, still stepping into the gap to plead and weep for his people.

**Rejected fasting and sacrifices.** God's declaration in 14:12—that even if they fast and bring burnt and grain offerings He will reject them—connects directly with Isaiah's scorching indictment: God does not choose a fast where people bow their heads on sackcloth and ashes while still quarreling and oppressing workers, but fasting joined to mercy and justice (Isaiah 58:3–7). Religious observance never functions as a talisman against judgment.

**Distinguishing true from false prophets across Scripture.** The false prophets of 14:13–15 wear the exact face Jeremiah exposed earlier—healing the nation's wound lightly by chanting "Peace, peace!" when there is no peace (6:14; 23:16–17)—while the lament of 14:19 echoes the crushed hopes of chapter 8 almost word for word (8:15). The Law of Moses provided a straightforward test: when someone presumes to speak in the LORD's name and the word fails to come true, it was never from God (Deuteronomy 18:20–22). In the New Testament, Jesus likewise warns His followers to watch out for false prophets arriving in sheep's clothing while inwardly ravenous wolves (Matthew 7:15).

**Praying "for the sake of Your name" from Moses to Daniel.** The plea in 14:7 and 14:21—asking God to act for His name's sake and remember His covenant—follows the classic Old Testament pattern of intercession. After the golden calf crisis, Moses pleaded with God to turn from fierce wrath for the honor of His name among the Egyptians and His oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Israel (Exodus 32:11–13). Later, during Babylonian exile, Daniel prayed in the very same vein—relying not on human righteousness, but on God's great mercy and on the city and people who bear His name (Daniel 9:18–19).

## Notes & Nuances

**Are the prayers in 14:7–9 and 14:19–22 spoken by the people or by Jeremiah?** Both passages speak in the plural "we" and sound deeply moving, dividing commentators into two major readings. Many scholars see these as formal communal prayers Judah recited during national fast days for rain—eloquent on the surface, yet rejected by God because the people still refused to forsake idols (14:10–12). Others hear Jeremiah himself stepping squarely into solidarity with his nation, taking their guilt on his own lips as "our" sin to intercede passionately for them. Under either reading, the tense dialogue lays bare a painful reality: Judah had drifted so far that last-minute petitions could no longer turn back the coming historical reckoning.

**Why does God forbid prayer for His people (14:11)?** Seeing God say "Do not pray for the well-being of this people" can unsettle readers, since Scripture everywhere calls us to persevere in praying for others. Placing the verse in its exact historical setting clarifies it: this is not a blanket rule for all believers in every age, but a direct disclosure to Jeremiah that the decree of exile over Judah is now settled. The crowds only wanted the drought lifted without changing their lives; continuing to pray them out of discipline would only feed their illusions. Remarkably, even after the ban, Jeremiah never stops caring for them or speaking honestly with God on their behalf (14:13; 14:19–22).

**If the public was misled by false prophets, why are they punished so severely in 14:16?** Jeremiah raises that very objection of fairness straight to God in 14:13, searching for grounds to soften the sentence. God's answer in 14:10 and 14:16 is candid: the crowd was no helpless flock forced into deception. They already loved wandering and craved soothing promises, choosing to trust flattering prophets instead of uncomfortable truth. In Scripture, deceitful leaders face grim accountability (14:15), yet listeners still bear responsibility for what they choose to believe and follow.

**Whose tears fall in 14:17, and what is "lasting peace" in 14:13?** In 14:17, the LORD instructs Jeremiah to deliver the words, "Let my eyes overflow with tears night and day without ceasing." Because God puts those words in the prophet's mouth, many interpreters hear the LORD Himself revealing His tearful heartbreak over His wounded people, while others hear Jeremiah weeping in deep sympathy with God's pain. As for "lasting peace" in 14:13, the Hebrew phrase *shelom emet* literally means "true peace" or "assured, enduring peace"—the bold guarantee false prophets kept selling to a city standing inches from collapse.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, praises the opening posture of 14:7 where the prayer sets aside all human merit to lean strictly on God's name. Yet he explains clearly why God still rejects the prayers and fasts in 14:10–12: terror of drought only squeezed out surface remorse without touching their inward love for sin. At 14:13–16, when Jeremiah tries to excuse the public as victims of false prophets, Calvin argues that excuse collapses because the people actively wanted flattery—leaving both liars and lovers of lies culpable. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah and the Lamentations, on chapter 14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's exposition of fear-driven outward repentance in 14:10–12 and shared accountability with false prophets in 14:13–16, though not verified verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses at 14:8—asking why God acts "like a traveler who stays but a night"—to remind readers that when God's people treat Him casually like a passing guest, they cannot complain if He seems absent in crisis. At the closing verse 14:22, Henry holds up the confession that no idol can send rain and only God does all these things as a pattern of patient faith: even while skies stay bone dry and God seems silent, believers choose to wait on Him alone. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Matthew Henry's familiar commentary and pastoral lessons on verses 8 and 22, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Jewish thinker and scholar, views passages like 14:11–18 as the height of the prophet's inner conflict: Jeremiah must stand on God's side announcing judgment while refusing to abandon his people, going so far as to look for excuses on their behalf before God (14:13). For Heschel, the nonstop tears of 14:17 do not contradict God's wrath, but unveil the anguished heart of God Himself when compelled to strike the people He loves. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: divine pathos and Jeremiah's torn intercessory role—including defending the people in chapter 14—sit squarely in the Jeremiah chapter of The Prophets, though exact pages were not checked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 14 (continuing into the opening of chapter 15) as a head-on clash between Jerusalem's routine liturgical rain-prayers and the LORD's uncompromising rejection. He spotlights the confrontation at the chapter's heart (14:13–16) between a smug theology of guaranteed "lasting peace"—insisting temple and city are untouchable—and the painful truth that a broken covenant cannot be bought off with ceremonies. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading chapter 14 as a rejected liturgy of drought-lament colliding with false-prophet peace theology is central to Brueggemann's commentary, though exact pages were not verified -->
