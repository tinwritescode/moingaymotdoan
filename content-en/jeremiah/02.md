# Jeremiah 2 — Forsaking the Fountain of Living Water for Broken Cisterns

## Story

Following the call in chapter 1, this is the first public message God sends Jeremiah to proclaim in the hearing of Jerusalem. The opening carries no thunderbolts, only the tenderness of a husband remembering the early days of marriage: the LORD still remembers "the devotion of your youth, your love as a bride," when Israel followed Him out into the wilderness, a land not sown. In those days Israel was set apart as holy, "the firstfruits of His harvest," and anyone who dared devour her incurred guilt and brought disaster upon themselves (2:1–3).

Suddenly the tone shifts into the atmosphere of a courtroom. The LORD summons the whole house of Jacob and all the clans of Israel to testify: "What fault did your fathers find in Me that they strayed so far from Me, and followed worthless idols, and became worthless themselves?" He recalls His past deliverance: He brought them up out of Egypt, led them through a desert of pits, drought, and the shadow of death, and brought them into a fertile land filled with abundant fruit. Yet the moment they set foot there, they defiled His heritage. Even the four classes charged with guiding the nation abandoned Him: the priests never asked where the LORD was, those who handled the law did not know Him, the shepherds rebelled against Him, and the prophets prophesied by Baal (2:4–8).

Therefore the LORD declares He will press His case against them and even against their grandchildren. He challenges them to cross west to the coasts of Cyprus and send east to Kedar to see if any pagan nation has ever traded away its gods—even though theirs are not gods at all. Yet God's own people have "exchanged their Glory for worthless idols." He calls on the very heavens to shudder in horror at two evils His people have committed: "They have forsaken Me, the fountain of living water, and they have dug their own cisterns—broken cisterns that cannot hold water!" (2:9–13).

The speech then turns to the raw wounds already visible across the land. Israel is no homeborn slave, so why has the country been plundered, its towns burned down, its soil laid waste as if roaring lions had mauled it, while even troops from Memphis and Tahpanhes in Egypt shave the crown of their heads? All of this happened simply because they forsook the LORD while He was leading them along the way. Yet instead of turning back, they run to Egypt to drink the waters of the Nile and run to Assyria to drink the waters of the Euphrates. Their own wickedness and apostasy will become the rod that disciplines them, teaching them firsthand how bitter and evil it is to forsake the LORD (2:14–19).

God's anguish intensifies through a barrage of searing images. Long ago He broke their yoke and snapped their chains to set them free, yet they declared, "I will not serve!" and prostituted themselves spiritually on every high hill and under every green tree. He planted them as a choice vine of pure stock, yet they degenerated into a wild, alien vine. Sin has soaked in so deeply that "although you wash with lye and use an abundance of soap," the stain remains before His eyes. Still they insist they are not defiled and have not chased after Baal, so God exposes their frantic conduct in the valley of idolatry: darting back and forth like a restless young camel, like a wild donkey in heat that no one can restrain. When urged to stop before their feet go bare and their throats run dry, they answer defiantly, "It is hopeless! I love foreign gods, and I must go after them" (2:20–25).

Just as a thief caught in the act hangs his head in shame, so the kings, officials, priests, and prophets of Israel will all be put to shame. In ordinary times they turn their backs on God, telling a piece of wood, "You are my father," and telling a stone, "You gave me birth"; then, the moment trouble hits, they cry out for God to rise and save them! God asks with sharp irony: where are the gods you made for yourselves—Judah has as many gods as it has towns, so let them rise up and save you! God struck them to correct them, yet they refused discipline, and their own swords devoured the prophets. His voice drops again into wounded tenderness: "Have I been a wilderness to Israel, or a land of dense darkness?" Does a young woman forget her jewelry, or a bride her wedding attire? Yet His people have forgotten Him for days without number (2:26–32).

The chapter closes with a decisive verdict. The people have grown so skilled at chasing lovers that they could teach wicked women their ways; on the hems of their robes clings the lifeblood of the innocent poor—not burglars caught breaking in, but innocent victims trampled openly. Worst of all is their brazen denial: they keep insisting, "I am innocent. Surely His anger will turn away from me!" That very claim of innocence compels God to bring them to judgment. They scurry back and forth switching allies, but just as they were humiliated by trusting Assyria, they will now leave Egypt with their hands clasped over their heads in disgrace, because the LORD has rejected every human support on which they rely (2:33–37).

## Key Takeaways

**In Scripture, sin is first and foremost a betrayal of love.** Chapter 2 does not begin with a list of broken statutes, but with the sweet memory of a newlywed marriage (2:2). God does not judge like a detached magistrate; He speaks like a betrayed spouse grieving as the one He loves walks off after strangers (2:31–32).

**People gradually become like whatever they worship.** Verse 2:5 voices a sobering law of the human heart: they "followed worthless idols, and became worthless themselves." When we give our hearts to hollow substitutes—whether carved wood and stone in antiquity or prestige and wealth today—our own lives grow hollow right along with them.

**Forsaking God means committing two follies at once.** Verse 2:13 captures the human tragedy in an unforgettable picture: abandoning "the fountain of living water" that stays forever fresh in order to labor over "broken cisterns that cannot hold water." Human souls are made thirsty; turning away from God does not end our thirst, but drives us to dig alternative reservoirs whose water leaks away the harder we try to hold it.

**Greater even than the danger of sinning is insisting we are innocent.** The saddest moments in this chapter appear in 2:23 and 2:35: with the blood of the poor still staining their garments and their footprints plain on the idolatrous hills, the people still claim they are spotless and have done nothing wrong. Refusing to own our guilt slams shut the door to repentance.

**Chasing worldly powers instead of God always ends in humiliation.** Drinking from Egypt's Nile or Assyria's Euphrates (2:18) pictures the search for security through shrewd political alliances. Every refuge outside God may promise temporary protection, yet in the end it delivers only disappointment and shame (2:36–37).

## Deeper Meaning

**Love in the wilderness.** The LORD's memory of Israel as a bride following Him through the desert (2:2) links directly to the prophet Hosea, who lived more than a century before Jeremiah. Hosea likewise portrayed God as a faithful husband promising one day to allure His people back into the wilderness, speak tenderly to them, and restore the love of their youth (Hosea 2:14–15).

**Trading glory for what is worthless.** Verse 2:5 echoes nearly word for word the closing verdict on the fall of the northern kingdom of Israel in the Book of Kings: they "followed worthless idols and became worthless themselves" (2 Kings 17:15). In the New Testament, the apostle Paul draws on the exact thought of 2:11 to diagnose the condition of all humanity: they "exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images of mortal man" (Romans 1:23).

**The fountain of living water in Jesus.** Throughout the Old Testament, the LORD Himself is the fountain of life (Psalms 36:9). In the New Testament, sitting beside Jacob's well, Jesus tells the Samaritan woman—who had likewise tried to quench her soul's thirst through five husbands like cracked cisterns—that He can give "living water" so that whoever drinks it will never thirst again (John 4:10–14). On the great day of the feast in Jerusalem, He stands up and calls everyone who is thirsty to come to Him and drink, promising that "streams of living water" will flow from within them (John 7:37–38).

**A choice vine turned wild.** The image of God planting Israel as a pure choice vine only to watch it sprout wild branches (2:21) echoes Isaiah's famous Song of the Vineyard: the owner did everything possible expecting good grapes, only to find the vineyard yielding wild fruit (Isaiah 5:1–4). Prophets across different generations looked on and saw the same heartbreak in the Gardener.

**Claiming innocence is self-deception.** The stubborn denial of guilt in 2:35 runs completely counter to the path of forgiveness. First John declares, "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves," whereas if we confess our sins, God is faithful and just to forgive us and cleanse us from all unrighteousness (1 John 1:8–10). What lye and abundant soap can never scrub away (2:22), only God's forgiveness can wash clean.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is the audience of chapter 2: Israel or Judah?** Chapter 2 opens with a message addressed to Jerusalem (2:2), yet immediately addresses the "house of Jacob" and the "house of Israel" (2:4), before speaking to "Judah" (2:28). By Jeremiah's day, the ten northern tribes—the kingdom of Israel—had fallen to Assyria more than a century earlier; yet the prophets routinely used the name "Israel" for the whole covenant people, including the surviving southern kingdom of Judah.

**Where are the ancient places named in this chapter?** The coasts of Cyprus—rendered "Kittim" in some versions (2:10)—refer to Cyprus and the Mediterranean shoreline to the west, while Kedar refers to nomadic Arabian tribes to the east; taken together, they mean looking everywhere from west to east. Memphis and Tahpanhes—rendered "Noph" and "Tahpanhes" in older translations (2:16)—were two major Egyptian cities; the verse may allude to Egyptian intervention and humiliation of Judah, culminating when Pharaoh Neco killed King Josiah at Megiddo and imposed tribute (2 Kings 23:29–34). The waters of Shihor are Egypt's Nile, and the River is Assyria's Euphrates (2:18).

**What does "you did not catch them breaking in" in 2:34 mean?** The Law of Moses permitted a homeowner to strike down a thief caught breaking into a house at night without incurring bloodguilt (Exodus 22:2). Here God exposes Judah's brutal oppression: the poor whose blood stains their clothes were no burglars killed in self-defense, but innocent victims struck down in broad daylight.

**The blunt language of 2:23–24.** Comparing the people to a restless young camel and a wild donkey in heat sounds stark and jarring to modern ears. Jeremiah deliberately employs shockingly vivid imagery to expose how completely out of control the nation had become in racing after Baal worship—which was bound up with fertility rituals—and chasing foreign alliances despite every plea to stop.

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, explains why 2:13 calls what the people did *two* evils rather than one. The first evil is ingratitude in turning their backs on God, who pours out every good gift unceasingly; the second evil is folly in tormenting themselves by laboring to dig counterfeit supports that bring nothing but disappointment. For Calvin, abandoning God is not only wicked, it is deeply irrational self-destruction. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's analysis of the "two evils" in 2:13 in his Jeremiah lectures, though without verifying exact wording on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws out the concrete contrast embedded in 2:13: a living spring flows continuously, clear, cool, and abundant, whereas water in a man-made cistern is merely stagnant rainwater—and stored in a cracked cistern at that, so it runs dry at the very moment we are thirstiest. **Tim Keller**, the New York pastor, sees in Scripture's critique of idolatry—epitomized by trading God for broken cisterns in this chapter—a piercing diagnosis of modern life.
