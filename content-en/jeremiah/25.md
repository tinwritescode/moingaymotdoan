# Jeremiah 25 — Seventy Years and the Cup of Wrath

## Story

Chapter 25 brings the reader back to a pivotal turning point eight years earlier than the two fig baskets of chapter 24: the fourth year of King Jehoiakim of Judah, which was the first year of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon—605 BC, the year Babylon rose to crush Egypt and dominate the entire Near East. Standing before all the people of Judah and the residents of Jerusalem, the prophet Jeremiah opens with a bitter reckoning. For twenty-three straight years, from the thirteenth year of King Josiah until that very day, he has spoken the word of the LORD to them again and again, yet they have refused to listen (25:1–3).

Nor was Jeremiah alone: long before him, the LORD repeatedly sent His servants the prophets to plead with the nation. Their message was plain and merciful: let every person turn from his evil way and wicked deeds and stop chasing other gods, and they would remain forever on the land God gave their ancestors. Yet Judah turned a deaf ear, provoking God with idols made by their own hands and bringing ruin onto themselves (25:4–7).

Because the door of repentance has been spurned for decades, the LORD of Hosts pronounces formal judgment—naming the northern enemy explicitly as His instrument. God declares He will summon all the peoples of the north together with "My servant Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon" against Judah and all the surrounding nations. Every sign of normal daily life will go dark: gone will be the sounds of joy and gladness, the voices of bridegroom and bride, the hum of millstones grinding grain each morning, and the warm glow of household lamps at dusk. The entire region will become a desolate ruin, and these nations will serve the king of Babylon for seventy years (25:8–11).

Yet the Babylonian Empire is no permanent master of history. Immediately afterward, the LORD declares that once those seventy years are completed, He will turn and punish the king of Babylon and that nation for their iniquity. The land of the Chaldeans will become an everlasting wasteland, fulfilling every word written in this book against the nations. Many nations and great kings will enslave the Babylonians in turn, and God will repay them exactly according to their deeds and the work of their hands (25:12–14).

From sentences on Judah and Babylon, the scene widens into a prophetic vision spanning the entire world. The LORD hands Jeremiah "the cup of the wine of wrath" and commands him to make every designated nation drink it until they stagger and go mad beneath the sword God sends among them (25:15–16). Jeremiah takes the cup and carries it outward in widening circles. First to drink are Jerusalem and the towns, kings, and officials of Judah. Next come Pharaoh of Egypt, all mixed peoples, the land of Uz, the four major cities of Philistia, then Edom, Moab, Ammon, the coastal cities of Tyre and Sidon, Arabian desert tribes, stretching all the way to distant eastern realms—Zimri, Elam, and Media—along with all the kings of the north and every kingdom on the face of the earth. At the very bottom of that sweeping list appears one cryptic name: the king of Sheshach—a coded name for Babylon itself—who must drink the cup after every other nation (25:17–26).

God puts a chilling command on Jeremiah's lips as he extends the cup: drink, get drunk, vomit, and fall down never to rise again. Should any nation shake its head and refuse to accept the cup from the prophet's hand, he must reply flatly that they will surely drink it. The logic is inescapable: if God is beginning His disaster right in Jerusalem, the city that bears His own name, how could any other nation possibly go unpunished when He summons a sword against all earth's inhabitants? (25:27–29).

Chapter 25 closes with a thunderous oracle echoing the final judgment of all humanity. The LORD roars from on high, thundering over His dwelling place and shouting like harvesters treading grapes in a winepress. He brings charges against the nations and enters into judgment with all flesh. A fierce tempest rises from the farthest ends of the earth, sweeping from nation to nation; the slain of the LORD lie scattered from one end of the earth to the other, neither mourned nor buried. Then "the shepherds" and "leaders of the flock"—the world's mighty kings and rulers—can only wail and roll in ashes with nowhere left to flee. The LORD has left His lair like a lion, turning their once-peaceful pastures into wasteland before His fierce anger (25:30–38).

## Key Takeaways

**God's patience stretches long, yet it does not last forever.** Twenty-three years of Jeremiah rising early to proclaim God's word—piled atop generations of prophets before him—proves God never rushes to judge (25:3–4). He summons disaster only after His people plug their ears against plea after plea to turn back (25:7–8). Looking at the sentence while overlooking twenty-three years of pleading misses God's true heart.

**God rules both foreign empires and their expiration dates.** Calling Nebuchadnezzar "My servant" shatters Judah's illusion that a pagan conqueror is some freak threat outside God's control (25:9). Yet at the same breath, setting a hard cap of "seventy years" and foretelling Babylon's own turn at the cup shatters every tyrant's delusion of absolute power (25:11–14; 25:26). Being used as an instrument in God's hands never absolves an empire of its own brutality.

**Judgment starts with God's people, yet it never stops there.** When the cup of wrath circles the globe, Jerusalem drinks first (25:18; 25:29). Bearing God's name grants no free pass for sin; on the contrary, knowing God's word and resisting it brings earlier accountability. Yet if God judges His own city so rigorously, no earthly power or nation can imagine itself immune from His justice.

**Everyday human joys grow fragile whenever society abandons God.** A nation's true peace lives not merely in fortresses and armies, but in wedding laughter, the morning grind of millstones, and the steady lamp at nightfall (25:10). Once people choose rebellion and counterfeit gods, those simplest, dearest blessings are the first lights to go out.

## Deeper Meaning

**The year 605 BC stands as the hinge of the entire book of Jeremiah.** The fourth year of King Jehoiakim and first year of Nebuchadnezzar anchors the book's biggest turning points: that year Nebuchadnezzar shattered Pharaoh Neco's army at Carchemish, redrawing the map of the Near East (46:2), and that same year God told Jeremiah to dictate twenty-three years of prophecies to Baruch on a scroll (36:1–2). Chapter 25 acts as the summing-up capstone closing the first half of Jeremiah (chapters 1–25) while opening the doorway to the oracles against the nations at the close of the book.

**The seventy-year prophecy reverberating across the end of the Old Testament.** The number seventy in 25:11–12 became the lifeline of hope sustaining Jewish exiles for decades. Jeremiah himself repeats it in his letter to Babylon promising God will bring them home (29:10). Near the close of exile, Daniel opens "the books" of Jeremiah, sees the seventy years nearing completion, and falls to his knees in national repentance (Daniel 9:2). Chronicles notes that those seventy desolate years gave the land the Sabbath rests Judah had neglected (2 Chronicles 36:21; cf. Leviticus 26:34–35), and Ezra opens with King Cyrus of Persia issuing the decree to return home "to fulfill the word of the LORD spoken by Jeremiah" (Ezra 1:1; cf. Zechariah 1:12).

**The cup of wrath stretching from the Old Testament to Gethsemane and Revelation.** The cup brimming with the wine of God's wrath handed to the wicked and the nations is one of Scripture's grandest recurring symbols (Psalms 75:8; Isaiah 51:17). At the close of the Bible, Revelation echoes both the cup of wrath poured on Babylon and the silencing of millstones, lamps, and bridegroom and bride inside the fallen city (Revelation 14:10; 18:22–23). Yet the most breathtaking turning point occurs in the Gospels: in the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus prayed, "Let this cup pass from Me," and then willingly took and drained the cup of judgment on the cross in humanity's place (Matthew 26:39). Because He drank that cup for us, the voices of joy and of bridegroom and bride can ring out once more (33:11).

**Judgment "beginning with the household of God" in the New Testament.** The incisive logic of 25:29—if calamity strikes the city bearing God's name first, how can other nations escape?—reappears when Ezekiel sees judgment begin right inside God's sanctuary (Ezekiel 9:6). Centuries later, the apostle Peter applies that exact principle to encourage a suffering Church: "it is time for judgment to begin with the family of God; and if it begins with us, what will the outcome be for those who disobey the gospel of God?" (1 Peter 4:17).

**God deploying foreign kings as historical instruments.** Calling Nebuchadnezzar "My servant" (25:9; 27:6; 43:10) parallels Isaiah calling King Cyrus of Persia "My shepherd" and "His anointed" (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1). Neither title turns a pagan emperor into a saint; both declare boldly that every earthly throne serves the purposes of the Most High. Meanwhile, the roaring lion and grape-treading imagery of 25:30 echoes prophets before and after Jeremiah, including Amos and Isaiah (Amos 1:2; Isaiah 63:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Calling Nebuchadnezzar "My servant" does not mean he was a godly worshiper.** In ancient Near Eastern diplomacy, a great king's "servant" meant a vassal or agent executing a ruler's decree. God gives Nebuchadnezzar that title to declare he is simply performing a time-limited assignment (25:9), not to praise his morals or faith. Proof appears three verses later when God vows to punish Nebuchadnezzar and Babylon for their own wickedness (25:12–14).

**How are the seventy years measured?** Scholars and readers have long noted two major historical reckonings, both remarkably close to seventy: counting from 605 BC (the year of this prophecy, when Nebuchadnezzar asserted dominance over Judah and took early captives like Daniel) to 538 BC (when Cyrus toppled Babylon and issued the decree of return) spans 67–68 years; counting from 586 BC (the burning of the Jerusalem temple) to 516 BC (the completion and dedication of the second temple) spans exactly 70 years. Many scholars also note that in ancient Near Eastern idiom, seventy years represented a full human lifespan—long enough for a rebellious generation to pass away and a fresh generation to rise.

**"Sheshach" in 25:26 is a cipher for Babylon.** Readers naturally wonder where on earth Sheshach lies on the map. Commentators ancient and modern recognize an ancient Hebrew letter-substitution code called *Atbash*: swapping the first letter of the alphabet with the last, the second with the second-to-last, and so on. Applying *Atbash* to *Babel* (Babylon) spells *Sheshach*, a code appearing once more near the book's end (51:41). Jeremiah may have used the code either to avoid naming the reigning superpower directly or to land a dramatic surprise punchline at the tail of the cup-list.

**The jarring violence and unburied corpses of 25:30–38.** Picturing God roaring, treading grapes, and leaving the slain scattered "like dung on the ground" unburied (25:33) weighs heavily on modern readers. Reading it within its genre of ancient prophetic war-judgment poetry helps keep perspective: it never celebrates cruelty, but strips away all romantic illusions to expose the raw horror unleashed when arrogant empires and exploitative "shepherds" drive the world toward ruin under God's righteous reckoning. Both halves of the chapter must stay held together: judgment is terrifyingly real, yet it arrives only after twenty-three patient years of pleading and still leaves open restoration after seventy years.

**Different chapter ordering between the Hebrew text and the ancient Greek Septuagint right here in chapter 25.** English Bibles, following the Hebrew Masoretic Text, place the full oracles against the nations near the end of the book (chapters 46–51). In the ancient Greek Septuagint, however, that entire block of foreign-nation oracles sits right in the middle of chapter 25, immediately following 25:13 ("all that Jeremiah prophesied against all the nations"), before verse 15 resumes with the cup of wrath. The Greek edition is also slightly shorter, omitting phrases like "My servant Nebuchadnezzar" and "Sheshach." This difference shows Jeremiah circulated in two early editions, with 25:13 functioning as the structural hinge joining the oracles on Judah to the oracles on the nations.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Bible, was one of the earliest Christian writers to unpack the riddle of "Sheshach" in 25:26. Showing Western readers how reversing the Hebrew alphabet turns *Babel* into *Sheshach*, he explained that Jeremiah used the coded name to avoid provoking immediate Babylonian retaliation against Jews living under their rule. **The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic Jewish repository of legal and scriptural discussion, preserves a celebrated debate over Jeremiah's seventy-year prophecy. The rabbis relate that both King Belshazzar of Babylon and King Ahasuerus of Persia tried calculating the seventy years to see whether Jewish restoration had expired, yet both miscalculated the starting point and threw premature banquets using Jerusalem's sacred vessels; only Daniel later matched the exact count from the temple's destruction. The story illustrates how ancient Jews clung to chapter 25's seventy-year pledge as their lifeline through exile. *(Babylonian Talmud, Tractate Megillah, opening chapter)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the discussion calculating Jeremiah's seventy years involving Belshazzar, Ahasuerus, and Daniel is a classic passage in Megillah 11b–12a -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pauses deeply over God calling Nebuchadnezzar "My servant" (25:9). The title offers zero endorsement of a ruthless king's piety; rather, it declares God's absolute sovereignty—even when wicked rulers act out of greed and arrogance, God guides their hand as His rod of discipline and after seventy years judges them for their own malice. For Calvin, Jeremiah's twenty-three years of preaching with no visible success (25:3) also stands as a permanent model of steady faithfulness for every minister of God's word. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's characteristic exposition of divine sovereignty over wicked instruments in 25:9 and Jeremiah's 23-year perseverance in 25:3, though not verified verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, highlights the dramatic contrast between twenty-three years of divine patience and the inescapable judgment closing the chapter. Not only does God discipline Judah's sins, He sets an exact seventy-year cap so affliction never swallows up the hope of the faithful. On the cup of wrath starting at Jerusalem (25:29), Henry warns that heightened privilege brings heightened responsibility: bearing God's name never shields a hardened heart. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral insights on divine patience, the seventy-year limit, and 25:29, though not checked verbatim -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, views chapter 25 as the dramatic pivot of the entire book of Jeremiah, setting little Judah's crisis onto the stage of global geopolitics. The cup of wrath simultaneously punctures Jerusalem's self-assurance and demystifies the Babylonian Empire by placing Sheshach at the end of the line to drink judgment too. For Brueggemann, the chapter's deepest message is that no empire or power system on earth is ultimate; every regime holds a limited lease and answers before the courtroom of the LORD. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 25)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Jeremiah 25 as the macro-political pivot both judging Judah and limiting Babylonian imperial power is a consistent hallmark of Brueggemann's commentary -->
