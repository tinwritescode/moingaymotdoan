# Jeremiah 31 — Everlasting Love and the New Covenant

## Story

Carrying straight forward from the promise closing chapter 30, the LORD opens chapter 31 with an embrace spanning the entire nation: "At that time, I will be the God of all the clans of Israel, and they will be My people." Survivors who escaped the sword will find grace right out in the wilderness, just like their ancestors leaving Egypt. From afar the LORD appears speaking one of the tenderest lines in the entire Old Testament: "I have loved you with an everlasting love; therefore I have drawn you with loving devotion." He vows to rebuild "Virgin Israel": once more she will pick up her tambourines and join joyful dances, once more she will plant vineyards on the hills of Samaria and enjoy their fruit herself. A day is coming when watchmen on the hills of Ephraim—the northern kingdom broken off and fallen over a century earlier—will call out, "Arise, let us go up to Zion, to the LORD our God!" (31:1–6).

The oracle bursts into jubilant shouting inviting every nation to listen. God will bring Israel's remnant home from the land of the north and the farthest ends of the earth in a vast company. Astonishingly, look who walks right inside that caravan: "the blind and the lame, expectant mothers and women in labor"—the frailest travelers ordinarily left behind on any brutal march. They return weeping and praying, while God personally leads them beside streams of water along a smooth, level road where none will stumble, "for I am a father to Israel, and Ephraim is My firstborn." The One who scattered Israel now gathers and watches over His flock like a shepherd, ransoming Jacob from hands too strong for him. They will shout joyfully atop Mount Zion, radiating delight over grain, new wine, oil, and young flocks; their souls will bloom like a well-watered garden. Young women will dance for joy alongside young men and elders alike, for God turns their mourning into gladness, satisfying priests and people alike with His bounty (31:7–14).

Suddenly the music drops into heart-wrenching weeping. "A voice is heard in Ramah, mourning and great weeping, Rachel weeping for her children and refusing to be comforted, because they are no more!" Rachel—ancestral mother of the northern tribes and Benjamin—rises in imagination from her roadside grave mourning her children marched off to exile. Yet the LORD comforts her gently: "Restrain your voice from weeping and your eyes from tears," for your work will be rewarded, there is hope for your future, and "your children will return to their own land" (31:15–17).

Next, God overhears the penitent groaning of Ephraim himself—standing for the northern kingdom. Ephraim admits he was disciplined like an untrained calf kicking against the yoke, crying out: "Restore me, and I will return, for You are the LORD my God!" Coming to his senses, he strikes his thigh in remorse and blushes for the disgrace of his youth. Hearing His turning child, the Father's heart melts completely: "Is not Ephraim a precious son to Me, a delightful child? Though I often speak against him, I still remember him. Therefore My heart yearns for him; I have great compassion for him." God urges His wandering daughter to set up roadmarks and guideposts along the highway home, for He has created something brand new on earth: "a woman will shelter a man" (31:18–22).

The vision of renewal widens southward into Judah. When God restores their fortunes, towns across Judah will bless Jerusalem once more: "May the LORD bless you, O righteous dwelling, O holy mountain!" Farmers and wandering shepherds will dwell side by side in peace, and every weary, sorrowful soul will be refreshed and satisfied. Abruptly Jeremiah drops a brief personal note right into the text: "At this I awoke and looked around, and my sleep had been most pleasant to me." Amid grim years of siege and tears, this vision of hope washed over him like a rare, sweet night's rest (31:23–26).

Starting at verse 27, four cascading promises opening with "Behold, the days are coming" lift the horizon into an utterly new age. First, God will sow both human seed and livestock across the houses of Israel and Judah; just as He watched over them to uproot, tear down, overthrow, and destroy because of sin, so now He will watch over them to build and to plant. At the same time, the bitter proverb exiles loved repeating—"The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge"—will vanish forever; each person will answer only for his own sin (31:27–30).

Next arrives the towering summit of the entire book of Jeremiah: the promise of a New Covenant. The LORD declares He will forge "a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah." It will differ fundamentally from the covenant He made when He took their ancestors by the hand out of Egypt—a covenant they shattered even though He was a faithful husband to them. In this new covenant, God heals humanity's hard heart from the inside out: "I will put My law in their minds and inscribe it on their hearts. And I will be their God, and they will be My people." No longer will neighbors or relatives have to lecture one another saying "Know the LORD!"—from the least to the greatest, every single person will know Him directly, because the bedrock of this covenant is complete pardon: "For I will forgive their iniquity and will remember their sins no more" (31:31–34).

Two closing paragraphs stamp an unbreakable seal across every pledge. First, the LORD of Hosts—who appoints the sun for daytime light, sets moon and stars by night, and stirs the roaring sea—swears by cosmic order itself: only if those fixed laws vanish, or if someone manages to measure the heavens above and fathom earth's foundations below, could Israel's offspring ever cease being a nation before Him or be cast off for all they have done (31:35–37). Finally, Jerusalem is surveyed and rebuilt landmark by landmark, from the Tower of Hananel to the Corner Gate, out past the hill of Gareb around to Goah; even "the whole valley of dead bodies and ashes" and every terrace out to the Kidron Valley and the corner of the Horse Gate on the east will become sacred to the LORD—never again to be uprooted or demolished forever (31:38–40).

## Key Takeaways

**God's love for His people rests on eternity, not on human track records.** Even while disciplining rebellious Ephraim, He calls him a "precious son," feels His heart ache with compassion (31:20), and draws His people with steadfast kindness (31:3). When people feel too broken to climb back on their own and whisper "Restore me, and I will return" (31:18), God takes the first step to open the door.

**God leaves none of the vulnerable behind on the journey home.** On any grueling migration, the blind, the lame, pregnant mothers, and women giving birth would be treated as liabilities left behind. Yet God places those very travelers right at the heart of the returning crowd and personally leads them along flat, watered roads (31:8–9). Divine power shines brightest carrying the most fragile.

**Humanity's core problem was never defective laws, but hearts unable to keep good laws.** The Sinai covenant cracked not because God's commands were flawed, but because unfaithful hearts broke trust even with a faithful Husband (31:32). Genuine transformation requires God's law moving from stone outside into the heart inside—rooted in total forgiveness that washes away past guilt (31:33–34).

**God's promises outlast every historical despair.** Binding His covenant loyalty to sun, moon, stars, and ocean waves (31:35–37), God affirms that His people's survival rests never on shaky human endurance, but on the unshakable power and oath of the Creator.

## Deeper Meaning

**The New Covenant giving its very name and core to the New Testament.** The phrase "new covenant" in 31:31 appears here for the only time in the entire Old Testament. Centuries later, on the night before His crucifixion, Jesus lifted the cup and declared: "This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is poured out for you" (Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25). Where Moses once sprinkled animal blood to ratify the old covenant engraved on stone tablets (Exodus 24:7–8), Jesus shed His own blood to inaugurate the new covenant Jeremiah foretold.

**From stone tablets outside to living hearts written by the Spirit.** Earlier Jeremiah mourned that Judah's sin was "engraved with a diamond point on the tablet of their hearts" (17:1). Here in chapter 31, God vows to inscribe His own law onto those very hearts (31:33), meshing seamlessly with Ezekiel's contemporary promise that God will replace hearts of stone with hearts of flesh and place His Spirit within His people to empower obedience (Ezekiel 36:26–27). The apostle Paul expands this triumphantly: believers are a letter written not with ink on stone, but by the Spirit of the living God on tablets of human hearts—ministers of a new covenant bringing life rather than a letter that condemns (2 Corinthians 3:3; 3:6).

**The longest Old Testament quotation in the entire New Testament.** The letter to the Hebrews quotes all four verses of 31:31–34 in full—the longest Old Testament citation anywhere in the New Testament—and repeats its climax again in chapter 10 (Hebrews 8:8–13; 10:16–17). For Hebrews, God's very announcement of a new covenant proves the old animal-sacrifice system was a temporary shadow incapable of cleansing consciences; only through Jesus' once-for-all sacrifice is the promise "I will remember their sins no more" completely fulfilled.

**Rachel's tears linking exile to the night of Bethlehem.** Rachel weeping in Ramah (31:15) is cited by Matthew's Gospel when King Herod slaughters Bethlehem's baby boys trying to kill the infant Jesus (Matthew 2:17–18). Reading Matthew with Jeremiah 31 open in full reveals that grief is never the final verse: right behind Rachel's tears comes God's promise that "your children will return to their own land" (31:16–17). Likewise in Matthew, right amid Bethlehem's agony, the Savior escapes to Egypt and returns home to end humanity's deepest exile.

**Jeremiah's six commissioning verbs coming full circle.** On the day God called him, Jeremiah received six verbs: four of uprooting, tearing down, destroying, and overthrowing, plus two of building and planting (1:10). Across nearly thirty opening chapters, the four tear-drenched verbs of demolition dominated his preaching. In 31:28, all six verbs reunite to mark salvation history's grand hinge: the exact same vigilance that once watched over tearing down now watches faithfully over building and planting anew.

**A Father's yearning heart triumphing over anger.** God's tender soliloquy over Ephraim in 31:20—still remembering him fondly whenever He speaks against him, His heart yearning with unstoppable mercy—beats in unison with Hosea's cry: "How can I give you up, O Ephraim?... My heart is turned within Me; all My compassion is aroused!" (Hosea 11:8–9). Divine justice is never icy abstraction; behind it beats the aching heart of a Father.

## Notes & Nuances

**The intriguing line in 31:22: "a woman will shelter a man."** This verse ranks among Jeremiah's most debated lines because the Hebrew verb carries multiple nuances (*surround*, *shelter*, *court*, *protect*). The most widely held view connects it directly with "faithless daughter" opening the verse: once unfaithful Israel fled her Husband, yet now God creates an upside-down wonder where the bride eagerly seeks, encircles, and cherishes her Husband, the LORD. Another reading hears a military reversal: peace will run so deep that even a vulnerable woman can shield a warrior without fear. Several early Church Fathers also saw a typological hint of the Virgin Mary carrying the Messiah inside her womb, though the immediate context speaks directly of Israel returning to God.

**The "sour grapes" proverb (31:29–30) and Exodus 20:5.** Exiles frequently quoted "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" to complain that they were innocent victims paying for their ancestors' failures—the exact proverb Ezekiel forcefully refuted over in Babylon (Ezekiel 18:2–4). Jeremiah neither denies that earlier generations' sins inflict painful fallout on descendants nor contradicts Exodus 20:5 (which speaks of consecutive generations hating God). Rather, the promise dismantles fatalistic blame-shifting: in the restored age, no one sits trapped under ancestral doom; every person stands directly accountable before God.

**Why is Rachel weeping at Ramah (31:15)?** Rachel was mother both to Joseph—ancestor of Ephraim and Manasseh, flagship tribes of the northern kingdom—and to Benjamin, whose territory included Ramah roughly five miles north of Jerusalem. While Genesis locates Rachel's grave along the road toward Bethlehem (Genesis 35:19), another early tradition places it near Benjamin's border beside Ramah (1 Samuel 10:2). Tragically, Ramah later became the exact staging ground where Babylonian troops assembled Judean captives—including Jeremiah himself—before marching them into exile (40:1). Thus one grieving mother's voice embraces both the north's fall in 722 BC and Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC.

**The New Covenant and Israel's enduring calling.** Reading 31:31–37 carefully makes one fact unmistakable: the New Covenant is pledged explicitly to "the house of Israel and the house of Judah," immediately sealed by God's cosmic oath that Israel's offspring will never vanish from His sight. For Jewish readers, this stands as an eternal guarantee of God's enduring covenant with their people. Among Christian interpreters, perspectives vary on how these promises unfold: many emphasize that through Jesus the New Covenant opens wide to graft Gentiles into God's one people, while—as the apostle Paul reminds the Church plainly in Romans—God has never rejected Israel, for His gifts and His calling are irrevocable (Romans 11:26–29).

**Jerusalem's vilest dump made holy.** Mentioning "the whole valley of dead bodies and ashes" in 31:40 points straight to the Hinnom Valley (Topheth) south of Jerusalem, where Judah committed the horror of burning children to idols and Jeremiah dubbed it "the Valley of Slaughter" (7:31–32; 19:2). The closing note of chapter 31 is stunning: God's redeeming grace does not merely renovate the clean temple mount—it claims the city's darkest stain, cleanses it completely, and consecrates it forever to the LORD.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, built his landmark treatise refuting the claim that humans can keep God's law by unaided willpower squarely upon the New Covenant of Jeremiah 31:31–34. For Augustine, old and new covenants do not differ in moral standards, but in power: the old covenant carved commands externally on stone tablets, demanding righteousness and condemning violators without supplying strength; in the new covenant, God pours love into human hearts through the Holy Spirit, inscribing His law internally so people delight in doing good freely. *(On the Spirit and the Letter, Chapters 19–25)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine anchoring De Spiritu et Littera against Pelagius on Jeremiah 31:31–34 paired with 2 Corinthians 3:6 is one of his most celebrated theological arguments; exact chapter range in English editions not checked -->

**Midrash Genesis Rabbah**, the classic fifth-century Jewish commentary, links Rachel weeping in 31:15–17 back to Jacob burying his beloved wife beside the road rather than inside the family cave at Machpelah. The rabbis teach that Jacob foresaw the day Babylonian armies would march his exiled descendants right past that road; he intentionally buried Rachel along the route so that in the hour of disaster she would rise from her tomb weeping and pleading for her children, moving God's compassion to pledge their return home. *(Genesis Rabbah, on Genesis 35)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall the Genesis Rabbah tradition (around section 82) linking Rachel's roadside burial with Jeremiah 31:15–17, though exact section number was not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, examines Ephraim's prayer in 31:18—"Restore me, and I will return"—to demonstrate that even genuine repentance is not self-generated human achievement, but a gift God grants beforehand. Expounding the New Covenant in 31:31–34, Calvin maintains it does not cancel the gracious substance of the Old Covenant, yet far surpasses it in clarity, inward vitality, and full pardon manifested in Christ. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 2, Chapter 11; Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 31)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's comparison of the two covenants in Institutes 2.11 and his grace-centered exposition of Jeremiah 31:18 are famous and widely cited -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, celebrates 31:3—"I have loved you with an everlasting love; therefore I have drawn you with loving devotion"—as the finest portrait of salvation's source: eternal love inside God's heart is the hidden spring, while daily acts of patient kindness pulling us close are the stream flowing from it. At God's melting compassion over Ephraim in 31:20, Henry remarks that the faintest penitent sigh from a turning child instantly touches the deepest tenderness of our heavenly Father. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 31)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the spring-and-stream metaphor on verse 3 and pastoral reflection on fatherly compassion in verse 20 match Matthew Henry's characteristic exposition, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, spotlights the raw yet tender emotion packed into 31:20: the Hebrew literally describes God's innermost depths churning with yearning for Ephraim, revealing a God who refuses stiff pride and lets parental love overpower anger. On the New Covenant (31:31–34), Brueggemann stresses that its novelty lies neither in discarding Torah nor erasing Israel, but in God's decisive act of forgiveness ("I will remember their sins no more") healing human hearts at the root so obedience springs freely from within. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 31)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing God's churning compassion in verse 20 and forgiveness as the foundation of the New Covenant in 31:31–34 are famous centerpieces of Brueggemann's commentary -->

**Derek Kidner**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, pauses warmly over the passenger list of returning exiles in 31:8—the blind, the lame, pregnant women, and mothers in labor—noting that the exact group any earthly commander would write off as dead weight is tenderly gathered right into the heart of God's homecoming column. He also stresses that the closing clause of the New Covenant in 31:34 ("I will forgive their iniquity and will remember their sins no more") is the master key unlocking every preceding promise: without complete pardon, no human can truly know God or carry His law inside the heart. *(The Message of Jeremiah, 1987, on chapter 31)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Kidner's comments on the vulnerable returnees of verse 8 and forgiveness as the linchpin of verse 34, though exact pages were not verified -->
