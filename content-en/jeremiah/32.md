# Jeremiah 32 — Buying a Field Under Siege

## Story

Right after the shining promises of the New Covenant in chapters 30 and 31, the narrative yanks readers straight back into the suffocating grip of Jerusalem's final siege. In the tenth year of King Zedekiah of Judah—the eighteenth year of King Nebuchadnezzar—the Babylonian army rings Jerusalem tight. At that moment Jeremiah sits confined in the courtyard of the guard inside the royal palace. King Zedekiah himself locked him up for daring to prophesy that the LORD is handing this city over to the king of Babylon, that Zedekiah will never escape the Chaldeans but will confront Babylon's king "face to face and see him eye to eye," and that he will be carried off to Babylon until God attends to him—no matter how fiercely Judah fights, they will not succeed (32:1–5).

Sitting under palace arrest inside a doomed city, Jeremiah receives an extraordinary advance notice from the LORD: his cousin Hanamel son of his uncle Shallum is coming to offer him his field at Anathoth, since Jeremiah holds the family right of redemption. Just as God said, Hanamel walks right into the courtyard of the guard pleading with Jeremiah to buy his field at Anathoth in Benjamin, where Jeremiah stands next in line to redeem and inherit it. Watching the scene unfold down to the exact detail, Jeremiah recognizes unmistakably that this is the word of the LORD (32:6–8).

Even though Anathoth lies just north of Jerusalem and is already overrun by Babylonian forces, Jeremiah handles the land purchase with full peacetime precision. He weighs out seventeen shekels of silver on scales to pay his cousin, draws up the legal deed in duplicate according to statute and custom—one copy sealed and one left unsealed—and has witnesses sign properly. Right in front of Hanamel, the witnesses, and every Judean sitting around the guard courtyard, he formally hands the deeds to his scribe Baruch son of Neriah. Publicly announcing the word of the LORD of Hosts, he directs Baruch to seal both deeds safely inside a clay jar so they last for years to come, because God has issued a ringing promise straight into despair: "Houses, fields, and vineyards will again be bought in this land" (32:9–15).

Only after finishing every legal detail in public does Jeremiah turn privately to pour out his heart to the LORD. He opens his prayer anchored on soaring confidence in the Creator: You made heaven and earth by Your great power and outstretched arm, so "nothing is too difficult for You!" You are the great and mighty God whose name is the LORD of Hosts, showing steadfast love to thousands while keeping eyes open on all human ways to repay each according to their deeds. You rescued Israel from Egypt with signs and wonders and brought them into this land flowing with milk and honey. Yet right after recounting God's faithfulness, Jeremiah lays stark reality straight before Him: the people refused to obey so You brought disaster; enemy siege ramps already reach the city walls, and by sword, famine, and plague the city is practically in Chaldean hands already. He ends with an exhale mixing obedience and utter bewilderment: You just told me to spend silver buying a field with signed witnesses, "even though the city has been delivered into the hands of the Chaldeans!" (32:16–25).

The LORD answers instantly, tossing Jeremiah's own opening confession right back as a question: "Behold, I am the LORD, the God of all flesh. Is anything too difficult for Me?" God neither sugarcoats reality nor cancels the sentence: yes, He is handing this city over to Nebuchadnezzar; the Chaldeans will march in and torch Jerusalem, burning down the very rooftops where residents burned incense to Baal and poured drink offerings to foreign gods. From youth onward, Israel and Judah alike—kings, officials, priests, prophets, and ordinary citizens—have done nothing but provoke Him. Instead of turning their faces toward Him, they turned their backs, despite His tireless, repeated warnings. Worst of all, they planted detestable idols inside the house bearing His name and built Baal high places down in the Valley of Ben-hinnom to pass their sons and daughters through fire to Molech—an abomination God never commanded and never once let enter His mind (32:26–35).

Yet just as judgment seems to lock every exit, God's word pivots completely. Concerning this very city people are writing off as lost to sword, famine, and plague, the LORD declares He will regather His people from all the lands where His anger drove them, bring them back right here, and let them live in safety. The covenant vow rings out again: "They will be My people, and I will be their God." He will give them "one heart and one way" to fear Him forever for their own good and the good of their children after them. He will forge an everlasting covenant guaranteeing both sides: He will never stop doing good to them, and He will place the fear of Him deep inside their hearts so they never turn away again. Then God utters one of the most passionate sentences in all of Scripture: "Yes, I will rejoice in doing them good, and I will faithfully plant them in this land with all My heart and with all My soul" (32:36–41).

The chapter closes by stamping God's full guarantee onto Jeremiah's newly purchased field. Just as surely as the LORD brought all this great disaster upon this people, so surely will He bring upon them every single good thing He has promised. Across the very countryside dismissed today as an empty wasteland handed over to Babylon without man or beast, people will once more weigh silver for fields, sign and seal deeds before witnesses throughout Benjamin, the villages around Jerusalem, the towns of Judah, the hill country, the foothills, and the Negev—for the LORD will surely restore their captives (32:42–44).

## Key Takeaways

**Real faith does not blink away catastrophe—it puts real life and real money into God's promised future.** Jeremiah held zero illusions that Jerusalem would survive; he was sitting in prison precisely for predicting its fall (32:3–5). Yet when God told him to buy land while enemy ramps crept up the walls, he weighed out silver, signed deeds, lined up witnesses, and sealed them in a clay jar (32:9–14). Biblical hope is never naive denial; it is tangible action taken inside total darkness based solely on God's word.

**Bringing honest perplexity to God after obeying is not unbelief.** Jeremiah carried out God's command completely in public before stepping aside to voice his confusion privately (32:16–25). Notice how he prays: before raising the paradox staring him in the face, he recalls God's creating power and saving history to frame his doubts rightly. God never scolds his honest question; He answers it with an even grander promise.

**God's faithfulness in judgment proves His faithfulness in salvation.** Verse 32:42 pairs both truths evenly: the exact same God who kept every solemn word of judgment will keep every gracious word of restoration. God neither bluffs when warning against sin nor exaggerates when promising homecoming.

**The everlasting covenant holds because God Himself secures both sides.** Earlier covenants cracked because fickle human hearts wandered off. Here God underwrites both halves: He refuses ever to turn away from blessing His people, and He plants holy reverence inside their hearts so they will never walk away from Him again (32:40). Even more astonishingly, He does this not out of reluctant duty, but "rejoicing" to plant them back "with all My heart and with all My soul" (32:41).

## Deeper Meaning

**"Is anything too difficult for Me?" connecting three landmarks of salvation history.** When Jeremiah confesses "nothing is too difficult for You" and God echoes back "Is anything too difficult for Me?" (32:17; 32:27), Scripture immediately rings back a thousand years to Abraham's tent when aged Sarah laughed at bearing a child: "Is anything too difficult for the LORD?" (Genesis 18:14). Centuries after Jeremiah, the angel Gabriel repeats that very bedrock to young Mary announcing the Savior's birth: "For nothing will be impossible with God" (Luke 1:37; cf. Matthew 19:26). Whenever human history hits a brick wall—a barren womb, a burning city, or humanity trapped in sin—the Creator's power opens a new beginning.

**The field at Anathoth echoing Abraham's cave at Machpelah and the New Testament "guarantee."** Long ago God promised Abraham all of Canaan, yet during his entire life he owned only one small parcel: the field and cave of Machpelah weighed out in silver before Hittite witnesses as a burial site for Sarah (Genesis 23:16–20). That modest plot stood as faith's down payment on promises for future generations. Over a millennium later, right as the nation stood to lose everything, Jeremiah weighed silver before witnesses for a plot at Anathoth (32:9–15). In the New Testament, Paul borrows a closely related legal image for the Holy Spirit: He is the seal and "pledge of our inheritance" (Ephesians 1:13–14)—just like sealed deeds resting safely inside an earthenware jar guaranteeing God's promised inheritance truly belongs to His people.

**The kinsman-redeemer pointing toward our Redeemer.** Hanamel approached Jeremiah because under Moses' law, land belonged to God and should never pass permanently outside the clan; when poverty forced someone to sell, the nearest relative held the right and duty to redeem it (Leviticus 25:23–28). That same law anchors the touching story of Boaz redeeming Naomi's field and marrying Ruth at the city gate (Ruth 4:1–6). Paying silver to redeem Anathoth's field under enemy siege casts Jeremiah as a living miniature of God Himself—our Redeemer willing to pay the price to buy back His people's lost heritage.

**"With all My heart and with all My soul"—God answering His own greatest commandment.** Moses' highest command called Israel to love the LORD "with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your strength" (Deuteronomy 6:5)—something history proved they could never manage unless God first transformed their hearts (Deuteronomy 30:6). Amazingly, in 32:41—the sole place in the entire Bible—God applies that exact phrase to Himself: He will plant them in the land "with all My heart and with all My soul." Before humans can ever love God wholeheartedly, God commits His whole heart to loving and keeping us.

**The Everlasting Covenant deepening the New Covenant.** Reading chapter 32 beside chapter 31 and contemporary Ezekiel fills out the full canvas of renewal. Where chapter 31 highlights pardoning sin and inscribing law on the heart (31:31–34), chapter 32 adds giving them "one heart and one way" and sealing an "everlasting covenant" planting enduring reverence inside them (32:38–40). That harmonizes seamlessly with Ezekiel in Babylon foretelling "one heart," replacing stone with flesh, and forging an everlasting "covenant of peace" (Ezekiel 11:19–20; 37:26).

## Notes & Nuances

**The field sat in Anathoth—the hometown that plotted Jeremiah's murder—and a Levitical town.** Jeremiah belonged to the priestly clan of Anathoth a few miles northeast of Jerusalem (1:1; Joshua 21:18), the exact village whose residents once schemed to kill him to silence his preaching (11:21–23). Yet when God commanded, he willingly put up silver to redeem his cousin's plot right there. Careful readers sometimes wonder how land could be bought when Leviticus 25:32–34 bars selling common Levitical pastureland. Most scholars note that Leviticus barred alienating Levitical property outside the tribe, whereas this transfer kept the land inside the same priestly family—or involved private family arable plots held within Benjamin's territory.

**Why two copies of the deed sealed inside a clay jar?** Executing legal contracts in duplicate—one tied and wax-sealed, one left open (32:11–14)—was standard ancient Near Eastern law. The open copy allowed everyday consultation, while the sealed master copy remained untouched unless a court dispute required verifying nobody had tampered with the text. Storing papyrus or parchment scrolls inside pottery jars guarded them against moisture and insects across decades of exile—the exact preservation method that later kept the Dead Sea Scrolls intact for two thousand years in desert caves.

**The grim fulfillment on King Zedekiah and the atrocity of Molech worship.** Two stark details merit honest, clear-eyed attention. First, the warning that Zedekiah would meet Babylon's king "eye to eye" and be taken to Babylon (32:4–5) unfolded with brutal precision when Jerusalem fell: Chaldean troops caught the fleeing king, brought him to Riblah, slaughtered his sons before his eyes, then blinded him and dragged him in chains to Babylon until death (39:5–7; 52:8–11; 2 Kings 25:6–7). Second, reading God's fiery anger handing the city to flames in 32:28–35 requires noticing the horror explicitly cited in 32:35: Judah made "their sons and daughters pass through the fire to Molech" down in the Hinnom Valley—a barbaric child-sacrifice rite strictly outlawed since Moses (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 18:10; Jeremiah 7:31). Judgment here is no arbitrary rage, but God's righteous stand against a culture so depraved it burned its own children in religion's name.

**Ancestral consequences (32:18) standing right beside individual accountability (32:19).** Inside his prayer, Jeremiah quotes both God's classic self-revelation repaying "the iniquity of the fathers into the laps of their children after them" (32:18; cf. Exodus 34:6–7) and immediately affirms that God watches to "reward each person according to his ways and according to the fruit of his deeds" (32:19). Readers fresh from chapter 31 (31:29–30) might wonder how those sit together. In reality they never cancel each other: earlier generations' sins do cast heavy historical shadows that children suffer through during war and exile, yet morally before God nobody is condemned unfairly for another's choices—each answers for their own path.

**Was seventeen shekels of silver cheap or costly?** Seventeen shekels weighed roughly seven ounces of silver—considerably below normal peacetime farmland prices. Many scholars view that low figure as reflecting wartime collapse while Babylonian troops occupied the area; others note it may have been a modest parcel or priced by harvests remaining until the Year of Jubilee under Leviticus 25. Whatever the market value, the story's heart lies not in bargain hunting, but in Jeremiah sinking real silver into land worth zero by every earthly calculation and sealing its deed for future generations.

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, treats God's pledge in 32:40—"I will put the fear of Me in their hearts, so that they will never turn away from Me"—as a linchpin verse on the perseverance of believers. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, admires the order of Jeremiah's actions: the prophet does not wait until every puzzle makes sense before obeying—he buys the field publicly first, and only afterward unloads his confusion in prayer. Calvin also highlights how Jeremiah opens his prayer by celebrating God's creating power in 32:17, steadying his faith before staring straight at perplexing circumstances. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, observes that spending silver on real estate while imprisoned offers the strongest proof Jeremiah genuinely believed his own preaching—trusting promises of return every bit as firmly as warnings of ruin. Henry also notes Jeremiah's meticulous care weighing silver, drawing contracts, and calling witnesses (32:9–12), reminding believers that trusting God never excuses sloppiness in financial transparency. **Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads buying the field at Anathoth as a defiant act of hope right when every military and political indicator signed Judah's death warrant. Putting real money into occupied soil under Babylonian swords publicly rejects both imperial arrogance and civic despair, betting tangible resources on a future only God can open. Brueggemann also praises the bracing honesty of Jeremiah's prayer (32:17–25): biblical faith holds both poles together—God's limitless power and siege ramps pressing the walls—without denying either. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 32)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading the Anathoth land purchase as subversive counter-imperial hope and highlighting the tension inside Jeremiah's prayer are hallmark theses of Brueggemann on chapter 32 -->

**Derek Kidner**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, contrasts Jeremiah's crisp composure completing legal business before onlookers with the candid bewilderment he shares only alone with God (32:24–25). He especially spotlights the phrase "with all My heart and with all My soul" in 32:41: the single spot in all of Scripture where language normally summoning humans to wholehearted devotion is turned around to express God's own passionate eagerness in replanting His people home. *(The Message of Jeremiah, 1987, on chapter 32)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Kidner contrasting Jeremiah's public poise with private prayer and highlighting the unique divine "all My heart and soul" in verse 41, though exact pages were not verified -->
