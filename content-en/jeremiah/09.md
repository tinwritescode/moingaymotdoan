# Jeremiah 9 — A Land of Lies and the Only Boast Worth Making

## Story

The unfinished grief at the end of chapter 8 bursts into open sobbing in the very first verse of chapter 9. Jeremiah longs, "Oh, that my head were a spring of water, and my eyes a fountain of tears!" so he might weep day and night for the slain of his people. Yet in the very next breath his heart whips to the opposite extreme: he wishes he had "a lodging place for travelers in the wilderness"—a bare roadside shelter out in the desert—so he could leave his people and walk far away from them, for they are all "adulterers, a band of treacherous men" (9:1–2).

Out of the prophet's exhaustion, the LORD's voice rises to expose a society rotted from its roots by deceit. He pictures them bending their tongues like bows to fire lies; they have grown powerful in the land not by truth, but by marching from one evil to the next while refusing to acknowledge Him. Trust between human beings has completely disintegrated: everyone must beware of their neighbors and dare not trust even their own brothers, for every brother "deceives" and every friend "walks in slander." They do not lie by slip of the tongue; they "have taught their tongues to speak lies" and weary themselves doing wrong. Because they live steeped in deceit, they refuse to know God (9:3–6).

Facing a sickness that has penetrated to the bone, the LORD of Hosts announces He will put them through the furnace: "Behold, I will refine and test them"—like a metalworker melting ore to separate out the dross—for what else can He do with the daughter of His people? Their tongues are "deadly arrows" shooting deceit; with their mouths they speak peace to their neighbors, while inwardly they "set an ambush." God asks squarely: should He not punish and avenge Himself on a nation like this? (9:7–9).

The oracle suddenly drops into a lament over a scorched landscape. Wailing rises for the mountains and the desert pastures burned so bare that no traveler passes through, no livestock is heard, and even birds of the sky and wild beasts have fled. The LORD Himself declares He will turn Jerusalem into heaps of rubble, "a haunt of jackals," and leave the towns of Judah a wasteland with no inhabitants (9:10–11).

Then a searching question rings out across the devastation: who is wise enough to understand this calamity, and to whom has the LORD spoken so they can explain why the whole land lies ruined like an untrodden desert? The LORD gives the blunt answer Himself: because they forsook the law He set before them, refused to obey His voice or walk in it, and instead followed the stubbornness of their own hearts to chase the Baals as their ancestors taught them. Therefore the God of Israel will feed this people "wormwood and give them poisoned water to drink," scatter them among unfamiliar nations that neither they nor their ancestors ever knew, and send the sword chasing after them until they are consumed (9:12–16).

The scene shifts into an eerie funeral rite. The LORD of Hosts orders them to summon "the wailing women"—skilled professional mourners who led ancient funeral dirges—to hurry and raise a lament over the nation until everyone's eyes stream with tears. Already a cry of ruin echoes from Zion because the land is shattered, homes have been torn down, and citizens leave their homeland in disgrace. God addresses the women directly, urging them to hear His word and teach both their daughters and their neighbors a funeral song with a bone-chilling line: "For death has climbed in through our windows; it has entered our fortresses to cut off the children from the streets, the young men from the town squares." Jeremiah is commanded to proclaim the final wartime verdict: corpses will fall across the countryside "like dung on the open field," like cut grain left scattered behind the reaper with no one to gather it up (9:17–22).

Chapter 9 closes with two brief declarations that land like twin hammer blows shattering every false human security. First, the LORD sweeps aside the three boasts people have prized across every generation: "Let not the wise man boast in his wisdom, nor the strong man in his strength, nor the rich man in his riches." Whoever wants to boast must boast in this alone—that they understand and know God, "that I am the LORD, who exercises loving devotion, justice, and righteousness on the earth—for I delight in these things" (9:23–24). Immediately after, He shatters outward religious pride as well: the day of judgment is coming when He will punish all who are "circumcised only in the flesh," lining Judah right up alongside neighboring pagan nations—Egypt, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and the desert tribes who "clip the hair of their temples." All those nations are uncircumcised, and the whole house of Israel, despite wearing the physical mark, is "uncircumcised in heart" (9:25–26).

## Key Takeaways

**Loving a broken community brings genuine inner tension.** Within two opening verses, Jeremiah longs both for eyes that become a fountain of tears weeping over his people and for a lonely desert shelter far away from them (9:1–2). Serving God does not turn anyone into unfeeling stone; deep compassion and sheer exhaustion often live side by side inside the same heart.

**When lying becomes a way of life, society unravels from the inside out.** Jeremiah is not merely cataloguing street criminals; he portrays a culture where everyone has "taught their tongues to speak lies," speaking peace aloud while laying traps in their hearts (9:5; 9:8). Once brothers cannot trust brothers and neighbors must guard against neighbors (9:4), a nation has already rotted hollow before any Babylonian soldier arrives.

**Not knowing God and living deceitfully go hand in hand.** Twice in one short span, God links people's deceit directly to the fact that "they do not know Me" (9:3; 9:6). No one can cling to dishonesty and still genuinely know God; a heart practiced at bending truth with neighbors inevitably shuts itself off from the truth of God.

**Cleverness, strength, and wealth are far too fragile to anchor a life.** The three things humanity most loves to flaunt—intellect, power, and money—can evaporate in a single crisis (9:23). The only solid ground worthy of pride is knowing the LORD and reflecting what delights Him: steadfast love, justice, and righteousness right here on earth (9:24).

**Outward religious badges never save an unchanged heart.** Judah trusted that physical circumcision proved they stood worlds apart from pagan nations. God pours ice water on that smugness by listing Judah straight alongside Egypt, Edom, and Moab (9:25–26). When the inside remains hardened, every sacred ritual outside becomes an empty shell.

## Deeper Meaning

**Ceaseless tears and longing to flee into the desert.** Jeremiah's wish that his eyes were a fountain of tears in 9:1 echoes powerfully in the book of Lamentations—traditionally linked with Jeremiah after Jerusalem's fall—where the writer weeps streams of water over the destruction of the daughter of his people (Lamentations 3:48–49). Meanwhile, his urge to escape into the wilderness in 9:2 closely mirrors King David's sigh when betrayed by a close companion inside a city full of treachery: wishing for the wings of a dove to fly far away and lodge in the desert (Psalms 55:6–8; 55:21). Both men knew the bitter loneliness of walking uprightly in a deceitful society.

**A stinging play on the ancestor Jacob's name.** When 9:4 warns that "every brother is a deceiver," the Hebrew original uses a verb from the exact same root as the name *Jacob*—the name his brother Esau bitterly complained about back in Genesis after Jacob cheated him twice (Genesis 27:36). Long ago God transformed Jacob and gave him the new name Israel, yet his descendants have reverted to his old scheming nature, turning the whole nation into a pack of "Jacobs" waiting to trip one another up.

**The refiner's fire and the cup of wormwood.** God's promise to "refine and test them" (9:7) runs across Scripture as an emblem of purifying affliction: in Isaiah, God refines His people in the furnace of affliction (Isaiah 48:10); in Malachi, the coming Lord sits like a silversmith purifying His people (Malachi 3:2–3); and in the New Testament, Peter reminds believers that faith more precious than gold is proved genuine by fire (1 Peter 1:7). Meanwhile, feeding them "wormwood" and "poisoned water" (9:15) recalls the covenant warning about a bitter root yielding poison and wormwood when Israel turns from God (Deuteronomy 29:18), reappears in the verdict against false prophets later in Jeremiah (23:15), and lends its name to the bitter star in Revelation (Revelation 8:10–11).

**The apostle Paul quotes 9:23–24 to redefine true glory.** Verses 9:23–24 rank among the most treasured words in Jeremiah, harmonizing deeply with Micah's summary of what God requires—to act justly, love mercy, and walk humbly (Micah 6:8). In the New Testament, confronting a Corinthian church obsessed with philosophical sophistication, social clout, and eloquence, Paul distills Jeremiah 9:23–24 into a crisp maxim: "Let him who boasts boast in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 1:31; 2 Corinthians 10:17). Under the light of the cross, every human boast of brilliance or muscle is laid low so Christ alone becomes our pride.

**Circumcision of the heart linking Moses to Paul.** The call for circumcised hearts in 9:25–26 was not invented by Jeremiah. Back in Deuteronomy, Moses urged the people to circumcise their stubborn hearts while promising that one day the LORD Himself would do it for them (Deuteronomy 10:16; 30:6), and Jeremiah had already sounded that summons near the opening of his book (4:4). Six centuries later, Paul develops the thought completely in Romans: a true Jew is not defined by an outward mark in the flesh, for "circumcision is circumcision of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the written code" (Romans 2:28–29).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is weeping in 9:10?** Just as at the close of chapter 8, the boundary between Jeremiah's voice and the LORD's voice in 9:10 blurs. Verse 10 says "I will take up a weeping and wailing for the mountains," and verse 11 flows directly into "I will make Jerusalem a heap of ruins"—unmistakably the voice of God. Many translations and commentators therefore hear the LORD Himself weeping over the burned countryside in 9:10, while others hear Jeremiah weeping in 9:10 before God speaks in 9:11. Either way, the passage makes clear that judgment does not issue from an unfeeling deity, but comes wrapped in profound sorrow.

**Hiring "wailing women" (9:17) and "death has climbed in through our windows" (9:21).** Across the ancient Near East, hiring trained female mourners to sing funeral laments was an everyday custom—the prophet Amos also mentions those skilled in wailing (Amos 5:16). What shocks the reader here is God summoning them to sing a dirge over a nation still walking around alive, because disaster sits right outside the gate. As for "death has climbed in through our windows; it has entered our fortresses" (9:21), the phrasing may echo ancient Canaanite imagery of the god of Death (*Mot*) slipping through palace windows; Jeremiah uses it to paint the grim reality of siege and plague—no city wall or royal palace is thick enough to lock death out from claiming the next generation.

**Why does God group Egypt, Edom, Ammon, and Moab with Judah as "circumcised" nations (9:25–26)?** Many readers assume only Israel practiced circumcision in antiquity. Historical evidence shows, however, that several ancient Near Eastern peoples—prominently the Egyptians, Edomites, Ammonites, Moabites, and many Arabian desert tribes—also practiced physical circumcision, though at different ages and with different religious meanings (whereas Philistines and Babylonians did not). That sharpens God's rebuke: judged solely by a mark in the flesh, Judah looks no different from its pagan neighbors; what ought to distinguish them as God's own is a heart wholly belonging to Him.

**What does "who clip the hair of their temples" mean in 9:26?** This phrase described nomadic Arab tribes inhabiting the deserts east and south of Judah, recognizable for shaving the hair at the edges of their temples—a custom Moses' law expressly forbade Israel to imitate.

**The stark language of God "avenging" Himself (9:9) and corpses lying "like dung on the open field" (9:22).** Hearing God ask "Should I not avenge Myself on such a nation as this?" alongside images of unburied bodies scattered across fields easily unsettles modern readers. In Old Testament usage, God's "vengeance" is never petty human spite, but the restoration of justice when deceit and exploitation overflow with no one left to defend the wronged. Meanwhile, the scene of unburied corpses states without disguise the brutal reality of military conquest that Judah's own stubbornness is bringing down on itself.

## What Commentators Say

**Maimonides (Rambam)**, the foremost twelfth-century medieval Jewish philosopher and scholar, chose Jeremiah 9:23–24 as the closing capstone of his most celebrated philosophical work. Following the verse's progression, Maimonides ranks four tiers of human perfection from lowest to highest: material possessions ("riches"), bodily vigor ("strength"), moral and intellectual virtue ("wisdom"), and highest of all, genuine knowledge of God. Crucially, he insists the verse does not stop at abstract contemplation of God; it immediately defines the LORD as exercising loving devotion, justice, and righteousness *on the earth*. Therefore, truly knowing God expresses itself in imitating Him through mercy, fairness, and righteousness in ordinary life. *(The Guide for the Perplexed, Part III, Chapter 54)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the fourfold hierarchy of human perfection built on Jeremiah 9:23–24 closes Part III, Chapter 54 of The Guide for the Perplexed and is one of Maimonides' best-known passages -->

**Jerome**, the fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Bible, notes in his exposition of 9:25–26 the historical background that Egyptians and several neighboring peoples also practiced bodily circumcision. From this he emphasizes that an external badge never guarantees standing before God; what counts is circumcision of the heart, tying straight into Paul's argument in Romans. On 9:23–24, Jerome reminds readers that all worldly brilliance, clout, and wealth fade away, leaving knowledge of God as the sole worthy boast. *(Commentary on Jeremiah, on chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Jerome explaining Egyptian circumcision on 9:25–26 and the vanity of worldly boasting on 9:23–24, though without verifying exact Latin wording -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, unpacks 9:23–24 carefully to guard against a common misreading: intelligence, strength, and wealth are good gifts from God in themselves, not evils. What God forbids is not possessing them, but *boasting* in them and relying on them as though they stood apart from Him. Turning to 9:25–26, Calvin applies the lesson straight to the Church: anyone who leans on outward signs—circumcision then, or baptism now—while keeping a hardened heart gains no shelter and only invites stricter judgment. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: matches Calvin's characteristic reasoning in his Jeremiah lectures on 9:23–26, though exact wording was not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses over the opening verses 9:1–2 as a portrait of a true shepherd: Jeremiah loves his people deeply enough to weep day and night, yet detests their deceit enough to long to flee into the desert—authentic love never means turning a blind eye to falsehood. Commenting on 9:23–24, he observes that the wise can lose their wits, the strong can lose their health, and the rich can be ruined overnight; knowing God is the one treasure no catastrophe can strip away. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: accurately recalls Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral points on 9:1–2 and 9:23–24, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads the final sections of chapter 9 side by side as a complete inversion of the Jerusalem royal court's value system. While the ruling elite pretend business goes on as usual, summoning "the wailing women" (9:17–21) forces the community to stare honestly at death climbing straight into its palaces. Immediately after that dirge, 9:23–24 knocks down the three pillars of every empire—royal wisdom, military power, and accumulated wealth—rebuilding the sole foundation for survival: knowing the God who acts in steadfast love, justice, and righteousness. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: fits Brueggemann's characteristic exposition of the wailing women and the dismantling of imperial pillars in 9:23–24, though not checked word for word -->
