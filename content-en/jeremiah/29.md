# Jeremiah 29 — The Letter to the Exiles

## Story

In the previous chapter, the false prophet Hananiah snapped Jeremiah's wooden yoke right inside the temple and guaranteed that exiles in Babylon would march home within two short years (28:2–4; 28:10–11). That intoxicating daydream swept not only through Jerusalem, but across the exiled community itself. Writing from the still-standing capital, Jeremiah dispatches a letter to the elders, priests, prophets, and all the people Nebuchadnezzar deported from Jerusalem to Babylon—the great deportation of 597 BC that carried away King Jeconiah, the queen mother, court officials, nobles, craftsmen, and metalsmiths. He entrusts the letter to two envoys King Zedekiah is sending on a tribute mission to Babylon: Elasah son of Shaphan and Gemariah son of Hilkiah (29:1–3).

The opening lines burst the fantasy of an early return immediately. The LORD of Hosts identifies the captives as those whom *He Himself* sent into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon, then hands them a stunning set of instructions: "Build houses and settle down. Plant gardens and eat their produce." Instead of living out of packed bags waiting for tomorrow's rescue, they must marry, have sons and daughters, and find spouses for their children so they "multiply there, and do not decrease." Even harder, God asks something cutting directly against every bitter instinct of conquered refugees: "Seek the peace and prosperity of the city to which I have sent you as exiles. Pray to the LORD on its behalf, for if it prospers, you too will prosper" (29:4–7).

Next, God warns them not to let self-styled prophets and diviners among them deceive them, nor to pay attention to the dreams they encourage. Those voices are prophesying lies in His name, for "I have not sent them." God states the true timeline plainly: only "when seventy years for Babylon are complete" will He attend to them, fulfill His gracious promise, and bring them back to their homeland (29:8–10).

Immediately after that daunting seventy-year horizon stands one of the tenderest promises in the entire book of Jeremiah. The LORD declares: "For I know the plans I have for you, plans for peace and not for disaster, to give you a future and a hope." When that hour arrives, they will call on Him and pray, and He will listen; they will seek Him and find Him when they "search for Me with all your heart." God promises to be found by them, gather them from all the nations where He banished them, and restore their captives to their ancestral soil (29:11–14).

The exiles, however, seem to object that God has already raised up prophets for them right there in Babylon. In answer, the letter pivots sharply toward those still left behind in Jerusalem—the king seated on David's throne and the kin who avoided the first deportation—whom the false prophets still point to as proof of safety. God vows to unleash "sword, famine, and plague" on them, making them "like rotten figs, so bad they cannot be eaten," scattering them across all kingdoms as an object of horror, scorn, and cursing. All of this falls because God "repeatedly sent My servants the prophets" to plead with them, yet they flatly refused to listen (29:15–19).

Turning back to the exiles in Babylon, God names two specific false prophets stirring up trouble: Ahab son of Kolaiah and Zedekiah son of Maaseiah. Not only are these two peddling lies in God's name, they have "committed outrageous acts in Israel" by committing adultery with their neighbors' wives. God will hand them over to King Nebuchadnezzar to be executed right before the exiles' eyes, turning their very names into a grim curse repeated among Judeans: "May the LORD treat you like Zedekiah and Ahab, whom the king of Babylon roasted in the fire!" (29:20–23).

The closing section recounts the furious backlash mailed back from Babylon to Jerusalem. A man named Shemaiah the Nehelamite writes angrily to the people of Jerusalem and directly to the priest Zephaniah son of Maaseiah, who replaced Jehoiada overseeing order in the temple. Shemaiah demands why Zephaniah—tasked with clamping stocks and neck irons on every "madman who acts like a prophet"—has failed to arrest Jeremiah of Anathoth for sending letters to Babylon saying exile will last a long time and telling people to build houses and plant gardens. Instead of jailing Jeremiah, however, Zephaniah brings the letter straight over and reads it aloud to him. At once the LORD instructs Jeremiah to dispatch a follow-up word to the exiles concerning Shemaiah: because he prophesied without being sent, led people to trust in lies, and preached rebellion against the LORD, God will punish both him and his descendants—none of his line will remain among the people, nor will he ever live to witness the good God is going to do for His people (29:24–32).

## Key Takeaways

**Exile never places God's people outside God's reach.** Three times in this letter, God emphasizes that *He Himself* sent them to Babylon (29:4; 29:7; 29:14). Deportation neither proved Babylonian gods outmuscled Israel's God nor meant God had walked away from His people. Far from homeland, temple, and altar, believers can still call out and find Him whenever they seek Him with all their heart (29:12–13).

**Mature faith learns to live steadily inside hard seasons instead of chasing overnight escape fantasies.** Telling homesick captives to build houses, plant orchards, and arrange weddings (29:5–6) sounded jarring to anyone hoping for tomorrow's rescue. Yet prophets peddling instant deliverance lured exiles into neglecting real life, flirting with rebellion, and inviting catastrophe. Facing reality honestly and living faithfully day by day is what safeguards the future.

**God's people are called to bless the very city where they live as outsiders.** The charge to seek the peace and pray for Babylon (29:7) turns conquered bitterness upside down. Believers must neither isolate themselves in resentment nor melt into Babylon's idolatry, but actively invest in the common flourishing of the community where God placed them.

**God's promise of hope does not erase a long journey—it lights up the destination.** God hides nothing about seventy long years in exile (29:10), yet sets right beside that number His unshakable pledge of good plans, peace, and a hopeful future (29:11). Divine discipline aims not to crush His people, but to draw their hearts wholly back to Him.

## Deeper Meaning

**The charge to multiply in Babylon echoing Genesis and Exodus.** Telling captives to raise families so they "multiply there, and do not decrease" (29:6) immediately echoes God's primal creation blessing over humanity (Genesis 1:28) and Israel's astonishing growth even under Egyptian slavery (Exodus 1:7). Even inside seemingly bleak exile, the river of life promised to Abraham's offspring never stops flowing.

**From praying for Zion's peace to praying for Babylon—and the New Testament Church.** Israel had long sung prayers for Jerusalem's peace (Psalms 122:6), while beside Babylon's rivers they hung up their harps weeping, unable to sing the LORD's song on foreign soil (Psalms 137:1–4). Jeremiah's command to pray for the enemy capital (29:7) opens a fresh vision for God's people living scattered through the world. In the New Testament, Paul instructs churches to pray for kings and all in authority so believers may lead peaceful, quiet lives (1 Timothy 2:1–2), while Peter addresses Christians as "foreigners and exiles" living honorably among unbelievers so onlookers see their good deeds and glorify God (1 Peter 2:11–12).

**Seventy years linking Jeremiah to Daniel and the return home.** The seventy-year horizon (29:10; cf. 25:11–12) anchored Jewish hope across decades abroad. Near its close, Daniel read Jeremiah's scrolls, recognized seventy years nearing fulfillment, and knelt in fasting and prayer for his people (Daniel 9:2). Biblical history records that right on schedule God stirred King Cyrus of Persia to decree the Jews' return to rebuild Jerusalem's temple, fulfilling the word spoken through Jeremiah (2 Chronicles 36:21–23; Ezra 1:1).

**Rotten figs and the inverted fate of exiles versus holdouts.** Branding Jerusalem's residents as "rotten figs, so bad they cannot be eaten" in 29:17 ties straight back to the vision of two fig baskets before the temple in chapter 24 (24:1–8). Overturning human intuition that residents still holding homes in the capital were the lucky ones, God insists Israel's future rides with the first wave of deportees refined to "return to Me with all their heart" (24:7). Meanwhile, the promise to find God when seeking Him wholeheartedly in 29:13 echoes Moses' ancient promise spoken on the brink of Canaan (Deuteronomy 4:29).

**Corrupt character unmasking false prophets.** Ahab and Zedekiah combined preaching lies in God's name with committing adultery with neighbors' wives (29:23). Their execution by fire foretold by Jeremiah (29:22) matches documented Babylonian capital punishment echoed in Daniel's fiery furnace (Daniel 3:6). Meanwhile, the quiet integrity of the priest Zephaniah—reading Shemaiah's complaint to Jeremiah rather than locking him in stocks (29:29), unlike Pashhur earlier (20:1–2)—reveals voices inside the temple still revering God's word; Zedekiah would repeatedly send Zephaniah to consult Jeremiah right up to Jerusalem's fall (21:1; 52:24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 29:11 is one of the most frequently misquoted verses in Scripture.** Countless people print "plans to prosper you and not to harm you" on greeting cards as a guarantee of smooth individual careers or quick material success. In Hebrew, however, "you" is plural—spoken to a whole community of deportees—and sits directly behind the announcement of seventy full years in Babylon (29:10). Most original recipients of this letter would grow old and die on foreign soil long before their grandchildren returned home. The verse still brings deep comfort to believers today, yet as assurance of God's enduring redemptive purpose rather than a promise bypassing everyday hardship.

**Measuring the seventy years of exile.** Historians observe two clear calculations matching that span. Counting from Nebuchadnezzar's first deportation in 605 BC to Cyrus's return edict in 538 BC runs just shy of seventy years; counting from the temple's destruction in 586 BC to the second temple's dedication in 516 BC runs exactly seventy years. Beyond calendar math, seventy years also symbolized one complete human lifespan in biblical usage—indicating exile would last a full generation.

**The grim image of two false prophets "roasted in the fire" (29:22).** Modern readers understandably recoil at seeing two men burned alive and immortalized in a curse. Scripture never applauds Babylonian cruelty. Historically, prophets preaching Babylon's imminent collapse and quick repatriation looked like seditious agitators to Nebuchadnezzar's court—crimes punishable by burning in Babylon (cf. Daniel 3:6). The passage bluntly warns that men combining moral squalor with fraud in God's name drag horrific ruin onto themselves.

**Familiar names weaving the historical web.** King Jeconiah named in 29:2 is the same monarch called Jehoiachin in Kings (2 Kings 24:12–16), exiled in 597 BC. And Elasah son of Shaphan—one of the couriers carrying Jeremiah's letter to Babylon (29:3)—came from the devout house of royal secretary Shaphan, whose family repeatedly shielded Jeremiah's life (26:24). Having a trustworthy ally inside Zedekiah's embassy made sure this unpopular letter actually reached the exiles' hands.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, quotes Jeremiah 29:7 directly to frame how God's people relate to earthly governments. In his masterpiece contrasting the City of God and the Earthly City, he argues that while pilgrimaging on earth, believers share civic order and peace with surrounding society; thus even when an earthly state does not worship the true God, Christians honor its just laws and pray for its peace. *(The City of God, Book 19, Chapter 26)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's citation of Jeremiah 29:7 in City of God 19.26 regarding earthly civic peace is famous and widely referenced -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, highlights the careful nuance inside the command to pray for Babylon in 29:7. Praying for a pagan empire's welfare neither endorses its sins nor excuses tyranny, but expresses submission under God's providence while cultivating gentleness rather than bitterness. On 29:11, Calvin observes that God speaks of "plans for peace" precisely when exiles see zero visible hope, training believers to anchor faith in God's sure word rather than shifting appearances. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 29)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's exposition on praying for pagan rulers in verse 7 and trusting God's word amid bleak circumstances in verse 11, though not verified verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws practical wisdom from the instruction to build houses and plant gardens in 29:5–7: when difficult circumstances cannot change overnight, wisdom accepts today's assignment wholeheartedly and seeks good for the place we inhabit. At 29:12–13, Henry notes a classic pattern in God's dealings: whenever God prepares to pour out delivering grace on His people, He first stirs up a spirit of earnest prayer and wholehearted seeking inside them. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 29)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: these pastoral observations on settling faithfully in exile and prayer preceding deliverance are signature Matthew Henry points on chapter 29, though not verified verbatim -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the fifth-century classic of Jewish law and commentary, preserves a vivid narrative tradition explaining why King Nebuchadnezzar roasted the false prophets Ahab and Zedekiah in 29:21–23. The rabbis tell how the pair abused God's name to seduce women in exile—even approaching Nebuchadnezzar's own daughter—prompting the furious king to throw them into the fiery furnace to test whether God would rescue them like Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah; instead they burned for their lies and adultery. *(Babylonian Talmud, Tractate Sanhedrin, on chapter 29)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall the Sanhedrin midrash contrasting Ahab and Zedekiah in Nebuchadnezzar's furnace with Daniel's three friends; exact folio number (commonly recalled as 93a) not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, sees Jeremiah 29 as a survival charter for a minority faith community living inside a mighty empire. For him, *shalom* (peace/prosperity) in 29:7 reaches far beyond the absence of war to encompass civic well-being, fairness, and stability for the entire city. Jeremiah rejects both extremes—shutting down zealots plotting premature revolt while refusing to let exiles assimilate and forget their identity or God's homecoming promise. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 29)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Jeremiah 29 as a minority exile strategy anchored in shalom—steering between withdrawal and assimilation—is a celebrated thesis of Brueggemann -->

**Tim Keller**, the New York pastor who died in 2023, frequently made Jeremiah 29:4–7 the cornerstone of his vision for Christian engagement in modern cities. For Keller, the letter charts a third way transcending both hating the city and uncritically mirroring the city: believers sink deep roots, build culture, serve neighbors, and pray for the common flourishing of their city while maintaining distinct, uncompromised allegiance to God. *(Generous Justice, 2010; Center Church, 2012)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jeremiah 29:4–7 is a foundational passage analyzed directly and repeatedly across both of Keller's books -->
