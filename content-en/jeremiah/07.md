# Jeremiah 7 — The Temple Gate Sermon

## Story

Following the warnings of a northern army in the previous chapter, the LORD sends Jeremiah straight to the most sensitive spot in the capital: the gate of the Jerusalem temple. He must stand right in the doorway of God's house and preach to all the people of Judah streaming inside to worship. The message begins with a plain condition: "Amend your ways and your deeds, and I will let you live in this place." God exposes the mantra the crowd keeps repeating like a protective charm: "This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD!" Amending their ways does not mean multiplying ceremonies; it means practicing real justice with one another, refusing to oppress foreigners, orphans, and widows, shedding no innocent blood, and not chasing after other gods. Only then will God let them continue dwelling securely in the land He gave their ancestors long ago (7:1–7).

Yet reality is a stark reversal of their boast. The people lean on deceptive words that cannot help: out in daily life they "steal, murder, commit adultery, swear falsely, burn incense to Baal," and follow unfamiliar foreign gods; then they step inside God's temple, stand before Him, and sigh in relief, "We are delivered!"—treating the sanctuary like a free pass to head right back out and commit every abomination again. The LORD asks a jolting question: "Has this house, which bears My Name, become a den of robbers in your sight?" To shatter the illusion that the temple can never fall, God tells them to go look at the ruins of Shiloh, where He first set His name to dwell before laying it waste because of Israel's wickedness. Though God spoke to them "again and again"—literally rising early to warn them—and called without getting an answer, He will now do to the Jerusalem temple exactly what He did to Shiloh, and thrust Judah from His sight just as He cast out all their northern kin, the descendants of Ephraim (7:8–15).

Then a breathtakingly grim command is given privately to Jeremiah: he must not pray, lift up a plea, or intercede for this people, because God will not listen. God points out what is happening openly across the towns of Judah and the streets of Jerusalem. Entire families team up enthusiastically in pagan worship: "The children gather wood, the fathers light the fire, and the women knead the dough to make cakes for the Queen of Heaven," while also pouring out drink offerings to other gods. God asks whether they really think they are only provoking Him—are they not actually harming themselves and bringing shame onto their own heads? Therefore the wrath of the Lord GOD will pour out like an unquenchable fire, consuming the land from people and livestock to the trees of the field and the produce of the soil (7:16–20).

The tone of judgment turns razor-sharp with irony as God addresses the offerings they still bring to the temple. By law, a burnt offering had to be consumed completely on the altar for God with none left for worshipers to eat; yet He tells them, "Add your burnt offerings to your other sacrifices and eat the meat yourselves!"—implying He wants none of His share, so they might as well pile it all together and feast on it. God explains that on the day He brought their ancestors out of Egypt, His primary demand was not rules about burnt offerings and sacrifices, but one simple command: "Obey My voice, and I will be your God, and you will be My people. Walk in all the ways I have commanded you, that it may go well with you." Instead, they refused to listen, followed the stubborn plots of their evil hearts, and "went backward rather than forward." From the day they left Egypt right up to the present, God "again and again" sent His prophets, yet they stiffened their necks and acted even worse than their ancestors. God tells Jeremiah ahead of time to speak every word even though they will ignore him, and to deliver a blunt verdict to their faces: this is a nation that refuses to obey the LORD's voice or accept correction; "Truth has perished; it has vanished from their lips" (7:21–28).

From confrontation, the scene shifts into a funeral dirge. Jerusalem is told to shear off her long hair and throw it away—an ancient gesture of mourning—and raise a lament on the barren heights, because the LORD has rejected and abandoned this generation. The reason named is horrifying: the people of Judah have not only placed "detestable things" inside God's own house to defile it, they have built high places at Topheth, in the Valley of Ben-hinnom, to "burn their sons and daughters in the fire." The LORD underscores His revulsion: "which I did not command, nor did it ever enter My mind" (7:29–31).

The penalty for that atrocity takes the very name of the site where it occurred. God declares that days are coming when people will no longer call that valley Topheth or the Valley of Ben-hinnom, but "the Valley of Slaughter," for bodies slain in the coming carnage will fill Topheth until no burial space remains. Corpses will lie exposed as food for the birds of the air and beasts of the earth with no one left to shoo them away. Chapter 7 closes in an eerie hush: across the towns of Judah and the streets of Jerusalem, every sound of joy and gladness will vanish, and the voices of bridegroom and bride will be heard no more, for the entire land will become a wasteland (7:32–34).

## Key Takeaways

**No sacred building can replace an upright life.** Judah chanted "the temple of the LORD" three times as though the mere presence of that grand stone edifice guaranteed safety (7:4). Jeremiah smashes that counterfeit security: God does not shelter behind sacred walls when outside those walls rule theft, murder, and exploitation of the helpless.

**A house of worship turns into a "den of robbers" when people use it to dodge accountability.** A robber's den is not where bandits commit their crimes; it is the hideout they slip back into afterward to feel safe. When people live dishonestly all week and then walk into God's house sighing "We are delivered!" (7:10), they convert worship into a safe house for sin.

**Obedience always precedes and outweighs ritual.** God's startling line telling the people to lump their burnt offerings with their other meat and eat it themselves (7:21) underscores a foundational principle: God desires a people who hear His voice and walk in justice (7:23). Lavish offerings become empty clutter whenever stubborn hearts march backward.

**Idolatry is never merely private—it draws in whole families and destroys life.** The snapshot of fathers lighting fires, mothers kneading dough, and children gathering sticks for the Queen of Heaven (7:18) shows how deeply error weaves itself into daily family routines across generations. Worse still, walking away from the Creator can slide all the way into monstrous cruelty—burning one's own children in the flames of Topheth (7:31).

**God's patience runs astonishingly deep, yet it is not endless.** Twice in this single chapter, God pictures Himself rising early day after day to call and send messengers pleading with His people (7:13; 7:25). He never rushes to punish; yet when generations plug their ears for centuries, a threshold finally arrives where even intercession is closed off (7:16).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus quoting "a den of robbers" right in the temple courts.** Over six hundred years after Jeremiah's sermon, Jesus walked into the Jerusalem temple, overturned the money-changers' tables, and quoted 7:11 directly: God's house was meant to be a house of prayer for all nations, "but you have made it a den of robbers!" (Mark 11:17). Every Jewish listener caught the terrifying implication behind that citation: Jesus was not merely objecting to courtyard commerce, but stepping into Jeremiah's shoes to announce judgment on a temple that had betrayed its true calling.

**The life-threatening aftermath of this sermon.** Chapter 7 records only the sermon itself, whereas chapter 26 of Jeremiah tells us what erupted the moment he finished saying "this house will become like Shiloh" (26:1–9). Priests, prophets, and mobs immediately seized Jeremiah and shouted that he deserved to die. Challenging religious pride and the vested interests wrapped around the sanctuary has always been the most dangerous thing a truth-teller can do.

**The historical warning of Shiloh.** At the mere mention of Shiloh (7:12), every Judean shuddered remembering the disaster in Eli the priest's day. Back then, Israel had likewise assumed that carrying the Ark of the Covenant into battle guaranteed victory regardless of their corrupt lives; instead they suffered a crushing defeat by the Philistines, the Ark was captured, and God abandoned the sanctuary at Shiloh (1 Samuel 4:10–11; Psalms 78:60–61). History repeated itself when a later generation forgot the old lesson and recreated the exact same lucky-charm mindset around the Jerusalem temple.

**The Ten Commandments echoing in the indictment.** The roster of crimes Jeremiah recites in 7:9—stealing, murdering, adultery, false oaths, worshiping other gods—mirrors the core of the Ten Commandments given at Sinai (Exodus 20:13–16). Meanwhile, God's reminder about the day He led them out of Egypt (7:22–23) connects straight back to the foundational covenant made before the sacrificial regulations were ever laid out: if they obey His voice and keep His covenant, they will be His treasured possession (Exodus 19:5–6). Prophets like Hosea and Micah joined that same testimony, declaring that God desires steadfast love, justice, and humble obedience far above thousands of offerings (Hosea 6:6; Micah 6:6–8).

**From the Valley of Ben-hinnom to the New Testament word for hell.** The ravine south of Jerusalem called the "Valley of Ben-hinnom" (7:31)—*Ge-Hinnom* in Hebrew—bore an indelible stain from child sacrifices to Molech under wicked kings like Manasseh, even after King Josiah tried to demolish and defile the site (2 Kings 21:6; 23:10). Later, that very place name *Ge-Hinnom* passed into Greek as *Gehenna*, the word Jesus repeatedly used in the Gospels, rendered "hell" in English translations, where the fire is never quenched (Mark 9:43).

**The silenced voices of bride and groom will ring out again.** The heartbreaking close of chapter 7—silencing the voices of bridegroom and bride in Jerusalem's streets (7:34)—is later echoed by Revelation to picture the fall of Babylon (Revelation 18:23). Yet inside Jeremiah itself, that funeral silence is not the last word: later in the book, God promises that in those very desolate streets people will once again hear shouts of joy and "the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" when He restores the captives of the land (33:10–11).

## Notes & Nuances

**God says "I did not speak to them or command them concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices" (7:22)—what about Leviticus?** This line jolts many readers, since the books of Moses obviously contain detailed sacrificial laws. Commentators generally highlight three helpful ways to read it. The most widespread view sees it as classic Hebrew rhetorical contrast: negating the first element to spotlight the second as overwhelmingly primary—just like Hosea's "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6). A second reading notes the exact historical moment mentioned in the verse: "on the day I brought them out of the land of Egypt," the original covenant forged at Sinai began with the Ten Commandments and a call to obedience (Exodus 19:5–6), while the sacrificial system followed later to serve that relationship rather than stand as an end in itself. Some modern scholars also hear a real tension between the prophetic tradition stressing ethics and temple circles preoccupied with ritual. Whichever angle one emphasizes, the verse's central thrust is unmistakable: sacrifice divorced from obedience was never what God wanted.

**Why does God forbid Jeremiah from praying for the people (7:16)?** Readers accustomed to Moses or Abraham pleading for sinful Israel find this ban on intercession—repeated later in the book (11:14; 15:1)—chilling and perplexing. Reading 7:16 in the context of the whole chapter helps clarify it: right in 7:3–7, God still holds the door open for them to amend their ways and remain in the land. Forbidding Jeremiah to intercede does not mean God rejects any individual who sincerely repents; rather, it signals that historically the nation as a whole has crossed the point of no return—like an illness left untreated too long—so the judgment of exile can no longer be prayed away.

**Child sacrifice at Topheth (7:31) and corpses left for birds and beasts (7:33).** Passing children through fire to Molech or foreign deities was an attested practice in the ancient Near East that infiltrated Judah, especially under King Manasseh (2 Kings 21:6). Verse 7:31 matters deeply because God flatly repudiates every religious excuse for such barbarity: He never commanded it, "nor did it ever enter My mind." As for unburied corpses left to scavengers (7:33), that represented the ultimate horror in the ancient imagination; it expresses not divine gloating, but the grim aftermath of siege and slaughter when a collapsed city lacks enough survivors to bury its dead.

**Who was the "Queen of Heaven" in 7:18?** Scripture leaves this goddess unnamed, though scholars commonly identify her with Ishtar (or Astarte), widely worshiped across Mesopotamia and Canaan and associated with the planet Venus, fertility, and harvests. Her cult was intensely family-centered—parents and children baking ceremonial cakes together—and ran so deep that years later, even as refugees in Egypt, Judeans still argued back against Jeremiah that disaster hit them only because they stopped burning incense to the "Queen of Heaven" (44:17–19).

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Bible, warns Christian readers commenting on 7:4 not to point fingers smugly at ancient Judah. If Jews once boasted by chanting "the temple of the LORD" while living wickedly, Christians can fall straight into the same trap by boasting that they belong to the Church while their lives remain filled with sin. **Maimonides (Rambam)**, the greatest medieval Jewish philosopher and scholar of the twelfth century, built one of the most celebrated Jewish explanations of the origin of sacrifice directly upon 7:22–23. He argued that God's primary and highest intention in bringing Israel out of Egypt was teaching them to know Him and obey His voice; the sacrificial system was a wise divine accommodation to deeply rooted ancient human habits—people unaccustomed to worshiping without altars and animal offerings—designed to wean them gently away from idolatry. *(The Guide for the Perplexed, Part III, Chapter 32)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Maimonides' famous argument citing Jeremiah 7:22–23 appears specifically in Part III, Chapter 32 of The Guide for the Perplexed and is widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, saw in the chant "the temple of the LORD" in 7:4 a mirror of the religious controversies of his own day. He remarked that the more a community loses genuine fear of God, the louder it clings to sacred outward titles—just as people once chanted the name of the temple, in his time people chanted the name of the Church—to shield themselves from the scrutiny of God's Word. On 7:22, Calvin likewise stresses comparative idiom: sacrifices are merely supporting instruments, and stripped of obedience they immediately become worthless. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's famous parallel between the temple chant of 7:4 and appeals to the Church in the Reformation, along with his reading of 7:22, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, unpacks the "den of robbers" image in 7:11 with keen insight: a robbers' den is the hideout where outlaws retreat after plundering to feel protected and plan their next raid. When people sin and then attend worship merely to buy cheap peace of mind without repenting, they treat God's house as just such a hideout. At 7:16, he calls the command forbidding the prophet to pray for the people one of the most dreadful marks of judgment in all of Scripture. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: both remarks on "den of robbers" in 7:11 and the intercession ban in 7:16 fit Matthew Henry's characteristic exposition, though not verified verbatim -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads the temple sermon as a frontal assault on the Jerusalem royal establishment's ideology of guaranteed immunity. He shows how leaders turned the temple into a rubber stamp underwriting existing power while severing God's presence from concrete justice toward foreigners, orphans, and widows (7:5–6). By dragging the ruins of Shiloh right into the temple courtyard (7:12–14), Jeremiah reminds the whole nation that God remains completely free, never captive to any building or regime. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading the temple sermon as a critique of royal security ideology and emphasizing God's freedom via Shiloh matches Brueggemann's exposition of chapter 7, though not verified verbatim -->
