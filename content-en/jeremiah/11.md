# Jeremiah 11 — The Broken Covenant and a Plot at Home

## Story

Following the laments over a ruined land and the plea for measured discipline at the close of chapter 10, the LORD opens a fresh commission that brings Judah straight back to the bedrock of its relationship with Him: the ancient covenant. God instructs Jeremiah to remind the people of Judah and the residents of Jerusalem of the curse bound up with that covenant: "Cursed is the man who does not obey the words of this covenant." It is the covenant He commanded their ancestors on the day He pulled them out of Egypt—out of the "iron-smelting furnace" of slavery—carrying a simple yet total promise: if they obey His voice, "you will be My people, and I will be your God," and He will fulfill His oath to give them "a land flowing with milk and honey." Hearing that solemn recital, Jeremiah bows in affirmation just like an ancient covenant assembly: "Amen, LORD!" (11:1–5).

The LORD immediately dispatches him to proclaim this message across the towns of Judah and the streets of Jerusalem: "Listen to the words of this covenant and carry them out!" God recalls His steady patience across centuries from the exodus right down to the present. Using an ordinary everyday image, He says He warned them "again and again"—literally rising early—urging only that they obey. Yet they refused to listen or even pay attention, each one following "the stubbornness of his evil heart." Consequently, every warning written into the covenant now comes crashing down on them—not because God broke faith, but because they broke their side of the vow (11:6–8).

Moving from ancient ancestors to the present moment, the LORD points straight at what is unfolding now: among the people of Judah and the residents of Jerusalem there is a "conspiracy"—a deliberate revolt against the King of the covenant. They have turned back to the sins of their forebears, chasing after other gods to serve them; both the house of Israel and the house of Judah have shattered the covenant God made with their ancestors. Therefore God declares He will bring upon them a disaster they cannot escape; when they cry out to Him then, He will not listen. The towns of Judah and the citizens of Jerusalem will run crying for help to the gods to whom they burn incense, yet in the hour of disaster those gods will not save a single soul. Jeremiah exposes how thoroughly idolatry has saturated the land with biting irony: Judah has "as many gods as you have towns," and Jerusalem has built as many altars to burn incense to Baal—whom God bluntly dubs "Shame"—as she has streets (11:9–13).

With the sentence sealed, the LORD issues Jeremiah a harsh command shutting down the prophet's most familiar role: "Do not pray for this people," do not lift a plea or petition for them, because when trouble hits and they call to God, He will not hear. Next comes an aching, stinging question aimed straight at the temple: what right does "My beloved" have inside God's house after committing so many vile schemes with idols? Can vows and "consecrated meat" turn disaster aside—especially while the people actually rejoice in doing evil? God recalls how at first He called them "a thriving olive tree, beautiful with well-formed fruit," planted by the LORD of Hosts Himself. Yet now, amid a mighty roar, He will set that olive tree ablaze and shatter its branches—because both the house of Israel and the house of Judah have brought ruin on themselves by burning incense to Baal and provoking Him (11:14–17).

Abruptly, the public oracle closes and gives way to Jeremiah's very first private cry before God. While proclaiming the covenant everywhere, the LORD revealed to him a dark plot aimed straight at his life. Jeremiah shudders realizing how innocently he had walked along "like a gentle lamb led to slaughter," completely unaware of the whispers behind his back: "Let us destroy the tree with its fruit; let us cut him off from the land of the living, that his name may be remembered no more." Stunned by the betrayal, the prophet reaches for no weapon of retaliation; instead he looks up to the LORD of Hosts—the One "who judges righteously and tests the heart and mind." He lays his entire case before God's courtroom and asks to witness God's justice on those plotting his death (11:18–20).

At the close of the chapter, the LORD names the conspirators outright, driving the knife even deeper into Jeremiah's heart: these are not Babylonian commanders or hostile strangers, but the men of Anathoth—his own hometown neighbors and priestly kinsmen (cf. 1:1). They have delivered a blunt ultimatum: "Do not prophesy in the name of the LORD, or you will die by our hand!" Answering their attempt to silence His messenger, the LORD of Hosts hands down a specific sentence on the men of Anathoth: in the year of their punishment, their young men will die by the sword, their sons and daughters will die of famine, and "no remnant will be left to them" when disaster strikes the men of Anathoth (11:21–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Covenant with God always weaves promise and responsibility together.** The LORD reminds Judah that He rescued them from Egypt's iron furnace first before asking for their obedience (11:4–5). He is no lucky charm to pocket while marching after our own stubbornness; discarding the terms of the covenant inevitably invites the curses of the covenant.

**When hearts turn away from God, temple rituals turn hollow.** Judah still brought consecrated meat into God's house regularly while lining every street outside with altars to Baal (11:13; 11:15). Genuine worship is never measured by the volume of offerings or the bustle of services, but by undivided faithfulness to the one true God.

**God's patience stretches long, yet it has a boundary.** The image of God rising early to plead with His people generation after generation shows how deeply He longed for them to turn back (11:7). Yet precisely because they stayed stubborn for so long, a sobering threshold arrives where God tells Jeremiah to stop interceding (11:14): when people insist on plugging their ears against loving warnings, they put themselves outside the reach of rescue.

**Faithfulness to God's word can make us hated right in our own hometown.** The people wanting Jeremiah dead were not foreigners, but the villagers of Anathoth who had known his face and name since boyhood (11:21). Speaking truth that disrupts the vested interests and religious habits of those around us often carries the cost of isolation and betrayal from those closest to us.

**In the face of bitter injustice, God's servants entrust their case to the One who reads every heart.** Jeremiah hides neither his shock nor his outrage upon uncovering the assassination plot, yet he refuses to take vengeance into his own hands; he commits his cause to the One "who judges righteously and tests the heart and mind" (11:20). Honest prayer dares to lay every raw hurt before God while leaving judgment safely in His hands.

## Deeper Meaning

**Direct echoes of Deuteronomy's covenant ceremony and Josiah's reform.** The opening of chapter 11 repeats core phrases from Deuteronomy nearly verbatim: Egypt as an iron-smelting furnace (Deuteronomy 4:20), the pledge "I will be your God, and you will be My people" (Leviticus 26:12), and especially the covenant curse to which the whole assembly answered "Amen!" (Deuteronomy 27:26). This also aligns closely with the defining event of Jeremiah's early ministry: King Josiah discovering the Book of the Covenant in the temple and renewing the covenant with the entire nation (2 Kings 23:1–3). Yet top-down royal reform failed to transform Judah's inner heart—paving the way for the breathtaking promise later in the book of a new covenant where God writes His law directly onto people's hearts instead of merely engraving stone or reciting words aloud (31:31–33).

**The broken olive tree and Paul's warning.** The picture of Israel as "a thriving olive tree, beautiful with well-formed fruit" scorched by fire and stripped of broken branches through unfaithfulness (11:16) returns forcefully in Romans. The apostle Paul uses that very olive-tree metaphor to explain why natural branches were broken off through unbelief while Gentiles, like wild olive shoots, were grafted in by faith—paired with a stern warning against arrogance, for if God did not spare the natural branches, neither will He spare those grafted in later (Romans 11:17–21).

**A gentle lamb led to slaughter—foreshadowing the Suffering Servant.** Jeremiah's self-portrait "like a gentle lamb led to slaughter," overhearing enemies plot to cut him off from the land of the living (11:19), carries a haunting likeness to Isaiah's Suffering Servant song: "like a lamb led to the slaughter," "cut off from the land of the living" (Isaiah 53:7–8). Even further, the scene of hometown Anathoth plotting to kill Jeremiah replays in Luke's Gospel, when the townsfolk of Nazareth furiously drag Jesus to the brow of the hill to throw Him off, prompting His famous words: "no prophet is accepted in his hometown" (Luke 4:24; 4:28–29).

**Entrusting wrongs to the One who tests heart and mind.** Jeremiah is the first voice in his book to commit his cause to the One "who judges righteously and tests the heart and mind" (11:20; cf. 20:12). In the New Testament, the apostle Peter urges believers to follow Jesus' example: when reviled and wronged, He did not retaliate, but "entrusted Himself to Him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:23). And in Revelation, the risen Christ claims the very title of the LORD from 11:20 as His own: "I am He who searches minds and hearts" (Revelation 2:23).

**Three times God forbids Jeremiah to intercede.** The command "Do not pray for this people" in 11:14 does not stand alone; it appears three times across the first half of Jeremiah (7:16; 11:14; 14:11). Ordinarily, a prophet's noblest duty—modeled by Abraham and Moses—was standing in the gap to plead for mercy. Closing the door to intercession three times marks a tragic historical turning point: Judah has crossed the threshold beyond which the Babylonian exile can no longer be averted.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does praying "let me see Your vengeance upon them" in 11:20 clash with Christian forgiveness?** Many readers wince hearing Jeremiah ask to watch God repay his would-be murderers, so unlike Jesus teaching us to pray for our enemies. Reading fairly in context highlights three key realities: first, Jeremiah refuses personal retaliation and hands the whole matter to the righteous Judge; second, the conspirators are not merely settling a personal grudge—they are trying to silence the word of God itself (11:21); third, under the Old Covenant, the faithful expected God's justice to be vindicated visibly in this life, so they voiced raw cries for vindication openly before God. Scripture records Jeremiah's honest pain without airbrushing it, while in the New Testament Jesus opens a higher path—absorbing enemies' blows and praying for the Father to forgive them (cf. 1 Peter 2:23).

**Why did the villagers of Anathoth want to murder one of their own (11:21)?** Anathoth sat just a few miles northeast of Jerusalem and was a priestly town (1:1). Many scholars point to a very concrete motive: when King Josiah launched his reforms based on the Book of the Covenant, he shut down all local rural shrines to centralize sacrifice exclusively at the Jerusalem temple (2 Kings 23:8–9). Jeremiah's vocal support for that covenant likely struck directly at the livelihood, prestige, and local pride of his fellow priests back home—turning him into a "traitor to his own town" in his relatives' eyes.

**Verse 11:23 says "no remnant will be left" to Anathoth, yet Ezra lists 128 men of Anathoth returning from Babylon—why?** Indeed, after the exile the returnee census records 128 men of Anathoth coming home (Ezra 2:23). That helps us read the exact scope of 11:21–23: God aims His sentence specifically at the faction of "the men of Anathoth" actively plotting Jeremiah's murder at that moment—none of those conspirators will survive Babylon's reckoning—not that the village site or every descendant of Anathoth would be wiped from history forever.

**A tricky Hebrew line in 11:15.** The wording around "consecrated meat" and vows in 11:15 is terse and compressed in Hebrew, leading English translations to render it slightly differently—for instance, "Can vows and consecrated meat avert your doom? When you do evil, then you rejoice." Across every translation, however, the core point stays unmistakable: temple vows and sacrificial meat cannot serve as a protective shield for a nation betraying its covenant.

**Jerome**, the fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Bible, reads 11:19—"like a gentle lamb led to slaughter"—on two levels. Historically, he notes the men of Anathoth plotting Jeremiah's death. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pauses carefully at two tough spots in chapter 11. On the ban against intercession in 11:14, he stresses that this signals Judah's stubbornness has crossed the tipping point, while reminding believers never to stop praying for others on their own initiative without direct prophetic revelation. On Jeremiah's plea to see God's vengeance in 11:20, Calvin distinguishes personal spite from zeal for God's justice: by placing his case before the One who tests heart and mind, Jeremiah leaves vengeance wholly to God rather than plotting revenge himself. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah and the Lamentations, on chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's characteristic treatment of the intercession ban in 11:14 and the plea for vindication in 11:20, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, highlights Jeremiah's brief "Amen, LORD!" in 11:5 right after the covenant curse. In Henry's view, the prophet affirms the fairness of God's law even when its words sting, placing himself under the same covenant alongside the people. At the end of the chapter, Henry links the pain of hometown Anathoth plotting murder with Nazareth rejecting Jesus, while commending the wisest reflex of any wronged believer in 11:20: laying the whole hurt before the One who searches hearts. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the reflections on "Amen" in verse 5, the Anathoth-Nazareth parallel, and committing one's case to God in verse 20 match Matthew Henry's style, though not verified verbatim -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Jewish thinker and scholar, views the hometown conspiracy from Anathoth at the close of chapter 11 as one of the starkest portraits of prophetic loneliness. For Heschel, Jeremiah is no outsider condemning Judah from afar; he loves his people painfully, yet finds himself trapped in an agonizing vise between blood relatives demanding his silence and God's inescapable call. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jeremiah's isolation and inner tension under rejection by his kinsmen at Anathoth are major motifs in the Jeremiah chapter of The Prophets, though exact pages were not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, highlights the sharp shift between the two halves of chapter 11: the first half (11:1–17) delivers a public indictment steeped in Deuteronomic covenant language, while the closing verses (11:18–23) abruptly launch the sequence of Jeremiah's aching personal "confessions."
