# Jeremiah 15 — Unrelenting Wrath and the Cry of the One Who Sits Alone

## Story

The earnest plea closing chapter 14 slams straight into a chilling answer at the opening of chapter 15. The LORD declares bluntly: "Even if Moses and Samuel should stand before Me, My heart would not go out to this people. Send them away from My presence, and let them go!" If they turn around asking where they should go, Jeremiah must lay out four grim destinations already appointed: those destined for plague, to plague; those for the sword, to the sword; those for famine, to famine; and those for captivity, to captivity. Behind those follow four terrifying destroyers: the sword to slay, dogs to tear, and birds of the sky and beasts of the earth to devour and destroy. A specific historical turning point is named aloud: because of King Manasseh son of Hezekiah and the atrocities he committed in Jerusalem, God will make Judah "an object of horror to all the kingdoms of the earth" (15:1–4).

The voice of judgment softens briefly into a sorrowful dirge over a city facing ruin: "Who will have pity on you, O Jerusalem? Who will mourn for you? Who will turn aside to ask about your welfare?" She herself rejected God and kept slipping backward, forcing Him to stretch out His hand to destroy: "I am weary of showing compassion." He winnowed them with a winnowing fork at the city gates and stripped them of children, yet still they refused to turn from their ways. Widows multiply beyond the grains of sand by the sea; a destroyer strikes at high noon; the mother of seven sons—the ancient emblem of complete blessing—grows faint and gasps for breath, "her sun has set while it is still day," while any remaining survivors are handed over to the enemy's sword (15:5–9).

From national disaster, the lens zooms sharply into Jeremiah's personal agony. Unable to hold back another moment, he erupts in despair: "Woe to me, my mother, that you have borne me, a man of strife and conflict in all the land!" He has neither lent money nor borrowed—the everyday financial disputes that breed resentment—yet everyone curses him. At once the LORD steps in to reassure His servant: surely He will strengthen him for good, and when disaster and distress hit, those very enemies will come begging to him. Interwoven right after that comfort stands a stark oracle aimed at Judah: "Can anyone smash iron—iron from the north—or bronze?" Because of all their sins across every border, God will hand all their wealth and treasures over as plunder without price, marching them into an unknown land with their enemies, for His anger has kindled a fire (15:10–14).

Yet Jeremiah's inner wound still throbs unhealed; he pours out one of the rawest, boldest laments in all of Scripture. He cries, "O LORD, You understand!" He begs God to remember him, care for him, and avenge him on his persecutors; do not let him be swept away while God patiently holds back against them, for he is enduring "reproach for Your sake." Then he looks back on his early joy in God's word: "Your words were found, and I ate them. Your words became a joy to me and the delight of my heart," because he bears God's name. Yet obeying God pushed him into crushing isolation: he never sat in the company of revelers celebrating, but "sat alone because of Your hand," filled with indignation over the nation's sin. His inner torment breaks out in a breathtakingly audacious accusation: "Why is my pain unending, and my wound incurable, refusing to be healed? You have indeed become like a deceptive brook to me, like waters that fail!" (15:15–18).

The LORD neither abandons His prophet nor indulges his bitterness. He answers with a direct, bracing summons addressed to Jeremiah himself: "If you return, I will restore you; you will stand in My presence. And if you speak words that are noble instead of worthless, you will be My spokesman." The crowd must turn to him; he must never bend to them. Once he plants his feet again where God stationed him, God renews His original vow: He will make him "a fortified wall of bronze" to this people; they will fight against him, yet never overpower him, because God is with him to rescue and deliver him—snatching him from the grip of the wicked and redeeming him from the grasp of the ruthless (15:19–21).

## Key Takeaways

**God's patience runs deep, yet it is not endless permissiveness.** Not even Moses and Samuel—Israel's two greatest intercessors—could reverse the verdict once stubbornness reached the breaking point (15:1; 15:6–7). The sobering line "I am weary of showing compassion" proves sin cannot bank forever on yesterday's grace to dodge tomorrow's consequences.

**Loyalty to God's word often brings loneliness and misunderstanding.** Jeremiah wronged nobody and owed nobody a penny, yet simply telling the truth God entrusted to him earned curses from the entire land (15:10). The man who "ate" God's words with delight soon found himself sitting "alone because of Your hand" (15:16–17). Authentic faith never guarantees universal applause.

**God's shoulders are broad enough for our rawest complaints, yet He still corrects His servants.** Jeremiah dared to accuse God of being "like a deceptive brook... like waters that fail" (15:18)—language skirting the very edge of blasphemy. God neither strikes him down nor walks away, yet neither does He pat his back and let bitterness fester. He calls His own prophet to "return" and separate what is noble from what is worthless (15:19).

**God does not promise to remove every opponent; He promises to keep us standing amid the siege.** At the chapter's close, the LORD does not claim Judah will stop fighting Jeremiah; quite the opposite, He states plainly, "They will fight against you but will never overcome you, for I am with you" (15:20). A believer's endurance rests not on smooth circumstances, but on the steady presence of the God who walks alongside us.

## Deeper Meaning

**Moses and Samuel—two pillars of Old Testament intercession.** God names these two figures specifically in 15:1 because Israel remembered them as the intercessors whose prayers turned away disaster for the entire nation: Moses after the golden calf crisis (Exodus 32:11–14) and Samuel when the people begged him not to stop crying out to save them from the Philistines (1 Samuel 7:8–9; 12:23; Psalms 99:6). Soon afterward, the prophet Ezekiel delivered a parallel warning: even if Noah, Daniel, and Job lived in the land, they could save only their own lives, not a stubborn nation (Ezekiel 14:14). Someone else's towering faith can never stand in for our own repentance.

**Four appointed judgments echoing into Revelation.** The fourfold ruin of 15:2–3—plague, sword, famine, and captivity alongside wild beasts—forms a classic biblical pattern of judgment. In the final book of the New Testament, when the fourth seal breaks open, the rider named Death astride the pale horse receives authority to kill by the same four scourges: sword, famine, plague, and the wild beasts of the earth (Revelation 6:8).

**The lament wishing never to have been born.** Jeremiah's outcry in 15:10—grieving that his mother ever bore him into a life of constant conflict—links directly with Job cursing the day of his birth amid ashes (Job 3:1–3) and will erupt even more violently in chapter 20 of this book (20:14–18). Even the metaphor of a "deceptive brook" in 15:18 directly recalls Job rebuking his friends for failing him like desert wadis drying up right when caravans need water most (Job 6:15–17). Scripture preserves these unfiltered laments so hurting people know they never need to hide their tears from God.

**Eating God's word: sweet on the tongue, bitter in the stomach.** Jeremiah's memory of "eating" God's words in deep joy (15:16) followed immediately by loneliness and pain (15:17–18) launches a vivid motif spanning Scripture. Shortly after Jeremiah, the prophet Ezekiel was told to eat a prophetic scroll that tasted sweet as honey in his mouth even though filled with mourning and woe (Ezekiel 3:1–3). In Revelation, the apostle John likewise swallows a little scroll—sweet as honey on his lips, yet turning sour in his stomach (Revelation 10:9–10). Receiving God's word is our highest delight, yet speaking it in a hostile world never comes cheap.

**A fortified wall of bronze—rekindling the original promise.** God's answer in 15:20 repeats nearly word for word the vow He spoke to Jeremiah on day one when the young man trembled at his calling: "I have made you a fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls... They will fight against you but will never overcome you, for I am with you to deliver you" (1:18–19). When His prophet is worn thin enough to quit, God does not invent a flashy new promise; He brings him back to his first calling and first assurance.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why is Jeremiah's generation judged "because of Manasseh" in 15:4?** King Manasseh reigned fifty-five years, erected pagan altars right inside the LORD's temple, sacrificed his son in fire, and drenched Jerusalem in innocent blood (2 Kings 21:1–6; 23:26; 24:3–4). Readers naturally wonder whether later generations are being punished unfairly for a king long dead. Reading straight into 15:6–7 gives the clear answer: Jeremiah's contemporaries are hardly innocent—they themselves have "rejected Me" and "have not returned from their ways." Manasseh's reign rotted the moral and spiritual bedrock of Judah, and his descendants kept walking right down that same path despite Josiah's brief reform.

**Does "I am weary of showing compassion" in 15:6 mean God has run out of mercy?** Not that God's merciful nature vanished. The Hebrew verb rendered "showing compassion" or "relenting" (*nacham*) describes holding back judgment out of pity. Speaking in vivid human terms, God reveals that He softened and postponed punishment again and again across generations, yet Judah only exploited His patience to keep rebelling—leaving Him no choice but to let the verdict stand.

**Sudden shifts in speaker across 15:11–14.** Alert readers notice that 15:11 brings God's reassurance to Jeremiah, yet 15:12–14 abruptly pivots to "iron from the north" and Judah's wealth handed over to plunder—verses 15:13–14 also reappear almost word for word in chapter 17 (17:3–4). The Hebrew wording of 15:11–12 is exceptionally compressed and difficult, so modern translations divide it differently—some reading 15:12 as asking whether ordinary iron (Judah, or even Jeremiah himself) can shatter tougher northern iron (Babylon). Even where translation details remain debated, the main thread stands clear: right inside an unstoppable foreign invasion, God keeps His hand over His prophet.

**How should we understand Jeremiah praying for "vengeance" (15:15) and calling God a "deceptive brook" (15:18)?** Both lines are startlingly candid. First, Jeremiah refuses to bottle up his resentment when persecuted by his own countrymen; he pours his hurt out to God rather than taking revenge himself. Second, a "deceptive brook" pictures seasonal wadis in arid lands—rushing full during winter rains, then bone-dry right when thirsty travelers count on them in summer heat. Jeremiah felt as if God's pledge to rescue him had dried up right when he needed it most. What stands out most is God's response in 15:19: He does not condemn Jeremiah's anguished cry as unforgivable, yet He squarely summons him to "return"—laying aside bitter self-pity ("worthless" speech) to hold fast to truthful speech ("noble" words) so he can continue serving as God's mouthpiece.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, the third-century Alexandrian scholar, devotes an entire homily to 15:10–19. In Jeremiah's lonely outcry in 15:10—having neither lent nor borrowed yet cursed by the whole earth—he sees a foreshadowing of Jesus Himself, wholly innocent and indebted to no one yet hated and condemned by the world. On 15:19, Origen interprets separating what is noble from what is worthless as the demanding discipline required of anyone preaching God's word: only by dividing what is sacred from what is base can human speech remain worthy to serve as God's mouth. *(Homilies on Jeremiah, Homily 14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Origen's Homily 14 focusing on Jeremiah 15:10–19, reading verse 10 christologically and applying verse 19 to preachers, though exact text was not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, points out that God's reference to Moses and Samuel in 15:1 is purely hypothetical to underscore the enormity of Judah's guilt, and cannot be used to justify praying to departed saints. Reaching 15:18–19, Calvin refuses to excuse Jeremiah: he states plainly that calling God a "deceptive brook" shows pain and impatience pushing the prophet past proper bounds. That is why God calls him to "return"—a tender yet firm rebuke proving that even God's greatest servants falter and stand in need of repentance. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah and the Lamentations, on chapter 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's characteristic argument on verse 1 regarding intercession of saints and his candid critique of Jeremiah's impatience in 15:18–19, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, dwells warmly on 15:16 and "eating" God's words: he urges believers not merely to let Scripture skim past their ears, but to digest it inwardly until it becomes genuine joy—even though loving God's word may require sitting "alone" (15:17) rather than joining the empty amusements of the world (cf. Psalms 1:1). On 15:19, he reminds readers that serving as God's mouthpiece requires setting aside hasty, fleshly grumbling first. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Matthew Henry's characteristic exposition and pastoral wisdom on verses 16–19, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Jewish thinker and scholar, regards 15:10–18 as one of Scripture's most transparent confessions of the inner price paid for a prophetic calling. Jeremiah's suffering springs not from a gloomy temperament, but from carrying God's own burden on his shoulders—simultaneously delighting in God's word (15:16) and absorbing public hatred and solitude (15:17) until his inner wound feels beyond healing. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Heschel's analysis of Jeremiah's solitude and inner wound in the chapter 15 confession matches the Jeremiah chapter of The Prophets, though exact pages were not checked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, highlights the dramatic balance of chapter 15: the opening half (15:1–9) announces decisively that God's relenting patience has run out ("I am weary of showing compassion"), while the second half (15:10–21) opens an unfiltered showdown between an exhausted prophet and his God. For Brueggemann, God's answer in 15:19 is astonishing: neither brushing off Jeremiah's anguish nor surrendering to it, God calls him back to his post—forging private despair into the endurance of a "fortified wall of bronze" standing firm amid a crumbling nation. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading the two-part structure of chapter 15 and the candid exchange of 15:18–20 aligns closely with Brueggemann's commentary, though exact pages were not verified -->
