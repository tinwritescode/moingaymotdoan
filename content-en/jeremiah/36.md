# Jeremiah 36 — The Scroll Thrown Into the Fire

## Story

In chapter 35, the Rechabites kept their ancestor's instructions across centuries, throwing Judah's hard-heartedness toward God into sharp relief (35:14–16). Now in chapter 36, set in that same reign of King Jehoiakim, royal defiance hits an unforgettable peak: the king personally slices up and burns the written word of God.

In the fourth year of King Jehoiakim, the LORD instructs Jeremiah to take a scroll and write down every single word He has spoken to him concerning Israel, Judah, and all the nations from King Josiah's reign right down to that day. God states His aim openly: hearing all the disaster He plans to bring, "perhaps the house of Judah will turn, every one from his evil way," so He may forgive their iniquity and sin. Jeremiah calls his loyal scribe Baruch son of Neriah; Jeremiah dictates aloud while Baruch inks every word of the LORD onto the scroll (36:1–4).

Because Jeremiah is "restricted" from entering the temple courts himself, he asks Baruch to go in his place on a fast day and read the scroll aloud in the hearing of Jerusalem's citizens and pilgrims visiting from the towns of Judah. Jeremiah still clings to hope that they will lay their plea before the LORD and turn from evil, because the wrath God has declared against this people is terrifyingly fierce. Baruch dutifully follows every instruction (36:5–8).

The moment arrives in the ninth month of Jehoiakim's fifth year, when all Jerusalem and visitors from Judah's towns proclaim a fast before the LORD. Standing in the chamber of the royal secretary Gemariah son of Shaphan, right in the upper courtyard by the entry of the New Gate of the temple, Baruch unrolls the scroll and reads aloud to the crowd. Listening in is Micaiah, son of Gemariah and grandson of Shaphan. As soon as he hears it, Micaiah rushes straight down to the royal palace, enters the secretary's chamber where the senior officials are assembled—Elishama, Delaiah, Elnathan, his own father Gemariah, Zedekiah, and the rest—and reports everything Baruch just read (36:9–13).

The officials promptly send Jehudi to summon Baruch along with the scroll. Baruch brings the scroll, sits down, and reads it through for them. When he finishes, the seasoned ministers "turn to one another in fear" and agree they must report every word to the king. They question Baruch closely on how he wrote it down. Baruch replies simply: "He dictated all these words to me, and I wrote them in ink on the scroll." Knowing King Jehoiakim's ruthless temper firsthand, the officials immediately urge Baruch and Jeremiah to go into hiding where nobody can track them down (36:14–19).

Depositing the scroll in the secretary Elishama's room, they walk into the courtyard and report to the king. Jehoiakim sends Jehudi to fetch the scroll and read it aloud before himself and all the standing officials. Being the ninth month—deep winter—the king sits in his winter apartment with a glowing brazier burning before him. Every time Jehudi finishes reading three or four columns, the king takes a scribe's knife, slices off that strip of parchment, and tosses it straight into the firepot. Read, slice, burn—again and again until the entire scroll turns to ash (36:20–23).

Scripture halts on a chilling observation: hearing such solemn warnings, neither the king nor any of his close attendants "showed any fear or tore their garments." Three officials—Elnathan, Delaiah, and Gemariah—step forward pleading with the king not to burn the scroll, yet he pays them zero attention. Worse still, Jehoiakim orders three royal agents to hunt down and arrest Baruch the scribe and Jeremiah the prophet—"but the LORD hid them" (36:24–26).

No sooner has the first scroll burned to ash than God's word comes again to Jeremiah: take another scroll and rewrite every single word King Jehoiakim just burned. This time a pointed personal sentence against the king joins the scroll: none of his line will sit on David's throne, his corpse will lie cast out to heat by day and frost by night, and every disaster God warned about will descend in full upon his family and all Judah. Once more Jeremiah hands a fresh scroll to Baruch and dictates word for word. Not a single word vanishes; instead, "many similar words were added to them" (36:27–32).

## Key Takeaways

**Right up to the brink of ruin, God still holds the door of repentance open.** For twenty-three straight years Jeremiah had spoken himself hoarse to deaf ears (25:3). Yet God still gathered every warning onto one scroll to be read aloud on a fast day with a tender word—"perhaps" they will turn so He can forgive (36:3; 36:7). Behind Scripture's sternest warnings beats a heart longing to save, never eager to destroy.

**People can burn paper and ink, yet nobody can burn the word of God.** Jehoiakim's penknife and firepot capture power's oldest delusion: imagining that erasing a text and arresting its speakers makes truth disappear. The outcome proved the exact reverse: the burned scroll came back thicker and sharper, while the man holding the knife lost both his dynasty and a decent grave (36:30–32).

**Numb indifference to God's word is deadlier than an angry outburst.** The palace splits into two stark reactions. Listening officials shivered in dread and scrambled to protect the prophet (36:16; 36:19). King Jehoiakim calmly trimmed columns into the coals like scrap paper, feeling no fear and tearing no garments (36:23–24). Once conscience hardens past feeling alarm at God's warning, a person walks beyond reach of rescue.

**God works through quiet courage standing behind the spotlight.** Blocked from stepping onto temple soil himself, Jeremiah still got God's word into the sanctuary and straight inside the royal winter palace through Baruch's risky reading, the steadfast courage of Shaphan's family, and three officials brave enough to urge restraint while everyone else froze (36:8; 36:10; 36:25). And when human safeguards reach their limit, God personally hides His servants (36:26).

## Deeper Meaning

**Father and son, two scrolls, two polar-opposite hearts.** Twenty years earlier, right inside that same temple, workers unearthed the Book of the Law and brought it to King Josiah—Jehoiakim's own father. The instant Josiah heard God's words, he "tore his clothes," humbled himself in tears, and moved God to delay judgment (2 Kings 22:11–13; 22:19). The contrast stings even sharper when we trace the names around both rooms: the royal secretary Shaphan who read that scroll to Josiah had a son Gemariah and grandson Micaiah who still revered God's word, whereas godly Josiah's own son sliced God's scroll into the fire (36:10–12; 36:23–24). Faith never passes automatically through bloodlines; every generation chooses how it answers God's voice.

**Replaced stone tablets and the rewritten scroll.** God instructing Jeremiah to take a second scroll and rewrite every word destroyed by defiance (36:28) directly echoes Moses on Mount Sinai. After the first stone tablets shattered over the golden calf rebellion, God told Moses to chisel two fresh tablets so He would rewrite every original word intact (Exodus 34:1). Human rebellion can smash containers, yet never erase God's covenant or His spoken truth.

**The messenger may be restricted, yet God's word is never chained.** Barred from walking into the temple (36:5), Jeremiah still watched God's word stride through the temple gate and right into the winter palace in Baruch's hands. Six centuries later, sitting chained in a Roman dungeon, the apostle Paul declared the identical reality: he suffered in chains like a criminal, "but the word of God is not chained" (2 Timothy 2:9). Across all Scripture rings the same guarantee: grass withers, flowers fade, heaven and earth pass away, yet God's word endures forever and accomplishes everything He sends it to do (Isaiah 40:8; 55:11; Matthew 24:35).

**History confirming the verdict on Jehoiakim.** Verse 36:30 foretold that none of Jehoiakim's offspring would endure on David's throne and his body would lie unburied—extending the earlier verdict that he would be buried like a donkey outside Jerusalem's gates (22:18–19). Indeed, his son Jehoiachin reigned barely three months before Babylonian exile (22:28–30; cf. 2 Kings 24:6–9), and Judah's last king Zedekiah was Jehoiakim's brother rather than his son. Moreover, right during that same pivotal fourth year—while Baruch sat scribing the scroll overwhelmed by exhaustion and grief—God gave Baruch a personal word of comfort preserved later in chapter 45 (45:1–3).

**God's patient heart spanning both Testaments.** That gentle "perhaps" echoing twice at the chapter's opening (36:3; 36:7) proves God is no icy executioner counting down the seconds. The New Testament unfolds the very same patience: the Lord is not slow in keeping His promise, but patient with us, not wanting anyone to perish, but everyone to come to repentance (2 Peter 3:9).

## Notes & Nuances

**The urgent international crisis behind these two dates.** Jehoiakim's fourth year (605 BC) reshaped the entire ancient Near East: Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon crushed Egypt at the Battle of Carchemish, casting Babylon's towering shadow over Judah. By the ninth month of Jehoiakim's fifth year (December 604 BC), Babylonian armies had swept down the Philistine coast and leveled Ashkelon, driving Jerusalem to proclaim an emergency national fast (36:9). Seen in that charged moment, it makes instant sense why royal ministers shivered hearing Baruch read, while stubborn Jehoiakim lashed out at hearing Babylon would come devastate the land (36:29).

**Why was Jeremiah "restricted" in 36:5?** The Hebrew term likely means he had been barred from entering the temple precinct after earlier clashes and arrests—such as being beaten in the stocks or nearly executed in the temple courtyard—rather than sitting locked inside a dungeon, since in 36:19 officials still urge both him and Baruch to go hide. Whether due to an official ban or ritual restriction, his inability to appear in person became the very catalyst that put his spoken prophecies into permanent written form for the first time.

**The inner tug-of-war inside Elnathan.** Elnathan son of Achbor, appearing among the councilors in 36:12 and urging Jehoiakim not to burn the scroll in 36:25, is the exact same official who earlier carried out Jehoiakim's order chasing the prophet Uriah into Egypt for execution (26:20–23)—and likely the king's own father-in-law (2 Kings 24:8). That quiet detail shows nobody in that court was simple cardboard: even someone who once abetted royal bloodshed could wake up at the eleventh hour and try to stay the king's hand.

**Does Jehoiakim's death contradict 2 Kings 24:6?** Second Kings 24:6 notes concisely that Jehoiakim "rested with his fathers," without recording the manner of his death. That never clashes with Jeremiah 36:30, because "rested with his fathers" in Kings is simply a standard royal obituary formula—used even for King Ahab who died bleeding out in a war chariot. Jehoiakim very likely died during internal turmoil or the Babylonian advance, suffering the disgraceful treatment of his remains just as foretold.

**Verse 36:32 opens a window on how the book of Jeremiah came together.** The closing sentence notes that the replacement scroll not only reproduced the burned original, but had "many similar words added to them." That precious glimpse illuminates how the book we hold today grew organically as Jeremiah and his faithful scribe Baruch collected, preserved, and expanded decades of sermons, oracles, and memoirs.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the fifth-century classic of Jewish law and commentary, points straight to Jehoiakim slicing and burning the scroll in 36:23–24 when ruling how Jews must react upon witnessing a sacred Scripture scroll burned. **Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 36 as a gripping symbolic showdown between royal power and prophetic word. In the king's hands rest penknife and fire—instruments trying to erase inconvenient reality and preserve the illusion of control; in Jeremiah and Baruch's hands rest only parchment and ink. Watching the scroll emerge expanded while Jehoiakim's dynasty ends declares that God's written word—never royal palaces or armies—survives exile to sustain Israel's life. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 36)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: contrasting royal ideology against the resilient prophetic scroll surviving into exile is Brueggemann's celebrated thesis on Jeremiah 36 -->
