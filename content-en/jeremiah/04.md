# Jeremiah 4 — Circumcise Your Hearts and the Earth Returns to Void

## Story

Chapter 4 opens as a direct answer to the model confession at the close of chapter 3. The LORD urges Israel to return genuinely, not halfway: if the people remove their detestable idols and swear by His name in truth, justice, and righteousness, then the nations will be blessed by Him and glory in Him. Turning to the people of Judah and Jerusalem, God employs two forceful metaphors: "Break up your unplowed ground, and do not sow among the thorns," and "Circumcise yourselves to the LORD, and remove the foreskins of your hearts!" If they persist in stubbornness, His wrath will blaze out like a fire no one can extinguish (4:1–4).

Right on the heels of that final plea to repent, the blast of the war trumpet rings across the land. Urgent orders fly: blow the trumpet throughout the land, raise a signal toward Zion, and flee for safety into the fortified cities, because a massive disaster is sweeping down from the north. A destroyer of nations has set out from his lair like a lion leaving its thicket, ready to turn the land into a wasteland and its towns into rubble. The whole nation must put on sackcloth and wail; on that day kings and officials will lose heart, priests will be horrified, and prophets will be astounded. Amid that terror, Jeremiah himself cries out in raw anguish: "Ah, Lord GOD, how completely You have deceived this people and Jerusalem by saying, 'You will have peace,' while a sword is at our throats!" (4:5–10).

The storm of war draws closer with every line. A scorching wind blows off the barren desert heights—not the gentle breeze a farmer uses to winnow grain from chaff, but a gale of judgment. The invading army advances like storm clouds, its chariots like a whirlwind, its horses swifter than eagles. From Dan in the far north to the hills of Ephraim right at Jerusalem's doorstep, grim bulletins race southward: besiegers have ringed the capital like watchmen guarding a field. Even inside that closing ring, a fervent plea still sounds: "Wash the evil from your heart, O Jerusalem, so that you may be saved." Yet finally God states plainly that their own ways and wicked deeds have brought this bitterness stabbing straight into their hearts (4:11–18).

The scene grows too unbearable for the listener to remain calm. A gut-wrenching cry bursts out: "My anguish, my anguish! I writhe in pain! Oh, the walls of my heart! My heart is pounding within me; I cannot keep silent!" His ears cannot escape the trumpet blast, the battle cry, crash piling upon crash; the whole land is ruined and its tents collapse in an instant. Then the LORD diagnoses the root of the tragedy: His people are foolish and do not know Him—senseless children who are "skilled in doing evil, but they do not know how to do good" (4:19–22).

Jeremiah then enters a chilling vision, watching the entire world unwind backward to the moment before creation. Four times he repeats, "I looked": looking at the earth, he sees it "formless and void"; looking at the heavens, their light is gone; the mountains quake and all the hills sway; not a single human being remains, and every bird of the sky has fled; fertile farmland has turned into wilderness and every city lies in ruins before the fierce anger of the LORD. Yet right inside that apocalyptic canvas, a single brief sentence preserves hope: "The whole land will be desolate, yet I will not finish its destruction." Even so, the decree has gone out: the earth will mourn and the heavens grow dark, for God has spoken and will not turn back (4:23–28).

The chapter closes with chaotic flight. At the roar of horsemen and archers, every town empties out as people scramble into thickets and climb among the rocks. Desperate Jerusalem is pictured as a woman dressing in scarlet, putting on gold ornaments, and widening her eyes with paint to lure back old lovers—all in vain, for the very ones she chased despise her and seek her life. The final note is a cry like a woman giving birth to her first child—the gasp of Daughter Zion stretching out her hands and moaning, "Woe to me! My soul is fainting before the murderers!" (4:29–31).

## Key Takeaways

**Real repentance requires breaking up the heart to its roots.** God does not ask Judah to stack a few extra rituals on top of old habits. He tells them to "break up your unplowed ground" instead of scattering seed among thorns (4:3), and to "remove the foreskins of your hearts" (4:4). Polishing behavior on the outside while leaving a hardened heart untouched is like sowing seed into a briar patch: nothing will ever survive.

**Human stubbornness unravels the whole fabric of life.** When people grow "skilled in doing evil, but they do not know how to do good" (4:22), the fallout is not a handful of minor inconveniences; it fractures the good order God built. The vision of the earth reverting to "formless and void" (4:23) reveals that sin always runs in reverse against God's creative work.

**A true prophet never gloats over judgment.** Reading 4:19–21, we see that Jeremiah does not stand on a pedestal lecturing his people with cold detachment. He doubles over in visceral pain, unable to stay silent as the trumpet of war closes in on his homeland. Loving the truth never excludes heartbreaking compassion for those walking into ruin.

**Even in the harshest sentence, God sets a limit of mercy.** Verse 4:27 is brief, yet it changes the entire horizon of the chapter: "The whole land will be desolate, yet I will not finish its destruction." God's anger is real and cannot be brushed aside, yet He refuses to wipe out His covenant and His people altogether.

## Deeper Meaning

**Rewinding to the opening of Genesis.** The phrase "formless and void" in 4:23 repeats the exact two Hebrew words describing the earth before God spoke light into being (Genesis 1:2). The entire passage from 4:23 to 4:26 plays like a film running backward through the six days of creation: light vanishes, mountains shake, humanity disappears, birds flee, and fruitful fields revert to barren desert. Abandoning the Creator pushes the world back toward chaos.

**Circumcision of the heart.** Already in Deuteronomy, Moses had reminded Israel that physical circumcision on the body was never enough; they needed to put away stubbornness, and God Himself would circumcise their hearts so they could love Him wholly (Deuteronomy 10:16; 30:6). Centuries later, the apostle Paul affirms the exact same reality: true circumcision is not outward and physical according to the letter, but inward, of the heart, by the Spirit (Romans 2:28–29).

**The promise of blessing to the nations.** Verse 4:2 ties Israel's repentance straight back to God's covenant promise to Abraham that all nations on earth would be blessed through his offspring (Genesis 12:3; 22:18). Israel was chosen not to hoard privilege for herself, but to live in such justice and truth that surrounding nations would see the glory of God.

**Sowing among thorns and being wise about what is good.** The charge to plow fresh ground rather than sow among thorns (4:3; cf. Hosea 10:12) anticipates Jesus' parable of the sower, where seed falling among thorns is choked by the worries of life and the deceitfulness of wealth (Matthew 13:7; 13:22). And the rebuke that the people are "skilled in doing evil, but they do not know how to do good" (4:22) stands in exact contrast to Paul's longing for believers to be "wise about what is good and innocent about what is evil" (Romans 16:19).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 4:10 say, "How completely You have deceived this people"?** That sentence startles readers: is Jeremiah accusing God of lying? Reading further in Jeremiah, we discover that the voices endlessly promising "Peace, peace!" belong to false prophets claiming to speak in the LORD's name (6:14; 14:13–14). Old Testament writers were accustomed to viewing every event under God's ultimate sovereignty, including His allowing false prophets to feed illusions to a people who refused to hear the truth (cf. 1 Kings 22:21–23). In 4:10, Jeremiah voices his stunned grief as the nation is lulled into a fantasy of safety while the sword already rests at its throat.

**Who cries out "My anguish, my anguish!" in 4:19–21?** Commentators offer two main readings: most hear the anguished voice of Jeremiah himself trembling before the vision of his homeland's devastation; others see Jeremiah's voice merging with God's own grief—especially since 4:22 immediately shifts to God speaking of "My people." Both readings reinforce one another: the prophet carries inside his own chest the very sorrow of God.

**Is the vision of 4:23–26 the end of the world or the Babylonian invasion?** Cosmic language—darkened skies, no human left standing—is a classic poetic device of the prophets to convey the staggering scale of the calamity about to strike Judah. Verses 4:27 and 4:29 make clear that the immediate horizon is the historical collapse of the land before horsemen and archers rather than the literal end of the planet—even though it foreshadows the final day of judgment.

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, unpacks the farming image in 4:3: "Break up your unplowed ground, and do not sow among the thorns." He compares an unrepentant heart to fallow soil hardened over years and overrun with briars; unless we submit to the plow of God's word cutting deep to uproot sin, every moral seed scattered on top is wasted. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: comparing God's word to a plow breaking up hard soil is a familiar Matthew Henry exposition of 4:3, though not verified verbatim -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Jewish thinker and scholar, cites the gut-wrenching cry of 4:19 and the uncreation vision of 4:23–26 as emotional peaks in the book of Jeremiah. He observes that the prophet's pain is so overwhelming that his own body feels as if it is breaking apart alongside his nation's fate—showing that a prophet never stands outside history delivering cold verdicts, but bears the entire tragedy within his own being. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Heschel cites 4:19 and the chapter 4 vision in his chapter on Jeremiah to highlight the prophet's deep empathy, though exact page numbers were not checked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, calls the poem of 4:23–26 a vision of *de-creation*, as the language of Genesis 1:2 returns to signal that an ordered world is collapsing back into chaos.
