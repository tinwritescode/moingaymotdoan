# Jeremiah 1 — The Lord Calls Jeremiah

## Story

The book opens like a historical chronicle, immediately naming the speaker and the turbulent era of his life. These are the words of Jeremiah son of Hilkiah, one of the priests living in the village of Anathoth in the territory of Benjamin, a few miles northeast of Jerusalem. The word of the LORD began to come to him in the thirteenth year of King Josiah of Judah, continued through the reign of King Jehoiakim, and stretched until the end of the eleventh year of King Zedekiah—the very year Jerusalem fell and its people were carried into exile in the fifth month. Just three opening verses pack in more than forty years of standing amid the death throes of the kingdom of Judah (1:1–3).

From that sweeping historical canvas, the narrative narrows to a private conversation between the LORD and a young man. Before assigning any task, God speaks to him about the true origin of his life: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you." Before he was even born, Jeremiah had been set apart by God and appointed as a prophet not merely to Judah, but to the nations (1:4–5).

Hearing this, Jeremiah immediately tries to back out. He pleads, "Ah, Lord GOD, I surely do not know how to speak, for I am only a child!" Faced with so heavy a charge, he sees only his own inexperience and inadequacy. Yet the LORD rejects age as an excuse. He brushes the objection aside, commanding him to go everywhere He sends him and speak everything He commands. Then He places a brief, unshakable promise into his heart: "Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you" (1:6–8).

At that very moment, the LORD reaches out His hand, touches Jeremiah's mouth, and declares, "Behold, I have put My words in your mouth." From that day forward, God appoints him over nations and kingdoms with a startling authority summed up in six verbs: "to uproot and tear down, to destroy and overthrow, to build and plant." The mission tilts heavily toward demolishing what has rotted through, yet it still closes with the work of building and planting anew (1:9–10).

As soon as the call ends, God shows Jeremiah two signs in succession. First He asks, "Jeremiah, what do you see?" He answers that he sees a branch of an almond tree. God commends him for seeing rightly and explains: He is "watching over My word to perform it." In Hebrew, the word for the almond tree—the first tree to blossom as winter ends—sounds nearly identical to the word *watching*. That ordinary sight reminds Jeremiah that God never falls asleep on the words He has spoken (1:11–12).

A second time God asks what he sees. Now a boiling pot appears before his eyes, tilting away from the north. God interprets it plainly: disaster from the north is about to pour out over all the inhabitants of the land. He Himself will summon all the clans of the kingdoms of the north to come, set up their thrones of judgment right at the entrance to Jerusalem's gates, and besiege her walls along with all the other cities of Judah. It is a just judgment for the wickedness the people have committed: they have forsaken the LORD, burned incense to other gods, and worshiped the works of their own hands (1:13–16).

The opening chapter closes with a bracing command woven together with a promise of protection. God tells Jeremiah to brace himself, stand up, and speak everything He commands: "Do not be terrified by them, or I will terrify you before them." He hides nothing of what waits ahead: the whole land—from the kings of Judah, its officials, and its priests to the common people—will rise up against him. Yet that very day, God makes him "a fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls." They will fight against him, but they will never overcome him, for God repeats once more the promise that launched his prophetic life: "I am with you to deliver you" (1:17–19).

## Key Takeaways

**A calling begins with God's choice, not with human readiness.** Jeremiah did not volunteer, nor did he possess polished eloquence to sway crowds. God knew him and set him apart before he ever took shape in the womb (1:5). When God sends someone out, He does not wait until they feel capable; He touches their very point of weakness and puts His own words in their mouth (1:9).

**God's word exercises real power over the destiny of nations.** Jeremiah wields no sword and commands no army—only the word of God placed on his lips. Yet God says He has appointed him over nations and kingdoms to uproot and tear down, to build and plant (1:10). On the surface, history looks like empires tearing one another apart; seen with eyes of faith, God Himself is watching over His word to bring it to pass amid every upheaval (1:12).

**Tearing down is not spiteful destruction, but clearing ground to build anew.** Out of the six verbs God hands Jeremiah, four describe demolition and the last two describe building and planting (1:10). Most of his message sounds harsh because a house rotted to its foundations cannot be patched up superficially. Even so, God's ultimate purpose remains healing and a fresh beginning after the rubble.

**Faithfulness does not promise an easy peace; it promises God's presence.** God does not sugarcoat the road ahead for this young man. He states plainly that everyone from kings to ordinary citizens will turn against him (1:18–19). What enables a fragile human being to stand firm like a bronze wall and an iron pillar is not natural toughness, but God's presence: He is with him to deliver him (1:8; 1:19).

**The root of disaster is forsaking God to worship what our own hands make.** The boiling pot is no senseless accident of history. God points straight to the cause: the people have forsaken Him, burned incense to other gods, and bowed down to things their own hands fashioned (1:16). Whenever people turn their backs on their Maker to lean on idols, collapse from within has already begun before any enemy reaches the city gates.

## Deeper Meaning

**A prophet walking in the footsteps of Moses.** The conversation calling Jeremiah strongly echoes the call of Moses at the burning bush. Moses had once protested that he was slow of speech, and God answered with the exact same twofold assurance: He would be with him, and He would be with his mouth to teach him what to say (Exodus 3:11–12; 4:10–12). Even more directly, the words "I have put My words in your mouth" (1:9) recall God's promise to raise up a prophet like Moses, putting His words in that prophet's mouth to declare everything He commands (Deuteronomy 18:18). Jeremiah's whole life is a summons calling Judah back to the covenant made through Moses.

**The hand of God touching human lips.** The LORD reaching out to touch Jeremiah's mouth recalls the prophet Isaiah, who trembled over his unclean lips until a seraph touched them with a burning coal from the altar to take away his guilt (Isaiah 6:5–7). The two scenes differ—one highlights cleansing, the other highlights entrusting the word—yet both converge on one truth: no one can presume to speak for God without a direct touch and commission from Him.

**Known from the womb.** God's declaration that He knew and formed Jeremiah in the womb (1:5) harmonizes with the psalmist's praise of the One who knitted us together in secret and saw all our days before even one of them came to be (Psalms 139:13–16). In the New Testament, the apostle Paul borrows Jeremiah's exact phrasing to describe his own calling: God had set him apart from his mother's womb to preach His Son among the Gentiles (Galatians 1:15–16).

**Six verbs tying the opening of the book to its closing hope.** The four verbs of uprooting and tearing down paired with the two verbs of building and planting in 1:10 are not merely a striking introduction; they form the skeleton of the entire book of Jeremiah. Later, when Judah sits exiled in Babylon with seemingly no road home, the LORD repeats the exact same pairing to promise He will bring them back to the land, build them up and not tear them down, plant them and not uproot them (24:6; 31:28). God's anger has a boundary, while His purpose of restoration endures.

**The promise "I am with you" reaching into the New Testament.** God's charge telling Jeremiah not to fear because He is with him (1:8; 1:19) is the lifeline sustaining everyone God sends into hostility. At the close of Matthew's Gospel, right before sending the disciples out to all nations to teach everything He commanded, Jesus leaves them with that same ancient assurance: "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (Matthew 28:20).

## Notes & Nuances

**The word "child" does not mean Jeremiah was a small boy.** In Hebrew, the term is used broadly to cover an unmarried young man or someone still young and untested before a weighty responsibility. When first called, Jeremiah was probably around seventeen to twenty years old—mature enough to grasp that he would have to confront seasoned kings, officials, and priests, yet young enough to feel overwhelmed.

**Jeremiah's father Hilkiah was likely not the high priest under Josiah.** Second Kings mentions a high priest named Hilkiah who discovered the Book of the Law in the temple in the eighteenth year of King Josiah (2 Kings 22:8). Most scholars, however, believe these were two different men sharing a common name. That Hilkiah belonged to the Zadokite line serving at the Jerusalem temple, whereas Jeremiah's family belonged to the priests of Anathoth—the village to which King Solomon had banished the priest Abiathar centuries earlier (1 Kings 2:26–27). If so, from his very upbringing Jeremiah stood on the margins of the temple establishment in the capital.

**Who is the enemy "from the north"?** Chapter 1 deliberately leaves the nation unnamed. In the thirteenth year of Josiah—around 627 BC—the Assyrian Empire was fading and Babylon was only beginning to rise. Reading onward through the book and Judah's history, we learn that this northern storm is the Babylonian army that will burn Jerusalem to the ground (2 Kings 25:8–11). Although Babylon lay to the east of Judah, ancient armies avoiding the impassable desert had to march north along the Fertile Crescent before sweeping down into Israel from the north.

**Reading 1:5 in the flow of the chapter.** The sentence "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you" is often cited to affirm the dignity of unborn life and God's intimate knowledge of every person—truths fully in harmony with the rest of Scripture (Psalms 139:13–16). Still, it helps to remember the verse's immediate purpose: affirming God's specific call placing Jeremiah as a prophet to the nations, rather than serving as a formula to label every personal preference of ours as a "predetermined plan."

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, the third-century Alexandrian scholar and biblical interpreter, opens his series of homilies on Jeremiah by asking why God began speaking to him in the thirteenth year of Josiah, a good king. In his view, this reveals God's mercy: He sends warnings during a season of relative calm, holding open the door of repentance before judgment strikes. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pauses carefully over the stern warning in 1:17: do not be terrified before them, lest God Himself terrify you before them. Calvin remarks that this is no cruel threat, but a remedy to forge courage in God's servants: whoever trembles before human authority and dodges the truth will eventually lose all courage; only the one who fears God above all else stands firm enough to speak plainly before kings and crowds. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's line of exposition on 1:17 contrasting fear of God with fear of people, though without checking the original text on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, highlights Jeremiah's humility in calling himself a child who does not know how to speak, treating it as a hopeful sign in someone God sends because he does not rely on his own cleverness. On the sign of the almond branch, Henry notes that the almond is the earliest tree to blossom in the year, so God uses it to warn the people not to dismiss judgment as far off: it is hastening faster than they imagine, for God is always awake over His word. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Matthew Henry's characteristic exposition of humility in 1:6 and the almond branch in 1:11–12, though without verifying against the original text -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Jewish thinker and scholar, sees right from the call in chapter 1 the portrait of a deeply sensitive man placed under a burden heavier than he can bear. He stresses that a prophet is no unfeeling loudspeaker broadcasting decrees from heaven, but someone who carries inside himself God's own grief, anger, and compassion over His people's unfaithfulness; thus Jeremiah's entire subsequent life becomes a fierce inner struggle between love for his fellow citizens and loyalty to God's word. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: divine pathos and Jeremiah's sensitive spirit are well-known central themes of the Jeremiah chapter in The Prophets; rated medium since summarized from memory without checking the exact page discussing chapter 1 -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, views the six verbs of 1:10—four of uprooting and tearing down, two of building and planting—as the master key unlocking the entire book of Jeremiah. In his reading, Jeremiah's message does not merely comment on contemporary politics; it actively dismantles false illusions about Jerusalem's invincibility, while even amid exile holding open the hope that God will rebuild and replant His people once again. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the twin motifs of plucking up (exile) and planting (homecoming) drawn from Jeremiah 1:10 form the famous overarching structure and central thesis of this commentary -->
