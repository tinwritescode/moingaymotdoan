# Jeremiah 43 — Stones Buried Before Pharaoh's Palace Gate

## Story

The moment Jeremiah finished proclaiming the LORD's message after ten days of waiting in the previous chapter, he ran straight into flat defiance. Azariah son of Hoshaiah (the same Jezaniah of chapter 42), Johanan son of Kareah, and "all the arrogant men" stood up together and threw the accusation in his face: "You are lying!" They insisted the LORD had never sent him to forbid them from settling in Egypt; instead, they claimed Baruch son of Neriah—Jeremiah's devoted scribe—was inciting him against them in order to hand the whole group over to the Chaldeans to be killed or dragged into exile in Babylon (43:1–3).

Their solemn oath to obey God whether the answer was pleasant or unpleasant (42:5–6) evaporated in an instant. Johanan, the commanders, and all the people flatly refused to obey the voice of the LORD telling them to remain in the land of Judah. They swept up the entire Judean remnant—including all those who had recently returned with joy from neighboring countries to rebuild their homeland (40:11–12)—men, women, children, and the king's daughters, every single person whom Nebuzaradan captain of the guard had placed in Gedaliah's care (43:4–6).

They even forced the prophet Jeremiah and Baruch son of Neriah to come along with them. The whole company streamed down into the land of Egypt and arrived at Tahpanhes, a major border fortress on the northeastern edge of the Nile Delta. The migration account closes with one brief, heavy clause: "for they did not obey the voice of the LORD" (43:6–7).

The commanders could drag the prophet wherever they pleased, yet no one could gag the mouth of God. Right there in Tahpanhes, the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah once more. God instructed him to perform a public sign in full view of the Judeans gathered there: he was to take large stones in his own hands and embed them in the mortar of the brick pavement right at the entrance to Pharaoh's palace in Tahpanhes (43:8–9).

Jeremiah then explained the meaning of that strange act before the refugees hoping to hide under Egypt's king. The LORD of Hosts, the God of Israel, declared that he himself would send for "My servant Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon," who would set his throne directly over the stones Jeremiah had just buried and spread his royal pavilion above them (43:10).

The Babylonian king's march into Egypt would be unstoppable: those destined for death would meet death, those destined for captivity would go into captivity, and those destined for the sword would fall by the sword. The LORD himself would kindle a fire in the temples of Egypt's gods; Nebuchadnezzar would burn them down and haul their idols away as captives. He would "wrap himself with the land of Egypt as a shepherd wraps himself in his garment," and then stroll away from there unharmed. He would also shatter the sacred pillars of Beth-shemesh—Egypt's famed center of sun worship—and burn the temples of Egypt's gods to ashes (43:11–13).

## Key Takeaways

**When hearts harden, people attack the truth-teller rather than face the truth.** Only moments earlier, Johanan and the commanders had humbly approached Jeremiah to ask for God's guidance. Yet the instant God's word contradicted their settled plan, they branded him a liar and cooked up a political conspiracy theory accusing Baruch of pulling the strings to sell them out to Babylon (43:2–3). Smearing the messenger's motives is always the easiest way for a stubborn conscience to dodge obedience.

**The stubbornness of leaders drags an entire community over the cliff.** Johanan and the military officers did not merely slip off to Egypt on their own; they rounded up every last Judean survivor—women, children, and recent returnees from surrounding lands—and even compelled Jeremiah and Baruch to march along (43:5–6). Because of the fear and pride of a handful of armed men, the last faint hope of restoring Judean life in the promised land was stamped out completely.

**God's word cannot be fenced in by borders or human will.** The Judean leaders may have hauled Jeremiah down to Tahpanhes hoping to keep him as a lucky charm, or to stop him from contradicting them back in Judah. Yet right on foreign soil, outside the gates of Pharaoh's palace, the word of the LORD spoke to him with undiminished clarity and authority (43:8–10). People may abandon the land where God placed them, but they can never outrun his sovereignty or his voice.

**Every refuge people choose outside God will eventually crumble.** The Judeans fled to Tahpanhes to shelter behind Pharaoh's fortified walls and Egypt's wealth. Yet God foretold that right on the palace terrace they considered impregnable, the king of Babylon would pitch his throne, torch the temples of their gods, and sweep up the entire kingdom as effortlessly as a shepherd throws a cloak over his shoulders (43:10–13). Trading God's promise for a worldly power only exchanges solid rock for a mirage.

## Deeper Meaning

**Brick mortar in Egypt revives the bitter memory of Exodus.** The detail of Jeremiah burying stones in the mortar of the brick pavement at Tahpanhes (43:9) strikes Israel's deepest collective memory: for centuries their ancestors had groaned in Egypt under brutal labor "in mortar and brick" beneath Pharaoh's whips (Exodus 1:14). God had stretched out his mighty arm to bring them out of that house of slavery, yet now their descendants marched voluntarily straight back to the land of bricks looking for peace—a tragic reversal fulfilling the chilling warning that covenant-breakers would be sent back to Egypt (Deuteronomy 28:68).

**Judgment once again falls on the gods of Egypt.** On the night of the original Passover, the LORD declared that he would "execute judgment against all the gods of Egypt" (Exodus 12:12). Centuries later at Tahpanhes, he proclaimed again that he would set fire to Egypt's temples and shatter the obelisks of Beth-shemesh—ancient On, the sun-worship center where Joseph once married a priest's daughter (43:12–13; Genesis 41:45). The very deities the Judean refugees would soon be tempted to honor in chapter 44 proved unable to save even their own statues from fire and captivity.

**A pagan monarch called "My servant."** For the third time in this book, God calls Nebuchadnezzar "My servant" (43:10; cf. 25:9; 27:6). The title never implies that Babylon's king was a devout worshiper of the LORD; rather, it asserts God's absolute rule over the history of empires. Just as Isaiah called the Persian king Cyrus God's anointed (Isaiah 45:1), and Ezekiel recorded God granting Egypt to Nebuchadnezzar as "wages" for the labor his army had performed for the LORD (Ezekiel 29:18–20), the mightiest powers on earth are ultimately temporary tools in the LORD's hands.

**Preaching in action when ears have clamped shut.** Jeremiah's act of sinking stones into the pavement outside Pharaoh's palace (43:9) continues a signature series of sign-acts throughout his ministry—like smashing a clay jar before the elders to portray Jerusalem shattered beyond repair (19:1–11). When people deliberately shut their ears against spoken sermons, God sends his prophet to stage a vivid drama right before their eyes, so that every time they crossed Pharaoh's courtyard, the memory of stones buried beneath their feet would remind them that God's word never falls to the ground.

**Pinning political treason on truth-tellers is an ancient tactic.** Unable to refute Jeremiah's message, Azariah and Johanan accused him of being manipulated by Baruch to betray the nation (43:2–3). Two centuries earlier, the priest Amaziah had similarly charged the prophet Amos with conspiring against the king in order to drive him out of Bethel (Amos 7:10–13). In the New Testament, Paul foretold a time when people would not endure sound teaching, but gather teachers to tell them what their itching ears longed to hear (2 Timothy 4:3–4). The same hardened heart repeats its script in every age.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did they make Baruch the scapegoat in verse 3?** Baruch was the loyal scribe who had written down Jeremiah's dictated scroll of prophecies (36:4). Rather than openly branding the aging, widely respected prophet an outright traitor by nature, Azariah and Johanan pulled a familiar political maneuver: blaming a "handler behind the scenes," implying that the younger assistant Baruch was steering an elderly Jeremiah toward a pro-Babylonian line. That spin made it easier to sway the crowd while appearing slightly less disrespectful toward Jeremiah's long reputation—even though one verse earlier they had already called him a liar to his face (43:2).

**Did Nebuchadnezzar actually invade Egypt as verses 10–13 foretold?** Ancient Babylonian records—specifically a clay chronicle tablet now housed in the British Museum—confirm that in his thirty-seventh regnal year (around 568–567 BC), Nebuchadnezzar launched a military campaign against Pharaoh Amasis of Egypt. Because the tablet is damaged in several places, historians cannot determine how far inland Babylonian forces marched or whether the king physically pitched his tent on the Tahpanhes terrace; nevertheless, the campaign aligns with both Jeremiah's and Ezekiel's prophecies that Babylonian power would strike Egypt and shatter the Judean refugees' illusion of safety (Ezekiel 29:18–20; 30:17–18).

**Two ways to translate the shepherd image in verse 12.** Most traditional English versions—including the BSB—follow the common sense of the Hebrew verb: Nebuchadnezzar will "wrap himself with the land of Egypt as a shepherd wraps himself in his garment," depicting the Babylonian king claiming the vast nation as casually as throwing on a cloak. However, the ancient Greek Septuagint and many modern scholars read the same verb in a slightly different sense—"pick clean of lice"—painting a picture of Babylon stripping Egypt clean of its wealth the way a shepherd picks vermin off his cloak before walking calmly away. Either rendering vividly exposes the humiliating helplessness of mighty Egypt.

**Where were Tahpanhes and Beth-shemesh?** Tahpanhes (Greek *Daphnae*, modern archaeological site Tell Defenneh) was a strategic fortress guarding the northeastern gateway of the Nile Delta, the first major stop for travelers entering Egypt from Judah; excavators there uncovered a large brick pavement directly in front of a fortified royal complex. Jeremiah had already mentioned Tahpanhes early in the book as a source of Judah's disgrace (2:16). Beth-shemesh means "House of the Sun" in Hebrew—the ancient city of On (Greek *Heliopolis*, now on the outskirts of Cairo), famed for its towering obelisks erected in honor of the sun god Ra.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, records the events of chapter 43 as the final fatal misstep of the Judean remnant. He narrates how Johanan and the commanders rejected Jeremiah's word, claiming Baruch had put him up to it to curry favor with Babylon, and forced both the people and Jeremiah and Baruch to flee into Egypt. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, focuses on the phrase "all the arrogant men" (43:2) to pinpoint the root disease of Azariah and Johanan: pride is what emboldens human beings to call the word of God a lie. In Calvin's analysis, blaming Baruch is the classic tactic of rebels who dare not declare open war on God directly, so they slander his human instruments instead. On calling Nebuchadnezzar "My servant" (43:10), Calvin clarifies that the title does not commend the pagan ruler's character, but displays God's sovereignty so vast that even tyrants unwittingly execute his verdicts. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 43)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's exposition of pride in 43:2, the ploy of blaming Baruch, and the meaning of "My servant" in 43:10; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, sees in verses 2–3 the stark unmasking of hypocrisy: moments after vowing unconditional obedience to God, these men were ready to call the aging prophet a liar to his face. Commenting on Jeremiah hearing God's voice right outside Pharaoh's palace despite being dragged to Tahpanhes against his will (43:8–9), Henry draws a comforting lesson: enemies may exile or confine God's messengers, yet no earthly power can sever communion between God and his servants or muzzle his word. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah chapter 43)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's characteristic reflections on the turnaround of hypocrites and the unbound word of God at Tahpanhes; original text not checked -->

**Derek Kidner**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, highlights the bitter symbolism of Jeremiah burying stones in the brick mortar at Tahpanhes (43:9). In his reading, the whole migration to Egypt is a tragic reversal of the Exodus: Israel's ancestors had been liberated from brickmaking slavery, only for their descendants to sprint voluntarily back to the very same clay. The stones hidden at the threshold of Pharaoh's palace served as an undeniable marker foretelling that on the exact spot where Judah trusted Egypt's might, Babylon's throne would stand. *(The Message of Jeremiah, 1987, on chapter 43)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Kidner reading the sign of stones buried in brick mortar as a reversal of the Exodus in chapter 43; exact page number not checked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 43 as a head-on collision between two incompatible ways of interpreting history. Johanan's military faction sees everything strictly through the lens of power politics and intrigue—reducing prophecy to a plot hatched by Baruch on Babylon's behalf (43:2–3)—while the narrator repeats a single theological diagnosis: they refused to obey the voice of the LORD (43:4; 43:7). Brueggemann also notes that foretelling the burning and capture of Egypt's gods (43:12) amounts to a declaration of war on the entire religious and political order in which the Judeans were placing their trust. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 43)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Brueggemann's analysis of the clash between Johanan's political reading and the LORD's voice, as well as the fall of Egypt's gods in chapter 43; exact page number not checked -->
