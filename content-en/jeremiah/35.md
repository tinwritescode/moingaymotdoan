# Jeremiah 35 — The Obedience of the Rechabites

## Story

Right on the heels of King Zedekiah and his officials breaking their covenant oath in chapter 34, the book of Jeremiah steps backward in time to "the days of Jehoiakim son of Josiah, king of Judah"—over a decade before Jerusalem fell—to set a shining model of obedience right alongside the capital's betrayal. The LORD instructs Jeremiah to seek out the Rechabite clan, invite them into one of the side chambers right inside the temple of the LORD, and offer them wine to drink (35:1–2).

Jeremiah carries out God's instructions to the letter. He brings Jaazaniah, his brothers, all his sons, and the entire house of the Rechabites into a chamber inside the temple—the room belonging to the sons of Hanan, "the man of God," right next to the officials' chamber and just above the room of Maaseiah the doorkeeper. Right in front of the assembled clan, Jeremiah sets out "pitchers full of wine and cups" and invites them: "Drink wine!" (35:3–5).

Without a moment's hesitation, the Rechabites politely decline: "We do not drink wine." They explain clearly the strict rule handed down across their family: long ago their ancestor "Jonadab son of Rechab" commanded his descendants forever never to drink wine, never to build permanent houses, never to sow seed, and never to plant or own vineyards—instead they must "live in tents all your days, so that you may live a long time in the land where you are sojourners." Generation after generation, every man along with his wife, sons, and daughters has obeyed their ancestor's instructions to the letter: no wine, no permanent houses, no fields or vineyards, living strictly in tents. They even add an honest footnote explaining why they happen to be inside Jerusalem's walled city today: solely when Nebuchadnezzar's Babylonian army and the Aramean forces marched into the countryside did they temporarily retreat inside Jerusalem to escape open-country warfare (35:6–11).

At that exact moment, the word of the LORD comes to Jeremiah, transforming that simple wine test into a blistering public indictment aimed straight at the people of Judah and the citizens of Jerusalem. God holds up two portraits side by side to put the whole nation to shame: the command of one mortal ancestor, Jonadab, forbidding his children to drink wine has been kept faithfully across centuries right down to this very day; meanwhile the living God has spoken to Judah "again and again," tirelessly sending all His servants the prophets pleading with them to abandon evil ways, amend their deeds, and stop following other gods so they might remain on the land He gave them—yet "you have not inclined your ear or listened to Me!" (35:12–15).

An airtight verdict falls immediately from that unanswerable comparison. Because Jonadab's descendants upheld their ancestor's command completely while Judah ignored God Himself, the LORD of Hosts vows He will bring upon Judah and every citizen of Jerusalem all the disaster He pronounced against them, "because I spoke to them, but they did not listen, and I called to them, but they did not answer" (35:16–17).

Closing the chapter stands a rare, extraordinarily generous blessing delivered by Jeremiah from the LORD specifically to the Rechabite clan. Because they kept every command and rule handed down by their forefather Jonadab, the LORD of Hosts makes them a solemn pledge: "Jonadab son of Rechab will never fail to have a man to stand before Me" (35:18–19).

## Key Takeaways

**Loyalty to sound convictions outshines bending with convenient circumstances.** The Rechabites had every plausible excuse imaginable to bend their rule that afternoon: war had already forced them out of tents into town, they sat inside God's own temple, and a renowned prophet of the LORD poured the wine himself (35:2–5; 35:11). Still they refused to use crisis or social pressure as cover to trade away their family's vow. Principles are genuinely tested only when compromising looks completely reasonable.

**God praised the Rechabites for their faithfulness, not for banning wine.** Scripture never requires all Israelites to abandon homes for tents or abstain entirely from wine like the Rechabites. The razor's edge of chapter 35 lies in the stark contrast of 35:14–16: if a marginal clan can honor a demanding rule from a long-dead forefather for over two centuries, what excuse does Judah have for brushing off the life-giving commands of the living God?

**God's tireless patience exposes how senseless human stubbornness is.** The vivid Hebrew idiom "again and again"—literally *rising early*—repeated in 35:14–15 pictures God as a devoted parent waking at first light day after day to call His children back through prophet after prophet. Jerusalem's coming ruin sprang never from divine impatience or harshness, but solely because "I spoke to them, but they did not listen, and I called to them, but they did not answer" (35:17).

**God notices and honors quiet faithfulness, however humble a person's background.** The Rechabites belonged neither to Jerusalem's aristocracy nor even to Israel's main tribes—they came from humble nomadic roots on society's edge. Yet right as Judah's royal line and elites braced for collapse, this outsider clan received an enduring promise: they will "never fail to have a man to stand before Me" (35:19).

## Deeper Meaning

**A deliberate contrast pairing chapter 34 and chapter 35.** Even though chapter 35 happened more than ten years earlier than chapter 34 (under Jehoiakim rather than Zedekiah), the editors of Jeremiah placed the two chapters side by side on purpose. In chapter 34, Judah's king and nobles swore a solemn temple covenant to free slaves and broke it almost immediately; in chapter 35, the Rechabite clan honored their forefather's charge faithfully across centuries without blinking. Set side by side, Jerusalem stands utterly without excuse.

**The historical roots of Jonadab and the Rechabites across the Old Testament.** Jonadab son of Rechab (35:6) was no obscure figure: roughly two and a half centuries before Jeremiah, during the purge of Baal worship in northern Israel, he met Jehu, clasped his hand in shared zeal, and rode in his chariot to dismantle Ahab's house and Baal's cult in Samaria (2 Kings 10:15–17). First Chronicles traces the Rechabites to the Kenites—nomadic kin of Moses' father-in-law who journeyed with Israel into the Promised Land (1 Chronicles 2:55). Their austere lifestyle of tents, no vineyards, and no wine preserved simplicity and insulated them from urban Canaanite luxury and Baal fertility religion. Remarkably, God's pledge in 35:19 shows tangible fruit right after the exile: in Nehemiah, a descendant of Rechab named Malchijah helped rebuild Jerusalem's Dung Gate (Nehemiah 3:14).

**Honoring parents and the privilege of "standing before Me forever."** When handing down his rule, Jonadab tied it to a clear purpose—"so that you may live a long time in the land where you are sojourners" (35:7)—directly echoing the fifth of the Ten Commandments promising long life to those who honor father and mother (Exodus 20:12). Even more striking, God's vow to the Rechabites in 35:19—"will never fail to have a man to stand before Me"—repeats nearly verbatim the covenant formula just pledged to David's royal dynasty and the Levitical priests in chapter 33 (33:18). In Old Testament usage, "standing before the LORD" describes privileged nearness in God's service; a faithful outsider tribe was elevated into lasting service before God. Meanwhile, God "rising early" to send prophets (35:14–15) reprises one of Jeremiah's signature refrains (7:25).

**Living in tents anticipating New Testament pilgrims.** The Rechabites' tent-dwelling identity as "sojourners" (35:7) does not command Christians to abandon houses, yet it mirrors a deep spiritual posture running through Scripture. Hebrews honors Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob because they "lived in tents" like strangers in the promised land while looking forward to a lasting city designed and built by God (Hebrews 11:9–10). Likewise, Peter urges believers to live as "foreigners and exiles," exercising self-control against worldly cravings (1 Peter 2:11).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was God tempting the Rechabites to sin by telling Jeremiah to offer them wine (35:2; 35:5)?** Many readers naturally ask this. Three points clear it up cleanly: first, refraining from wine was Jonadab's family rule rather than a binding command of Moses' law for all Israel—drinking wine was not a sin against God's law; second, Jeremiah's offer served as a public prophetic object lesson inside the temple precincts—much like God testing Abraham's faith—to display the Rechabites' unshakable loyalty right in front of Jerusalem's residents; third, God knew full well they would decline, and their prompt refusal supplied the irrefutable punchline for the sermon that followed (35:13–17).

**Does chapter 35 forbid Christians from drinking wine or owning homes?** No. Abstaining from wine, houses, and farming was a distinct clan vow initiated by Jonadab to keep nomadic simplicity and avoid slipping into Canaanite urban culture tied to Baal (god of wine and harvests). In 35:18–19, God rewards them not because tent-living or abstinence is a universal standard of holiness for everyone, but because "you have obeyed the command of your forefather Jonadab." Even so, their story continues inspiring anyone who freely embraces simplicity and self-discipline to stay faithful to God.

**Dating the invasion of 35:11: when did Babylon and Aram attack Judah?** Verse 35:1 sets the scene during King Jehoiakim's reign (609–598 BC), while 35:11 explains the Rechabites fled into Jerusalem to escape "the army of the Chaldeans and the army of the Arameans." This aligns seamlessly with 2 Kings 24:2: around 600–598 BC, after Jehoiakim rebelled against Babylon, Nebuchadnezzar unleashed raiding bands of Chaldeans alongside Aramean, Moabite, and Ammonite vassals across Judah's countryside, driving open-country dwellers like the Rechabites inside Jerusalem's walls for shelter.

**Two helpful details on names and the closing promise.** "Jaazaniah son of Jeremiah" in 35:3 bears a father's name identical to the prophet's, yet he is simply a Rechabite clansman sharing a common name, not the prophet Jeremiah himself. And scholars read the closing promise—"never fail to have a man to stand before Me" (35:19)—along two complementary lines: either the Rechabite clan endured and eventually joined temple service (ancient Jewish tradition even records them marrying into priestly families), or more broadly, faithful descendants from their line would always stand welcomed and preserved before God.

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, focuses on God's piercing argument from the lesser to the greater in 35:14–16. Calvin clarifies that offering wine never endorses rigid asceticism as a binding Christian rule, but sharpens an undeniable contrast: a long-dead mortal laid down a demanding rule bringing zero material profit and still commanded centuries of obedience, whereas the living Creator handed down life-giving laws only to be brushed off by His own people day after day. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 35)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's lesser-to-greater argument on Jeremiah 35—contrasting a dead ancestor with the living God while rejecting mandatory asceticism—is a clear, signature emphasis of his Jeremiah lectures -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws warm lessons for family life and faith from chapter 35. He admires how the Rechabites never grew self-righteous or scolded others who drank wine, simply explaining their family rule calmly and sticking to it. Henry also notices their sensible flexibility—sheltering inside Jerusalem's walls during war to preserve life (35:11) while still refusing wine—demonstrating wise rather than blind obedience, and proving through 35:19 that genuine honor for parents never goes unrewarded by God. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 35)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral remarks on the Rechabites' gracious firmness, prudent flexibility in verse 11, and honoring parents, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, admires the sharp editorial artistry placing the Rechabites straight after the broken slave covenant of chapter 34. Living on the margins of Jerusalem's urban elite and royal power, this small outsider community guarded the exact virtue Judah's palace and nobles lost: keeping promises and staying faithful to ancestral memory.
