# Jeremiah 5 — Searching the Streets for One Righteous Person

## Story

Right after the scene of Jerusalem gasping before her enemies at the close of chapter 4, the LORD launches an astonishing inspection right in the heart of the city. He sends Jeremiah roaming up and down the streets and searching the public squares to see whether he can find a single person who acts justly and seeks the truth: "if you can find a single person who acts justly and seeks the truth, then I will pardon this city." At first glance it sounds easy, yet the surface turns out to be thin paint. People still swear pious oaths saying, "As surely as the LORD lives!"—yet every oath is a lie. Jeremiah reports back to God in dismay: the LORD's eyes look for faithfulness, yet He has struck them and they felt no grief, crushed them and they refused correction; they "have made their faces harder than stone and refused to repent." At first Jeremiah tells himself he must only have met the poor and unlearned, who do not know the law of God. So he goes to the prominent and educated, those who know the ways of the LORD. The result is even worse: they too have uniformly "broken off the yoke and torn off the chains," openly snapping every bond of the covenant. Because their sins pile so high, judgment will stalk them like a lion from the forest, a desert wolf, and a leopard prowling outside their towns, tearing to pieces anyone who ventures out (5:1–6).

From that fruitless search, the LORD puts a bitter question directly to the people: "How can I forgive you for this?" Jerusalem's children have forsaken Him to swear by things that are not gods. He fed them until they were full, yet they turned to adultery and thronged the houses of prostitutes. Scripture uses blistering imagery, comparing them to well-fed, lusty stallions neighing after their neighbors' wives. Confronting this brazen decay, the refrain of judgment rings out for the first time: "Should I not punish them for these things? Should I not avenge Myself on such a nation as this?" (5:7–9).

The voice of judgment immediately issues marching orders to a destroyer, returning to the familiar image of a ruined vineyard. God commands the attackers to go up through her terraced rows and destroy, stripping away her branches because they no longer belong to the LORD—"yet do not make a full end." Both the house of Israel and the house of Judah have dealt utterly treacherously with Him. They blatantly deny God's authority and lull themselves to sleep: "He will do nothing! No harm will come to us; we will never see sword or famine." To them, warning prophets are just empty wind with no divine word inside them, so the disasters they predict will rebound onto their own heads. Facing such contempt, the LORD God of Hosts turns to Jeremiah and announces a stark confrontation: "I will make My words a fire in your mouth, and this people the wood it consumes" (5:10–14).

To prove that prophetic words are no idle breeze, God pronounces a concrete sentence over the house of Israel. He will summon from afar an ancient, mighty nation speaking a language they cannot understand. Their quiver gapes like an open grave, and every soldier is a seasoned warrior. The invaders will devour the harvests and bread meant for their sons and daughters, consume their flocks, herds, vines, and fig trees, and batter down with the sword the fortified cities in which they placed all their trust. Yet even in the middle of that seeming total ruin, God once again checks His sentence with His earlier pledge: "yet even in those days, I will not make a full end of you." And He prepares Jeremiah in advance with the answer for the day the people ask bewilderedly why the LORD has done all this to them: just as they forsook Him to serve foreign gods in their own land, so now they will serve foreigners in a land that is not theirs (5:15–19).

The next oracle addresses the house of Jacob and Judah, bluntly calling them a foolish and senseless people "who have eyes but do not see, who have ears but do not hear." The LORD summons nature itself as a witness against human stubbornness. He is the One who "placed the sand as a boundary for the sea" by an everlasting decree: that fragile strip of sand holds back roaring, tossing waves that can never cross it. Even the furious ocean obeys its Maker, yet God's people carry stubborn, rebellious hearts, turning aside and walking away without a trace of fear. They never pause to revere the LORD who faithfully sends "both autumn and spring rains" in season and preserves the appointed weeks of harvest to keep them fed. If the skies dry up and blessing withdraws from the land, the fault does not lie with God; their own sins and iniquities have blocked the path of good things (5:20–25).

Judah's corruption goes far beyond idol shrines; it saturates everyday social life. Wicked men lurk among God's people like fowlers crouching to set snares and trap fellow citizens. Their houses are packed with deceit "like cages full of birds," making them rich, powerful, fat, and sleek. Their wickedness knows no bounds: they brush aside the case of the fatherless to get ahead and refuse to defend the rights of the needy. Looking at this shameless injustice, the refrain of judgment echoes a second time, carrying the exact same weight: shall God not punish and avenge Himself on a nation like this? (5:26–29).

At the close of the chapter, Jeremiah distills the tragedy of an entire era into two chilling verses. An appalling and horrible thing has happened right inside the land: the prophets prophesy lies, and the priests rule on their own authority by backing those lies. Even worse than the corrupt leaders is the appetite of the crowd beneath them: "And My people love it this way! But what will you do in the end?" That unanswered question hangs suspended over the entire nation, needing no further commentary (5:30–31).

## Key Takeaways

**Knowing the law and willfully rebelling is deadlier than ignorance.** At first Jeremiah hoped the moral rot was confined to poor, uneducated people who simply did not know God's ways (5:4). When he turned to the upper class—people well versed in the law—he discovered they were the very ones who had deliberately "broken off the yoke and torn off the chains" (5:5). Knowledge of God without obedience never makes people better; it turns rebellion into a calculated choice.

**Religious oaths on the outside cannot mask rot on the inside.** The citizens of Jerusalem still invoked the LORD's name when swearing oaths (5:2) and still expected peace, while their daily lives brimmed with adultery, fraud, and exploitation of the weak. Religion reduced to pious vocabulary stripped of faithfulness and justice never fools God.

**Rejecting the Creator inevitably shatters social justice.** Once the people stopped revering God—the One who sets boundaries for the sea and gives seasonal rain (5:22–24)—they stopped respecting moral boundaries with their neighbors too. The powerful treated the vulnerable like trapped birds, growing rich off the backs of orphans and the poor (5:26–28). False worship and human oppression always walk hand in hand.

**Corrupt leaders are sustained by crowds hungry for soothing lies.** Chapter 5 ends with a piercing diagnosis: prophets lie, priests leverage those lies to stay in control, yet the true tragedy is that "My people love it this way!" (5:31). Societies rarely crumble merely because of a few crooked figures at the top; they crumble when ordinary citizens prefer flattering falsehood over uncomfortable truth.

**Even in scorching judgment, God's mercy has not shut the door.** The chapter thunders with wild beasts, invading armies, and consuming fire, yet twice God puts a boundary on His own sentence: "yet do not make a full end" (5:10) and "I will not make a full end of you" (5:18). His discipline prunes away dead branches rather than erasing His people from the face of the earth.

## Deeper Meaning

**Fewer righteous people than even Abraham's bargain for Sodom.** The search for one just person through Jerusalem's streets (5:1) immediately brings to mind Abraham interceding for Sodom. Back then, God promised to spare Sodom if ten righteous people could be found (Genesis 18:32). Here He lowers the bar all the way down to *one person*—yet God's own holy capital comes up empty. Later, the prophet Ezekiel hears the same heartbreak when God searches for one person to stand in the breach in the wall and finds no one (Ezekiel 22:30). The apostle Paul widens that verdict across all humanity: "There is no one righteous, not even one" (Romans 3:10–12). Human helplessness prepares the way for the one Righteous One who steps in to stand for us all.

**The covenant curses from Moses' day come to life.** The picture of a distant nation speaking an unknown tongue, devouring harvests and livestock, and besieging fortified towns (5:15–17) was not improvised by Jeremiah. Moses had foretold nearly every one of those details centuries earlier should Israel break the covenant (Deuteronomy 28:49–52). Likewise, sin blocking the autumn and spring rains (5:24–25) connects straight back to the blessings and warnings of Deuteronomy (Deuteronomy 11:14–17). The calamity closing in on Judah proves that God's ancient words to their ancestors have never lost their force.

**Eyes that do not see, ears that do not hear.** The indictment of a foolish people in 5:21 echoes the charge God once gave the prophet Isaiah about a nation ever hearing yet never understanding, ever seeing yet never perceiving (Isaiah 6:9–10). Centuries later, Jesus Himself uses that aching question to shake His disciples awake when their hearts are slow to grasp His miracles: "Having eyes, do you not see? And having ears, do you not hear?" (Mark 8:18). The most terrifying blindness in Scripture is not a flaw in our eyes, but a heart that locks itself shut against what God is doing.

**The One who sets the sea's boundary.** God's reminder that He made sand the barrier holding back the raging waves (5:22) resounds alongside the majestic poetry of Job, where God asks who shut in the sea behind doors when it burst forth and fixed limits where its proud waves must halt (Job 38:8–11). The entire cosmos moves within the gentle yet awesome discipline of its Creator—only free human beings dare step across the boundaries He appoints.

**The craving for soothing voices stretches into the New Testament.** The spectacle of prophets telling lies while the people "love it this way" (5:31) was not unique to seventh-century Judah. The apostle Paul warns Timothy that a time will come when people will not endure sound doctrine, but with itching ears will gather around themselves teachers who say whatever their desires crave (2 Timothy 4:3–4). In every age, people are tempted to prefer flattering sedatives over truth that calls for change.

**The breached vineyard wall and the word as fire.** The order to climb the vineyard walls to tear them down and prune away bad branches (5:10) recalls Isaiah's vineyard song, where God breaks down the vineyard's wall because it yields only wild grapes (Isaiah 5:5). And the image of God's word turning into fire in Jeremiah's mouth to devour dry wood (5:14) returns later in this very book, when God asks, "Is not My word like fire, and like a hammer that smashes a rock?" (23:29). God's word is never harmless wind; whoever scorns it eventually meets its heat.

## Notes & Nuances

**Could Jerusalem really not contain a single righteous person (5:1)—what about Jeremiah himself?** Reading 5:1 as a literal headcount immediately runs into trouble, since Jeremiah himself—and later Baruch and several faithful officials—lived right there. This is classic prophetic hyperbole emphasizing that corruption had spread through every layer of society, from market squares to noble estates, until no upright community or leadership class remained to hold the city together.

**How should we read the blunt sexual imagery in 5:7–8?** Picturing people crowding brothels and comparing men to well-fed stallions neighing after neighbors' wives sounds stark, even coarse, to modern readers. Commentators commonly hear two interwoven layers here: first, genuine sexual license and adultery flourishing in a season of prosperity; second, the recurring metaphor from earlier chapters of Jeremiah casting idolatry as spiritual adultery betraying the covenant with God (cf. 3:1–9). Jeremiah deliberately chooses jarring language to strip away polite disguises from sins the public had normalized.

**Talk of God "avenging" Himself (5:9; 5:29) and invading violence (5:15–17).** Many modern readers recoil when Scripture speaks of God avenging Himself on a nation and describes invaders wiping out crops and cities. In Hebrew, the verb translated *avenge* here does not mean petty spite or vindictive rage, but the righteous Judge restoring justice after the powerful have trampled orphans and the poor (5:28; cf. Exodus 22:22–24) and ignored every warning. These verses must also be read alongside the two moments where God explicitly limits the judgment: "yet do not make a full end" (5:10) and "I will not make a full end of you" (5:18). God's judgment is severe and truly painful, yet never senseless annihilation.

**Are drought and crop failure always direct consequences of sin (5:24–25)?** Under the Sinai covenant with Israel in the Promised Land, God explicitly tied obedience to timely rain and rebellion to drought (Deuteronomy 11:14–17). Jeremiah is calling them back to the covenant terms their own nation had ratified. Yet the wider witness of Scripture—above all the book of Job—warns us never to turn that covenant promise into a mechanical formula condemning people who suffer disasters or hardship today as guilty of secret sin.

**Jerome**, the fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Bible, pauses right at 5:1 with a very practical question: why does God tell Jeremiah to search Jerusalem for a single righteous person to spare the city when Jeremiah himself is standing right there? **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pays special attention to the closing verse 5:31 where the people "love it this way" as false prophets lie. For Calvin, the public cannot shift all blame onto corrupt leaders and pose as innocent dupes. Their own appetite for flattery and hatred of correction bred those false prophets in the first place: when people only want voices that stroke their desires, they end up getting the exact teachers they deserve. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's characteristic point on the crowd's complicity in 5:31, though without checking exact phrasing on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, sets 5:1 beside the story of Sodom to marvel at God's astonishing patience: Sodom needed ten righteous people to be spared, yet because Jerusalem is His own city, God lowers the count to a single person and still finds none. At 5:22 he draws out the stinging contrast: the fiercest ocean waves submit to a thin barrier of sand God has set, while rational human beings refuse to bow before His clear law. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the Sodom parallel on 5:1 and the waves-versus-sand contrast on 5:22 are signature Matthew Henry points on chapter 5, though not verified verbatim -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Jewish thinker and scholar, reads Jeremiah's denunciations of dishonest wealth and indifference to the orphan and needy in chapter 5 as a hallmark of the prophetic conscience toward everyday injustice. In his view, practices society shrugs off as ordinary business sharp dealing or routine apathy appear before God as grave outrages capable of shaking the destiny of an entire nation. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: how prophets view everyday social injustice is a central theme of The Prophets tied directly to passages like Jeremiah 5:26–28, though specific page numbers were not checked -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 5 as an anatomy of a society that has lost the capacity to examine itself. He underscores the tight bond at the chapter's close: as false prophets, priests, and public alike settle happily into an illusion of safety (5:30–31), an economy exploiting the vulnerable (5:26–28) gets wrapped in a comfortable religious veneer.
