# Jeremiah 24 — Two Baskets of Figs

## Story

Following the oracles against kings and false prophets in the preceding chapters, Jeremiah anchors readers to a concrete historical milestone: right after King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon carried off King Jeconiah son of Jehoiakim of Judah, together with the officials, craftsmen, and metalsmiths from Jerusalem to Babylon—the first major deportation in 597 BC. At that precise moment, the LORD shows Jeremiah a vision: two baskets of figs set right in front of the temple of the LORD (24:1).

The two baskets could not look more different. One basket holds very good figs, luscious like early-ripening figs; the other holds very bad figs, so rotten they cannot be eaten. The LORD asks His prophet, "What do you see, Jeremiah?" Jeremiah reports back plainly what sits before his eyes: he sees figs—"the good ones are very good, but the bad ones are very bad, indeed so bad they cannot be eaten" (24:2–3).

Then the LORD unfolds the meaning of the vision, starting with the basket of good figs. His words flip completely upside down the assumptions of everyone still sitting safely inside Jerusalem: just like those prime figs, He will regard the Judean exiles—whom He Himself sent away from Jerusalem to the land of the Chaldeans—"for their good" (24:4–5).

Going far beyond temporary protection, God promises full restoration for that exiled community. He will keep His eyes on them for good, bring them back to this land, and "build them up and not tear them down; I will plant them and not uproot them." The richest blessing of all transforms them from the inside out: "I will give them a heart to know Me, that I am the LORD. They will be My people, and I will be their God, for they will return to Me with all their heart" (24:6–7).

By stark contrast, the basket of rotten figs represents the very crowd convinced they are the favored survivors: King Zedekiah, his officials, the remnant remaining in Jerusalem, and those who fled to settle in Egypt. Just as people throw away rotten figs unfit to eat, God will deal with them. He will make them an object of horror and disaster across every kingdom on earth—a reproach, a byword, a taunt, and a curse wherever He drives them. Finally, God will send sword, famine, and plague among them until they are wiped out from the land He gave them and their ancestors (24:8–10).

## Key Takeaways

**God often reads a situation in the exact opposite way human eyes do.** Right after the 597 BC deportation, anyone left standing in Jerusalem naturally assumed God spared them because they were better, while those marched off to Babylon suffered heavier wrath. The vision of two fig baskets turns that prejudice upside down: the very exiles stripped of homes and homeland sit inside God's protective care (24:5–6), while the comfortable crowd left behind in the capital heads straight toward ruin (24:8–10).

**God's discipline can be the very road He uses to save us.** God does not treat the Babylonian deportation as an unlucky accident; He declares He Himself sent those captives to Babylon "for their good" (24:5). Sometimes being pulled out of comfortable crutches and walking through loss is how God purifies and safeguards His people's future rather than letting them rot inside a stubborn society.

**A heart that knows God is His gift, not a trophy of human effort.** In this chapter, the exiles are not "good figs" because they were holier than everyone else before exile. God Himself sets His eyes on them, God Himself pledges "I will give them a heart to know Me," and out of that grace they "return to Me with all their heart" (24:6–7). Human hope always starts with God's initiating grace.

**Stubbornly resisting God's discipline leads beyond rescue.** King Zedekiah and those clinging to Jerusalem or escaping to Egypt embody the refusal to bow under God's word, trusting instead in false security and clever political escapes (24:8). When people harden themselves against correction, neither the Promised Land nor the standing temple can shield them from collapse (24:9–10).

## Deeper Meaning

**Historical backdrop matching Second Kings detail for detail.** This vision unfolds right after the event recorded in 2 Kings 24:14–16: Babylon's king exiled Jehoiachin (Jeconiah) along with the royal family, nobles, and a thousand craftsmen and smiths, leaving only the poorer population behind and installing Zedekiah as a puppet king. Later in 29:16–18, writing a letter to the exiles, Jeremiah invokes this very metaphor of inedible rotten figs to foretell the doom awaiting Zedekiah and Jerusalem's holdouts; meanwhile the warning aimed at refugees in Egypt in 24:8 comes tragically true after Jerusalem's fall (42:15–17).

**Direct echoes of Jeremiah's original calling.** God's question "What do you see, Jeremiah?" in 24:3 mirrors the exact phrasing of his first two visions—the almond branch and the boiling pot—when God called him as a youth (1:11–13). And the promise to the exiles in 24:6—"I will build them up and not tear them down; I will plant them and not uproot them"—directly picks up the six verbs of his lifelong commission in 1:10: uproot, tear down, destroy, overthrow, build, and plant. The two baskets prove that after painful uprooting, God's rebuilding and replanting have already begun among the exiles.

**A matching promise from across the rivers of Babylon.** At the very same time Jeremiah announced this vision in Jerusalem, the prophet Ezekiel—himself one of the 597 BC deportees—heard Jerusalem's residents sneering down at the exiles and claiming, "This land has been given to us as a possession!" (Ezekiel 11:15). Through Ezekiel, God delivered the exact twin of Jeremiah 24: He Himself would become a temporary sanctuary for the exiles, gather them back to Israel, and swap their heart of stone for a new heart of flesh (Ezekiel 11:16–19; 36:26). Two prophets separated by hundreds of miles proclaimed the exact same hope.

**Early seeds of the New Covenant.** Verse 24:7—"I will give them a heart to know Me, that I am the LORD. They will be My people, and I will be their God"—is one of the earliest buds of the New Covenant promise Jeremiah will unfold in full in chapter 31 (31:33–34). Throughout redemptive history, what God's people need most is never merely getting old real estate back, but receiving a recreated heart that truly knows Him.

**Two baskets before the temple and Moses' covenant curses.** Under the Law of Moses, Israelites placed firstfruits in baskets and set them before the temple as an offering to the LORD (Deuteronomy 26:2), and Hosea recalled early Israel as precious as "early fruit on the fig tree in its first season" (Hosea 9:10). Setting two fig baskets right outside the temple puts the entire nation on display to weigh which part is truly fit for God. Meanwhile, the verdict over the rotten basket—becoming a horror, byword, taunt, and curse among all nations (24:9)—draws straight from the covenant curses of Deuteronomy (Deuteronomy 28:25; 28:37).

## Notes & Nuances

**Jeremiah himself stayed in Jerusalem and was later dragged to Egypt—so how does that fit?** Reading 24:8–10, many readers immediately ask: if everyone remaining in Jerusalem and heading to Egypt counts as "bad figs," what about faithful figures like Jeremiah, Baruch, or Ebed-melech? The two baskets symbolize the collective trajectories of two rival communities and policies—the 597 exiles accepting discipline in Babylon versus Zedekiah's defiant court in Jerusalem—not a blanket individual verdict condemning every single resident. Jeremiah stayed behind to bear witness, was later hauled to Egypt against his will, and remained safely under God's care throughout.

**Why were "early-ripening figs" prized so highly?** In Israel, fig trees yield two main harvests each year. The early crop ripening around June is prized for its rich sweetness and eaten fresh immediately rather than stored (24:2; cf. Hosea 9:10). Rotten, worm-eaten figs, by contrast, serve no purpose except dumping out.

**The 597 BC exiles were not morally superior beforehand.** Reading 24:5–7, it is easy to assume the deportees of 597 BC were already saintly people earning God's praise. Yet just two chapters earlier God pronounced severe judgment on King Jeconiah who led that very deportation (22:24–30), and chapter 29 reveals plenty of immoral false prophets operating right inside the Babylonian exile community. They become "good figs" in God's eyes not through prior perfection, but because they stand under His refining hand—and because God sovereignly chooses that broken community as the seedbed where He will plant a new heart and rebuild His nation (24:6–7).

**Why does 24:10 sound so stark and sweeping?** The announcement of sword, famine, plague, and erasure from the land underlines the gravity of Jerusalem's approaching 586 BC collapse. In prophetic style, God paints an uncompromising contrast—one basket very good, one very bad, with no middle basket—to shatter Zedekiah's fantasy that he could defy God's word while clinging safely to Jerusalem or running to Egypt.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, observes that the vision of two fig baskets overturns conventional human measures of blessing and disaster. Those carried off to Babylon lost all outward splendor yet bowed under God's corrective hand, earning His regard as good figs promised a heart to know Him; those left smugly in Jerusalem assumed they had escaped judgment, yet stubbornly refused correction until they spoiled like inedible figs. *(Commentary on Jeremiah, on chapter 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Jerome's exposition on the reversal between humbled exiles and complacent holdouts in chapter 24, though not checked verbatim in Latin -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pauses deeply over 24:7: "I will give them a heart to know Me." Humans cannot manufacture a heart that knows God or turn back by unaided willpower; God must take the first step, regenerating the inner heart before genuine repentance can blossom. Drawing a practical lesson from the two baskets, Calvin urges believers neither to envy sinners coasting comfortably nor to despair under hardship, since God's discipline often conceals long-term mercy. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah, on chapter 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's signature emphasis on prevenient grace giving a heart to know God in 24:7 and the beneficial aim of discipline in 24:5–6, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws a clear pastoral truth from the two baskets placed before the temple: among people wearing the same covenant name, deep differences exist that only God's eyes distinguish clearly. The exiles became "good figs" precisely because adversity humbled their pride and awakened them to seek God, whereas brief comfort in Jerusalem only hardened Zedekiah and his nobles. For Henry, receiving a heart to know God in 24:7 outshines even the promise of returning home. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jeremiah 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral lessons on refining adversity and the supreme gift of a heart to know God in chapter 24, though not verified verbatim -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic treasury of Jewish law and commentary, preserves a striking tradition about the "craftsmen and metalsmiths" deported alongside King Jeconiah (24:1; cf. 2 Kings 24:14–16). Ancient rabbis read those two titles metaphorically as master Torah scholars—when one spoke all others fell silent to listen, and once they closed a legal ruling no one could reopen it. On that view, God deporting them to Babylon ahead of Jerusalem's collapse was an act of mercy "for their good" (24:5), planting academies of Scripture ahead of time so Jewish faith could survive exile. *(Babylonian Talmud, Tractates Gittin and Sanhedrin)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the rabbinic reading of craftsmen and smiths as Torah scholars sent ahead to Babylon is famous in Gittin and Sanhedrin, though whether citing 2 Kings 24 or Jeremiah 24 directly was not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, highlights the political and theological shockwave Jeremiah 24 sent through Jerusalem's royal court. Holding the temple, palace, and land, Zedekiah and his officials naturally assumed they were Israel's legitimate mainstream. God's word strips away that claim: Israel's future does not belong to custodians propping up a decaying establishment, but to the exiled community passing through brokenness and allowing God to rebuild them from the ground up with a new heart. *(A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming, 1998, on chapter 24)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Jeremiah 24 as declaring that Israel's future runs exclusively through the 597 exiles rather than Jerusalem's old regime is Brueggemann's core thesis on this chapter -->
