# Judges 6 — The Call of Gideon

## Story

Deborah's forty years of peace fade, and Israel slips right back into the familiar rut: doing evil before the LORD. This time God hands them over to Midian for seven years. This new oppression devastates differently from Moab or Canaan: instead of garrisons collecting tribute, seasonal raids strip the countryside bare. No sooner does Israel sow its seed than Midianites, Amalekites, and eastern tribes swarm across like thick locust clouds with countless men and camels. Pitching camp across the land all the way to Gaza, they ruin every crop and leave neither sheep, oxen, nor donkeys alive. Terrified, Israelites flee into mountain dens, caves, and strongholds. Starved to sheer desperation, Israel cries out to the LORD once more (6:1–6).

This time, however, God does not immediately send a military deliverer. Instead, He sends an unnamed prophet straight to the nation's conscience. The prophet recites God's faithfulness: bringing them up from Egypt's slavery, rescuing them from every oppressor, driving out nations, and giving them this land. He gave one clear charge: do not fear or worship the gods of the Amorites among whom you live. "But you have not obeyed My voice." Abruptly the prophet's sermon stops dead—without offering a single promise of rescue (6:7–10).

Yet God's grace still comes quietly knocking. The angel of the LORD comes and sits beneath the oak at Ophrah belonging to Joash the Abiezrite. His son Gideon is hidden down inside a winepress—a sunken stone pit built for pressing grapes rather than an open windy threshing floor—secretly beating out wheat out of sight of Midianite raiders. The angel greets this hiding farmer with a stunning title: "The LORD is with you, O mighty man of valor!" Pent-up bitterness bursts straight from Gideon's chest: if the LORD truly is with us, why has all this misery hit us? Where are all those Exodus wonders our fathers told us about? Hasn't the LORD abandoned us straight into Midian's hand? Refusing to get bogged down debating, the LORD turns straight to him: "Go in the strength you have and save Israel from the hand of Midian. Am I not sending you?" Instantly Gideon protests: his clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and he is the very least in his father's house. God replies concisely and firmly: "Surely I will be with you, and you will strike down all the Midianites as one man" (6:11–16).

Still hesitant to believe, Gideon begs for a confirming sign and asks the visitor to wait while he brings an offering. Even amid famine, he generously prepares a young goat and an ephah of flour—roughly half a bushel—into unleavened cakes, packing meat in a basket and broth in a pot to carry beneath the oak. Following the angel's direction, he lays meat and cakes atop a rock and pours broth over them. The angel touches the food with the tip of his staff: instantly fire flares straight up from the rock consuming meat and bread completely, and the angel vanishes from sight. Staggered, Gideon realizes he has faced the angel of the LORD and cries out in mortal dread. Gently the LORD reassures him: "Peace be with you. Do not be afraid, for you will not die." Right there Gideon builds an altar naming it *The LORD Is Peace* (*Yahweh Shalom*) (6:17–24).

That very night God assigns Gideon his first battle—not against Midian in the valley, but against idolatry right inside his own backyard. God orders him to take his father's young bull and a second seven-year-old bull, pull down his father's Baal altar, chop down the Asherah pole beside it, build a proper altar to the LORD atop the stronghold, and burn that second bull using the shattered Asherah wood as firewood. Taking ten servants, Gideon obeys completely—yet too terrified of his father's household and townsmen to act in daylight, he does the whole job under cover of night. Next morning, discovering Baal's altar smashed and the Asherah burned as fuel, angry townsmen track down the culprit and storm Joash's house demanding Gideon's death. Astonishingly, Gideon's own father—owner of Baal's altar—steps forward defending his son with razor-sharp logic: "Are you contending for Baal? ... If he is a god, let him contend for himself!" The crowd stands speechless, and from that day Gideon earns the nickname *Jerubbaal*—meaning *let Baal contend with him* (6:25–32).

Soon all Midian, Amalek, and the eastern tribes recross the Jordan, blanketing the Valley of Jezreel with their camp. At that exact hour, the Spirit of the LORD *clothed* Gideon—wrapping him like armor. Blowing the trumpet, he rallies the Abiezrites—the very neighbors demanding his death days earlier—and sends messengers across Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali, all marching out to join him (6:33–35).

Armies stand assembled, God's promise rings clear, and rock-bursting fire still lingers fresh in memory—yet Gideon's nerves still tremble. Spreading a wool fleece on the threshing floor, he asks God for another sign: if morning dew drenches only the fleece while surrounding ground stays dry, he will know God will save Israel by his hand. Rising early next morning, he wrings a full bowl of water straight from the fleece. Still unsure, he timidly begs God not to get angry and asks for one final reverse test: tonight let the fleece stay bone-dry while dew covers all the ground. Through the night, God patiently grants his exact request once again (6:36–40).

## Key Takeaways

**God sees people through the lens of grace, not merely present weakness.** When the angel hailed Gideon as a "mighty man of valor" (6:12), he sat crouched inside a winepress dodging raiders. That greeting was neither sarcasm nor flattery—it declared who Gideon would become once the LORD stood with him (6:16). Our strength in serving God flows from the One who sends us, never pedigree or native boldness.

**Before rescuing a nation, clear idols out of your own yard first.** Before letting Gideon blow a war trumpet against Midian, God sent him to smash Baal's altar right inside his father's compound (6:25–26). Midian's raids outside were merely symptoms of Baal worship inside. Spiritual battles out in the world cannot be won while rival altars stand untouched inside our own homes and hearts.

**Even trembling obedience gets honored and used by God.** Gideon tore down Baal's shrine after dark because he feared his family and neighbors (6:27). Scripture reports his fear candidly without airbrushing it—yet God blessed his obedience and even turned Baal's own proprietor, his father Joash, into Gideon's boldest defender next morning (6:31). Courage is not feeling zero fear; it is obeying God even with shaking knees.

**God's patience vastly outmeasures human doubt.** Gideon protested, demanded fire on rock, and then asked for two back-to-back fleece signs (6:17; 6:36–40). Instead of snapping or dismissing him, God stooped gently to meet every anxious question, coaxing fragile faith upright.

## Deeper Meaning

**Gideon's calling deliberately echoes the call of Moses.** Reading 6:11–17 beside the burning bush reveals matching brushstrokes down to every beat: the angel of the LORD appears amid harsh oppression, commissions a deliverer, hears humble protest—Moses asks "Who am I?" (Exodus 3:11), Gideon sighs "I am the least" (6:15)—and answers with the identical anchor promise: "Surely I will be with you" (Exodus 3:12; 6:16; cf. Jeremiah 1:6–8). Scripture reminds readers that the God of the Exodus still rescues His people.

**God's power shines brightest in weakness.** As Gideon rattled off every strike against himself—weakest clan, smallest son—God never argued he was secretly impressive. He simply promised His presence. Centuries later, Paul summed up this same divine pattern in Christ's word to him: "My power is perfected in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9).

***The LORD Is Peace*—meeting a holy God and walking away alive.** Old Testament believers knew sinners could not look on God's face and live (Exodus 33:20; cf. Genesis 32:30), making Gideon's panic in 6:22 completely understandable. Yet God's answer was peace—marked by *Yahweh Shalom* (6:24), declaring that the Holy One approaches weak sinners not to crush them, but to reconcile and grant peace. In the New Testament, Jesus Himself is proclaimed "our peace" (Ephesians 2:14).

**Silent idols crumble before the living God.** Joash's biting challenge—if Baal really is a god, let him fight his own battles (6:31)—unmasked fake gods completely. Fire had just blazed from solid rock for Gideon (6:21), while smashing Baal's altar and burning Asherah's pole (Deuteronomy 7:5) brought not a spark from Baal. Centuries later atop Mount Carmel, Elijah staged that identical showdown before four hundred fifty Baal prophets: dead silence from Baal, blazing fire from the LORD (1 Kings 18:38–39).

**Stumbling faith still makes the roll of honor.** Despite chapter 6's portrait of an anxious, sign-seeking Gideon, the New Testament still lists Gideon first among the judges in its Hall of Faith (Hebrews 11:32). God measures faith not by never wrestling with doubt, but by ultimately stepping out on His word.

## Notes & Nuances

***Putting out a fleece* is never a recommended blueprint for guidance.** Christians frequently borrow "putting out a fleece" to ask God for magical signs when picking jobs or spouses. Read carefully, however, 6:36–40 shows Gideon was not *discovering* God's will—he already knew God's clear word—"just as You have said" (6:36)—and had just watched fire burst out of stone (6:21)! Both fleece tests exposed wavering anxiety, leaving even Gideon afraid he might provoke God's anger (6:39). Scripture explicitly warns against putting God to the test (Deuteronomy 6:16). This scene celebrates God's astonishing gentleness, not Gideon's testing method.

**Who is *the angel of the LORD* in this chapter?** Watch the pronouns shift across 6:11–23: verses 11–12 name "the angel of the LORD," yet verses 14 and 16 call him directly "the LORD," and in verse 22 Gideon fears death for seeing the angel face to face. Many commentators recognize here no ordinary created angel, but God Himself appearing in visible human form—widely understood by Christian theologians as a pre-incarnate appearance of God the Son.

**Gideon's bitter question in 6:13 misses half the truth.** Lamenting "the LORD has forsaken us" (6:13), Gideon stared solely at Israel's pain while completely bypassing the unnamed prophet's message just verses earlier (6:8–10): Israel had abandoned God for idols—proven by a full Baal shrine standing right on his own father's lawn (6:25)! Humans constantly blame God for feeling absent in suffering while ignoring the unfaithfulness parked inside our own homes.

**Why did non-priest Gideon offer a burnt sacrifice?** Normally Mosaic law restricted sacrifices to Aaronic priests at God's chosen sanctuary. Here, however, God directly ordered Gideon to build an altar and sacrifice a seven-year-old bull right atop Baal's ruined shrine (6:25–26)—an extraordinary command amid rampant apostasy declaring the LORD's supremacy on pagan ground. Tellingly, the bull's seven years match Israel's exact seven years under Midian (6:1).

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, third-century Alexandrian theologian, reads Gideon's twin fleece signs (6:36–40) as a grand prophetic emblem of Israel and the nations in salvation history. First, the dew of divine grace fell exclusively on the fleece (Israel) while surrounding ground (the Gentile nations) lay dry; then with Christ's coming and the gospel spreading worldwide, grace drenched the whole earth while the fleece stayed dry. Origen also reads beating wheat inside a winepress spiritually: trials strip chaff away so pure grain of God's word emerges. *(Homilies on Judges, Homilies 7 and 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Origen's classic typological exposition of Gideon's fleece representing Israel and the Gentiles is the centerpiece of Homily 8 in Homilies on Judges -->

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo, continues this early church typology viewing Gideon's fleece as foreshadowing Christ's salvation resting initially with Israel before overflowing to every nation. **Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, vividly recounts the starvation under Midian explaining why Gideon hid inside a winepress rather than threshing openly in fields. He frames Gideon's skeptical reply to the angel as an understandable reaction from a desperate young man before emphasizing divine empowerment confirmed through rock-born fire and the twin fleece miracles. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book V)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus narrates Gideon's hidden threshing and confirming signs in Antiquities Book V; exact section numbers not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, draws a vital lesson from Gideon's midnight raid on Baal's shrine: before anyone leads national revival, reformation must begin inside their own household. Praising Joash's shrewd defense (6:31), Henry notes a courageous child obeying God can awaken a sleeping parent's conscience. **Tim Keller**, New York pastor who died in 2023, zeroes in on Gideon's complaint ("if the LORD is with us, why has all this happened?") to expose human self-justification: we readily fault God for our misery while overlooking idols sitting right in our living rooms. Keller likewise warns Christians against treating "putting out a fleece" as a model of faith: already holding God's crystal-clear word, demanding extra signs betrayed doubt—while the chapter's real hero is God's stubborn grace refusing to give up on a wavering man. *(Judges for You, 2013, on chapter 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking Gideon's 6:13 protest to his father's Baal altar and critiquing fleece-testing as doubt overcome by patient grace are core themes of Keller's Judges for You on chapter 6 -->
