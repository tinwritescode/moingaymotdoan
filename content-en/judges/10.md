# Judges 10 — When God Could Bear Israel's Misery No Longer

## Story

After the bloody nightmare of Abimelech's reign, the land entered a quiet stretch lasting nearly half a century under two little-known judges. The first was Tola son of Puah, the son of Dodo, a man of Issachar who lived in Shamir in the hill country of Ephraim. Scripture records simply that he rose to save Israel, judged the nation for twenty-three years, died, and was buried in his hometown (10:1–2). Following him came Jair of Gilead on the eastern side of the Jordan, who judged Israel for twenty-two years. He was a wealthy chieftain with a sprawling family: he had thirty sons who rode thirty donkeys and controlled thirty towns in Gilead—a district that still bore the name "Havvoth-jair" long afterward. When Jair died, he was buried in Kamon (10:3–5).

Yet the moment that peace ended, Israel plunged into deeper apostasy than at any earlier point in the book. No longer content with secretly serving Baal and the Ashtoreths as previous generations had done, they now imported seven full sets of foreign gods from all directions: the Baals, the Ashtoreths, and the gods of Aram, Sidon, Moab, the Ammonites, and the Philistines. The text summarizes the tragedy in one chilling line: "They forsook the LORD and did not serve Him." The anger of the LORD burned against Israel, and He sold them into the grip of two powers squeezing from opposite sides—the Philistines along the western coast and the Ammonites from the east. For eighteen years, the Israelites living east of the Jordan in Gilead were crushed and shattered without mercy. Not stopping there, the Ammonites even crossed the Jordan to raid Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim on the west, plunging the entire nation into severe distress (10:6–9).

Backed against the wall, the Israelites cried out to the LORD and confessed their sin: they had forsaken their God to serve the Baals. Yet for the first time in the book of Judges, the LORD did not immediately raise up a deliverer. Instead, He answered with a long, anguished indictment. He counted off seven foreign oppressors from whom He had rescued them in the past—the Egyptians, the Amorites, the Ammonites, the Philistines, the Sidonians, the Amalekites, and the Maonites—every time they cried out, He had delivered them. Yet the moment they felt safe, they turned their backs on Him for other gods. He declared firmly, "Therefore I will no longer deliver you." Then He delivered a piercing taunt: "Go and cry out to the gods you have chosen. Let them save you in your time of trouble!" (10:10–14).

Confronted with that blunt refusal, the Israelites did not walk away to other gods; they humbled themselves far more deeply. This time they dropped all excuses and bargaining, confessing their guilt outright and surrendering to God's justice: "We have sinned. Do to us whatever seems good to You, but please deliver us today!" More importantly than any words, they acted in genuine change: they got rid of the foreign gods from among them and returned to serving the LORD. Witnessing His people in such misery and seeing their return, Scripture records a sentence that stirs the heart: "He could no longer bear the misery of Israel" (10:15–16).

At that very moment, war stood right on the doorstep. The Ammonites mobilized in full force and encamped right inside Gilead. The Israelites scrambled to assemble and pitched camp opposite them at Mizpah. Yet inside the Israelite camp there was still no God-appointed commander in sight. Looking anxiously across at the enemy lines, the people and the leaders of Gilead could only turn to one another in desperation: whoever dared launch the fight against the Ammonites would be made ruler over all the inhabitants of Gilead—the exact question that sets the stage for the dramatic entrance of Jephthah in the next chapter (10:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Sin never stays small; the longer it is tolerated, the wider it spreads.** In the opening chapters, Israel drifted only into worshiping Baal and the Ashtoreths of Canaan. By chapter 10, the catalog of idols has swelled to seven sets of foreign deities from every quarter (10:6). People imagine they can take just one small step away from God and stop there, yet once the heart leaves the living God, it turns into an open marketplace welcoming every false god around.

**True repentance does not stop at crying for help in crisis; it proves itself through decisive action.** Israel's first outcry in 10:10 sounded completely orthodox—they confessed sin and called on God's name—yet in reality they kept their idols tucked away at home and tried to use the LORD as an emergency escape hatch. Only when God flatly turned them away did they drop their sense of entitlement, submit to His rightful discipline, and tear the foreign gods out from among them (10:15–16). Repentance that refuses to part with what pulled us away from God is merely regret over consequences, not a true return to Him.

**Idols always abandon their worshipers when disaster hits.** God's challenge in 10:14—telling them to go summon the seven sets of gods they chose—lays bare the utter helplessness of every idol. While life runs smoothly, money, power, applause, or sinful habits promise the moon; yet on the day calamity strikes, not one of them can lift a finger to rescue anyone.

**Grace is not a vending machine you press on demand, but the aching heart of the living God.** God's warning that He would no longer save them (10:13) delivers a sobering lesson: never take God's mercy for granted, continuing in sin on the assumption He will always clean up the mess. Yet the very next line—that His soul could no longer bear Israel's misery (10:16)—reveals a compassion deeper than all human calculation; He is never indifferent to the tears of children who genuinely turn back.

**Quiet years of ordinary stability are a precious gift from God.** Tola and Jair have no famous battles or dazzling miracles recorded; both are covered in five brief verses (10:1–5). Yet through them the nation enjoyed forty-five years of steady leadership—an entire generation growing up in peace. In God's eyes, quiet, faithful stewardship that holds a community together day by day is often worth every bit as much as headline-making exploits.

## Deeper Meaning

**Seven groups of foreign gods mirror seven past acts of divine rescue.** Reading closely uncovers a carefully crafted symmetry: in 10:6 Israel runs after seven sets of gods (Baals, Ashtoreths, Aram, Sidon, Moab, Ammon, and Philistia), and right away in 10:11–12 the LORD recalls exactly seven enemies from whom He had saved them (Egypt, the Amorites, Ammon, Philistia, Sidon, Amalek, and Maon). Seven in Scripture signifies fullness: as complete as God's saving faithfulness had been toward them, just as complete was their unfaithfulness toward Him. Salvation history is always the ground on which God reminds His people whose they are.

**The taunt against helpless idols echoes through the prophets.** God telling Israel to cry out to the gods they had chosen (10:14) reaches back directly to Moses' farewell song, which warned of the day a faithless nation would hear God ask where their trusted gods were and why they did not rise up to protect them (Deuteronomy 32:37–38). Centuries later, as Jerusalem stood on the brink of ruin, the prophet Jeremiah voiced the exact same stinging challenge: Judah had as many gods as cities, so let them get up and save them in their time of trouble (Jeremiah 2:28).

**A God who cannot bear to abandon His people is a scarlet thread across the Old Testament.** Verse 10:16—that God could no longer bear Israel's misery—opens a rare window straight into the heart of God. We feel that exact heartbeat again in Hosea, where God cries out amid righteous judgment: "My heart is churned within Me; all My compassion is aroused" (Hosea 11:8). Isaiah likewise testifies, "In all their distress, He too was distressed" (Isaiah 63:9). Looking back over the entire era of the Judges, the psalmist summed up this exact story: time and again He delivered them while they kept rebelling, yet when He heard their cry in distress, He remembered His covenant and relented in His abundant love (Psalms 106:43–45).

**Two kinds of sorrow and true repentance in the New Testament.** The back-and-forth between 10:10 and 10:15–16 illustrates the sharp distinction Paul draws in his second letter to Corinth: worldly sorrow grieves only over getting caught and suffering fallout, whereas "godly sorrow brings repentance that leads to salvation" and genuine turnaround (2 Corinthians 7:10). Only when Israel surrendered to God's discipline and cleared out their idols did they move from fear of punishment into authentic repentance.

**A structural hinge opening the book's final two major narratives.** Verse 10:7 names two oppressors rising together: the Ammonites in the east and the Philistines in the west. In fact, this verse serves as the narrative hinge for the rest of the main judge stories: Jephthah's saga (chapters 11–12) confronts the Ammonite threat, while Samson's saga (chapters 13–16) engages Philistine domination—a conflict that will stretch all the way into the days of Samuel and King David.

## Notes & Nuances

**The Hebrew wording behind "He could no longer bear" in 10:16 carries two striking nuances.** Literally, the Hebrew reads *His soul grew short* because of Israel's misery. Translators and scholars generally read this idiom in two complementary ways. The most widespread reading—reflected in the BSB's "He could no longer bear the misery of Israel" and older translations' "His soul was grieved"—pictures compassionate anguish so acute that God cannot endure watching His people suffer any longer. Several modern scholars also observe that "soul growing short" elsewhere in the Old Testament can express *losing patience* or *growing thoroughly exasperated*—meaning God was worn out by Israel's endless cycle of rebellion and groaning. Read alongside 10:17–18 (where God does not immediately send a noble deliverer as before, leaving Gilead's elders to go recruit Jephthah themselves), both notes ring together: the aching compassion of a Father who cannot turn away, intertwined with deep frustration over a stubborn people.

**What is the significance of Jair's thirty sons riding thirty donkeys?** (10:4). In that era, warhorses belonged to foreign armies, while donkeys were the prized mounts of nobility and rulers (5:10). Jair having thirty sons—pointing to polygamy like Gideon's—riding thirty donkeys and governing thirty towns in "Havvoth-jair," a district originally named after an earlier ancestor in Moses' time (Numbers 32:41), signals outward prosperity and stability, yet also hints that Israel's judges were increasingly taking on the air of hereditary chieftains.

**Why are Tola and Jair called "minor judges"?** The book of Judges features six judges given extended stories (Othniel, Ehud, Deborah, Gideon, Jephthah, and Samson) and six given only brief summaries (Shamgar, Tola, Jair, Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon). Calling them "minor" does not imply inferior faith or lesser contribution—Tola and Jair together steadied Israel for forty-five years—only that the narrator devotes fewer verses to them. One telling phrase in 10:1 stands out: Scripture says Tola "arose to save Israel" right "after Abimelech," implying that Abimelech's homegrown tyranny was just as much an oppression needing deliverance as any foreign invader!

**Does God's apparent change of mind between 10:13 and 10:16 contradict His faithfulness?** In 10:13 God declares He will not save them again, yet three verses later His compassion is moved. In reality, God's refusal in 10:13–14 responded to the half-hearted confession of 10:10 while the people still clung to their idols. That stern refusal acted like a surgeon's scalpel, exposing their insincerity and drawing out true humility and the removal of their foreign gods in 10:15–16. God was not fickle; the people's posture changed, and His abiding mercy (2:18) met their sincere turning.
