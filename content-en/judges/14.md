# Judges 14 — Samson's Wedding and Riddle

## Story

Samson had barely reached adulthood before he did something that startled his parents. He went down to Timnah, a Philistine town just below his home hills, and fell for a young woman there. Returning home, he told his father and mother bluntly, "I have seen a Philistine woman in Timnah; now get her for me as my wife." His parents tried to reason with him: was there no acceptable woman among his own relatives or anywhere in all Israel, that he had to take a wife from "the uncircumcised Philistines"? Samson brushed aside every argument with a single demand: "Get her for me, for she pleases my eyes." Yet the narrator pulls back a hidden curtain: his father and mother did not realize "this was from the LORD," who was seeking an occasion to confront the Philistines, then ruling over Israel (14:1–4).

Reluctantly giving in, his parents went down with Samson to Timnah to arrange the match. Near the vineyards of Timnah, a young lion suddenly burst out roaring straight at him. Instantly, "the Spirit of the LORD came powerfully upon him." Empty-handed, without a single weapon, the young man tore the fierce beast apart as effortlessly as someone tears a young goat. Afterward, he brushed himself off and kept walking without breathing a word to his father or mother. Then he went on down, spoke with the Philistine woman, and liked her very much (14:5–7).

Some time later, Samson headed back to Timnah for the wedding. Along the road, he turned aside to inspect the lion's carcass and found a swarm of bees and honey inside the dried skeleton. Scooping the honey out with his bare hands, he ate as he walked along. When he caught up with his parents, he gave them some to eat too, yet kept it completely secret that he had scraped the honey out of a dead lion (14:8–9).

While Samson's father went down to the bride's home to finalize arrangements, Samson hosted a seven-day wedding feast, as young men customarily did. Seeing the burly Israelite groom arrive, the bride's people assigned thirty young Philistine men to be his companions for the celebration. During the festivities, Samson proposed a wager: if they could solve his riddle within the seven days of the feast, he would pay them thirty linen garments and thirty sets of festive clothes; if they failed, they would owe him the same. The thirty men accepted eagerly. Samson posed his cryptic riddle: "Out of the eater came something to eat, and out of the strong came something sweet." For three whole days they racked their brains and came up empty (14:10–14).

When they still could not crack it by wits alone, the Philistine companions cornered the new bride with a brutal threat: coax the answer out of your husband, or we will burn you and your father's household to the ground. Terrified for her life, Samson's wife wept nonstop before him, accusing him of hating her and not truly loving her since he kept the riddle secret. Samson protested that he had not even told his own father and mother, so why should he tell her? Yet she cried relentlessly throughout the rest of the feast until she wore him down completely, and on the seventh day he finally gave in and told her. At once she passed the answer straight to her countrymen (14:15–17).

Before sunset on the seventh day, the men of the town marched up to Samson with a smug answer: "What is sweeter than honey? And what is stronger than a lion?" Realizing immediately he had been betrayed from inside his own chamber, Samson shot back a stinging retort: "If you had not plowed with my heifer, you would not have solved my riddle" (14:18).

Right then, the Spirit of the LORD came powerfully upon him again. Samson marched down to the Philistine city of Ashkelon, struck down thirty men, stripped their garments, and brought the clothing back to pay off the riddle winners in full. Burning with anger, he stormed back up to his father's house, abandoning his new bride altogether. Meanwhile, the bride's father promptly gave Samson's wife away to one of the Philistine men who had served as his best man at the wedding (14:19–20).

## Key Takeaways

**Living by whatever "pleases the eyes" is the first step down every slippery slope.** Samson never asked whether this marriage honored God or fit his Nazirite calling. His sole criterion was "she pleases my eyes" (14:3). Whenever impulse and appetite steer the wheel instead of God's word, even the most gifted servant walks straight into trouble.

**God still steers His sovereign purpose right through human impulsiveness.** Samson went down to Timnah purely chasing outward beauty, yet God used that unwise decision to pry open a clash with Israel's Philistine overlords (14:4). Humans follow selfish desires, yet God's sovereignty towers far above human foolishness.

**Spectacular gifts never substitute for moral maturity.** Samson could rip a lion apart with his bare hands (14:6), yet he could not restrain his impulses, guard the boundaries of his Nazirite vow, or withstand a few days of tears (14:17). Physical power or brilliant talent becomes downright dangerous without self-control and character.

**When both sides rely on gamesmanship and intimidation, conflict inevitably boils into bloodshed.** From Samson's provocative wager (14:14), to the companions' savage threat to burn a family alive (14:15), to the lethal raid on Ashkelon (14:19), the entire chapter escalates bitterly step by step. Sin never stays contained inside a harmless game.

## Deeper Meaning

**The phrase "right in my eyes"—a mirror held up to the entire book of Judges.** In Hebrew, Samson's line "she is right in my eyes" (14:3) uses the exact same expression that rings out like a closing verdict at the end of the book: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (17:6; 21:25). Samson is not merely a champion fighting foreigners; he is a living miniature of Israel itself—set apart for God in name, yet ruled by "the lust of the eyes" (1 John 2:16).

**God's mysterious sovereignty over flawed human choices.** Verse 14:4 is one of the most profound theological lines in the Old Testament: Samson chose wrongly, yet "this was from the LORD." It echoes Joseph's famous words to the brothers who had sold him into Egypt: "As for you, what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good" (Genesis 50:20). God is never the author of sin, yet He holds power enough to weave even tangled human threads into His tapestry of redemption.

**Two lion-slayers—Samson and David.** In the Old Testament, both Samson and David killed a lion before confronting the Philistines, yet setting the two side by side reveals a world of difference. David killed a lion to defend his father's flock and looked back on the moment in humble gratitude: "The LORD who delivered me from the paw of the lion" (1 Samuel 17:34–37). Samson wandered into Timnah's vineyards, hid the kill from his parents, and turned the carcass into party trivia for a wedding bet (14:5–14). Equal strength yields two opposite destinies depending on whether one seeks God's honor or feeds one's own ego.

**The first hairline cracks in the Nazirite vow.** Before Samson's birth, the angel had spelled out a life set apart from vineyards, strong drink, and anything unclean (13:4–5; cf. Numbers 6:3–7). Yet in his very first adult chapter, Samson strolls straight into the vineyards of Timnah (14:5), reaches into a lion's rotting carcass—unclean under Mosaic law (14:8–9; cf. Leviticus 11:27)—hides the source so his parents unwittingly share defiled honey, and throws a seven-day drinking feast after local custom (14:10). Quietly dismantling sacred fences one by one paves the road straight to the razor of chapter 16.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did God approve of Samson marrying a foreign woman?** The Law of Moses prohibited Israelites from intermarrying with idol-worshiping nations in the land lest their hearts be turned away from God (Exodus 34:16; Deuteronomy 7:3–4). Samson's parents gave sound, scriptural counsel (14:3). When 14:4 notes that this "was from the LORD," Scripture is not commending Samson's stubbornness. Commentators distinguish carefully between God's moral will (what He commands) and His sovereign providence (how He overrules human actions to advance His saving plan). At a moment when Israel had grown comfortable coexisting with Philistine occupiers, God used this ill-advised marriage to shatter their deadly compromise.

**The Spirit empowering Samson to kill thirty men at Ashkelon makes hard reading.** Modern readers are rightly startled: Samson loses a bet in a fury over his wife, and the Spirit of the LORD rushes on him so he kills thirty men for their clothes (14:19)? Two contexts help keep the reading fair. First, the Philistines were not peaceful neighbors, but a hostile military power subjugating Israel for forty years (13:1; 14:4), and Ashkelon was one of their five chief city-states. Second, throughout Judges, the Spirit coming upon a judge grants supernatural strength to strike an oppressor, without endorsing the judge's personal rage or petty vengeance. Scripture reports both God's power and Samson's rough, unruly character without airbrushing either.

**The "fourth day" versus "seventh day" detail in verses 15 and 17.** In the Hebrew Masoretic text, the companions fail to solve the riddle for three days (14:14), then threaten Samson's wife "on the seventh day" (14:15), while verse 17 says she wept throughout the seven days until he told her on the seventh. Ancient witnesses such as the Greek Septuagint and Syriac—followed by several modern English translations, including the BSB—read "on the fourth day" in verse 15, which flows seamlessly: three days of guessing on their own, intimidation on day four, and nonstop weeping until Samson breaks on day seven. In Hebrew script, the words for "fourth" and "seventh" look quite similar and could easily be slipped by a copyist, though the main storyline remains unaffected.

**Samson's remark about "plowing with my heifer" in verse 18.** This biting idiom compares his wife to a young cow the Philistine men hitched up and "plowed" to extract his secret. It also exposes Samson's coarse, dismissive view of women—a flaw that will keep triggering wreckage in the chapters ahead.

**The Mishnah and the Babylonian Talmud**, two foundational texts of Jewish tradition, summed up Samson's entire life in one crisp, piercing verdict: Samson followed his eyes, and therefore the Philistines gouged out his eyes.
