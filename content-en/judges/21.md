# Judges 21 — Preserving the Tribe of Benjamin

## Story

As the smoke from the civil war in the previous chapter began to clear, the Israelites looked in horror at what their own hands had wrought. They had nearly wiped out the tribe of Benjamin, leaving only six hundred men stranded on the rock of Rimmon; meanwhile, in the heat of their anger at Mizpah, they had sworn a severe oath: "Not one of us will give his daughter in marriage to a Benjaminite." Now the whole nation gathered at Bethel, sat before God until evening, lifted up their voices, and "wept bitterly," crying out, "O LORD, God of Israel, why has this happened in Israel, that today one tribe should be missing from Israel?" Early the next morning, they built an altar on the spot and presented burnt offerings and peace offerings (21:1–4).

Remorse surged as they thought of Benjamin, their own brother tribe, now standing on the brink of extinction from the twelve tribes. Yet they had tied their own hands not with one solemn oath at Mizpah, but with two. Alongside the ban on giving daughters to Benjamin, they had also sworn, "Whoever does not come up to the LORD at Mizpah shall surely be put to death." Caught in a trap of their own making, they asked whether any Israelite community had failed to appear at the assembly at Mizpah, while agonizing over how to find wives for the surviving Benjaminites now that they had sworn by the LORD not to give them any of their daughters (21:5–7).

Checking the roll, they discovered one town completely absent: not a single person from Jabesh-gilead across the Jordan had come to the camp for the assembly at Mizpah. The congregation promptly used the second oath to wiggle out of the dilemma created by the first. They selected twelve thousand of their bravest fighting men and dispatched them to Jabesh-gilead with a cold-blooded order: strike down the residents with the sword, including women and children—"completely destroy every male and every woman who has slept with a man." After the massacre, the soldiers found four hundred young virgins who had never slept with a man and marched them all back to the camp at Shiloh in the land of Canaan (21:8–12).

Only then did the whole assembly send word to the rock of Rimmon, offering peace to the six hundred Benjaminite warriors who had been hiding there for four months. The Benjaminites returned, and Israel gave them the four hundred surviving young women from Jabesh-gilead as wives. Yet four hundred women divided among six hundred men were "not enough for them"—two hundred men still had no wives (21:13–14).

Once again the people felt grieved for Benjamin, telling themselves that "the LORD had made a breach in the tribes of Israel." The elders of the assembly sat down to scheme: every Benjaminite woman had been annihilated, so where could they get wives for the remaining two hundred men so that Benjamin's lineage and tribal inheritance would survive and no tribe would be blotted out of Israel? Still, the Mizpah oath hung like a millstone over their heads: "Cursed is anyone who gives a wife to Benjamin" (21:15–18).

Casting about for a loophole, the elders hit upon a way to sidestep their oath. They reminded one another of the annual festival of the LORD held at Shiloh—located "north of Bethel, east of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and south of Lebonah." They instructed the two hundred unmarried Benjaminites to hide in the surrounding vineyards; whenever the young women of Shiloh came out to join in the festival dances, each man was to rush out, seize a wife for himself, and carry her straight back to the land of Benjamin. The elders even rehearsed an excuse in case the girls' fathers or brothers came to complain: they would plead with those families to show grace because there had not been enough wives for every Benjaminite from the war, and reassure them that since the fathers had not voluntarily "given" their daughters away, they were not guilty of breaking the oath (21:19–22).

The Benjaminites did exactly as instructed. Right in the middle of the dancing at Shiloh, they burst from the vineyards, abducted enough wives for themselves, and returned to their inheritance, where they rebuilt their burned-out towns and settled down. With that finished, the Israelites dispersed, each man returning to his own tribe, clan, and inheritance. The entire book of Judges closes with a single haunting line: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (21:23–25).

## Key Takeaways

**People find it remarkably easy to blame God for disasters their own hands created.** At Bethel, Israel wept loudly and asked God why this tragedy had happened to leave them short a tribe (21:3), then complained that "the LORD had made a breach in the tribes of Israel" (21:15). Yet it was their own rash oath at Mizpah (21:1) and their own unrestrained bloodlust at the end of chapter 20 that had wiped out every woman and child in Benjamin's towns (20:48). They wept loudly and built an altar quickly, yet across the whole of chapter 21 they never once knelt to confess their own sin or ask what God wanted them to do.

**Patching one crime with another only multiplies human suffering.** Distressed at having nearly annihilated Benjamin, the elders sent twelve thousand soldiers to massacre the entire town of Jabesh-gilead—slaughtering the parents and brothers of four hundred young women before handing those orphaned victims over to the surviving men of Benjamin (21:10–14). Still two hundred wives short, they engineered an ambush to kidnap innocent young women right in the middle of a worship festival at Shiloh (21:20–23). This closing tragedy began in chapter 19 with the violent abuse of one woman and ends in chapter 21 with the forced seizure of six hundred more women—all in the name of preserving national unity.

**Keeping the letter of an oath while trampling conscience is the darkest form of hypocrisy.** The Israelites trembled at the curse they had pronounced on anyone who "gave" a daughter to Benjamin (21:18), yet calmly plotted for men to "seize" them so the fathers could technically deny having given them away (21:22). They guarded every syllable of a foolish vow while trampling God's clearest commands concerning human life and the dignity of the vulnerable. When religion shrinks into word games designed to soothe conscience, it becomes a shield for cruelty.

**Full religious ceremonies can never replace a heart surrendered to God as King.** Throughout chapter 21, Israel never abandons religion on the surface: they travel to Bethel to weep before God, build an altar for burnt and peace offerings (21:2–4), observe an annual festival at Shiloh (21:19), and treat oaths sworn in the LORD's name with deadly seriousness (21:7). Yet they never once inquire of the LORD, and God remains completely silent from first verse to last. The closing sentence diagnoses the disease at its root: when "there was no king in Israel" (21:25), everyone climbs onto the throne of their own heart and manages to convince themselves that even their cruelest schemes are right.

## Deeper Meaning

**Israel ended up treating its own brothers exactly like pagan enemies.** Both major actions in chapter 21 borrow legal language originally reserved for hostile nations outside the covenant. The vow banning intermarriage with Benjamin (21:1) echoes God's prohibition against marrying into the seven idolatrous nations of Canaan (Deuteronomy 7:3). Likewise, the command to wipe out Jabesh-gilead—executing every male and every woman who had slept with a man, sparing only young virgins (21:11–12)—replays nearly word for word the punitive campaign against Midian in the wilderness (Numbers 31:17–18). By the end of Judges, not only has Israel failed to drive Canaanite ways out of the land, it has turned around and treated its own kin as foreign enemies.

**The Law of Moses already provided a gracious escape from rash vows, yet Israel chose cruelty instead.** The shadow of Jephthah from the middle of the book resurfaces at the end: earlier, Jephthah blurted out a reckless vow and stubbornly carried it out to his own daughter's ruin (11:30–31; 11:35); now the whole assembly binds itself with a hotheaded vow at Mizpah and shatters hundreds of innocent families. Yet right inside the Law of Moses, Leviticus had provided a merciful way out: anyone who spoke a thoughtless oath and realized their guilt could simply confess their sin and bring a guilt offering before the LORD (Leviticus 5:4–6). God never demands new sins to uphold an unlawful vow. Centuries later, Jesus flatly warned against reckless oath-taking (Matthew 5:33–37) and fiercely condemned legalistic loopholes that observe technicalities while abandoning justice and mercy (Matthew 23:23; Mark 7:9–13).

**A tear-stained bond between Benjamin and Jabesh-gilead echoes into the books of Samuel.** When four hundred young women of Jabesh-gilead became mothers to the next generation of Benjaminites, a remarkable tie of kinship was forged between two otherwise distant communities. Generations later, when King Nahash the Ammonite besieged Jabesh-gilead and threatened to gouge out every citizen's right eye, the man who immediately cut up a yoke of oxen and rallied all Israel to rescue Jabesh-gilead was Saul—Israel's first king, hailing from Gibeah of Benjamin (1 Samuel 11:1–11). And when Saul and his sons later fell in battle against the Philistines, it was the valiant men of Jabesh-gilead who marched all night to take their bodies down from the wall of Beth-shan and give them an honorable burial—a loyalty so deep that King David afterward sent messengers to bless them for it (1 Samuel 31:11–13; 2 Samuel 2:4–7).

**Amid human depravity, God's grace preserved a remnant of Benjamin for redemptive history.** Looking at how the elders salvaged Benjamin in chapter 21, we see only shame and violence. Yet God's overarching story is never shut down by human sin. Through that tiny surviving remnant, the tribe of Benjamin did not vanish from the covenant map: out of this tribe later came not only King Saul, but also Mordecai and Queen Esther, who delivered the Jewish people from genocide across the Persian Empire (Esther 2:5–7), and in the New Testament the apostle Paul—who carried the gospel across the Roman world while still naming himself "of the tribe of Benjamin" (Philippians 3:5; Romans 11:1). God never endorsed the atrocities of chapter 21, yet He wove redemption straight out of the wreckage humans left behind.

**The final verse of Judges awakens a longing for a true King.** Verse 21:25 brings to a close the fourfold refrain of the book's final chapters (17:6; 18:1; 19:1; 21:25), echoing Moses' ancient warning before Israel entered the land never to live where "everyone does what seems right in his own eyes" (Deuteronomy 12:8)—the very path Proverbs describes as seeming right to a man while ending in death (Proverbs 14:12). Right after this dark final page, the Bible turns to the book of Ruth—"In the days when the judges ruled" (Ruth 1:1)—quietly tracing the lineage of King David in Bethlehem (Ruth 4:17–22). Yet even human kings of flesh and blood would later stumble, reminding us of Gideon's earlier confession, "The LORD will rule over you" (8:23). The entire book of Judges is a deep ache pointing forward to Christ—the righteous King who does not merely impose outward order, but renews human hearts from the inside out.

## Notes & Nuances

**This is one of the most violent and painful chapters in Scripture: recording an act never means approving it.** Slaughtering the entire population of Jabesh-gilead—women and children included—to seize four hundred virgins (21:10–12), then orchestrating an ambush to abduct two hundred more young women during a religious festival (21:20–23), are indefensible atrocities. Reading this chapter responsibly requires distinguishing between what Scripture *reports* and what God *commands*. Throughout chapter 21, God never directs them to attack Jabesh-gilead, never invents the Shiloh ambush, and never speaks a single word of endorsement. The narrator drops the verdict "there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (21:25) right after the abductions to condemn the entire moral collapse, not to admire the elders' cleverness.

**Israel's "grief" (or "repentance") in 21:6 and 21:15 is not genuine repentance before God.** Some older translations render the Hebrew verb here as Israel "repenting" for Benjamin (21:6; 21:15), which can mislead modern readers into thinking they were humbly confessing sin, while modern versions such as the Berean Standard Bible capture the sense more precisely as being "grieved" or "filled with compassion." The Hebrew word denotes emotional pity, sorrow, or regret over devastating consequences rather than true turning from wrongdoing. That emotional regret differs sharply from genuine repentance: it never brings them to their knees to confess their excess or their rash oath, and instead drives them into fresh atrocities to fix the mess.

**Why does 21:19 give such unusually detailed directions to Shiloh?** Shiloh had housed the tabernacle for generations (1 Samuel 1:3), so people living during the era of the judges hardly needed directions to find it. Yet 21:19 maps it out painstakingly: "north of Bethel, east of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and south of Lebonah." Many scholars suggest that by the time Judges reached its final written form centuries later, Shiloh had long lain in ruins, prompting the author to clarify its location for later readers. Several literary critics also catch a chilling edge in the elders' speech: they give step-by-step geographical coordinates for hunting down women as if briefing a raiding party. The "yearly festival of the LORD" featuring dancing amid the vineyards is commonly understood to be the Feast of Tabernacles celebrating the autumn harvest (Leviticus 23:39–41).

**Is the "no king" refrain merely political propaganda for monarchy?** Many Old Testament scholars observe that chapters 19–21 clearly pave the way for kingship in the books of Samuel—even tilting subtly toward David's Bethlehem in contrast to the stain of Saul's Gibeah. Yet read within the whole sweep of Judges—where Abimelech's self-made kingship brought bloodshed in chapter 9 and Gideon declared that the LORD alone would rule (8:23)—verse 21:25 carries two layers of meaning together. Israel genuinely needed righteous earthly order to curb the tyranny of the strong over the weak, yet at a deeper level they had dethroned God as King in their own hearts. When people live solely by their own impulses and then turn around to blame the LORD for the fallout, a proverb sums up all of chapter 21 with painful clarity: "A man's own folly subverts his way, yet his heart rages against the LORD" (Proverbs 19:3).

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, places the events of chapter 21 near the beginning of the judges' era, stressing the eleven tribes' remorse at seeing Benjamin nearly extinguished and the two harsh measures they adopted: punishing Jabesh-gilead for skipping the campaign, then stationing the remaining two hundred Benjaminites in the vineyards of Shiloh to abduct wives during a festival. Josephus spells out the elders' rationale for the loophole: because they had not personally given their daughters away, the oath remained unbroken, and if the fathers protested, the council would urge them to calm down and blame themselves for failing to guard their daughters carefully. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Josephus recounting the strike on Jabesh-gilead, the Shiloh abductions, and the elders' argument urging fathers to blame their own lack of vigilance in Book 5, exact section number not verified -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, classic Jewish collection of law and interpretation, recalls chapter 21 when explaining the origins of the joyful holiday *Tu B'Av* (the fifteenth of Av), when young women dressed in white went out to dance in the vineyards. According to rabbinic tradition, one reason the day became a celebration was that the tribes of Israel finally permitted intermarriage with Benjamin again: the sages analyzed the exact wording of the Mizpah oath—"not one of *us*"—to conclude that the prohibition bound only the generation that swore it, not their descendants. *(Babylonian Talmud, Tractates Ta'anit and Bava Batra)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall the tradition linking Judges 21 to the fifteenth of Av and interpreting "of us" in chapter 21 verse 1 in Ta'anit 30b and Bava Batra 121a, exact wording not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, criticizes both Israel's attitude and its solutions in chapter 21 with blunt honesty. He exposes the absurdity of the people weeping at Bethel and asking God why such a disaster had struck as though He were to blame, when their own unchecked fury in chapter 20 had driven Benjamin to the edge of extinction. Rather than humbly confessing the folly of their Mizpah oath, Henry writes, they patched one crime with another—massacring Jabesh-gilead and abetting kidnappings at Shiloh—while the book's closing verse demonstrates how swiftly a society without righteous governance plunges into chaos. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Judges chapter 21)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Henry's three main observations on blaming God at Bethel, fixing sin with fresh atrocities, and the closing verse of chapter 21, exact wording not verified -->

**Robert Alter**, Jewish-American scholar of biblical literature, underscores the bitter sarcasm in the narrator's closing chapter. The elders pride themselves on technical cleverness as they orchestrate abductions at Shiloh so fathers cannot technically be accused of "giving" their daughters away—a petty legal dodge that only lays bare the moral bankruptcy of the entire community. Alter also notes how detailed geographical directions to Shiloh sit right alongside a brutal kidnapping scheme before concluding with the refrain "there was no king in Israel," propelling the story straight toward the quest for monarchy in the books of Samuel. *(Ancient Israel: The Former Prophets, 2013)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Alter's literary analysis of the elders' verbal loophole and the transitional role of chapter 21 verse 25 toward kingship in his annotated translation, exact page numbers not verified -->

**Tim Keller**, New York pastor who died in 2023, reads chapter 21 as rock bottom for the entire book of Judges: every human attempt to rescue ourselves without returning to God's kingship spirals straight into fresh violence. For Keller, the closing verdict "there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" describes not just ancient political anarchy, but the universal disorder of the human heart: when each person crowns himself king, freedom turns into brutality against the weak.
