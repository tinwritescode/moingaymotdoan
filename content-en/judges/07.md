# Judges 7 — Three Hundred Men Rout Midian

## Story

Early the next morning, Jerubbaal—that is, Gideon—and all the troops who had gathered with him set out and camped beside the spring of Harod (a name in Hebrew meaning *trembling*). North of them in the valley, near the hill of Moreh, stretched the vast encampment of the Midianite coalition. Israel's army numbered thirty-two thousand men—already vastly outnumbered by the enemy—yet the LORD spoke a word that startled Gideon: "You have too many people for Me to deliver Midian into their hands." God stated His concern plainly: if such a large force won the battle, Israel would boast against Him, saying, "My own hand has saved me." He commanded Gideon to proclaim that anyone who was fearful and trembling could turn back and go home. As a result, twenty-two thousand men—more than two-thirds of the army—immediately withdrew, leaving only ten thousand (7:1–3).

Looking at the ten thousand who remained, the LORD still said, "There are still too many people." He instructed Gideon to lead the whole company down to the water so He could test them once more. Anyone who cupped water in his hand and lapped it with his tongue like a dog was to be set apart on one side; anyone who knelt down and bowed over the water to drink was to be set apart on the other. In the end, only three hundred men brought water to their mouths with their hands and lapped it, while the other nine thousand seven hundred knelt down to drink. The LORD declared that He would use those very three hundred men to save Israel and deliver Midian into Gideon's hand. The other nine thousand seven hundred were sent back to their tents, leaving their provisions and ram's horns with the three hundred who remained on the hillside, looking down at the Midianite camp spread out across the valley below (7:4–8).

That night, the LORD commanded Gideon to go down and attack the Midianite camp at once, for He had delivered it into his hands. Yet knowing His servant was still trembling before an enemy army as thick as locusts and camels as countless as the sand on the seashore, God gently provided a way to steady him: if he was still afraid, he should take his servant Purah and slip down to the outposts of the enemy camp to listen. Just as the two crept up to the edge of the camp, they overheard a Midianite soldier telling his friend a strange dream: he had seen a round loaf of barley bread—the coarse, cheap bread of the poor—tumbling into the Midianite camp, striking a tent so hard that it collapsed and turned completely upside down. His companion interpreted the dream on the spot: "This is nothing less than the sword of Gideon"; God had already delivered the entire Midianite camp into his hand. Hearing the enemy's own mouth confirm God's promise, Gideon bowed down and worshiped God right there in the dark of night, then hurried back to rouse the three hundred men: "Get up, for the LORD has delivered the camp of Midian into your hands!" (7:9–15).

Gideon divided the three hundred men into three companies, handing out the strangest weapons ever seen in warfare: no swords, spears, or shields, but to each man a trumpet, an empty clay jar, and a torch hidden inside the jar. He instructed all three companies to watch him and follow his lead: as soon as they reached the edge of the camp and heard him blow his trumpet, all three sides were to blast their trumpets in unison and shout, "For the LORD and for Gideon!" (7:16–18).

Right at the beginning of the middle watch—around ten o'clock at night, just after the enemy sentries had changed shifts—Gideon led his company of one hundred to the outskirts of the camp. Suddenly, all three companies blew their trumpets together and smashed their clay jars to pieces. Holding the blazing torches in their left hands as light erupted through the blackness, and blowing their trumpets relentlessly with their right, three hundred voices roared: "A sword for the LORD and for Gideon!" They simply held their ground around the perimeter, never even rushing into hand-to-hand combat. Jolted from sleep in sheer panic, seeing three hundred torches flare and hearing three hundred trumpets blast as though three hundred massive regiments were closing in, the entire Midianite camp bolted, shrieking in chaos. The LORD Himself turned every man's sword against his own companion throughout the camp, and the survivors fled headlong toward the Jordan River (7:19–22).

Only then were men from the tribes of Naphtali, Asher, and Manasseh called back out to pursue the fleeing remnants. Gideon also sent messengers throughout the hill country of Ephraim, urging them to come down and seize the fords along the Jordan River as far as Beth-barah. The Ephraimites quickly captured all the crossings, seized two Midianite commanders named Oreb (meaning *raven*) and Zeeb (meaning *wolf*), killed Oreb at the rock of Oreb and Zeeb at the winepress of Zeeb, and brought the heads of the two commanders across the Jordan to Gideon (7:23–25).

## Key Takeaways

**God deliberately trims away human strength so that all the glory belongs to Him.** To human eyes, thirty-two thousand soldiers facing an ocean of Midianites was already hopelessly small. Yet God still called them too many, cutting the force down to ten thousand and then reducing it to a mere three hundred (7:2–7). God did not do this to toy with Gideon, but to heal the deepest sickness of the human heart—the proud boast, "My own hand has saved me" (7:2). As long as we lean on our own strength, we steal God's glory; only when every earthly prop is stripped away do we clearly see His saving hand.

**God does not merely issue commands; He tenderly upholds His frightened servant.** On the night before the battle, God saw that Gideon's heart was still anxious even though he did not ask for another sign. God Himself took the initiative, telling him to slip down to the edge of the enemy camp with his servant and overhear the dream so his courage would be strengthened (7:9–11). God does not mock trembling faith; He knows human limits and supplies fresh strength at the exact moment it is needed most.

**Worship comes before victory.** The instant Gideon heard the Midianite soldier interpret the dream, he did not rush straight back to muster his troops; he bowed down and worshiped God right at the edge of the enemy lines (7:15). All the lingering fear that had dogged him since chapter 6 vanished in that very moment. When a person genuinely humbles himself in worship before God, the fear of human power loses its grip.

**Ordinary things in God's hands are enough to topple arrogant powers.** A cheap loaf of barley bread rolls down and flattens the splendid tent of a Midianite commander (7:13); three hundred men armed only with ram's horns and empty clay jars scatter an army as numerous as locusts (7:16; 7:21–22). In the kingdom of God, effectiveness is never measured by the impressiveness of the instrument, but by the presence of the One who wields it.

## Deeper Meaning

**The LORD does not save by many or by few.** The principle in 7:2–7 echoes from one end of Scripture to the other. In the days of King Saul, young Prince Jonathan dared to climb up against a Philistine garrison with only his armor-bearer, trusting that nothing can hinder the LORD from saving, whether by many or by few (1 Samuel 14:6). The prophet Zechariah reminded God's people that His work succeeds not by might nor by power, but by His Spirit (Zechariah 4:6). In the New Testament, the apostle Paul sums up this startling divine pattern: God chose the foolish, the weak, and the lowly things of the world "so that no one may boast in His presence" (1 Corinthians 1:27–29).

**Trumpets, battle cries, and enemies thrown into self-destruction.** The scene of three hundred men encircling the camp, blowing trumpets, and shouting without drawing a sword immediately recalls the fall of Jericho, when the priests blew the trumpets and the whole nation shouted (Joshua 6:16). Likewise, the LORD causing the Midianites to turn their swords on one another in the darkness (7:22) would happen again centuries later in the reign of King Jehoshaphat, when the armies of Ammon and Moab slaughtered each other while Judah stood singing praises to the LORD (2 Chronicles 20:23). Scripture drives home one refrain again and again: the battle belongs to the LORD.

**Light bursting out from broken clay jars.** The three hundred torches hidden inside clay jars could not shine a single beam as long as the jars stayed intact; only when the jars were shattered did the fire suddenly blaze out and strike terror into the enemy (7:16; 7:20). The apostle Paul later drew on a closely related image, writing that God's servants carry the treasure of the gospel in "jars of clay" so that everyone can see this surpassing power comes from God and not from fragile human strength (2 Corinthians 4:7).

**"The day of Midian" becomes a prophetic portrait of the Messiah.** The extraordinary victory of chapter 7 burned itself into Israel's memory for centuries. The psalmist prayed that God would deal with hostile nations just as He had dealt with Oreb and Zeeb long ago (Psalms 83:11). Even more strikingly, when the prophet Isaiah foretold the birth of the Messiah—the Child who would shatter every yoke of human oppression—he compared that great liberation to "the day of Midian's defeat" (Isaiah 9:4; cf. Isaiah 10:26).

**The Law of Moses regarding the fearful is put into practice.** The command allowing anyone who was afraid and trembling to return home (7:3) was not a spontaneous notion of Gideon's; it was the humane, practical statute already written into Deuteronomy, designed to keep one soldier's panic from melting the courage of the entire army (Deuteronomy 20:8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Were the three hundred who lapped water more elite and alert warriors?** Countless sermons like to speculate that the three hundred who scooped water to their mouths by hand were savvy soldiers keeping their eyes lifted for enemies, while the nine thousand seven hundred who knelt to drink were careless and unguarded. In reality, the text says not a single word praising those three hundred as smarter or braver. On the contrary, the description of "lapping with the tongue like a dog" sounds anything but heroic, and the sole purpose God announces in 7:2 is to slash the troop count so low that Israel would have zero grounds for boasting. In other words, God chose them not because they were impressive, but precisely because they were so few!

**The cry "A sword for the LORD and for Gideon"—clever tactics or a seed of pride?** There are two ways to read Gideon's pairing of his own name alongside the LORD's in 7:18 and 7:20. One reading sees an early sprout of self-exaltation, a tendency that will grow and lead to tragedy in chapter 8. Another, fairer reading notes that just moments earlier in 7:14, Gideon had overheard the Midianite soldier call the barley loaf in the dream "the sword of Gideon"; so he deliberately placed his own name in the midnight battle cry to strike straight at the terror already spreading through enemy ranks. Either way, the delicious irony is that not one of the three hundred men actually held a sword in his hand—one hand held a torch, and the other held a trumpet (7:20)!

**Why was it a loaf of barley bread in the dream?** (7:13). In the ancient world, barley was the cheap grain eaten by poor farmers and fed to livestock, far inferior to the wheat enjoyed by the wealthy. Moreover, the tent in the dream symbolized the nomadic lifestyle of the Midianites, whereas barley bread represented the settled peasant life of Israel—and Gideon himself had been threshing grain in secret inside a winepress back in chapter 6 (6:11). The dream made the Midianite soldiers shudder because the impoverished farmers they despised were about to roll down and crush their mighty camp.

**Nighttime tactics and supernatural intervention work side by side.** Attacking at the start of the middle watch right after the guards changed shifts caught sentries whose eyes had not yet adjusted to the dark, while the rest of the camp was in its deepest sleep. In ancient warfare, usually only the commander of each company carried a trumpet and a torch, so when three hundred horns blasted and three hundred flames flared at once from three directions, the Midianites assumed an enormous army was closing the ring around them. Yet that shrewd psychological tactic only opened the battle; the text states plainly that it was the LORD Himself who turned their swords against one another (7:22).

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, the third-century Alexandrian theologian, read the story of the three hundred men and their shattered clay jars as a vivid spiritual portrait of the Christian life. In his view, the earthen jar represents the weak, fragile body of the believer, while the torch concealed inside is the light of God's Word and the Holy Spirit. As long as the jar remains whole and unbroken—that is, as long as the fleshly ego refuses to yield—the light within cannot shine out; only when the clay vessel is broken in sacrifice and humility does the light of Christ blaze forth to scatter the powers of darkness. *(Homilies on Judges, Homily 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Origen's allegorical reading of the broken clay jars revealing the torchlight as the believer's body and the light of Christ is a well-known highlight of Homily 9 on Judges -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, offered a surprising explanation of the test at the water, directly opposite the modern preaching habit of praising the three hundred as elite warriors. Josephus recounted that God told Gideon to keep the most *cowardly* men—those so panicked by the enemy that they did not dare kneel calmly to drink, but hurriedly scooped water up to lap it in haste. According to Josephus, God deliberately kept the weakest, most timid band so that victory over Midian would unmistakably belong to God's power rather than the soldiers' prowess. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus's distinctive explanation in Book 5 that the 300 who lapped water were the most fearful so that victory would belong wholly to God is very well known -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, offered a sharp observation on why God pared down the army in chapter 7 verse 2: in human eyes Gideon's force was already far too small, yet in God's eyes it was far too large, because human pride is quick to steal the credit of the Savior. Henry also paused over the moving moment when Gideon heard the dream and immediately worshiped God right at the edge of the enemy camp (7:15): even surrounded by danger and standing on the brink of a major battle, a grateful heart never delays bowing down to honor God. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Judges chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's remarks on the paradox of "too many" in verse 2 and Gideon's immediate worship in verse 15 are familiar elements of his Judges 7 commentary, not checked against the exact text -->

According to Keller, humanity's deepest sin is not merely doing bad things, but proudly believing we can save ourselves by our own strength or moral record.
