# Judges 3 — Othniel, Ehud, and Shamgar

## Story

Chapter 3 closes the book's introduction by listing the nations God left inside Canaan—the five Philistine lords, all the Canaanites, the Sidonians, and the Hivites of Mount Lebanon. God left them for twin reasons: both "to teach warfare to the descendants of the Israelites who had not known it before" under Joshua, and "to test Israel, to find out whether they would obey the LORD's commandments" given through Moses. Israel fails that test almost immediately. Settling right alongside six native peoples, they "took their daughters in marriage, gave their own daughters to their sons, and served their gods" (3:1–6).

Now the downward cycle sketched in chapter 2 begins spinning in earnest. Israel forgets the LORD to worship the Baals and Asherahs. Burning with anger, God hands them over to Cushan-rishathaim, king of Aram-naharaim (Mesopotamia), who oppresses them for eight years. When Israel cries out to the LORD, He raises up the first judge: Othniel son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother—the same bold warrior who captured Debir in chapter 1. "The Spirit of the LORD came upon him"; he judges Israel, marches to war, and defeats the Aramean king. The land enjoys forty years of peace until Othniel dies (3:7–11).

Once Othniel dies, Israel returns to evil. This time the LORD strengthens Eglon, king of Moab to the east. Allying with Ammon and Amalek, Eglon defeats Israel, seizes the City of Palms—Jericho's gateway into the Promised Land—and holds Israel in subjection for eighteen years (3:12–14).

Again Israel cries out, and God raises a startlingly unconventional deliverer: Ehud son of Gera, a Benjaminite who is "a left-handed man" (literally *restricted in his right hand*). Selected to carry Israel's annual tribute to Moab's king, Ehud forges a double-edged dagger roughly a foot and a half long and straps it beneath his clothing on his right thigh—the side guards rarely frisk. Presenting tribute to Eglon—whom Scripture sketches briskly as "a very fat man"—Ehud escorts the tribute bearers partway home, then turns back alone from the carved stone idols near Gilgal, announcing a secret message for the king. Eglon waves every attendant out of his cool upper chamber. Alone with the monarch, Ehud steps close and declares: "I have a message from God for you." As Eglon rises from his throne, Ehud reaches across with his left hand, draws the dagger from his right thigh, and plunges it deep into the king's belly. "Even the handle sank in after the blade, and his fat closed in over it," the blade protruding out the back. Leaving the weapon inside, Ehud slips out onto the porch, shuts and locks the roof-chamber doors behind him, and escapes (3:15–23).

Outside, Eglon's servants see the locked doors and linger awkwardly saying, "He must be relieving himself in the cool room." Waiting until embarrassment overwhelms them, they fetch a key, unlock the door—and freeze staring at their master sprawled dead on the floor. That crucial delay buys Ehud time to pass the stone idols and escape into Ephraim's hills. Sounding a trumpet to rally Israel, he charges down at their head shouting: "Follow me, for the LORD has delivered your enemies the Moabites into your hand." Seizing the Jordan fords to block Moab's escape, Israel strikes down ten thousand vigorous Moabite warriors with no survivors. Moab falls subdued, and the land enjoys eighty years of peace (3:24–30).

At the chapter's close, one intriguing figure flashes past in a single unforgettable sentence: Shamgar son of Anath. Without detailing his tribe or campaign, Scripture notes simply that he "struck down six hundred Philistines with an oxgoad, and he too saved Israel" (3:31).

## Key Takeaways

**God uses both textbook heroes and unlikely misfits to save His people.** Othniel comes spotless from noble Judah and Caleb's household, empowered by God's Spirit in open battle (3:9–10). Right after him steps Ehud, disabled in his right hand and relying on daring stealth (3:15–21), followed by Shamgar wielding nothing but a farm prod (3:31). Grace never insists on glossy credentials; God loves working through surprising people.

**What the world labels a weakness can become God's sharpest advantage.** In ancient culture, the right hand embodied strength while a crippled right hand signaled disability. Yet Ehud's left-handedness slipped a concealed blade right past royal guards searching the opposite hip (3:16–21). God does not wait until our flaws disappear before using us—often He works directly through them.

**Each relapse into sin carries a steeper cost.** Under Othniel, oppression lasted eight years (3:8); by Ehud's day it more than doubled to eighteen years (3:14), with enemies occupying the very City of Palms inside Canaan (3:13). Repeating sin never stays flat—its grip tightens every turn.

**Deliverance flows from God, never fancy weapons.** Othniel triumphed because "the Spirit of the LORD came upon him" (3:10); Ehud rallied Israel trusting God had already handed Moab over (3:28); Shamgar dropped six hundred iron-armed Philistines holding a wooden oxgoad (3:31). Victory never hangs on giant swords or vast armies.

## Deeper Meaning

**The weak shaming the strong—a golden thread leading straight to the cross.** These first three deliverers establish a rhythm pulsing across all Scripture: God loves turning worldly power upside down. A right-hand-restricted envoy topples a bloated tyrant; a farmer's stick scatters six hundred elite soldiers. Over a millennium later, Paul captures this exact gospel pattern at the cross: "God chose the weak things of the world to shame the strong," so no flesh may boast before Him (1 Corinthians 1:27–29).

**The City of Palms—losing the very gateway of past victory.** The City of Palms refers to Jericho's oasis (Deuteronomy 34:3; cf. Joshua 6:26), site of Israel's miraculous opening triumph in Canaan. Watching Moab's king march across the Jordan to occupy that exact gateway (3:13) stung like a national slap: drifting from God forfeited the very threshold God had flung open for their fathers.

**The ironic stamp of Benjamin.** In Hebrew, *Benjamin* literally means *son of the right hand*. Delightful irony rings when Benjaminite Ehud turns out right-hand-restricted and left-handed (3:15)! That detail resurfaces grimly near the book's end, when Benjamin fields "seven hundred select left-handed troops" able to sling stones at a single hair without missing (20:16)—suggesting Benjaminites may even have trained left-handed fighters deliberately to throw foes off balance.

**Mixed marriages confirm Moses' oldest warning.** Verse 3:6 lays out the crisp three-step slide: living together, intermarrying, and serving their gods—matching verbatim Moses' warning before crossing the Jordan that interfaith unions would lure Israel's hearts away from the LORD (Deuteronomy 7:3–4). Ignored warnings came due within a single generation.

**Shamgar hints at gathering Philistine storms ahead.** Though granted just one verse in 3:31, Shamgar echoes again in Deborah's song: "In the days of Shamgar son of Anath," highways stood deserted as bandits ruled the roads (5:6). That brief closing note signals that while the Jordan Valley rested eighty years, Philistine pressure along the southwestern coast was already rising—foreshadowing the massive clashes of Samson's day.

## Notes & Nuances

**What ethical questions arise around Ehud's deception and assassination of Eglon?** Many readers understandably pause over Ehud claiming "a message from God" to clear the room and stab a king. Two balanced points help read honestly. First, this was no private peacetime murder for personal grudge, but a wartime strike cutting off the head of a foreign occupier who had subjugated and bled Israel for eighteen years. Second, Scripture narrates the episode bluntly without praising deception or recommending political assassination for later believers. It affirms simply that God raised Ehud to deliver His people (3:15)—just as throughout Judges God works through deeply flawed figures without endorsing every tactic they use.

**Eglon's episode brims with biting ancient political satire.** In Hebrew, 3:15–25 crackles with sharp wordplay mocking the tyrant. *Eglon* sounds straight like *fatted calf*—fittingly, his vast belly swallows the dagger hilt like a fattened beast readied for slaughter. Verse 3:24's polite "relieving himself" literally reads *covering his feet*—a classic Hebrew euphemism for using the toilet! Picturing royal guards shuffling red-faced outside a locked latrine while the assassin escapes turns a terrifying oppressor into a biting laughingstock. Even the chapter's first oppressor, *Cushan-rishathaim*, sounds in Hebrew like *Cushan of double wickedness*—likely a sarcastic Israelite nickname for their tormentor.

**Did *left-handed* mean physical disability or ambidextrous training?** Literally the Hebrew reads *bound/restricted in his right hand*, implying impairment or weakness. The ancient Greek Septuagint rendered it *ambidextrous*. Whether Ehud's right hand was naturally impaired or deliberately bound in youth for specialized Benjaminite combat training, the story's punch holds: his unusual handedness completely fooled Eglon's security.

**Was Shamgar son of Anath even an Israelite?** *Shamgar* is non-Hebrew, and *Anath* names a Canaanite war goddess—as well as the town Beth-anath mentioned in 1:33. Many scholars suspect Shamgar was a foreigner or came from a Canaanite-influenced border household living beside Philistia. If so, God's surprise grace shines brighter still: amid an unfaithful Israel, God used a border outsider swinging an eight-foot iron-tipped cattle prod to rescue His people.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, third-century Alexandrian theologian, read Ehud through the ancient Greek Septuagint rendering him *ambidextrous*. Drawing spiritual allegory, Origen portrays Ehud as a seasoned spiritual warrior using both hands like right hands—standing firm in prosperity and adversity alike while wielding the double-edged sword of God's word against sin. **Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, retells Ehud's story in a far more dignified register than the raw Hebrew text. He portrays Ehud as a young resident of Jericho who carefully cultivated King Eglon's trust through repeated gifts before catching him alone at midday in his summer pavilion. Tellingly, Josephus scrubs out nearly all earthy details—the fat swallowing the hilt and servants waiting outside the toilet—polishing the episode for refined Greco-Roman readers. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book V)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus embellishes Ehud gaining Eglon's confidence and omits the coarser satirical details in Antiquities Book V; exact section numbers not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, addresses the ethical discomfort of Ehud stabbing Eglon directly. Ehud acted never as a private assassin for revenge, Henry stresses, nor may anyone ever invoke this event to excuse regicide or private murder; he carried out an extraordinary divine commission liberating an occupied nation from a foreign invader. Turning to Shamgar, Henry notes crisply that when God chooses to save, He never runs short of weapons—a farmer's oxgoad in obedient hands suffices against an army. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Judges 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: defending Ehud's unique wartime mandate while barring private imitation and praising God's use of Shamgar's oxgoad are classic Matthew Henry notes on Judges 3; exact text not checked -->

**Robert Alter**, American-Jewish literary scholar of the Bible, showcases the brilliant political satire woven into Ehud and Eglon's scene. Analyzing the Hebrew pun linking Eglon to a fatted calf, the grotesque image of fat engulfing the blade, and servants standing mortified outside a locked restroom, Alter shows how the biblical narrator deploys earthy, biting humor to strip away the pretensions of an oppressor who thought himself untouchable. *(The Art of Biblical Narrative; Ancient Israel: The Former Prophets, 2013)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Alter's literary analysis of political satire, the Eglon pun, and earthy comedy in the Ehud narrative is famously and widely cited -->

**Tim Keller**, New York pastor who died in 2023, pairs the opening two judges to spotlight grace's upside-down methods. Textbook hero Othniel from royal Judah demonstrates salvation comes solely through God's Spirit; right afterward God picks marginalized, right-hand-impaired Ehud from tiny Benjamin, turning society's overlooked weakness into the very instrument of rescue. This pattern of triumphing through weakness points straight to Jesus, who conquered evil not with an emperor's sword, but through a despised cross. *(Judges for You, 2013, on chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: contrasting textbook Othniel with marginalized Ehud to point toward the cross is Keller's central argument on chapter 3 in Judges for You -->
