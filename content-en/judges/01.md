# Judges 1 — Halfhearted Victories After Joshua's Death

## Story

The book opens over a vast leadership void: "After the death of Joshua" (1:1). The commander who brought Israel into the Promised Land has passed away, yet Canaan remains only partly taken. With no designated successor stepping forward, the Israelites inquire directly of the LORD: "Who will be the first to go up and fight for us against the Canaanites?" God replies: "Judah shall go up. Indeed, I have delivered the land into his hand." Judah invites brother tribe Simeon to march with them into Judah's allotment first, promising to return the favor in Simeon's territory afterward. Simeon agrees (1:1–3).

Their campaign opens brilliantly. The LORD delivers Canaanites and Perizzites into their hands, striking down ten thousand men at Bezek. When local lord Adoni-bezek flees, Judah catches him and cuts off his thumbs and big toes—a brutal mutilation keeping a warrior from gripping weapons or keeping balance in battle. Remarkably, the captured tyrant confesses: seventy kings with thumbs and big toes cut off once scavenged scraps beneath his table; now, "As I have done, so God has repaid me." Brought to Jerusalem, he dies there (1:4–7).

Judah attacks Jerusalem, puts it to the sword, and sets the city ablaze before campaigning against Canaanites across the hills, the Negev, and the western foothills. They strike Hebron (formerly Kiriath-arba), defeating the three giant clans of Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai, then advance on Debir (formerly Kiriath-sepher). Caleb announces a prize: whoever captures Debir wins his daughter Acsah in marriage. Othniel, Caleb's younger kinsman, storms the town and weds Acsah. Traveling to her new home, Acsah urges Othniel to ask her father for a field, then dismounts her donkey herself. When Caleb asks what she wants, she speaks up boldly: "Give me a blessing. Since you have given me land in the Negev, give me springs of water as well!" Caleb grants her both the upper and lower springs (1:8–15).

Momentum holds a bit longer. Descendants of the Kenite—Moses' father-in-law—go up with Judah to settle in the wilderness south of Arad. Keeping its word, Judah marches with Simeon against Zephath, utterly destroys it, and renames it Hormah. Judah also takes Gaza, Ashkelon, and Ekron, while Caleb receives Hebron as Moses promised and drives out Anak's three sons. Then, right mid-stride, the triumph stumbles sharply: "The LORD was with Judah, and they took possession of the hill country; but they could not drive out the inhabitants of the plains because they had chariots of iron" (1:16–20).

From here the shadows deepen rapidly. The Benjaminites fail to drive out the Jebusites in Jerusalem, who stay living alongside them "to this day" (1:21). Farther north, the house of Joseph marches against Bethel (formerly Luz), with the LORD at their side. Their scouts spot a man walking out of town and promise him mercy if he shows them a way in. He points out the entrance; Joseph puts the city to the sword yet lets the man and his household walk free. Far from joining God's people, the man migrates to Hittite country, builds a new city, and names it right back after the old pagan town: Luz (1:22–26).

After Bethel unfolds a bleak roll call of unfinished business across the northern tribes. Manasseh fails to expel residents of Beth-shean, Taanach, Dor, Ibleam, and Megiddo because the Canaanites stay determined to remain. Even after Israel grows strong, they merely "forced the Canaanites into slave labor, but they never drove them out completely." Ephraim leaves Canaanites living in Gezer; Zebulun leaves them in Kitron and Nahalol under forced labor. By Asher and Naphtali, the phrasing flips upside down completely: no longer do Canaanites dwell among Israelites—instead, Asher and Naphtali "lived among the Canaanite inhabitants of the land" (1:27–33).

Rock bottom arrives with Dan: "The Amorites forced the Danites into the hill country and did not allow them to come down into the plain." Only later does Joseph's stronger hand press those surrounding Amorites into forced labor. Chapter 1 closes tracing the Amorites' own territory—a chilling stamp on a Promised Land left half-claimed (1:34–36).

## Key Takeaways

**Halfhearted obedience launches a long downward slide.** Chapter 1 moves cleanly down a slope: starting with Judah consulting God and winning boldly (1:1–15), stalling before iron chariots (1:19), compromising to keep Canaanites as cheap labor (1:28), and hitting bottom as Dan gets shoved back into the hills by foes (1:34). Cut obedience in half, and the half we spare eventually swallows our whole life.

**Pragmatic calculation routinely defeats faithfulness.** While weak, tribes claimed enemies were too strong to expel. Yet even once Israel grew powerful (1:28), they still kept Canaanites around for forced labor. Driving them out took sweat and risk; taxing their labor felt easier and immediately profitable. Compromise rarely begins with open defiance of God—it usually starts with a "smart" business calculation.

**Visible fears quickly eclipse invisible promises.** One single verse sets two jarring clauses side by side: the LORD was with Judah, yet they failed to clear the plains because of iron chariots (1:19). Iron never outmatched God's hand; human eyes simply stared harder at enemy hardware than at the God walking beside them.

**Even in declining times, wholehearted faith still shines.** Caleb, Othniel, and Acsah glow like bright embers amid gathering gloom (1:12–15). Caleb keeps faith's flame burning since Moses' days; Othniel steps boldly into challenge; and Acsah knows dry Negev land needs water, trusting her generous father to give springs as well.

**Refuse to become a mirror image of the world's cruelty.** Severing Adoni-bezek's thumbs and big toes copied that very Canaanite tyrant's brutality (1:6–7). On the book's opening page, the alarm bell already rings: Israel is quietly starting to act just like the surrounding nations.

## Deeper Meaning

**Joshua's long shadow raises the question of who will lead God's people.** Joshua opened with "After the death of Moses the servant of the LORD" (Joshua 1:1), immediately raising up Joshua to succeed him. Judges opens identically—"After the death of Joshua" (1:1; cf. Joshua 24:29–30)—yet names zero human successor. That silence launches the book's overarching question: who is Israel's true King? The answer points beyond earthly rulers like David all the way to Jesus, rising from the very tribe of Judah.

**Twin gates bookending the start and end of Judges.** At the book's opening, Israel asks who should go up first and God answers Judah—marching against Canaanites (1:1–2). Near the book's tragic finish, Israel asks the exact same question and hears the exact same answer, Judah—only now marching into civil war to slaughter their own Benjaminite brothers (20:18). A community that compromises with sin outside eventually tears itself to shreds within.

**Judah steps forward fulfilling father Jacob's blessing.** Dying Jacob foretold that Judah's hand would rest on his enemies' neck and the royal scepter would never depart from him (Genesis 49:8–10). God naming Judah first (1:2) extends that crimson thread—from Othniel onward to King David, who finally takes Jerusalem's Zion fortress from the Jebusites (2 Samuel 5:6–7), culminating in Jesus the Lion of Judah.

**The informant at Bethel contrasts sharply with Rahab.** Scouting Bethel (1:22–26) deliberately echoes scouting Jericho in Joshua (Joshua 2:12–14). Both times Israel pledges mercy for inside help. Yet Rahab embraced the LORD and joined Israel, while Bethel's informant merely sold out his town to save his skin and went off to build another pagan Luz. Calling Bethel by its old name Luz—where Jacob once stood in awe of the house of God (Genesis 28:19)—signals how thinly Joshua's faith now runs.

**Iron chariots and forgotten promises.** Earlier, Joseph's house trembled over Canaanite iron chariots, yet Joshua promised flatly: "you will drive out the Canaanites, even though they have iron chariots and are strong" (Joshua 17:16–18). By Judges 1, those very chariots halt God's people dead in their tracks (1:19). Israel stalled not because God changed His promise, but because they forgot it.

## Notes & Nuances

**Do the accounts of Jerusalem and Caleb clash with the book of Joshua?** Verse 1:8 reports Judah capturing and burning Jerusalem, whereas 1:21 says Benjamin failed to expel Jebusites there, and Joshua 15:63 says Judah failed to expel them. Because Jerusalem sat straddling the Judah-Benjamin border, Judah likely raided and burned the lower town without cracking the steep hilltop citadel—or Jebusites swiftly reoccupied the ruins. Likewise, Caleb, Othniel, and Acsah (1:11–15) overlap Joshua 15:13–19 because the author of Judges gathers key episodes around Joshua's death topically to paint the full backdrop rather than logging strict day-by-day sequence.

**The command to drive out Canaanites remains deeply challenging.** Modern readers naturally feel weighed down reading of city assaults and bans on living among native populations. Scripture frames this inside two clear boundaries. First, over four centuries earlier, God delayed granting the land because "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Genesis 15:16): Scripture presents this as a singular historical judgment on a culture steeped in brutal evils like child sacrifice, never unprovoked aggression. Second, Mosaic law states a strictly religious—never racial—reason: "for they will turn your sons away from following Me to serve other gods" (Deuteronomy 7:1–5). Outsiders who feared God like Rahab or the Kenites (1:16) were welcomed warmly into Israel. Scholars also recognize conventional ancient war hyperbole (*utter destruction*) summarizing decisive victory even while local inhabitants survived. Even so, this stays an acute tension in the Old Testament and never serves as a charter for religious violence today.

**Mutilating Adoni-bezek's thumbs and big toes was never commanded by God.** Scripture records Judah's act coolly (1:6–7) without praising it or claiming divine mandate; Mosaic law never authorized mutilating captives. The scene simultaneously reveals poetic divine justice catching up with a cruel tyrant while hinting darkly that Israel is adopting Canaanite brutality.

**What were the *iron chariots* of 1:19?** At the dawn of the Iron Age, Canaanite war chariots used lightweight wooden frames pulled rapidly by horses, reinforced with iron rims and axles. Formidable on flat valleys, they became useless up rocky hills—explaining why Judah conquered highlands easily yet stalled on the plains.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, immediately probes chapter 1's most puzzling line (1:19): how can Scripture say the LORD was with Judah yet they could not clear enemy iron chariots? Never, Augustine insists, does this suggest God's omnipotence faltered before human iron. **Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, critiques his own ancestors candidly in describing the post-Joshua years. Once Israel stopped driving out Canaanites to collect tribute and labor instead, ease and greed softened their resolve, tempting them to prize short-term profit above God's commands. **Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, reads Adoni-bezek's confession as a vivid witness to God's righteous governance, often letting cruel rulers taste the very harm they dealt others. Turning to Acsah asking her father for upper and lower springs, Henry draws a warm invitation to prayer: just as an earthly father gladly gives extra gifts to his daughter, our heavenly Father delights to give His children both spiritual blessings and daily earthly provision when they ask boldly. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Judges 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing retributive justice on Adoni-bezek and applying Acsah's petition for springs to prayer are classic Matthew Henry insights on Judges 1; exact wording not checked -->

**Tim Keller**, New York pastor who died in 2023, reads all of chapter 1 as a portrait of incomplete obedience. From copying Adoni-bezek's mutilation to keeping Canaanites as forced laborers, Israel traded wholehearted loyalty for economic and cultural convenience.
