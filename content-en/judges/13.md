# Judges 13 — The Birth of Samson

## Story

After the bloodshed and turmoil of Jephthah's era and the quiet terms of Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon, Israel slid right back into its old ways. Again they "did evil in the sight of the LORD," so He handed them over to the Philistines for forty years—the longest stretch of oppression anywhere in the book. Strangely enough, across all forty of those years, Scripture does not record a single cry from Israel calling out to God for help (13:1).

Into that silence, the story zooms in on an ordinary couple in the village of Zorah, from the tribe of Dan. The husband's name was Manoah; his wife was "barren and childless." One day, the angel of the LORD appeared to the woman with startling news: she would conceive and give birth to a son. Attached to the promise came strict lifelong requirements for both mother and child. Starting immediately, the mother was to drink no wine or strong drink and eat nothing unclean. As for the boy, "no razor shall ever touch his head," because from the womb he was to be set apart as a Nazirite to God, and "he will begin the deliverance of Israel from the hand of the Philistines" (13:2–5).

The wife hurried home to tell her husband. She said a "man of God" had visited her, with an appearance like an angel of God, "very awesome," so majestic she had not dared ask where he came from, nor had he told her his name. She repeated the promise of a son, the strict dietary restrictions, and added that the boy would be a Nazirite to God "from the womb until the day of his death" (13:6–7).

Hearing his wife's account, Manoah prayed to the LORD, asking Him to send the messenger back once more to teach them how to raise the boy who was about to be born. God answered his prayer. Yet when the angel returned, He appeared once again to the wife while she was sitting out in the field, and Manoah was not with her. She quickly ran to fetch her husband: "Look, the man who appeared to me the other day is here!" Manoah got up and followed his wife out into the field (13:8–11).

Standing before the stranger, Manoah confirmed whether he was the one who had spoken to his wife, then asked eagerly, "When your words come to pass, what will be the boy's rule of life and mission?" The angel offered no brand-new parenting formula, simply repeating the exact instructions given earlier to his wife: she must abstain from everything produced by the grapevine, drink no wine or strong drink, eat nothing unclean, and carefully observe everything commanded her (13:11–14).

Manoah still had not realized that the visitor standing in front of him was the angel of the LORD. He tried to detain the guest so he could prepare a young goat for a meal. The angel declined, saying that even if detained He would not eat the food, though if Manoah wanted to prepare a burnt offering, he should offer it to the LORD. Manoah pressed further: "What is your name, so that we may honor you when your word comes true?" The angel gave a mysterious answer: "Why do you ask my name, since it is beyond understanding?" (13:15–18).

Manoah then took a young goat along with a grain offering and sacrificed it on a rock to the LORD. Right before the couple's eyes, the angel performed an astonishing wonder: as the flame blazed upward from the stone altar toward heaven, "the angel of the LORD ascended in the flame of the altar." Terrified, Manoah and his wife fell facedown to the ground. The angel vanished and never appeared to them again, and in that instant Manoah realized with a jolt who had been standing before them (13:19–21).

Seized with dread, Manoah cried out to his wife, "We are going to die, because we have seen God!" Yet his wife proved far more level-headed and discerning than her husband. She reasoned calmly: if the LORD had meant to kill them, why would He have accepted a burnt offering and grain offering from their hands, why would He have shown them all these wonders, and why would He have announced the good news of a son to be born? (13:22–23).

The promise came true. She gave birth to a son and named him Samson. The boy grew up under the blessing of the LORD. As Samson reached manhood, the Spirit of the LORD began to stir him at Mahaneh-dan, the region lying between Zorah and Eshtaol—setting the stage for a life at once extraordinary and deeply tragic (13:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**God's grace moves first even when His people do not bother crying out.** Earlier in Judges, whenever foreign enemies crushed Israel, the people cried out to the LORD and He raised up a deliverer (3:9; 6:6–7). By the time the Philistines dominated them for forty years, Israel had grown so numb they did not even ask for rescue (13:1). Yet God did not walk away. On His own initiative, He stepped into an obscure family to launch deliverance. Salvation never depends on humanity working up enough earnestness to seek God first.

**God delights to begin great work from complete human helplessness.** Samson was born to a barren mother in an ordinary home from a vulnerable border tribe pressed up against Philistine territory (13:2). Long before Samson flexed superhuman muscles, his very birth declared that saving strength never springs from natural human capability, but solely as a free gift from God.

**Being set apart for God begins with concrete daily habits inside the home.** Samson's calling did not wait until he lifted a weapon in battle. From the womb, his life was wrapped in the discipline of a Nazirite: no grapes or wine, nothing unclean, no razor on his head (13:4–5). For a child to be consecrated to God, parents themselves must first walk in discipline and obedience.

**Steady faith anchors itself in what God has already done, not in vague panic.** The moment Manoah realized he had seen God, his mind jumped straight to death (13:22). His wife witnessed the exact same fire and ascension, yet she reasoned from the solid evidence of grace: God accepted their sacrifice and gave them a promise, so He came to bring life, not destruction (13:23). Healthy faith never ignores God's awesome holiness, yet always views it alongside the mercy He has already shown.

## Deeper Meaning

**Barren mothers across the story of redemption.** An angel announcing a special child to an infertile woman forms one of Scripture's great recurring rhythms: Sarah bearing Isaac (Genesis 18:10–11), Rebekah bearing Jacob (Genesis 25:21), Rachel bearing Joseph (Genesis 30:22), Hannah bearing the prophet Samuel (1 Samuel 1:5; 1 Samuel 1:11), and in the New Testament Elizabeth bearing John the Baptist (Luke 1:13–15) alongside the virgin Mary bearing Jesus (Luke 1:26–31). Every time a barren womb blooms with life, Scripture reminds us that the future of God's people rests on His creative power, not on human biology or survival skill.

**A lifelong Nazirite vow—an ideal left unfulfilled in Samson.** Under the Law of Moses, a Nazirite vow was voluntary and temporary: for a set period of consecration to God, the person abstained from all grape products, left their hair uncut, and stayed away from any dead body (Numbers 6:1–8). Samson's case was extraordinary because God Himself appointed the vow from the womb to the grave (13:5; 13:7). In the entire Old Testament, only Samson and Samuel (1 Samuel 1:11) were dedicated for life like this; in the New Testament, John the Baptist likewise abstained from wine and was filled with the Spirit from the womb (Luke 1:15). Tragically, over the next chapters Samson will break every marker of that vow one by one—exposing the yawning chasm between God's high calling and this judge's weakness.

**The angel of the LORD and the name "Wonderful."** Just as when He appeared to Jacob beside the Jabbok (Genesis 32:29–30) and to Gideon beneath the oak (6:20–23), the angel here is at once distinguished from the LORD and yet speaks and receives awe as God Himself. When Manoah asked His name, He answered that His name is "wonderful" or "beyond understanding" (13:18)—the very same Hebrew root behind the title "Wonderful Counselor" that Isaiah gave to the coming Messianic King (Isaiah 9:6). New Testament readers looking back on this scene have long glimpsed here the presence of Christ Himself before His incarnation.

**A deliverer who only "begins" the rescue.** In the Hebrew of 13:5, the angel's words specifically say that Samson will *begin* to deliver Israel from the Philistines. Reading Samson's whole story confirms it: he never breaks the Philistine yoke completely; that task had to wait through Samuel's ministry until King David finally shattered Philistine power (2 Samuel 5:17–25). Only with the coming of Jesus did humanity receive a Deliverer who finishes salvation to the very end, unmarred by any sin of His own.

**The Spirit's stirring in a deeply flawed man.** The closing verse reports that "the Spirit of the LORD began to stir him" in the Danite borderlands (13:25). The Hebrew verb conveys a forceful, rhythmic impulse, like a drumbeat rousing someone to their feet. Even though Samson's later years would be scarred by baffling moral failures, the letter to the Hebrews still includes his name in the gallery of faith (Hebrews 11:32)—not to gloss over his sins, but to show that God's Spirit still accomplishes His purpose through cracked clay vessels.

## Notes & Nuances

**Manoah's wife has no name, yet she shines brightest in the chapter.** All through chapter 13 she is never named, called only Manoah's wife or "the woman." Yet the angel appears directly to her twice in a row, even after Manoah prays to meet the messenger himself (13:3; 13:9). She immediately senses the visitor's divine awe (13:6), whereas Manoah fumbles through repetitive questions, insists on cooking dinner, presses for a name, and then panics about dying (13:11–22). It is her clear-eyed logic in verse 23 that steadies her husband's heart. Scripture often elevates an unnamed figure above a named one to overturn human obsession with status.

**Why did Manoah expect to die after seeing the angel?** Israelites knew well God's word to Moses: "no one can see Me and live" (Exodus 33:20). God's absolute holiness blazes like the sun, and sinful humans cannot stand face to face with it unharmed. Jacob had marveled that he "saw God face to face, and yet my life was spared" (Genesis 32:30), and Gideon had felt the same jolt of terror before God reassured him (6:22–23). Manoah's fear was not superstition, but a genuine reaction to the majesty of the Holy One.

**Why does the wife's retelling in verse 7 differ slightly from the angel's words in verse 5?** When reporting to Manoah, she omits the razor prohibition and the mission to deliver Israel from the Philistines, while adding that the boy will be a Nazirite "until the day of his death" (13:5; 13:7). In ancient biblical storytelling, characters rarely repeat speeches word for word—both to keep the prose lively and to highlight what struck deepest in the speaker's heart: awe that her son would belong entirely to God for his whole life.

**Why does the angel omit the ban on corpses in chapter 13?** The Nazirite code in Numbers contains three main prohibitions: grape products, cutting hair, and contact with dead bodies (Numbers 6:3–7). In chapter 13, the angel highlights abstaining from wine and unclean food (for the mother) and never cutting hair (for the son), without specifically mentioning corpses. Some commentators note this fits Samson's vocation as a warrior who would inevitably handle slain enemies on the battlefield—yet when he later scoops honey out of a lion's carcass in chapter 14, his cavalier disregard for purity laws still stands out plainly.

**The meaning of Samson's name.** In Hebrew, *Samson* is linked to the word for *sun*, meaning something like "little sun" or "sunny one." Right near his hometown of Zorah sat Beth-shemesh, meaning "house of the sun"—a clue that this border zone beside Philistine territory absorbed strong influence from Canaanite sun worship. A name both dazzling and faintly tinged with pagan culture foreshadows Samson's deeply divided life: consecrated to the LORD from the womb, yet endlessly drawn toward the Philistine world.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, retold the encounter of chapter 13 with a vivid human twist. He described Manoah's wife as renowned for being the most beautiful woman in the region, and Manoah as passionately in love with her to the point of extreme jealousy. In Josephus's telling, when Manoah heard his wife rave about a handsome stranger visiting her out in the field, jealousy flared up and prompted his prayer for the visitor to return so he could see for himself; only when the guest ascended in the altar's flame did his suspicion dissolve into awe. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Josephus adding the wife's beauty and Manoah's jealousy in Book 5's account of Samson's birth, not checked for exact section numbers -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, commended Manoah's prayer in verse 8 as a fine model for parents everywhere: he did not ask for wealth or fame for his coming child, but for wisdom to know how to raise him according to God's will. Precisely when His people were most apathetic, God stepped in on His own to raise up a deliverer from a barren womb.
