# Judges 9 — The Thornbush Becomes King

## Story

Hardly had Gideon been laid to rest when the seed of ambition he had unwittingly sown in the name Abimelech sprouted into catastrophe. Abimelech went to Shechem—the hometown of his mother, Gideon's concubine—and persuaded his maternal uncles and their clan to whisper a calculated question into the ears of all the citizens: was it better for all seventy sons of Jerubbaal to rule over them, or for just one man to be king? Then he played the kinship card: "Remember that I am your own flesh and blood." The citizens of Shechem warmed to the idea because he was one of their own. They drew seventy pieces of silver from the treasury of the temple of Baal-berith and handed them to him—one silver coin for every human life. With that blood money, Abimelech hired a gang of reckless, worthless ruffians, marched to Ophrah, and executed all his brothers on a single stone—counted as seventy men. Only Jotham, Gideon's youngest son, managed to hide and escape. Once the massacre was done, the citizens of Shechem and all Beth-millo gathered beside the famous oak of the pillar at Shechem and solemnly crowned Abimelech king (9:1–6).

When word reached him, Jotham climbed to the crest of Mount Gerizim overlooking the valley of Shechem and shouted down a razor-sharp fable. Once upon a time, he cried, the trees went out to anoint a king to rule over them. They approached the olive tree, but the olive tree declined: "Should I give up my oil, by which both gods and men are honored, to go sway over the trees?" Next they invited the fig tree and then the grapevine, and both shook their heads: neither would abandon sweet fruit or cheering wine just to wave uselessly above the other trees. At last the whole forest turned to the thornbush—a low, barren shrub good for nothing but catching fire. The thornbush eagerly accepted with pompous swagger: if they truly meant to anoint it king, let them come "take refuge in my shade"; if not, fire would burst out from the thornbush and burn up even the towering cedars of Lebanon! Having finished his parable, Jotham indicted Shechem's ingratitude and bloodshed against the house of his father, who had risked his life to save them from Midian. He laid a blunt curse before them: if murdering seventy brothers and crowning a concubine's son was an act of integrity, let them enjoy one another; if not, may fire blaze from Abimelech to consume the citizens of Shechem, and fire blaze from Shechem to consume Abimelech in return. With those words, Jotham fled to Beer for safety (9:7–21).

Abimelech sat on his bloodstained throne for three years. Then God sent an evil spirit to stir up suspicion and hostility between him and the citizens of Shechem, so that the spilled blood of Jerubbaal's seventy sons would recoil on both the mastermind and his accomplices. The men of Shechem began undermining him by setting ambushes on the hilltops and robbing everyone who passed along the roads through Abimelech's territory. Into that widening rift stepped a man named Gaal son of Ebed, who moved into Shechem with his brothers and quickly won the townspeople's trust. At grape harvest time, the citizens went out to the vineyards, trod the grapes, and held a drunken festival inside the temple of their god, cursing Abimelech. Emboldened by wine, Gaal taunted the crowd: who was Abimelech that the men of Shechem, descendants of ancient Hamor, should serve him? He boasted that if only this city were under his command, he would drive Abimelech out, and sent a brazen dare telling Abimelech to muster his army and come out to fight (9:22–29).

Zebul, the governor of the city appointed by Abimelech, burned with anger when he heard Gaal's swaggering words. He secretly sent messengers to tip off Abimelech, advising him to march by night, lie in ambush in the fields, and attack at sunrise. Abimelech split his force into four companies hiding around Shechem. The next morning, Gaal stepped out to the city gate, spotted troops descending from the hilltops, and pointed them out to Zebul. Zebul coolly brushed him off: "You are seeing the shadows of the mountains as if they were men!" Only when Gaal saw two columns closing in right before his eyes—one coming down from the central ridge and another along the road by the Diviners' Oak—did Zebul drop the mask and jeer: where was that big mouth that sneered at Abimelech yesterday? Now go out and fight him! Cornered, Gaal led the citizens of Shechem out to battle, was routed back to the gate with wounded and dead strewn along the ground, and watched Abimelech pull back to Arumah while Zebul promptly expelled Gaal and his brothers from Shechem (9:30–41).

Assuming the danger had passed, the people of Shechem opened the gates the next day and went out to work in the fields. Tipped off again, Abimelech divided his men into three ambush units. He led one company straight for the city gate to cut off retreat while the other two swept into the open fields and cut everyone down. All that day he assaulted Shechem, captured the city, killed every inhabitant, demolished the buildings, and sowed the ruins with salt as a curse of permanent barrenness. Hearing of the city's fall, about a thousand men and women from the tower of Shechem fled into the fortified vault of the temple of El-berith. Abimelech immediately marched his troops up Mount Zalmon, swung an ax to chop off a tree branch, hoisted it onto his shoulder, and ordered every soldier to do the same. They piled the brushwood around the stronghold and set it ablaze. Flames roared up and burned all one thousand people inside to death—just as the curse had foretold fire bursting from the thornbush (9:42–49).

Still unsatisfied, Abimelech marched on to attack the neighboring town of Thebez. After capturing the lower town, he confronted a strong tower in the center where all the men and women of the city had locked themselves inside and climbed to the roof. Repeating his trademark tactic, Abimelech charged up to the entrance of the tower to set it on fire. Yet the instant he stepped close to the door, an unnamed woman on the roof dropped an upper millstone straight onto his head, crushing his skull. Dying yet clinging fiercely to his wounded male pride, Abimelech gasped to his young armor-bearer beside him: "Draw your sword and kill me, lest they say of me, 'A woman killed him!'" The young soldier ran him through, and seeing their commander dead, the Israelites disbanded and went home. The chapter closes with a clear verdict from heaven: God repaid in full the wickedness of Abimelech in murdering his seventy brothers and all the wickedness of the citizens of Shechem, bringing Jotham's curse to pass down to the last detail (9:50–57).

## Key Takeaways

**The least capable and most selfish people often crave power the most fiercely.** Jotham's fable exposes a timeless irony of public life (9:8–15). Fruitful trees—olive, fig, and vine—are busy doing work that blesses others and have no interest in lording it over the forest. Only the barren thornbush, unable to yield good fruit or cast real shade, jumps at the crown and promises shelter to trees vastly taller than itself. When a community hands itself over to someone who chases power at any price, what it gets is not shade, but consuming fire.

**Alliances built on bloodshed and dirty money inevitably turn on themselves.** Abimelech and Shechem joined hands not for justice or the fear of God, but for temple silver from Baal, the blood of seventy innocent men, and tribal favoritism (9:2–6). That partnership barely lasted three years (9:22). The moment shared advantage dries up, co-conspirators become bitter enemies who burn each other to ashes.

**God's silence does not mean God ignores evil.** Throughout chapter 9, the covenant name "the LORD" never appears even once; the air is thick with Baal-berith, plots, drunken boasts, and swords. Yet two pivotal verses pull back the curtain of history: God Himself sent an evil spirit to sow division (9:23), and God Himself rendered exact justice upon both Abimelech and Shechem (9:56–57). Crime may look unpunished for a season, but it never escapes the judgment of the righteous Judge.

**Blind pride dies refusing to look up.** Abimelech's final moments are at once tragic and bitingly ironic: with his skull split open by a millstone and eternity seconds away, his sole worry is saving masculine face before public gossip (9:53–54). Those who live entirely by ego and violence often die obsessed with the petty image of themselves.

**A community that chooses a false god inherits rulers shaped by that god.** The people of Shechem worshiped Baal-berith, funded the slaughter of seventy brothers with that temple's silver, and ended up burned alive inside the very shrine that could not save them (9:4; 9:46–49). We become like whatever we worship—and reap the ruin of every false refuge we trust.

## Deeper Meaning

**Shechem shifts from the site of God's covenant to a coronation bought with Baal's silver.** The scene of Shechem gathering by the oak of the pillar to crown Abimelech (9:6) stirs a piercing memory. Right there at Shechem, beneath that very oak and great stone, Joshua had once assembled the tribes to swear loyal covenant worship to the LORD alone (Joshua 24:1; Joshua 24:25–26). Now that same sacred landmark witnesses a brazen betrayal of the covenant, using Baal's treasury to buy brothers' blood and enthrone Israel's first—and worst—king.

**A curse rings out from the mountain of blessing.** Jotham chose to stand atop Mount Gerizim to denounce the citizens of Shechem (9:7). In the Law of Moses, Mount Gerizim was where six tribes were to stand and pronounce blessing when Israel walked faithfully, opposite Mount Ebal where the curses were spoken (Deuteronomy 11:29; Deuteronomy 27:12). Yet Shechem's crime was so grave that even the mountain of blessing had to become a pulpit of judgment!

**An evil spirit from God and the mystery of divine sovereignty.** Verse 9:23 states that God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the citizens of Shechem—a line that startles many readers. Later we encounter the same phenomenon with King Saul: when Saul hardened himself in rebellion, the Spirit of the LORD departed from him and an evil spirit from the LORD tormented him (1 Samuel 16:14). Scripture never makes God the author of evil; rather, He exercises absolute sovereignty even over dark forces, turning the treachery and suspicion already festering among wicked people into the instrument of their mutual judgment.

**Abimelech's prideful dying wish becomes a byword for generations.** Abimelech ordered his soldier to run him through so no one would ever say a woman killed him (9:54). Yet biblical history refused to humor his vanity. Centuries later, under King David, when General Joab coached a messenger on reporting the siege of Rabbah, he cited the textbook military lesson everyone knew by heart: "Who struck Abimelech son of Jerubbesheth? Did not a woman drop an upper millstone on him from the wall so that he died in Thebez?" (2 Samuel 11:21). Just as arrogant Sisera fell to Jael's tent peg (4:21), the bully who despised the weak collapsed beneath an ordinary kitchen stone dropped by an unnamed woman.

**The just law of sowing and reaping runs through both Testaments.** The verdict closing the chapter (9:56–57) stands as an early witness to the rule Paul states in the New Testament: "Do not be deceived: God is not mocked. Whatever a man sows, he will reap in return" (Galatians 6:7). Abimelech killed seventy brothers on one stone and died by one stone crashing onto his head; Shechem pulled silver out of Baal-berith's temple to bankroll murder and ended up incinerated inside that very temple.

## Notes & Nuances

**Abimelech is not a judge raised up by God.** Reading straight through Judges, it is easy to assume that any figure given a full chapter is one of Israel's judges. Yet the text never calls Abimelech a judge, never says the Spirit of the LORD came upon him, and mentions no foreign oppressor threatening Israel at the time. Verse 9:22 uses the verb for reigning or ruling as a prince, completely unlike the deliverance language used for the true judges before him. Chapter 9 serves as a warning portrait of self-appointed kingship, the polar opposite of God's saving work.

**Why did Abimelech sow the ruined city of Shechem with salt?** (9:45). Spreading enough actual salt across an entire town to render the soil permanently sterile would have been impractical in antiquity, when salt was precious; most scholars recognize this as a symbolic curse ritual. In the Old Testament, land covered with salt and sulfur is an image of a cursed, uninhabitable wasteland like Sodom and Gomorrah (Deuteronomy 29:23). Scattering salt over the rubble was Abimelech's way of declaring Shechem blotted off the map forever—even though the town was eventually rebuilt centuries later.

**The figure of "seventy" slain brothers and Jotham's survival.** Verse 8:30 notes that Gideon had seventy sons, apart from Abimelech by his concubine; then 9:5 reports that Abimelech killed his seventy brothers even though Jotham escaped. In ancient historical writing, "seventy" is a standard round number representing the complete household of Gideon's full-born sons rather than a modern headcount—much as sixty-nine slain brothers are still spoken of collectively as the seventy.

**The burning of a thousand civilians and the "evil spirit" make hard reading.** Chapter 9 overflows with grim violence: the slaughter of seventy brothers, field workers cut down, and a thousand men and women burned alive in a temple stronghold. Scripture neither sanitizes nor commends any of this; the narrator explicitly brands it wickedness (9:56–57). Likewise, the phrase "God sent an evil spirit" (9:23) reflects Hebrew idiom, which traces every ultimate outcome back to God's sovereign rule—including when He lifts His restraining hand and lets a spirit of mistrust and hostility erupt between wicked conspirators who were already treacherous at heart.

**The true shade of a real king.** Jotham's fable does not necessarily condemn monarchy in every form—Scripture later embraces King David and promises the Messiah as eternal King. What Jotham condemns is predatory kingship seized through bloodshed, ingratitude, and the empty swagger of a "thornbush," standing in stark contrast to God's true King, who does not make others die for Him, but lays down His own life to shelter His people.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, narrated Abimelech's brief reign as a stark moral warning against fratricide and tyrannical ambition. Recounting Jotham's outcry from Mount Gerizim and Abimelech's ignominious death beneath a millstone at Thebez, Josephus stressed that even when a despot seizes power through intrigue and violence, divine justice catches up and exacts full repayment in this very life. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Josephus's narrative emphasis and moral framing of Abimelech in Book 5, not verified word for word -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew two sharp insights from this chapter. On Jotham's fable in chapter 9 verses 8 through 15, he observed that those who are genuinely useful and fruitful in a community are usually humble and reluctant to grasp power, whereas the most worthless person craves office most fiercely and is ready to burn everything down if crossed. Coming to chapter 9 verses 53 and 54, Henry noted the poetic precision of divine retribution: Abimelech slaughtered seventy brothers on a stone and perished by a millstone, while his frantic attempt to hide being killed by a woman backfired completely because Scripture immortalized that very disgrace for all ages. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Judges chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's characteristic insights on the useful trees versus the thornbush and the stone-for-stone retribution, not checked against the exact text -->
