# Judges 15 — Fire in the Grainfields and the Donkey's Jawbone at Lehi

## Story

After storming back to his father's house in anger at the end of chapter 14, Samson cooled down some time later, during the wheat harvest, and went back to Timnah with a young goat to visit his wife. He announced outright that he wanted to go into his wife's room, only to find her father blocking the door. Her father explained that he assumed Samson thoroughly hated her, so he had given her to the companion who had attended Samson at the wedding. Hoping to smooth things over, he offered her younger sister—who was "more beautiful than she"—in her place. Samson refused the trade. Coldly, he declared, "This time I will be blameless in doing harm to the Philistines" (15:1–3).

True to his word, Samson went out and caught three hundred foxes, tied them tail to tail in pairs, and fastened a torch between every pair of tails. He set the torches ablaze and turned the panicked animals loose into the Philistines' grainfields. Fire swept through both the stacked sheaves and the standing grain, spreading even into the vineyards and olive groves—striking directly at the Philistines' livelihood. When the Philistines asked who had done this and learned it was Samson because his father-in-law in Timnah had given his wife to his companion, they marched up and burned both the woman and her father to death in fire (15:4–6).

Hearing of it, Samson did not stop; he swore an even harsher retaliation: "Since you have done this, I will not stop until I have taken vengeance on you." He attacked the Philistines and "struck them down ruthlessly with a great slaughter," then withdrew to live alone in a cave in the rock of Etam in the territory of Judah (15:7–8).

The Philistines immediately mobilized, marched up, camped in Judah, and spread out against Lehi. When the men of Judah anxiously asked why they had come, the Philistines gave a terse reply: they had come only to bind Samson and "do to him as he has done to us." Instead of rallying around their judge to drive out the foreign occupiers, three thousand men of Judah marched down to the cave at Etam to scold Samson: "Do you not realize that the Philistines rule over us? What have you done to us?" Samson answered briefly according to the bare code of retaliation: "I have done to them what they did to me." The men of Judah told him plainly that they had come to tie him up and hand him over to the enemy. Notably, Samson did not lift a hand against his own countrymen; he simply made them swear they would not kill him themselves, then stood still as they bound him tightly with two new ropes and led him up from the rock (15:9–13).

As they brought him to Lehi, the Philistines came rushing out to meet him with triumphant shouts. At that very moment, the Spirit of the LORD came powerfully upon Samson. The two new ropes binding his arms snapped "like flax burned with fire," falling to the ground. Spotting a fresh jawbone of a donkey nearby, he reached out, grabbed it, and used that piece of bone to strike down a thousand Philistine men. When the battle was over, he chanted a boastful verse: "With the jawbone of a donkey I have piled them into heaps. With the jawbone of a donkey I have slain a thousand men." Then he tossed the jawbone aside and named the place Ramath-lehi—meaning Jawbone Hill (15:14–17).

Yet immediately after that stunning victory, the giant suddenly fell into a desperate, near-fatal thirst. For the very first time in his story, Samson had to cry out to the LORD: "You have accomplished this great deliverance through Your servant. Must I now die of thirst and fall into the hands of the uncircumcised?" God answered his cry. He split open the hollow place right there in Lehi, and fresh water gushed out. Samson drank, "his strength returned, and he was revived." The spring was named En-hakkore—meaning the Spring of the Caller. The chapter closes with a summary line: in the days of the Philistines' rule over Israel, Samson judged Israel for twenty years (15:18–20).

## Key Takeaways

**The spiral of vengeance only magnifies pain without end.** From a lost wife to burning grainfields, from burning grainfields to the fiery murder of a father and daughter in Timnah, and on into wave after wave of slaughter—both the Philistines and Samson repeat the exact same rationale: whatever the other side did, we will pay back in kind (15:10–11). When people mistake retaliation for justice, the fires of hatred end up consuming the most vulnerable first.

**Long-term compromise makes people comfortable with slavery.** The most painful sentence in chapter 15 comes not from the enemy, but from three thousand men of Judah: "Do you not realize that the Philistines rule over us?" (15:11). They could muster three thousand soldiers to arrest one of their own brothers, yet lacked the slightest courage to stand up against their oppressors. Once we grow accustomed to living peacefully under sin or subjugation, the very person sent to deliver us starts looking like a troublemaker disturbing our comfort.

**The power to deliver belongs to God, not to weapons or human hands.** Samson had neither sword nor spear—only two hands just freed from ropes and a donkey's jawbone picked up off the dirt (15:14–15). Only when the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon him did the ropes snap and one man overcome a thousand. Real strength lies not in the tool, but in the One standing behind the person who acts.

**Thirst pulls the hero back to his proper place.** One moment Samson was boasting loudly, "I have slain a thousand men," without a single mention of God (15:16); the next moment, simple thirst brought him to his knees (15:18). Sometimes God lets us hit the floor of our own weakness right after our greatest triumph, so we stop congratulating ourselves and finally confess that every deliverance comes from His hand.

## Deeper Meaning

**Bound by his own people and handed over to foreigners.** The scene of three thousand men of Judah tying Samson up and turning him over to the Philistines to keep the peace (15:11–13) carries a striking echo when read alongside the New Testament. Centuries later, His own people and chief priests likewise bound Jesus—the true Deliverer—and handed Him over to the Roman governor (John 18:35). Neither deliverer fought back against his own countrymen. Yet there is a profound difference: Samson snapped his ropes to slay a thousand enemies, while Jesus willingly let ropes and iron nails hold Him to the cross in order to save the very people who rejected Him.

**The promise that one would chase a thousand amid national decline.** Before his death, Joshua had reminded Israel of God's promise: "One of you can put a thousand to flight, because the LORD your God fights for you" (Joshua 23:10). At Lehi, that promise came true in a bittersweet way: the whole tribe of Judah—once the mightiest of tribes—bowed down to foreign overlords, leaving Samson alone with a donkey's jawbone to rout a thousand enemy soldiers (15:15). God's grace and faithfulness hold firm even when His people lose all their courage.

**Water pouring out of the rock.** When Samson collapsed in exhaustion and cried out on the brink of dying from thirst, God split open the hollow rock at Lehi so water flowed out to revive him (15:18–19). That miracle reaches straight back to Israel's defining memory in the wilderness, when God told Moses to strike the rock at Horeb so water would pour forth and save the people from thirst (Exodus 17:6). Though Samson remained proud and deeply flawed, God kept the promise voiced in the Psalms: "Call upon Me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you" (Psalms 50:15).

**Fear can never purchase safety.** Back in chapter 14, Samson's wife had betrayed her husband out of sheer terror at the threat to "burn you and your father's household with fire" (14:15). Yet here in chapter 15, the Philistines turned around and burned both her and her father alive anyway (15:6). When people compromise with evil in hopes of saving their own skin, the very thing they dread most usually catches up with them in the end.

## Notes & Nuances

**The fierce violence and the tying of torches to three hundred foxes make this a hard passage to read.** The image of Samson catching three hundred foxes, tying their tails together with burning torches to destroy crops (15:4–5), followed by the Philistines burning a father and daughter alive (15:6) and waves of bloody slaughter (15:8; 15:15), is deeply unsettling to modern readers. Reading this chapter requires a foundational principle of biblical interpretation: faithful reporting is not the same as moral approval. The author of Judges is depicting a brutal era honestly—an era in which even the judge God used was driven largely by personal vendetta. On the practical detail, the Hebrew word rendered "foxes" in the Berean Standard Bible and most English versions often refers to jackals, which hunted in large packs across ancient Palestine and were far easier to trap in numbers than solitary foxes; burning wheat and olive groves at harvest time was a devastating economic strike against an occupying power.

**The "fresh" jawbone and another crack in the Nazirite vow.** Calling the jawbone "fresh" in 15:15 means it came from a recently dead donkey—not yet dry and brittle, so it was still heavy, sturdy, and sharp-edged enough to serve as a weapon. Under the Nazirite law in Numbers, however, someone consecrated to the LORD was forbidden to go near a dead body (Numbers 6:6–7). Just as when he reached into the lion's carcass in chapter 14, Samson brushed aside the outward boundaries of his vow. The narrative shows God still delivering Israel despite Samson's flaws, while the mounting consequences are quietly building toward chapter 16.

**Samson's Hebrew wordplay in verse 16.** In Hebrew, the word for "donkey" and the word for "heap" sound virtually identical. Samson's victory chant is therefore rhythmic, sharp, and swaggering—something like saying, "With a donkey's jawbone, I have made heaps upon heaps!" Tellingly, throughout those two poetic lines he mentions only the jawbone and "I," without a single word about the Spirit of the LORD—a stark contrast to his humbled prayer in verse 18 just moments later.

**Why does verse 20 already summarize Samson's twenty years as judge?** The closing formula "And Samson judged Israel for twenty years" in 15:20 matches the standard conclusion of other judges' biographies, yet Samson's story continues straight into chapter 16 and ends with the exact same formula a second time (16:31). Scholars commonly view chapters 13–15 as a first movement rounding off the season when Samson stood in his strength and triumphed over the Philistines, while chapter 16 opens the tragic final act recounting his downfall and death.
