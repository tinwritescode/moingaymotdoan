# Exodus 24 — Sealing the Covenant in Blood and the Meal on the Mountain

## Story

The Ten Commandments and the *Book of the Covenant* have been spoken. Now comes the formal signing of the covenant between God and Israel. God calls Moses—along with Aaron, Aaron's two sons Nadab and Abihu, and seventy of Israel's elders—to come up the mountain and worship from a distance; Moses alone may draw near to the LORD, while the rest of the people must stay below (24:1–2).

Before climbing the mountain, Moses relays to the entire nation all the words and ordinances God has just given. With one voice, the people answer, "All the words that the LORD has spoken, we will do" (24:3). Moses writes down every word in a book. Early the next morning, he builds an altar at the foot of the mountain and sets up twelve stone pillars representing the twelve tribes of Israel. He then sends young Israelite men to offer burnt offerings and sacrifice young bulls as peace offerings to the LORD (24:4–5).

The covenant ceremony unfolds with deep solemnity. Moses catches half of the sacrificial blood in bowls and sprinkles the other half against the altar—representing God's side of the covenant. Then he takes the *Book of the Covenant* and reads it aloud to the people. Once more, the people pledge together, "All that the LORD has spoken we will do, and we will be obedient." At that moment Moses takes the remaining blood, sprinkles it over the people, and declares, "This is the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you in accordance with all these words" (24:6–8).

With the covenant sealed in blood, Moses ascends the mountain with Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and the seventy elders. There an astonishing sight greets them: "they saw the God of Israel," and beneath His feet lay something like a pavement of sapphire, as clear as the bright sky itself. Yet God did not lay a hand on Israel's leaders to harm them; instead, "they saw Him, and they ate and drank" right in His presence (24:9–11).

When the covenant meal ends, the LORD calls Moses higher still onto the mountaintop to receive two stone tablets inscribed with the law and commandments God Himself has written. Moses sets out with his assistant Joshua, telling the elders to wait below and to bring any disputes to Aaron and Hur while he is gone. As Moses climbs, a dense cloud blankets the summit. The glory of the LORD settles on Mount Sinai, looking "like a consuming fire" to the Israelites watching from below. For six days the cloud covers the mountain; on the seventh day God calls to Moses from inside the cloud. Moses walks straight into the cloud and stays on the mountain forty days and forty nights—the very time during which God shows him the blueprint of the tabernacle starting in chapter 25 (24:12–18).

## Key Takeaways

**God's covenant is never forced; both sides enter it openly.** Israel hears the full terms of the covenant twice and twice responds aloud, *we will do it* (24:3; 24:7). Moses also puts every word in writing so no one can deny or forget what was pledged (24:4).

**Blood sprinkled on both altar and people binds the two parties in a life-and-death commitment.** In the ancient world, solemn covenants were sealed with sacrificial blood. Half the blood goes on the altar (God's side) and half on the people (Israel's side), expressing two truths at once: sin must be cleansed for people to enter fellowship with the Holy One, and this pledge is so binding that life itself stands behind it (24:6–8).

**The goal of the covenant is eating and drinking in peace in God's presence.** Only chapters earlier, the people shook in terror of dying at the sound of God's voice (20:19), yet once the covenant is sealed in blood, the seventy elders, Moses, and Aaron's family sit down to eat and drink safely before God without harm (24:11). Law and sacrifice are not meant to push humanity away; they open the table to feast with God.

**The mediator must step into the deepest place ordinary people cannot go.** The people wait at the foot of the mountain, the seventy elders climb partway up, Joshua goes a bit farther, and Moses alone walks into the blazing cloud of glory for forty days and nights (24:1–2; 24:13–18). Standing between a holy God and a fragile people is a lonely calling, yet an indispensable one.

**Six days of waiting before the seventh teach patience in God's presence.** Moses reaches the summit, yet God does not speak right away; he waits six full days inside the cloud until God calls on the seventh (24:16). Drawing close to God requires quiet stillness and waiting on His timing rather than human hurry.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus Himself repeats the words "This is the blood of the covenant" at the Last Supper.** Holding up the cup for His disciples on the night before His crucifixion, Jesus declares that this is His blood of the covenant, poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins (Matthew 26:28; Mark 14:24). The old covenant at Sinai was sealed with the blood of young bulls; the new covenant is sealed with the blood of God's own Son.

**The letter to the Hebrews points directly to the ceremony of chapter 24 to prove the superiority of the new covenant.** Its author recalls Moses reading the law, sprinkling blood over the scroll and the people, and declaring, "This is the blood of the covenant" (Hebrews 9:18–22). If animal blood under the old covenant could only cleanse outwardly and serve as a shadow, how much more does the blood of Christ, offered once for all, cleanse our consciences to serve the living God (Hebrews 9:13–14).

**The elders' meal on the mountain foreshadows the Lord's Supper and the marriage supper of the Lamb.** Seeing Israel's representatives eat and drink in peace beneath God's sapphire-bright pavement (24:10–11) echoes in the Communion table believers share whenever they remember the new covenant (1 Corinthians 11:25), and reaches its climax at the marriage supper of the Lamb, where God's redeemed people will see His face forever (Revelation 19:9; 22:4).

**Forty days and nights on Mount Sinai establish a recurring biblical rhythm.** Moses spends forty days on the mountain receiving God's word (24:18); centuries later, the prophet Elijah journeys forty days back to that very same mountain (1 Kings 19:8); and Jesus fasts forty days in the wilderness before launching His public ministry (Matthew 4:2). Across Scripture, forty days consistently marks a season of spiritual preparation before a decisive turning point in redemption.

## Notes & Nuances

**Saying they "saw the God of Israel" (24:10–11) does not contradict "no one can see Me and live" (33:20; John 1:18).** Notice the remarkable restraint of the narrator: there is not a single word describing God's face or form. The leaders only dare look down at what lies *under His feet*—a pavement bright as sapphire and clear sky. It was a veiled, gracious manifestation given to confirm the covenant, not a direct sight of God's infinite essence.

**Why did young men offer the sacrifices in verse 24:5 instead of priests?** At this stage, the priesthood of Aaron and his sons had not yet been established (God only institutes it in chapters 28–29). Before a dedicated priestly order existed, firstborn sons or selected young men acting on behalf of the families carried out the slaughter of sacrifices under Moses' supervision.

**The forty days and nights in verse 24:18 are also when the people below lose their patience.** Moses leaves Aaron and Hur in charge and steps into the cloud (24:14). While God reveals every detail of the tabernacle up on the summit (chapters 25–31), down at the base of the mountain the people grow restless at Moses' delay, and Aaron himself caves in and casts a golden calf for them to worship (32:1–4). Chapter 24 quietly sets the stage for the tragedy to come.

**The twelve pillars in verse 24:4 are not idolatrous sacred stones.** In the ancient Near East, standing stone pillars were sometimes used in pagan worship—something forbidden in verse 23:24—but here they serve strictly as memorial markers representing the twelve tribes standing as witnesses to the covenant, much like the stone Joshua later raises as a witness (Joshua 24:26–27).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, explains the scene of the elders *seeing the God of Israel* in verse 24:10 through his well-known principle of God's *accommodation*. **Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, focuses on Moses sprinkling half the blood on the altar and half on the people (24:6–8). He compares it to two parties signing a shared covenant document, yet stresses an even deeper truth: both the worship we offer God and our own sinful selves require Christ's atoning blood to be accepted by God. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: connecting the twofold sprinkling of blood directly to the blood of Christ is very characteristic of Matthew Henry on chapter 24; exact original wording not verified -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, points out that the shared meal on the mountain (24:11) reflects a widespread ancient Near Eastern custom for sealing a treaty, just like the meal Jacob and Laban shared when making peace (Genesis 31:54). Having the nation's leaders dine in God's presence confirms that the relationship between God and Israel has entered a state of peace and close fellowship. *(Exploring Exodus)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: explaining the covenant meal through ancient Near Eastern treaty customs is a familiar observation of Sarna; unsure whether in Exploring Exodus or the JPS Torah Commentary -->
