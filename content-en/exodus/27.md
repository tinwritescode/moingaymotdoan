# Exodus 27 — The Bronze Altar, the Courtyard, and the Continual Lamp

## Story

Stepping out of the enclosed tent into the open-air space surrounding it, chapter 27 brings us to the courtyard visible to every Israelite who comes to worship. The very first object greeting a worshiper just inside the courtyard gate is the altar of burnt offering. Square in shape, it is built hollow from acacia boards and overlaid with bronze to withstand constant fire, with four horns rising seamlessly from its four corners. Inside sits a bronze mesh grate to support the fire and sacrifice, paired with a complete set of bronze utensils: pots for ashes, shovels, sprinkling bowls, meat forks, and firepans. Bronze rings along two sides hold bronze-overlaid poles so the altar can be lifted and carried whenever the camp moves on (27:1–8).

Enclosing both the tabernacle and the bronze altar is a rectangular open space called the *courtyard*. Its perimeter fence consists of white curtains of finely spun linen stretched across posts set in bronze bases, fitted with silver hooks and bands, and anchored to the ground with bronze tent pegs. The entire courtyard has just one entrance, facing east. That generous gateway is screened by an embroidered curtain made of the familiar four materials—finely spun linen with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn—hanging on four posts (27:9–19).

At the end of the chapter, God gives a command involving the entire community: the Israelites must bring pure oil of pressed olives to fuel the golden lampstand standing in the Holy Place, just outside the veil in front of the Testimony. Aaron and his sons are charged with tending the lamps every day before the LORD so they burn continually "from evening until morning." This is established as a permanent statute across every generation of Israel (27:20–21).

## Key Takeaways

**The road to God begins at the altar of sacrifice.** Stepping through the single east gate of the courtyard, the very first thing an Israelite encounters is not the ark of the covenant, but the blazing bronze altar (27:1–8). No one can draw near to a holy God while brushing past the reality of sin and the need for atoning sacrifice.

**There is only one gate in, yet it stands wide open to welcome worshipers.** White linen walls enclose three sides and most of the east front, marking off sacred space from the camp outside, yet the broad entrance welcomes every Israelite coming to worship (27:9–16). Access to God is not cobbled together from dozens of self-made paths; it passes through the one door He has provided.

**The light in God's house must never be allowed to go out.** Even when desert night closes in, lamps fed by pure olive oil keep glowing inside the Holy Place from dusk till dawn (27:20–21). The people bring the oil and the priests tend the flame—unceasing worship is the shared calling of the whole community, reminding them that God keeps sleepless watch among His people.

**Courtyard furnishings are made of bronze to endure fire and desert elements.** While gold gleams inside the tent, everything out in the open courtyard—the altar, its mesh grate, ash pots, post bases, and tent pegs—is crafted from bronze (27:2–4; 27:19). Every detail blends sacred order with real practicality for nomadic life in the wilderness.

## Deeper Meaning

**The bronze altar foreshadows the cross where Christ offered Himself once for all.** At the bronze altar of chapter 27, priests had to offer sacrifices day after day without ever fully removing sin. The letter to the Hebrews sets that endless routine beside the sacrifice of Jesus: He offered for all time one single sacrifice for sins and then sat down at the right hand of God forever (Hebrews 10:10–14). An altar of wood and bronze was only the shadow; the cross is the reality that clears human sin completely.

**The courtyard's single gate echoes Jesus' own words.** In John's Gospel, Jesus declares that He is the gate—whoever enters through Him will be saved (John 10:9)—just as He is the One who *pitched His tabernacle* among us (John 1:14). Just as no one could enter the tabernacle courtyard except through the east gate and past the bronze altar, no one comes to the Father except through Jesus (John 14:6).

**The lamp burning continually before the LORD points to Christ the Light of the World and His Church in the dark.** Jesus calls Himself the light of the world (John 8:12), and in Revelation He walks among seven golden lampstands symbolizing the churches (Revelation 1:12–20). Throughout Scripture, olive oil is closely linked with the Holy Spirit: only supplied by that pure oil can the Church's witness keep burning steadily through a dark world.

**The letter to the Hebrews reminds believers of a far greater altar than the tabernacle's.** After expounding the priesthood and sacrifices across chapters 8 through 10, the writer concludes that believers have an altar from which those who cling to the old rituals have no right to eat, because Jesus suffered outside the city gate to sanctify His people by His own blood (Hebrews 13:10–12).

## Notes & Nuances

**The four horns at the altar's corners (27:2) symbolized strength and refuge.** In biblical imagery, a *horn* stands for power and deliverance. During sin offerings, the priest applied blood to the four horns of the altar (29:12), and someone in danger or fearing vengeance would sometimes rush to grasp those horns to plead for mercy (1 Kings 1:50)—though a deliberate murderer still had to be taken away and executed (21:14).

**How did a wooden altar overlaid with bronze keep from burning up?** Verse 27:8 states that the altar was built hollow from acacia boards plated with bronze. Many commentators suggest that whenever Israel set up camp, they packed the hollow interior beneath the bronze grate with earth or stones—echoing the earlier instruction about an earthen altar (20:24)—shielding the bronze-clad wooden sides from the heat while letting them empty the earth to travel lightly when breaking camp.

**"Pure oil of pressed olives" (27:20) meant the very finest grade of olive oil.** Ancient workers produced the clearest oil by gently pounding olives in a mortar and collecting the first drops that seeped out, rather than crushing pits and all under a heavy millstone. That pure oil burned cleanly with bright, steady light and minimal smoke—ideal for the enclosed room inside the tabernacle.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, highlights how the entire congregation was responsible for bringing olive oil for Aaron to keep the lamps burning (27:20–21). He draws a simple lesson for church life: the people share the duty of supplying what worship requires, while ministers bear the duty of faithfully tending the light of God's word so it always shines brightly in His house. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 27)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: drawing a practical lesson on partnership between people supplying oil and priests tending the lamps is very characteristic of Matthew Henry on the close of chapter 27; exact original wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, sees the white linen curtains encircling the courtyard (27:9–18) as a visual sermon on purity. No one may stroll casually into God's presence, he notes; that white wall separating God's court from the surrounding camp taught Israel to lay aside all worldly defilement when approaching Him. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading the courtyard barrier as a lesson on separating the sacred from the profane fits Calvin's theology closely; exact location on chapter 27 not verified -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the celebrated 19th-century London preacher, often used the placement of the bronze altar right inside the courtyard gate to underscore the centrality of the cross. Anyone hoping to press deeper into the Holy Place or intimacy with God, he said, must pass by the altar of sacrifice first; there is no shortcut around the blood of atonement. *(Sermons on the Tabernacle — Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: pointing out that a worshiper must pass the bronze altar before entering the Holy Place is a familiar theme in Spurgeon's preaching; exact sermon title not recalled -->

**Umberto Cassuto**, the Italian-born Jewish scholar, explains why chapter 27 shifts from courtyard dimensions to the command for lamp oil in its final two verses (27:20–21): it serves as a natural bridge into chapter 28's instructions for Aaron and his sons' priesthood.
