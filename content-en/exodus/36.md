# Exodus 36 — Overflowing Generosity and Raising the Tabernacle Frame

## Story

Moses summons Bezalel, Oholiab, and every gifted artisan whose heart stirs them to step into the work. Right before Moses, they receive the entire stockpile of offerings the Israelites have brought so they can begin crafting everything for the sanctuary (36:1–3).

Then something extraordinary happens. Morning after morning, the people keep streaming in with more freewill offerings. They give so generously that the artisans actually have to lay down their tools, walk over to Moses, and report that the people are bringing far more than the work requires. Moses immediately sends a proclamation echoing through the camp: let no man or woman make another single gift for the sanctuary! Only under a direct order do the people finally hold back, because the supplies already on hand are "more than enough to perform all the work" (36:3–7).

With skilled workers ready and materials overflowing, the builders get to work—following every dimension from the blueprint God showed Moses on the mountain (chapter 26). First comes the tabernacle's innermost layer: ten sweeping curtains of finely spun linen woven with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn and skillfully embroidered with cherubim. Each curtain measures twenty-eight cubits long by four cubits wide. They stitch five curtains into one great panel and the other five into a second panel, work fifty blue loops along the matching edges, and link the two panels with fifty gold clasps so the tabernacle forms a single unified whole (36:8–13).

Draped over that ornate linen ceiling is a sturdier tent made of goat hair: eleven curtains, each thirty cubits long and four cubits wide, joined into a set of five and a set of six and fastened together with fifty bronze clasps. Over the top, shielding everything from desert sun and storms, they add two protective coverings: a layer of ram skins dyed red, and an outermost layer of fine leather (36:14–19).

With the coverings ready, they construct the upright walls out of acacia wood—tough, durable timber built for desert life. Each upright frame stands ten cubits tall and a cubit and a half wide, with two bottom tenons locking snugly into two silver bases. Along the south side stand twenty frames resting on forty silver bases; along the north side, twenty more frames on forty silver bases; and across the western rear, six frames plus two corner frames reinforced with rings, resting on sixteen silver bases. To keep the entire skeleton rigid, they fit five crossbars along each wall, with the middle bar running straight through from end to end. Every frame, ring, and crossbar is overlaid with gleaming gold (36:20–34).

Finally, they craft two crucial hanging curtains. The inner veil separating off the Most Holy Place is woven of blue, purple, and scarlet yarn and finely spun linen with cherubim worked into the fabric, hung by gold hooks from four gold-overlaid acacia posts set in four silver bases. The outer entrance curtain is embroidered in those same vivid colors and hung on five posts whose tops and bands are plated in gold, while their feet stand in five bronze bases (36:35–38).

## Key Takeaways

**When hearts are truly stirred, the challenge is not scarcity—it is surplus.** In ordinary life, leaders usually have to plead and push just to raise enough funding for a shared project. Here the exact opposite unfolds: Moses has to issue an order telling people to *stop* giving. Such is the power of gratitude in people who have just tasted profound forgiveness.

**God's workers need integrity every bit as much as skill.** The craftsmen could easily have stayed quiet, kept accepting extra gold and silver, or pocketed the leftovers. Instead, they paused their work and told Moses straight out that they already had more than enough. Honesty in handling gifts given to God matters no less than artistry in shaping them.

**Even hidden, ordinary parts hold up God's house.** Most of chapter 36 reads like a technical construction log filled with cubits, bronze clasps, silver bases, and wooden crossbars. Yet it is those very silver sockets sitting in the desert sand and crossbars tucked out of sight that keep the glorious cherubim curtains from collapsing in a wilderness wind.

**The closer you step toward God, the more precious the materials become.** Moving from outside to inside, the tabernacle follows a deliberate progression: out at the entrance sit bronze bases and rugged leather shields braving the weather; inside rise gold-plated frames anchored in silver and cherubim curtains fastened with gold clasps. The architecture itself preaches a quiet sermon about deepening holiness as humanity approaches God.

## Deeper Meaning

**The cherubim-woven veil recalls the closed gate of Eden.** Embroidered across the curtain guarding the Most Holy Place are cherubim (36:35)—the very angelic guardians stationed east of Eden to block the path back to the tree of life after humanity fell (Genesis 3:24). That curtain both protected sinful people from the blazing holiness of God and reminded them that the way home remained barred.

**The curtain torn in two on the cross.** Centuries later, at the exact moment Jesus breathed his last on the cross, the temple curtain—the direct successor to the veil in 36:35—was ripped in two from top to bottom (Matthew 27:51). The letter to the Hebrews explains that Jesus' own body is the new and living way straight through that curtain, flinging open the Most Holy Place to everyone who trusts him (Hebrews 10:19–20).

**Overflowing generosity is a hallmark of grace.** The sight of Israelites bringing freewill offerings morning after morning until there is too much echoes the churches of Macedonia in the New Testament: even out of deep poverty, they overflowed with joy and pleaded for the privilege of giving beyond their ability (2 Corinthians 8:1–4). When grace truly sinks in, closed fists open wide.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why do chapters 36–39 repeat chapters 25–30 almost word for word?** Modern readers easily feel their eyes glazing over when every cubit and clasp from chapter 26 reappears here. To an ancient audience, however, that deliberate repetition carried tremendous weight: it proved that Israel—the very people who had just rebelled catastrophically with the golden calf—now obeyed down to the finest detail, neither adding to nor trimming back a single word God commanded.

**How long is a "cubit"?** The ancient unit called a *cubit* measured roughly the distance from an adult's elbow to the tip of the middle finger—about 18 inches (45 centimeters). Each upright frame standing ten cubits tall was therefore about 15 feet (4.5 meters) high, making the tabernacle grand enough to awe yet compact enough for the community to dismantle and carry through the desert.

**What was the outer "fine leather" made of?** The phrase *fine leather* (36:19)—translated in older or other versions as dugong, dolphin, or badger skins—renders an ancient Hebrew term whose exact meaning scholars still debate. Some link it to the hide of a Red Sea marine mammal such as a dugong or dolphin; others understand it as finely tanned or blue-dyed leather. Whatever exact material was used, its function is clear: it served as a tough, weatherproof outer coat protecting the sanctuary from desert sun and rain.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, marvels at the rare spectacle of Moses having to hold the people back from giving in 36:5–7. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer, praises the integrity of Bezalel and his fellow artisans for proactively asking Moses to halt the offerings. **Victor Hamilton**, an American Old Testament scholar, draws attention to the striking contrast between the tabernacle's plain, weather-beaten exterior of animal hides and its radiant interior of gold and cherubim-embroidered linen.
