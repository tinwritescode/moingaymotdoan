# Exodus 11 — Warning of the Final Plague

## Story

This chapter is brief, yet it hangs like the heavy stillness before the fiercest storm. The LORD tells Moses that He will bring just one more plague upon Pharaoh and Egypt. This time Pharaoh will not merely let Israel go—he will drive them out of the country altogether. God instructs the Israelites, men and women alike, to ask their Egyptian neighbors for articles of silver and gold. The LORD gives the Israelites favor in the eyes of the Egyptians, and by now Moses himself has become deeply respected throughout Egypt, both by Pharaoh's officials and by the ordinary people (11:1–3).

Moses then delivers his final announcement to Pharaoh before walking out of the palace. Passing on the LORD's words, he declares that about midnight, God Himself will move through the land of Egypt. Every firstborn son in the country will die—from the firstborn of Pharaoh sitting on his throne down to the firstborn of the servant girl grinding behind her hand mill, along with the firstborn of all the livestock. A cry of grief will rise across all of Egypt louder than anything ever heard before or ever to be heard again (11:4–6).

In stark contrast to that terror will be complete calm on Israel's side: "not even a dog will snarl at man or beast," so that everyone will clearly see the distinction the LORD makes between His people and the Egyptians. Looking straight at the king, Moses tells him that Pharaoh's own officials will soon come bowing before him, pleading with him to take all the people who follow him and leave. With that, hot with anger, Moses storms out of Pharaoh's presence (11:7–8).

The final two verses bring the long confrontation between Moses and Pharaoh since chapter 7 to a close. The LORD reminds Moses that Pharaoh will not listen, so that God's wonders may be multiplied in the land. Moses and Aaron performed all these wonders before Pharaoh, yet the LORD hardened the king's heart, and he still refused to let the Israelites go (11:9–10).

## Key Takeaways

**God Himself steps in now—no longer through Moses' or Aaron's staff.** In the first nine plagues, Moses and Aaron raised a staff or tossed soot into the air. For the tenth plague, God says plainly, "About midnight I will go throughout Egypt" (11:4). Every intermediary steps aside; Pharaoh now stands face to face with the very God he once dismissed with a sneer, asking who the LORD even was (5:2).

**Judgment spares neither power nor rank.** From the crown prince slated for the throne to the oldest child of the lowliest servant girl crouching behind a hand mill, every layer of Egyptian society bears the fallout (11:5). All of Pharaoh's royal authority cannot shield his own son.

**Moses' anger is righteous outrage at callous stubbornness.** After countless trips into the palace, this is the only time Scripture describes Moses leaving Pharaoh "hot with anger" (11:8). He is not furious over a personal slight; he is heartbroken and indignant at a king who would rather watch his entire nation collapse and every firstborn die than humble himself.

**The slaves do not leave empty-handed.** The command to ask the Egyptians for silver and gold (11:2) fulfills the exact promise God gave Abraham centuries earlier—that his descendants would leave the land of their enslavement with great wealth (Genesis 15:13–14). This is not theft; it is centuries of unpaid wages finally settled in full.

**Not even a dog snarls—peace that comes from God.** On a night when the entire nation is shaken by wailing, the image of not even a single dog snarling in the Israelite camp (11:7) evokes an extraordinary stillness. The God who keeps His people is able to shelter them down to the smallest detail.

## Deeper Meaning

**The sentence against Egypt's firstborn answers Pharaoh's original crime.** Back in chapter 1, Pharaoh ordered every Hebrew baby boy to be thrown into the Nile (1:22). Then in chapter 4, God called Israel "My firstborn son" and warned Pharaoh upfront that if he refused to release God's firstborn, God would strike down Pharaoh's own firstborn (4:22–23). The tenth plague is neither sudden nor arbitrary; it is justice returning upon Pharaoh the very cruelty he dealt out for years.

**God's promise to Abraham is never forgotten.** More than four hundred years separate the night God spoke to Abraham in the darkness of the covenant (Genesis 15:13–14) from this looming night in Egypt. Anyone might have assumed God had forgotten, yet every detail—right down to walking out with wealth—arrives right on schedule.

**Israel's later law for freeing servants carries the memory of this night.** Later in the Law of Moses, God instructs Israel that whenever they set a Hebrew servant free, they must never send them away empty-handed, but supply them generously from their own abundance (Deuteronomy 15:13–14). Why? Because Israel themselves had once been slaves, and the LORD brought them out abundantly supplied.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did the Israelites *ask* the Egyptians for silver and gold—were they tricking them by borrowing and running away?** Some older English translations render this word as *borrow*, leaving many readers uneasy about whether God taught His people to deceive their neighbors. In the original Hebrew, however, the verb simply means to *ask* or *request*, which is why the Berean Standard Bible translates it as "ask" (11:2). By this point the Egyptians were terrified, held Moses in high regard, and had their hearts inclined by the LORD (11:3), so they willingly handed over silver and gold as restitution or parting gifts to hasten Israel's departure.

**Why did even the servant girl's son behind the hand mill have to suffer?** This question troubles many readers. In the ancient world, a nation was viewed as a single unified body bound up with its king; when a ruler representing the whole country defied God, the entire kingdom shared the consequences. Even so, Scripture already showed that any Egyptian who feared the LORD's word could escape disaster (9:20), and in chapter 12 a large mixed multitude of non-Israelites leaves Egypt to join Israel (12:38).

**The timeline between chapters 10 and 11.** Reading quickly, it can sound as if Moses went back to see Pharaoh one more time right after saying, "I will never see your face again" (10:29). In reality, the first three verses of chapter 11 are a flashback recalling what God had already told Moses earlier, while verses 4–8 (11:4–8) continue Moses' speech during that same final confrontation at the end of chapter 10, right before he walks out of the palace in anger.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the 4th–5th century North African bishop, answered critics who objected to the Israelites taking silver and gold from the Egyptians. He explained that this was not theft, because God—the sovereign Owner of all things—commanded it as fair payment for years of exploited labor. Even more famously, Augustine turned *the gold and silver of the Egyptians* into a celebrated metaphor for truth and learning outside the Church: believers may take what is good and true from pagan scholarship and put it to God's service, just as Egyptian gold and silver were later used to build the Tabernacle. *(On Christian Doctrine, Book II, Chapter 40)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's metaphor of the gold and silver of the Egyptians for the use of secular learning in On Christian Doctrine II.40 is one of his most frequently quoted passages -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, also defended this command against charges of injustice. He pointed out that the Israelites did not plunder by force or trickery; God Himself softened the Egyptians' hearts so that they parted with their goods willingly (11:3). For Calvin, the episode teaches that God holds absolute authority over human hearts and all earthly wealth, able to compensate His children as He pleases. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin certainly explains asking for silver and gold as a just reward made possible by God inclining the Egyptians' hearts; exact wording on chapter 11 not yet verified -->
