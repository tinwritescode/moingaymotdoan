# Exodus 25 — The Ark of the Covenant, the Table, and the Lampstand

## Story

Moses has climbed Mount Sinai, stepped into the cloud, and stayed there forty days and forty nights (24:18). During those days, God does not hand down more laws about how neighbors are to treat one another. He turns to something else: He wants Israel to contribute materials to build a place for Him. Yet He tells Moses to accept an offering only from someone "whose heart compels him" (25:1–2). The materials needed include gold, silver, and bronze; blue, purple, and scarlet yarn; fine linen, goat hair, and animal skins; acacia wood, a hardy desert timber; lamp oil, spices, and precious stones (25:3–7). A crowd of former slaves happens to possess such riches because, on the night they left, the Egyptians had given them silver, gold, and fine clothing (12:35–36).

Then God states the reason behind it all: "And they are to make a sanctuary for Me, so that I may dwell among them" (25:8). That sanctuary is a large tent—called the tabernacle—designed to be set up, taken down, and carried alongside the people through the wilderness. Moses is not free to invent his own design; God will show him the pattern for every piece (25:9).

The first item God describes is a chest, later called the ark of the covenant. Made of acacia wood, it measures two and a half cubits long, and a cubit and a half wide and high. A "cubit" is roughly the distance from elbow to fingertip, about 18 inches (45 centimeters), making the ark just under four feet long and roughly waist-high to an adult. It is overlaid with pure gold inside and out. Four gold rings sit on its four corners to hold two carrying poles, and God directs that the poles stay in the rings permanently and never be removed (25:10–15). Inside the ark, Moses is to place "the Testimony"—the two stone tablets God is about to give him, engraved with His law (25:16; cf. 31:18).

On top of the ark rests a cover of pure gold called the mercy seat, the place where God extends grace. At its two ends stand two golden cherubim—winged heavenly creatures—facing each other, looking down toward the mercy seat with their wings spread upward to overshadow it (25:17–21). Then God speaks the most important promise in the whole chapter: "I will meet with you there." Above the mercy seat, between the two cherubim, God will speak with Moses and deliver His commands for the people (25:22).

The second item is a table of acacia wood, about three feet (90 centimeters) long and the same height as the ark, likewise overlaid with gold, bordered with a decorative rim, and fitted with poles for carrying. On it sit plates, dishes, pitchers, and bowls of pure gold. And on that table sits bread—the Bread of the Presence—kept "before Me at all times" (25:23–30). Leviticus tells us there were twelve loaves, one for each tribe, replaced with fresh bread every Sabbath (Leviticus 24:5–8).

The third item is the lampstand—no ordinary lamp. Hammered out of a single solid piece of pure gold, it has a central shaft with six branches curving out from either side. Along every branch are cups shaped like almond blossoms, complete with buds and petals, so the whole lampstand resembles a blooming almond tree. Atop its seven tips rest seven lamps. The lampstand and all its accessories require a full talent of pure gold—roughly seventy-five pounds (25:31–39).

At the close of the chapter, God reminds Moses once more: "See to it that you make everything according to the pattern shown you on the mountain" (25:40).

## Key Takeaways

**God wants to dwell right among His people.** This is the key verse of chapter 25 and of all the chapters that follow (25:8). The gods of other nations were pictured perched on distant mountaintops or locked inside massive stone temples, far from ordinary folks. Israel's God asks for a tent pitched right among His people's tents, walking the same desert road with them.

**God wants gifts that come from a willing heart.** God had every right to levy a tax. Instead, He asks only for offerings from those whose hearts move them to give (25:2). God's dwelling place is built on gratitude, never coercion.

**The meeting place with God is a place of mercy.** Inside the ark lie the tablets of the law—the very standard that exposes where humanity falls short. Yet God does not arrange to meet Moses on the bare stone tablets; He meets him on the lid resting above them, the mercy seat (25:21–22). The law remains right there, but God's mercy covers the space between the law and His people.

**God comes close, yet remains holy.** Everything in this chapter is precious: pure gold, durable timber, master craftsmanship. Two cherubim stand guard on either side. God truly draws near to His people, yet He never becomes casual or ordinary. No one may redesign His house at will; everything must match the pattern He reveals (25:9; 25:40).

**God is always ready to travel with His people.** The carrying poles must remain in the rings of the ark (25:15). God's ark is never bolted down to one fixed spot; wherever His people move, He moves with them.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus "pitched His tent" among us.** The apostle John writes, "The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14). The Greek verb translated "made His dwelling" literally means *pitched His tent*. John adds, "We have seen His glory." What God began with a desert tent, He brought to fulfillment when He became a human being and lived among us.

**The pattern on the mountain.** The letter to the Hebrews quotes the final verse of this chapter word for word: "See to it that you make everything according to the pattern shown you on the mountain" (Hebrews 8:5). Its author understands that the earthly tabernacle was a copy and shadow of a heavenly reality. A shadow is real and instructive, yet it always points to something far greater than itself.

**The mercy seat and the cross.** The ancient Greek Old Testament translates the mercy seat with a word that Hebrews also uses when describing the cherubim overshadowing "the mercy seat" (Hebrews 9:5). The apostle Paul uses that very same Greek word for Jesus: God presented Him as "the atoning sacrifice" (Romans 3:25). Paul's point is breathtaking: the place where sinners meet God's forgiveness is no longer a golden lid inside a tent, but a Person.

**Cherubim who once barred the way now stand in attendance.** Scripture first mentions cherubim at the gate of Eden, wielding a flaming sword to guard the road to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24). Here cherubim appear again, yet now they stand flanking the very spot where God meets with humanity. The doorway shut in Eden cracks open once more. Later, Israel would praise God as the One "enthroned above the cherubim" (Psalms 99:1).

**Bread and light.** Bread always lies before God, and lamps always burn inside the tent. Jesus declares, "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35). And in the final book of Scripture, seven golden lampstands symbolize the churches—communities placed to shine God's light in the world (Revelation 1:20).

**A tree in bloom.** The lampstand looks like an almond tree bursting into flower. Many commentators hear an echo of the tree of life in the garden of Eden (Genesis 2:9). The almond tree returns later too: when God confirms His choice of Aaron as priest, Aaron's dry staff buds, blossoms, and bears ripe almonds right inside this very tent (Numbers 17:8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Measurements are only approximate.** A "cubit" is not a modern standardized unit like a foot or a meter, but an ancient measure based on the distance from elbow to fingertip, roughly 18 inches (45 centimeters). All modern figures given in this chapter are close estimates.

**No one is certain what the outer animal skins were.** The Hebrew word in verse 25:5 (rendered "fine leather" in the BSB) is very rare. Some translations take it to mean the hide of a sea mammal such as a dugong or sea cow, others understand it as finely tanned leather, and still others as goatskin. There is no need to insist on a single rendering.

**Do not hunt for hidden symbolism in every single ring.** Scripture describes each furnishing in detail yet rarely assigns secret meanings to every clasp or measurement. Even the letter to the Hebrews, after naming the ark and cherubim, remarks, "we cannot discuss these things in detail now" (Hebrews 9:5). Grasping the big truth—that God longs to dwell among His people—matters far more than guessing meanings for every number.

**The ark of the covenant was never a lucky charm.** Years later, Israel hauled the ark onto a battlefield, assuming that having the box guaranteed victory. They suffered a crushing defeat, and the enemy captured the ark (1 Samuel 4:3–11). God promised to dwell among His people, not to be locked inside a chest for anyone to manipulate at will.

**The name "mercy seat."** The Hebrew term behind it is closely linked to the words for "covering" and "atonement." The BSB and many traditional English Bibles call it the "mercy seat," while other translations render it the "atonement cover"—both capturing the same heart of the image, the lid from which God grants grace and forgiveness.

## What Commentators Say

**Gregory of Nyssa**, a 4th-century bishop in Asia Minor, wrote *The Life of Moses* as a spiritual map for the soul. In his reading, the heavenly tabernacle shown to Moses on the mountain—the tent not made by human hands—is Christ Himself, whom Paul calls the power of God and the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24). **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, turns to verse 25:40 to insist that God does not leave human beings free to invent their own ways of worshiping Him. Even Moses, the greatest leader in Israel, could neither add to nor subtract from the pattern God revealed. True worship, Calvin argues, must follow God's word rather than human taste or cleverness. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin frequently uses Exodus 25:40 and Hebrews 8:5 to make this point; unsure whether he states it in his commentary on chapter 25 itself or elsewhere -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, pauses over verse 25:2, where God accepts offerings only from willing hearts. God needs no one's wealth, Henry remarks, and gifts dragged out by compulsion bring Him no delight; what God looks for is a willing heart coming with the gift. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: this point on willing offerings almost certainly appears at the opening of chapter 25; exact phrasing not verified -->

**Umberto Cassuto**, the Italian-born Jewish scholar who later lived in Israel, views the tabernacle as a continuation of Israel's encounter at Mount Sinai. At the mountain, the people witnessed God's glory resting on the summit once; the tabernacle ensured that same glory would stay in their midst and journey with them once they left Sinai behind. *(A Commentary on the Book of Exodus)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: linking the tabernacle directly to Mount Sinai is a key argument of Cassuto, though also found in Ramban; Cassuto's exact presentation not verified -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, illuminates the two cherubim by comparing Israel's neighbors, where royal thrones were often flanked by winged creatures. The mercy seat and cherubim thus resemble a royal throne, with the ark itself as the footstool.
