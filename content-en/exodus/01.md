# Exodus 1 — A People Growing Under Oppression

## Story

Genesis closes with a coffin in Egypt. Before he died, Joseph told his brothers that God would surely come to their aid and bring them back to the promised land, and he made them swear to carry his bones with them when that day came (Genesis 50:24–26). Exodus picks up right there. It recites the names of the eleven sons of Jacob who went down to Egypt with their father, seventy people in all, since Joseph was already there (1:1–5; cf. Genesis 46:27).

Years pass. Joseph dies, his brothers die, and that entire generation passes away. Yet their descendants are fruitful and multiply rapidly, growing exceedingly numerous and powerful until the land is filled with them (1:6–7). The seventy who arrived long ago have become a nation.

Then a new king rises to power in Egypt, one "who did not know Joseph" (1:8). He does not remember—or chooses not to remember—that a Hebrew once saved the entire country from famine. All he sees is a huge foreign population, and fear takes hold of him. He tells his people, "let us deal shrewdly with them," lest war break out and they join Egypt's enemies, fight against them, and leave the country (1:9–10).

His first shrewd scheme is forced labor. The Egyptians appoint taskmasters to crush the Israelites with heavy burdens, making them build Pithom and Rameses as store cities for Pharaoh (1:11). Yet astonishingly, the more they are oppressed, the more they multiply and spread. The Egyptians come to dread them and drive them even harder—working them ruthlessly with mortar and brick and every kind of labor in the fields. Scripture says the Egyptians "made their lives bitter" (1:12–14).

The second scheme is far darker. The king summons two Hebrew midwives named Shiphrah and Puah and gives them orders: when you help the Hebrew women give birth, if the child is a son, kill him; if it is a daughter, let her live (1:15–16).

But the midwives "feared God." They disobey the king's command and let the baby boys live (1:17). Summoned before Pharaoh to explain themselves, they tell him that Hebrew women are not like Egyptian women—they are vigorous and give birth before a midwife even arrives (1:18–19).

God is good to the midwives, and the people keep multiplying and growing stronger. Because the midwives feared God, He gives them families of their own (1:20–21).

So the king turns to his third scheme, no longer hiding behind closed doors. Pharaoh commands all his people: every son born to the Hebrews you must "throw into the Nile," while every daughter may live (1:22). The chapter ends right there, on the banks of the Nile, where newborn boys are to be cast into the water.

## Key Takeaways

**God's promise keeps moving forward even when God seems silent.** Throughout the first half of this chapter, God's name never appears. There are no divine speeches, no miracles. Yet Israel keeps multiplying, exactly as God promised Abraham. God's silence never means God's absence.

**Fear gives birth to oppression.** Pharaoh does not hate the Hebrews because they have done anything wrong. He fears them because they are numerous. That fear turns welcome guests into slaves, and then turns slaves into targets for slaughter. History has repeated this exact path many times since: noticing a different group growing larger, giving in to fear, and moving to crush them.

**The harder they are pressed down, the more they rise.** Pharaoh tries scheme after scheme, each crueler than the last, and every single one fails. The plans of the most powerful ruler of the ancient world cannot defeat the promise of God.

**Ordinary, powerless people can stand up against evil.** The two midwives hold no political power. They are simply two women delivering babies. Yet when the king's decree collides with the fear of God, they choose God. Scripture records their names—while the mighty king goes unnamed.

## Deeper Meaning

**The blessing of creation is still at work.** Verse 1:7 echoes the very words of God's blessing on humanity at the dawn of the world: be fruitful, multiply, and fill the earth (Genesis 1:28). God repeated that blessing to Noah and to Jacob (Genesis 9:1; 35:11). Now, in a foreign land, that blessing is unfolding before everyone's eyes. Anyone who opposes the flourishing of this people is fighting against God's own purpose in creating the world.

**What God foretold long before.** Back in Abraham's day, God told him that his descendants would be foreigners in a land not their own, enslaved and mistreated for four hundred years—yet He also promised to judge that nation and bring Abraham's offspring out with great possessions (Genesis 15:13–14). Chapter 1 shows the first half of that word coming true. The reader knows the second half is on its way.

**Another king slaughtering baby boys.** Centuries later, when Jesus is born, King Herod likewise orders all the baby boys in Bethlehem to be killed (Matthew 2:16). And like Moses, Jesus escapes and is later called "Out of Egypt" (Matthew 2:15; cf. Hosea 11:1). Matthew tells the story as if to say: the great deliverance of old is beginning all over again.

**Obeying God rather than men.** When forbidden to preach about Jesus, the apostles reply that they must obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29). The two midwives lived that conviction more than a thousand years earlier. When Stephen later recounts Israel's history, he too recalls the king who "knew nothing of Joseph" and forced their ancestors to abandon their newborns (Acts 7:17–19).

**The river of death.** Pharaoh chooses the Nile as the instrument to kill Hebrew infants. Yet in the very next chapter, that same river saves a baby boy (2:3–6). And in the end, Pharaoh's own army drowns in the sea (14:28; 15:4). Trying to kill by water and perishing in water—Exodus tells the story this way to display the poetic justice of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did the midwives lie?** Their answer to Pharaoh was probably not the whole truth, and readers have wrestled with this for centuries. Some argue that lying is always wrong: God blessed the midwives for their compassion and reverence for Him, not for their deception. Others argue that deceiving someone bent on murdering innocent children in order to save lives is right, much like Rahab hiding the spies later on. Scripture itself offers no commentary on their excuse; it states plainly what God honored: they feared God (1:21).

**Were the midwives Hebrew or Egyptian?** The Hebrew phrase in 1:15 can be read either as "the Hebrew midwives" or "the midwives to the Hebrews." The names Shiphrah and Puah sound Semitic, so most readers understand them to be Hebrew women. Still, some interpreters since ancient times have suggested they were God-fearing Egyptians. Since two women could hardly deliver babies for an entire growing nation by themselves, they were likely the heads of a larger guild of midwives.

**Who was this Pharaoh?** The Bible never gives his name. "Pharaoh" is a royal title for the king of Egypt, not a personal name. Scholars also disagree on the date of the Exodus. <!-- low: two common dating views, cited from memory -->Some calculate from the 480-year figure in Kings (1 Kings 6:1) and place it around the fifteenth century BC; many others point to the name of the city Rameses and place it in the thirteenth century.<!-- /low --> Either way, you do not need the king's personal name to understand the story.

**Why does this chapter use the name "Hebrews"?** The Egyptians call Jacob's descendants "Hebrews," while the narrator typically calls them "Israelites." Both refer to the same people; "Hebrew" was commonly the term outsiders used for them.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, held that lying is wrong in every circumstance. Why, then, did God bless the midwives? He answers that God rewarded their mercy and kindness, not their falsehood, pardoning their weakness because of their benevolence.
