# Exodus 10 — Locusts and Darkness

## Story

The LORD sends Moses back to Pharaoh, but first He gives a new reason behind this whole succession of signs: He has hardened the hearts of Pharaoh and his officials not only so Egypt will know who He is, but so Moses and Israel will have a story to tell their children and grandchildren for generations to come, that they too may know He is the LORD (10:1–2).

Moses and Aaron go in and put a piercing question straight to the king: "How long will you refuse to humble yourself before Me?" If you still refuse to let My people go, tomorrow locusts will blanket the face of the land, devour everything left standing after the hail, strip every tree, and fill your palace and every home in Egypt—a swarm unlike anything your fathers or grandfathers ever saw. With that, Moses turns around and walks straight out without waiting for an answer (10:3–6).

By now Pharaoh's own officials have had enough. They speak up bluntly: how long will this man be a snare to us? Let the people go worship the LORD their God—do you not yet realize that Egypt is in ruins? Pharaoh has Moses and Aaron brought back and asks who exactly will be going. Moses is unmistakable: young and old, sons and daughters, flocks and herds—everyone and everything must go, for we are holding a feast to the LORD. Pharaoh bristles with sarcasm, accusing them of plotting escape: only the men may go, while women and children stay behind as hostages. Then Moses and Aaron are driven from Pharaoh's presence (10:7–11).

At the LORD's command, Moses stretches his staff over Egypt. All that day and night, God sends an east wind sweeping across the country, and by morning it carries in locusts beyond counting—more than ever existed before or will ever exist again. They blacken the face of the land and eat every plant and every piece of fruit spared by the hail; not a single green thing remains on any tree or plant in all of Egypt (10:12–15).

Panicked, Pharaoh hurriedly summons the two brothers. This time he confesses sinning against both the LORD and against Moses and Aaron, begging forgiveness just once more and pleading with them to pray away this deadly plague. Moses leaves and appeals to the LORD. God shifts the wind into a powerful west wind that sweeps the locusts off and drives them into the Red Sea—not one locust remains. Yet the LORD hardens Pharaoh's heart again, and he refuses to let the Israelites go (10:16–20).

The ninth plague falls without warning. Moses lifts his hand toward heaven, and thick darkness blankets all of Egypt for three straight days—a "palpable darkness," darkness so heavy you could feel it. For three days no one can see anyone else, and no one dares get up from where they sit. Yet all the Israelites have light in their dwellings (10:21–23).

Pharaoh calls Moses in for one final bargain: go worship the LORD, take your little ones with you—only leave your flocks and herds behind. Moses refuses to budge an inch: our livestock must come with us, "not a hoof will be left behind," because we need them for offerings to the LORD and won't know exactly what to sacrifice until we arrive. Once again the LORD hardens Pharaoh's heart, and the king refuses. Enraged, Pharaoh orders Moses out with a death threat: never let me see your face again, because the day you see my face, you will die. Moses answers with chilling calm: "As you say, I will never see your face again" (10:24–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Remembering what God has done is an inheritance passed down to the next generation.** At the opening of chapter 10, God states plainly that these signs are meant to be retold to children and grandchildren (10:1–2). Faith endures by remembering God's real faithfulness in history, not by chasing fleeting feelings.

**Stubborn pride will watch an entire country burn just to save face.** Even Pharaoh's own advisors cry out that Egypt is already in ruins (10:7). Yet Pharaoh clings to control through petty bargains—first keeping the women and children, then keeping the livestock. Pride blinds a person to their own collapse.

**Not a single hoof can stay behind in slavery.** Moses's famous line—"not a hoof will be left behind" (10:26)—captures the wholeness of God's deliverance. Had Israel left families or flocks in Egypt, they would soon have been pulled back into bondage. When God claims a people for Himself, He claims all of life, family and possessions alike.

**Darkness strikes straight at Egypt's supreme god.** In Egyptian religion, the sun god Ra reigned as lord of sky and light, and Pharaoh was revered as the son of the sun god. Three days of impenetrable blackness expose both king and sun god as completely powerless, while light shines only where the people of the true God live (10:22–23).

**Pharaoh's threat unknowingly seals his own farewell.** He threatens Moses that seeing his face again will mean death (10:28), yet the reality is reversed: death is marching toward Pharaoh's own palace, and Moses will never need to come begging before him again.

## Deeper Meaning

**Locusts and darkness become the language of the Day of the LORD.** The prophet Joel pictures a devastating locust invasion stripping the land bare to summon people to rend their hearts before the Day of the LORD (Joel 1:4). In Revelation, locusts swarming out of the smoke of the abyss (Revelation 9:3) and darkness plunging the beast's kingdom into gloom (Revelation 16:10) both echo straight back to chapter 10.

**Darkness over the land foreshadows the three hours of darkness at the cross.** In Egypt, three days of darkness descend right before the death of the firstborn in the final plague (10:22). Centuries later, as the only Son of God hangs on the cross, darkness again covers the land for three hours (Matthew 27:45). The Light of the world willingly stepped into the darkness of judgment to bring all who trust Him out of the domain of darkness.

**Light in Israel's homes pictures God's own presence.** Surrounded by a country swallowed in blackness, the light in Goshen (10:23) reminds us that true light comes not from the sun or earthly kingdoms, but from God Himself. When Jesus comes, He declares Himself the light of the world, promising that whoever follows Him will never walk in darkness but will have the light of life (John 8:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**The east wind and west wind match Egypt's geography closely.** An east wind blowing off the Sinai and Arabian deserts naturally sweeps locust swarms into the Nile valley (10:13), while a strong west wind drives them back out over the Red Sea (10:19). As in other plagues, God can harness natural forces familiar to the region—yet the exact timing, unmatched scale, and instant obedience to Moses's word mark the hand of God.

**How can darkness be *"palpable"*—something you can feel?** The phrase in 10:21 captures a suffocating, oppressive blackness, and has reminded many scholars of Egypt's notorious *khamsin* wind—a scorching desert sandstorm that fills the air with dust so thick it blots out the sun for days. Whether or not a sandstorm was involved, the fact that Israel still had light in their homes leaves no doubt this was a direct miracle of God.

**Moses says in 10:29 he will never see Pharaoh's face again—so what about chapter 11 and 12:31?** In chapter 11 (11:4–8), Moses is still finishing his final words during this very same audience at the close of chapter 10, right before storming out of the palace hot with anger (11:8). And on the night of the tenth plague, when Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron (12:31), many scholars understand that the king sent messengers in the middle of the night rather than meeting them face-to-face again.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, viewed Pharaoh's four compromises across chapters 8 through 10 as a classic picture of how the world and the enemy try to hold back anyone turning to God: if you can't be stopped outright, you're urged to worship inside Egypt (8:25), or step out but not go far (8:28), or go alone without your family (10:11), or leave your possessions behind (10:24). **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, drew attention to God's command that Moses recount these signs to children and grandchildren (10:2). God's mercies and judgments are never meant solely for the one generation that witnesses them; parents carry a sacred duty to pass the story of faith down to their children, or even the greatest wonders will fade into oblivion. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin stresses the parental duty to pass on the story to children from 10:2; exact wording on chapter 10 not verified -->

**Terence Fretheim**, American Old Testament professor, reads the ninth plague of darkness as creation being pulled backward toward the primordial chaos before day one of Genesis, when darkness covered the face of the deep. By defying the Creator, Pharaoh harms not only Israel but drags his own land into decreation, while the light of the first day remains shining only where God's people dwell. *(Exodus, Interpretation)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading the plague of darkness as a reversal of Genesis aligns closely with Fretheim's overarching thesis; unsure of his exact wording on chapter 10 -->

**Nahum Sarna**, American Jewish biblical scholar, underscored the religious shock of three days of darkness for Egypt. To Egyptians, the sun was the supreme deity guaranteeing cosmic order and life, and Pharaoh stood as that god's living embodiment on earth. When the sun goes dark for three straight days and Pharaoh stands helpless, the theological foundation of Egypt's monarchy receives a devastating blow just before the final plague falls. *(Exploring Exodus, 1986)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: connecting the plague of darkness to the sun god Ra and Pharaoh's divine kingship is a familiar point in Sarna; exact wording not verified -->
