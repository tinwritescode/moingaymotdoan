# Exodus 3 — The Burning Bush

## Story

Moses is now shepherding the flock of his father-in-law Jethro, the priest of Midian. One day he leads the sheep to the far side of the wilderness and arrives at Horeb, the mountain of God (3:1).

There the angel of the LORD appears to him in a blazing fire from inside a bush. Moses watches in amazement: the bush is engulfed in flames, yet it does not burn up. "I must go over and see this marvelous sight," he thinks. "Why is the bush not burning up?" (3:2–3).

Seeing him turn aside to look, God calls out from inside the bush: "Moses, Moses!" "Here I am," Moses answers. God tells him not to come any closer and to take off his sandals, because the place where he is standing is holy ground. "I am the God of your father," He says, "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." Moses hides his face, afraid to look at God (3:4–6).

Then the LORD speaks: I have indeed seen the affliction of My people in Egypt, I have heard them crying out because of their oppressors, and I know their suffering. I have come down to rescue them from the hand of the Egyptians and bring them up into a good and spacious land, "a land flowing with milk and honey"—the home of the Canaanite nations (3:7–9).

Then comes the twist Moses never saw coming: "Therefore, go! I am sending you to Pharaoh to bring My people the Israelites out of Egypt" (3:10).

Moses recoils: "Who am I, that I should go to Pharaoh and bring the Israelites out of Egypt?" Forty years earlier he had tried to play the liberator on his own and failed miserably; now he has no confidence left in himself. God does not answer by telling Moses who Moses is, but by telling him who God is: "I will surely be with you." And this will be the sign that I have sent you: once you have brought the people out of Egypt, all of you will worship God on this very mountain (3:11–12).

Moses asks another question: if I go to the Israelites and say the God of your fathers has sent me, and they ask what His name is, what should I tell them? God answers, "I AM WHO I AM." Tell the Israelites: "I AM has sent me to you" (3:13–14).

God adds: say to them that the LORD—the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—has sent me to you. That is My name forever, how I am to be remembered from generation to generation. Go gather the elders of Israel and tell them that the LORD has attended to them, has seen what is being done to them in Egypt, and has promised to bring them up out of their affliction into a land flowing with milk and honey (3:15–17).

The elders will listen to you, God says. Then go with them to the king of Egypt and ask permission to take a three-day journey into the wilderness to sacrifice to the LORD your God. Yet I know the king of Egypt will not let you go unless a mighty hand compels him. So I will stretch out My hand and strike Egypt with all the wonders I will perform among them, and after that he will release you (3:18–20).

God adds one more promise: I will grant this people favor in the eyes of the Egyptians so that when you leave, you will not go empty-handed. Every woman will ask her Egyptian neighbors for silver, gold jewelry, and clothing to put on your sons and daughters, and in this way you will plunder the Egyptians (3:21–22). The former slaves will walk out as people finally paid their due.

## Key Takeaways

**God meets people in the middle of ordinary daily work.** Moses is not performing a grand religious ritual; he is simply tending his father-in-law's sheep on the far edge of the desert (3:1). Right in the middle of that quiet routine, the fire of God suddenly blazes out of an ordinary thornbush (3:2).

**Holiness calls for reverence, yet does not push people away.** God tells Moses to remove his sandals because the ground beneath him is holy (3:5). He is no tame deity to approach casually—yet He takes the initiative to call Moses by name and introduce Himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (3:4; 3:6).

**God sees, hears, knows, and comes down to rescue.** Facing the agony of an enslaved people, God does not stay aloof in the distance. He declares that He has seen their affliction, heard their cries, knows their sufferings, and has come down to deliver them from the hand of the Egyptians (3:7–8).

**God answers the question *who am I?* not with Moses's abilities, but with His own presence.** When Moses feels utterly inadequate and asks who he is to face Pharaoh (3:11), God does not flatter his talents or remind him of his palace education. He offers one decisive promise: "I will surely be with you" (3:12).

**The name *I AM WHO I AM* reveals God's freedom and faithfulness.** When Moses asks for His name, God declares, "I AM WHO I AM" (3:14), linking it directly to His covenant name, *the LORD* (Hebrew *Yahweh*) (3:15). He depends on no one, cannot be defined or controlled by any earthly power, and remains faithfully present to keep His promises across every generation.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus claims the very *I AM* name of the burning bush.** Throughout the Gospel of John, Jesus repeatedly uses the self-declaration *I am*—most strikingly when He tells the Jewish leaders that before Abraham was born, "I am" (John 8:58). His listeners understand immediately that He is claiming equality with the God who spoke from the burning bush, and they pick up stones to kill Him (John 8:59).

**The God of the living.** When debating the Sadducees about the resurrection, Jesus quotes verse 3:6—God calling Himself the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob centuries after their deaths—to declare that God is not the God of the dead, but of the living (Mark 12:26–27).

**A fire that does not consume the bush.** Across church history, the bush burning brightly without turning to ash (3:2) has been seen as a picture of God's people passing through trials without being destroyed because God dwells in their midst, and also as an image of God's blazing holiness dwelling among fragile human beings without consuming them (Deuteronomy 4:24; 2 Corinthians 4:7–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 3:2 say *the angel of the LORD*, while 3:4 says *God* called out from the bush?** This is a familiar pattern in the Old Testament (Genesis 16:7–13; 22:11–16). In scenes like this, *the angel of the LORD* is not an ordinary created messenger, but the LORD Himself appearing in a visible form that human eyes can bear.

**Why does the sign God promises Moses in 3:12 happen *after* the mission is done?** Normally we want a reassuring sign up front before taking a single step. Yet God tells Moses the proof that He sent him will come afterward, when Moses leads the freed nation to worship God right back on Mount Horeb (3:12)—a promise fulfilled in chapter 19. Faith means stepping out before seeing the complete outcome.

**Was asking the Egyptians for silver and gold (3:21–22) dishonest?** In Hebrew, the verb in 3:22 means *ask* (as the BSB renders it) rather than *borrow*—a nuance sometimes misunderstood from older English translations. After the plagues, the Egyptians will gladly hand over gold and silver just to see Israel leave quickly (12:35–36). Moreover, it functioned as overdue wages for generations of unpaid labor, anticipating the later law commanding Israelites never to release a servant empty-handed (Deuteronomy 15:13–14).

**Augustine of Hippo**, fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, reflected often on the name "I AM WHO I AM" in 3:14. Every creature in the universe changes—what once was, what will be, what passes away—whereas God alone truly *is*, unchanging and the source of all being. Yet in the very next verse (3:15), He calls Himself the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob: the eternal One stoops down in mercy to bind Himself to changeable, mortal people. *(Expositions on the Book of Psalms)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: contrasting the unchanging name in 3:14 with the covenant name in 3:15 is a very familiar theme in Augustine; unsure which exact psalm exposition -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, read Moses's hesitation in 3:11 as genuine humility forged by forty years in Midian, a sharp contrast to his brash self-confidence as a young man. Calvin emphasizes that God's reply—"I will surely be with you" (3:12)—is more than enough to crush every fear, because the power of a calling rests in the One who sends, not in the one who is sent. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, Exodus chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin comments on Moses's transformation and God's promise of presence in 3:11–12 in his Pentateuch commentary; exact wording not verified -->
