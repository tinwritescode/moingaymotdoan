# Exodus 38 — The Bronze Altar, the Courtyard, and the Inventory

## Story

The last chapter described the golden furnishings tucked inside the sanctuary. Now the scene steps out into the open courtyard, where every piece shifts to bronze. Largest of all is the altar of burnt offering: five cubits square and three cubits high. With a cubit equal to roughly half a meter (about 18 inches), the altar measures a little over seven feet (more than two meters) on each side and stands nearly chest-high to an adult. Built hollow from acacia planks and overlaid with bronze, it has horns rising from its four corners. Alongside it, the artisans fashion bronze pots, shovels, sprinkling bowls, meat forks, firepans, a bronze mesh grate, plus rings and carrying poles (38:1–7).

Next comes the bronze washing basin and its stand. Scripture includes a striking detail: the bronze for the basin came from "the mirrors of the women who served at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting" (38:8). Ancient mirrors were not glass, but smooth plates of burnished bronze. Here the priests will wash their hands and feet every time they enter to minister (30:18–21).

Surrounding the tabernacle is the courtyard, enclosed by finely spun linen curtains hanging from upright posts: one hundred cubits along the south side and one hundred along the north, and fifty cubits across the west (38:9–12)—roughly 150 feet (45 meters) long by 75 feet (23 meters) wide. The eastern front also spans fifty cubits, split into two fifteen-cubit wings framing a wide central gateway (38:13–15). The posts stand in bronze bases, fitted with silver hooks and bands and capped with silver plating (38:16–17).

The entrance does not stay bare; across the twenty-cubit opening hangs a curtain embroidered in blue, purple, and scarlet yarn. Both the linen fence and the embroidered gate stand five cubits tall—over seven feet (more than two meters)—high enough that anyone standing outside cannot peer in. Every tent peg anchoring the tabernacle and its courtyard is cast in bronze (38:18–20).

At this point, Scripture pauses to open the ledger. The inventory is overseen by Ithamar, son of Aaron the priest. The record again credits the two chief craftsmen: Bezalel from the tribe of Judah and his partner Oholiab from the tribe of Dan (38:21–23).

The gold offered comes to twenty-nine talents and seven hundred thirty shekels—roughly a full ton (38:24). The silver is not a freewill gift at all, but census ransom money: every man twenty years old and upward contributed half a shekel (30:12–16). With 603,550 men counted, the silver totals exactly one hundred talents and 1,775 shekels—more than three tons. The arithmetic balances down to the last shekel. The hundred talents are cast into one hundred heavy bases, one talent apiece, while the leftover silver furnishes the hooks, bands, and silver plating for the courtyard posts (38:25–28).

The bronze donated reaches seventy talents and 2,400 shekels—nearly two and a half tons. It supplies the bases for the Tent of Meeting entrance, the bronze altar with its grate and utensils, all the courtyard bases, and every tent peg (38:29–31). Astonishingly, even with mountains of metal like these, chapter 36 tells us the people kept bringing so much that Moses had to send word through the camp telling them to stop (36:5–7).

## Key Takeaways

**The road to God starts at the altar.** Anyone stepping through the courtyard gate meets the altar of burnt offering first, and the washing basin right behind it. Before coming close to a holy God, sacrifice must be made and cleansing must happen. The courtyard layout itself teaches how human beings approach God.

**Mirrors for admiring oneself become a basin for cleansing.** Polished bronze plates once held up to check one's appearance are melted down into a basin for washing. The women who surrendered their mirrors lost nothing of lasting worth; what once served outward vanity now serves the whole nation's purity before God.

**The entire nation stands on an equal foundation.** The silver forming the sanctuary's heavy bases came from the exact same half-shekel paid by every man—no more, no less. The rich were not allowed to pay extra, and the poor were not permitted to pay less (30:15). Literally and symbolically, the tabernacle rested on every person's equal share.

**Gifts given for God's work deserve transparent books.** Scripture does not hesitate to list every talent and shekel, nor to name the official auditing the accounts. Resources dedicated to God call for the highest accountability. Centuries later, Paul took the exact same care with a church collection, aiming to do what is right "not only in the eyes of the Lord, but also in the eyes of men" (2 Corinthians 8:20–21).

**The courtyard fence both sets a boundary and opens a door.** White linen rising higher than any human head made clear that no one could stroll casually into holy space. Yet right in the middle of that wall opened a wide gateway draped in rich, welcoming colors. God is fiercely holy—and still he opens a door.

## Deeper Meaning

**One altar, one final sacrifice.** Day after day, year after year, burnt offerings smoked on that bronze altar. The letter to the Hebrews tells believers that "We have an altar" (Hebrews 13:10)—the cross—where Jesus "offered for all time one sacrifice for sins" and sat down at God's right hand (Hebrews 10:12). What the bronze altar had to repeat endlessly, Christ completed once for all.

**Washing hands and feet before serving.** The priests had already been bathed completely at their ordination (29:4), yet every time they stepped up to serve they still washed their hands and feet (30:19–21). Jesus echoed that exact truth while washing his disciples' feet: "Whoever has already bathed needs only to wash his feet" (John 13:10). People who already belong to God still need daily cleansing. Hebrews likewise invites believers to draw near with "our bodies washed with pure water" (Hebrews 10:22).

**Mirrors and God's word.** James compares someone who hears God's word without acting on it to a person who glances at his face in a mirror and walks away immediately forgetting how he looks (James 1:23–24). A washing basin forged out of mirrors reminds us that Scripture both reveals our stains and provides the place to wash them clean. Paul writes that believers behold and reflect the Lord's glory as in a mirror, steadily being transformed into his image (2 Corinthians 3:18).

**Redeemed with something far costlier than silver.** Each half-shekel of silver was called "a ransom for his life," sparing the people from plague when counted (30:12). Down into New Testament times, Jewish men still paid this annual temple tax—and Jesus paid it too (Matthew 17:24–27). Yet Peter reminds believers that they were redeemed "not with perishable things such as silver or gold," but with the precious blood of Christ (1 Peter 1:18–19).

**The women serving at the Tent of Meeting.** Verse 38:8 is the first time Scripture mentions a group of women serving at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting. They surface again generations later, during the days of the priest Eli, when his corrupt sons mistreated them (1 Samuel 2:22). Scripture never spells out their exact duties, yet it shows that right from the start, women served around the sanctuary where God dwelled.

## Notes & Nuances

**Which Tent of Meeting, if the tabernacle had not been set up yet?** Verse 38:8 mentions women who served "at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting," even though the tabernacle is not raised until chapter 40. Two straightforward explanations exist. Either the narrator identifies them by the ministry role later readers already knew them for, or they were already serving at the temporary tent Moses pitched outside the camp, which Scripture also calls the Tent of Meeting (33:7).

**The women's mirrors in Jewish tradition.** An ancient Jewish story preserved by the medieval commentator Rashi says Moses at first hesitated to accept the mirrors, viewing them as instruments of vanity. God told him to accept them gladly: back in Egypt, those very mirrors had helped wives adorn themselves to comfort their exhausted husbands after brutal days of slavery, keeping Israel's families alive and growing. Though an extra-biblical legend rather than Scripture, it shows how long readers have pondered this fascinating detail.

**Where did such huge weights of metal come from?** A ton of gold and over three tons of silver represent staggering wealth for a people freshly freed from bondage. Exodus already explained the source: on the night they left Egypt, God moved the Egyptians to hand over generous gifts of gold and silver (12:35–36). Keep in mind also that modern weight equivalents are estimates, since we cannot be certain of the exact weight of an ancient sanctuary shekel.

**The figure of 603,550 men.** This matches down to the last digit the census total at the start of Numbers (Numbers 1:46), a count taken *after* the tabernacle was already standing. Some readers think the silver was collected first and the full roster finalized afterward; others suggest this accounting summary was compiled later once the complete census data were in hand. Many scholars also debate whether the Hebrew word translated "thousand" may refer to a military clan or unit rather than a literal count of one thousand individuals. Faithful Bible readers hold differing views on this question.
