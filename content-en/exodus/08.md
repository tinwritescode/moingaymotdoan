# Exodus 8 — Frogs, Gnats, and Flies

## Story

After the Nile turns to blood, Pharaoh still refuses to let the people go. The LORD sends Moses back into the palace with a warning: if you keep holding My people back, frogs will swarm out of the Nile and overrun the whole country—climbing right into your palace, into your bedroom and onto your bed, into the homes of your officials and people, even into your ovens and kneading bowls (8:1–4).

Aaron stretches out his hand with his staff over the rivers, canals, and ponds, and frogs swarm up to cover the entire land of Egypt. Pharaoh's magicians copy the sign with their secret arts—which only brings up more frogs without being able to get rid of a single one (8:5–7).

For the first time, Pharaoh has to summon Moses and Aaron and ask them to pray: ask the LORD to take the frogs away from me and my people, and I will let your people go sacrifice to the LORD. Moses lets Pharaoh himself pick the exact time, so the king will see this is no lucky coincidence but the power of God. "Tomorrow," Pharaoh replies. Moses leaves and cries out to the LORD. Sure enough, the frogs in the houses, courtyards, and fields die off. People pile them into massive heaps, and a foul stench fills the land. Yet the moment Pharaoh sees relief, he goes back on his word and hardens his heart, just as the LORD had said (8:8–15).

The third plague arrives without any warning. At the LORD's command, Aaron strikes the dust of the earth with his staff, and all the dust across Egypt turns into swarms of gnats covering both people and animals. The magicians try to reproduce the sign, but this time they fail completely. They tell Pharaoh plainly, "This is the finger of God." Still, Pharaoh's heart remains hardened (8:16–19).

For the fourth plague, God sends Moses early in the morning to meet Pharaoh by the water, warning that thick swarms of flies will invade the palace, the homes, and the very ground of Egypt. Yet this time something new happens: God will set apart the land of Goshen, where the Israelites live, so not a single swarm of flies will appear there—proving that the LORD Himself is present in the land (8:20–23).

The next day, dense swarms of flies pour in and ruin the land. Pharaoh hastily summons Moses and Aaron and tries to bargain: go sacrifice to your God, he says, right here inside Egypt. Moses refuses—the sacrifices Israel offers to the LORD would be detestable to the Egyptians, and doing so in front of them would provoke them to stone the Israelites. They must take a three-day journey into the wilderness as God commands. Pharaoh concedes a half-step: go into the wilderness, only don't go very far—and pray for me. Moses agrees, warning Pharaoh not to act deceitfully again. He steps outside and prays, and the LORD removes every single fly. Yet once again Pharaoh hardens his heart and refuses to let the people go (8:24–32).

## Key Takeaways

**Pharaoh wants relief from pain, not a change of heart.** When frogs are crawling across his bed and flies are ruining his palace, the king is quick to call Moses in and make promises. The moment the discomfort passes, his stubbornness snaps right back (8:15; 8:32). Fearing consequences is a very different thing from true repentance.

**God lets Pharaoh choose the hour.** When Moses asks Pharaoh when he wants the frogs gone, the king says tomorrow—and right on schedule the next day, they die (8:9–13). That small detail demolishes any notion that these were mere weather quirks or natural coincidences. God commands every hour and every minute.

**Human power runs out of rope very quickly.** The magicians manage to mimic blood and frogs, yet they can only worsen the misery, never remove a single frog. By the third plague they hit a wall and are forced to admit before their king that this is God's own hand (8:18–19).

**God begins drawing a clear line around His people.** Starting with the flies, Goshen stays untouched while the rest of Egypt reels (8:22–23). That protection shows the plagues are not blind disasters; they carry a clear purpose and an exact address.

**Halfway worship is not real obedience.** Pharaoh tries two compromises—worship inside Egypt, or go out but don't go far (8:25; 8:28). Moses turns both down. The freedom God demands for His people is not a tightly leashed arrangement still under Pharaoh's thumb.

## Deeper Meaning

**"The finger of God" returns on the lips of Jesus.** Centuries later, when critics question Jesus's authority in driving out demons, He answers with the very phrase Egypt's magicians used: "if I drive out demons by the finger of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20). The same power that broke Egypt's hold over Israel is now at work in Jesus, breaking the hold of darkness over human lives.

**The image of frogs echoes in Revelation.** In the Bible's closing book, demonic spirits sent out to deceive the kings of the earth are pictured looking like frogs (Revelation 16:13). The memory of Egypt's plagues becomes Scripture's shared vocabulary for God's judgment on proud empires that defy Him.

**The magicians become emblems of resisting the truth.** In the New Testament, Paul cites the two magicians remembered in Jewish tradition as Jannes and Jambres, who opposed Moses, to warn against people who resist the truth—whose folly will eventually become obvious to everyone (2 Timothy 3:8).

**The boundary around Goshen points ahead to God's keeping grace.** Setting a line between God's people and Egypt (8:22–23) is about more than geography. It foreshadows Passover night in chapter 12, when the lamb's blood distinguishes spared homes from judged ones, and points further to God's faithful shelter over all who belong to Him.

## Notes & Nuances

**The exact species behind *gnats* and *flies* are still debated.** The Berean Standard Bible translates the third plague as *gnats* and the fourth as *swarms of flies*, though translators and scholars still discuss whether the third refers to gnats, mosquitoes, or lice, and whether the fourth means horseflies, beetles, or biting insects in general. Either way, the scene is unmistakable: the entire country is overrun by swarms that make daily life unbearable.

**Why on earth did Pharaoh say "Tomorrow" instead of *right now*?** Readers often chuckle at that reply (8:10). Who would want one more night sleeping with frogs? Perhaps he wanted to look composed and save face, or perhaps he hoped the frogs might withdraw on their own overnight so he wouldn't owe Moses anything. Whatever his motive, the answer lays bare the bizarre stubbornness of a heart resisting God.

**Why would Israel's sacrifices be detestable to the Egyptians?** Egyptians revered certain animals—such as cows and rams—as sacred or linked to their gods (8:26). Slaughtering those very animals as sacrifices in full view of Egyptians would have looked like a shocking religious insult and could easily have sparked violence.

**Verse numbering in chapter 8—including 8:16–19—differs in some translations and commentaries.** In the Hebrew Bible, the first four verses of chapter 8 (8:1–4) are grouped at the end of chapter 7, shifting the verse numbers throughout chapter 8 by four whenever you check Jewish translations or technical commentaries. Most English Bibles, including the Berean Standard Bible, follow the standard numbering used here.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, commented on the magicians' confession that the plague was "the finger of God" (8:19). Even adversaries are eventually cornered into recognizing God's power when their own tricks fail, he observed—yet that recognition stays strictly in the head and never softens Pharaoh's heart. **Nahum Sarna**, American Jewish biblical scholar, pointed out that the Egyptian goddess Heqet was depicted with the head of a frog and honored as a guardian of childbirth and fertility.
