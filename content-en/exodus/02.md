# Exodus 2 — The Baby in the Basket

## Story

Under the shadow of Pharaoh's decree to throw every Hebrew baby boy into the Nile, a man of the house of Levi marries a woman who is also from Levi. She conceives and gives birth to a son. Seeing that he is a beautiful child, she hides him for three months (2:1–2).

When she can hide him no longer, she weaves a papyrus basket, waterproofs it with tar and pitch, places the child inside, and sets it among the reeds along the bank of the Nile. The baby's sister stands at a distance to watch what will happen to him (2:3–4). The very river Pharaoh meant as an instrument of death becomes the mother's hiding place for her son.

Just then, Pharaoh's daughter comes down to bathe in the Nile while her attendants walk along the bank. Spotting the basket among the reeds, she sends her maidservant to fetch it. When she opens it, she sees the little boy crying. Her heart is moved with compassion, and she says, "This is one of the Hebrew children" (2:5–6).

Immediately the boy's sister steps forward: would the princess like her to go call one of the Hebrew women to nurse the child for her? "Go ahead," Pharaoh's daughter replies. The girl runs straight home and fetches the baby's own mother (2:7–8).

Pharaoh's daughter tells the mother, "Take this child and nurse him for me, and I will pay your wages." So the woman carries her own baby home to nurse him—and gets paid by the royal court to do it (2:9). Pharaoh's genocidal decree is quietly undone by his own daughter.

When the boy grows older, his mother brings him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he becomes her son. She names him Moses, explaining, "I drew him out of the water" (2:10).

Years pass, and Moses grows to manhood. One day he goes out to visit his own people and sees their bitter forced labor. He watches an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his kinsmen. Looking this way and that and seeing no one around, Moses strikes the Egyptian dead and buries him in the sand (2:11–12).

The next day he goes out again and sees two Hebrews fighting. He asks the one in the wrong why he is attacking his companion. The man snaps back: "Who made you ruler and judge over us? Are you planning to kill me as you killed the Egyptian?" Fear grips Moses—what he did has clearly become known (2:13–14).

When Pharaoh hears of it, he seeks to kill Moses. Moses flees from Pharaoh, settles in the land of Midian, and sits down beside a well (2:15). The prince raised in Egypt's palace is now a fugitive in the desert.

The priest of Midian has seven daughters who come to draw water and fill the troughs for their father's flock. Some local shepherds come along and drive them away, so Moses rises to defend the women and waters their flock for them. When they return home early, their father Reuel asks why they are back so soon. "An Egyptian rescued us from the shepherds," they tell him, "and even drew water for us and watered the flock." Their father asks, "Where is he? Why did you leave the man behind? Invite him to have something to eat" (2:16–20).

Moses agrees to stay with the household, and Reuel gives him his daughter Zipporah in marriage. She bears a son, and Moses names him Gershom, saying, "I have become a foreigner in a foreign land" (2:21–22).

A long time passes, and the king of Egypt dies. The Israelites still groan and cry out under their slavery, and their cry for rescue rises up to God. God hears their groaning, remembers His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, looks upon the Israelites, and takes notice of their plight (2:23–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Deliverance begins with the quiet courage of women.** Beneath Pharaoh's order to murder every Hebrew boy, Moses's mother hides her son for three months and weaves a basket for the river, his sister keeps watch from afar and quickly thinks to fetch a nurse, and an Egyptian princess feels compassion and adopts him as her own (2:2–10). Right inside the palace of the ruler who ordered genocide, Israel's deliverer is raised to adulthood.

**Hot-headed human zeal cannot accomplish God's deliverance.** Once grown, Moses knows clearly who his people are and wants to defend his brothers, yet his way of helping is to kill an Egyptian and hide the body in the sand (2:11–12). The result is not liberation, but suspicion from his own countrymen and forty years of exile in Midian (2:14–15).

**The wilderness is God's training ground for leaders.** In Midian, the former Egyptian prince learns the humble life of a foreign shepherd, stepping up to defend vulnerable women at a well and building a quiet family life (2:16–22). The man who once lashed out in violence against an oppressor must learn patience tending sheep in the desert before he is ready to shepherd a nation.

**God never falls asleep to the cries of the oppressed.** At the end of the chapter, four verbs rush together with God as their subject: He heard their groaning, He remembered His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, He saw the Israelites, and He took notice of them (2:24–25). Just when the people feel completely abandoned, history is about to turn a decisive corner.

## Deeper Meaning

**Moses's basket echoes Noah's ark.** In Hebrew, the word used for Moses's *basket* (2:3) is the exact same word used for Noah's *ark* in the flood (Genesis 6:14)—the only two places in the entire Bible where that word appears. Just as the pitch-coated ark carried Noah safely through waters of destruction to begin humanity anew, this tar-coated basket carries Moses safely through a river of death to birth a new nation.

**The Savior also escapes a massacre of infants.** The pattern of a cruel king ordering the slaughter of baby boys to protect his throne, while the chosen deliverer miraculously escapes, repeats almost point for point in Jesus's infancy under King Herod's decree (Matthew 2:13–16).

**Choosing suffering with God's people over royal privilege.** The New Testament looks back on Moses's turning point at age forty not simply as a rash mistake, but as a defining choice of faith: he chose to suffer alongside the people of God rather than enjoy the fleeting comforts of Pharaoh's palace (Hebrews 11:24–26; Acts 7:23–25).

## Notes & Nuances

**Is the name *Moses* Egyptian or Hebrew?** Remarkably, it resonates in both languages. In ancient Egyptian, *mose* means *born of* or *son*, a common element in royal Egyptian names. In Hebrew, the sound *Moshe* connects to the verb *to draw out*, matching the princess's explanation in 2:10: "I drew him out of the water."

**Was Moses's father-in-law named Reuel (2:18) or Jethro (3:1)?** Exodus uses both names for him, and Judges also mentions the name Hobab (Judges 4:11). Reuel may have been his personal name or the name of the clan patriarch, while Jethro may have served as an honorific title for the chief priest of Midian.

**Saying God *remembered* and *took notice* (literally *knew*) in 2:24–25 does not mean He had forgotten.** In biblical language, when God "remembers" a covenant, it means the moment has come for Him to act on His promise (Genesis 8:1). And when He "knows" or "takes notice," it is not mere awareness of facts, but deep, compassionate involvement that moves Him to intervene.
