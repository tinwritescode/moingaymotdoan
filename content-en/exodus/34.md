# Exodus 34 — The Lord's Compassionate Name and Two New Tablets

## Story

The broken covenant is about to be mended. God tells Moses to chisel out two new stone tablets just like the originals, and he will write the same words on them again. Early the next morning, Moses must climb Mount Sinai completely alone—no one else may come up, and not even sheep or cattle may graze along the lower slopes. Moses carves the two tablets, rises at daybreak, and carries the stones back up the mountain just as God commanded (34:1–4).

The LORD descends in a cloud, stands right beside Moses, and proclaims his name. Passing in front of him, God declares one of the most solemn self-portraits in all of Scripture: "The LORD, the LORD God, is compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness, maintaining loving devotion to a thousand generations, forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin. Yet He will by no means leave the guilty unpunished; He will visit the iniquity of the fathers on their children and grandchildren to the third and fourth generations" (34:5–7).

At the sound of God's name, Moses immediately drops to the ground in worship. He voices the plea weighing heaviest on his heart: Lord, please travel right in the middle of us—even though this is a stiff-necked people—forgive our wickedness and sin, and claim us as your own inheritance (34:8–9).

God says yes and announces the renewal of his covenant. He promises to perform wonders never before seen in any nation on earth, driving out the six native peoples to clear Israel's path into the land. Yet the promise comes paired with an uncompromising warning: never make a treaty with the land's inhabitants, or they will become a deadly trap. Israel must tear down their altars and smash their sacred pillars, never sharing in their sacrificial meals or marrying their children lest they be drawn into worshiping foreign gods—because the LORD is "a jealous God," meaning he will never share his people's loyalty with any rival (34:10–16).

God then reviews the core rhythms of faithful worship: make no cast idols; keep the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread in the spring month of Abib to remember the exodus from Egypt; dedicate every firstborn son and male animal to the LORD, redeeming both sons and donkeys; and observe the seventh-day Sabbath rest, even during the frantic weeks of plowing and harvest. Three times a year—at the Feast of Unleavened Bread, the Feast of Weeks at the wheat harvest, and the Feast of Ingathering at the turn of the year—every Israelite man is to appear before God, who promises to guard their borders so no neighbor will even covet their land while they are away. Along with these come reminders about the Passover sacrifice, bringing the best firstfruits of the soil, and not cooking a young goat in its mother's milk (34:17–26).

God instructs Moses to write down these covenant words. Moses remains on the mountain with the LORD for forty days and forty nights without eating bread or drinking water, and the LORD inscribes the Ten Commandments onto the two new stone tablets (34:27–28).

When Moses walks down the mountain holding the two tablets of the Testimony, he has no idea that the skin of his face has turned radiant from talking with the LORD. The moment Aaron and the Israelites see his face shining, they pull back in fear, afraid to come near. Moses has to call out to them—first Aaron and the community leaders, then the rest of the nation—so he can pass along every word God gave him. Once he finishes speaking, Moses drapes a veil over his face. From then on, a regular pattern takes shape: whenever he enters the LORD's presence to speak with him, he lifts the veil; when he comes back out and delivers God's words, he puts the veil back on until the next time he goes in (34:29–35).

## Key Takeaways

**God defines himself first and foremost by mercy.** When God announces his own character in 34:6–7, the opening portrait overflows with grace: compassionate, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loyal love and faithfulness. His steadfast love stretches to a *thousand generations*, whereas judgment extends to the *third and fourth*. God's justice is real—he will never shrug off hardened rebellion—yet compassion lies at the very center of who he is.

**Forgiveness never lowers God's standard.** The second pair of stone tablets bears the exact same Ten Commandments as the first (34:1; 34:28). Being forgiven does not turn the covenant into something casual or watered down. God pardons the golden calf so his people can walk forward in faithfulness, not slide back into carelessness.

**Loyalty to God has to reach everyday work and worry.** Every command reviewed here touches daily bread and seasonal survival: stopping work on the Sabbath right in the middle of harvest, leaving home three times a year for pilgrimage, and trusting God to watch over the fields while you are gone. Trusting God is never just lofty vows spoken on a mountaintop—it means daring to let go of work and lean on him.

**Spending time near God changes a person from the inside out.** Moses' face did not glow because he tried to project an aura of holiness; he did not even realize it was shining. That radiance was simply the natural reflection of lingering long in God's presence.

## Deeper Meaning

**God's self-revelation in 34:6–7 is the verse the Bible quotes most often about itself.** Across the Old Testament—from Moses interceding a second time (Numbers 14:18), to Nehemiah's prayer (Nehemiah 9:17), to the psalmists (Psalms 86:15; 103:8; 145:8), to the prophets Joel and Jonah (Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2)—Scripture returns again and again to these very words whenever it speaks of who God is. And in the New Testament, John's description of Jesus as "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14) directly echoes the Hebrew phrase translated here as "abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness."

**Moses' veil and the new covenant.** The apostle Paul unpacks the meaning of the veil in 34:33–35 when writing to the Corinthians (2 Corinthians 3:7–18). As Paul explains it, Moses wore the veil so the Israelites would not watch that shining glory gradually fade away. Glorious as the old covenant was, it was temporary; the new covenant in Christ carries a glory that never fades. Whenever someone turns to the Lord, the veil is lifted, so that with unveiled faces we behold his glory and are progressively transformed into his likeness.

**Forty days of fasting before a new beginning.** Moses fasts forty days and forty nights on Mount Sinai (34:28), just as the prophet Elijah later journeys forty days to Mount Horeb (1 Kings 19:8), and Jesus fasts forty days in the wilderness before launching his public ministry (Matthew 4:2). All three moments mark a major fresh chapter in the story of redemption.

## Notes & Nuances

**Is it unfair for God to punish to the "third and fourth generations"?** Verse 34:7 can easily be misread as God unfairly punishing innocent grandchildren. Reading it alongside the rest of Scripture brings clarity: first, the parallel wording in the Ten Commandments specifies generations of those who *hate* God, repeating the rebellion of their forebears (cf. 20:5); second, it reflects the sobering reality that one generation's sin naturally spills over onto the children and grandchildren living under the same roof. Scripture explicitly insists that children are not put to death for their parents' sins (Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20).

**What does "do not cook a young goat in its mother's milk" mean?** This intriguing rule in 34:26 appears three times in the Pentateuch (23:19; Deuteronomy 14:21). Scholars suggest it may have targeted a Canaanite fertility ritual, or simply cultivated basic moral sensitivity—refusing to boil a young creature in the very milk meant to nourish its life. Later Jewish tradition built the dietary rule separating meat and dairy on this verse.

**Who wrote on the new stone tablets?** Verse 34:1 states plainly that the LORD will write on them, whereas a quick glance at 34:28 can make it sound like Moses wrote them because Moses is the subject of the previous sentence. Comparing 34:1 with Deuteronomy 10:4 makes the distinction clear: Moses wrote the covenant terms down in a scroll (34:27), while the LORD himself wrote the Ten Commandments on the stone tablets.

**Where did the idea of Moses having horns come from?** Michelangelo's famous statue of Moses sports two horns on his forehead because the ancient Latin translation (the Vulgate) misread a Hebrew verb in 34:29. That verb means *to send out rays of light*, yet its Hebrew root looks nearly identical to the word for *horn*.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer, calls God's self-proclamation in 34:6–7 the most vivid portrait of God anywhere in Scripture. He observes that God does not invite Moses to speculate about his abstract essence in heaven, but teaches him to know God through how he deals with humanity—overflowing with mercy yet unwavering in justice, inspiring both warm trust and reverent awe. *(Harmony of the Last Four Books of Moses, on Exodus chapter 34)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: arguing that God is known through his character toward humanity rather than abstract essence is quintessential Calvin; exact chapter 34 wording not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, pauses over the detail that Moses "was unaware that his face had become radiant" (34:29). From that quiet touch he draws a searching lesson in humility: the people who walk closest to God and reflect his beauty most clearly are usually the very last ones to notice or advertise their own glow. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Exodus chapter 34)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: well-known and frequently cited observation from Matthew Henry on 34:29 -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, describes 34:6–7 as the *core credo* of the entire Old Testament. He highlights the deliberate tension woven right into these two verses: boundless, forgiving mercy on one side, and justice demanding accountability on the other. In Brueggemann's view, the whole biblical story unfolds around how God lives inside that tension with his people without ever surrendering either side. *(Theology of the Old Testament, 1997)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing the internal theological tension of Exodus 34:6–7 is one of the famous pillars of Brueggemann's Theology of the Old Testament -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic collection of rabbinic debate and commentary, reveres 34:6–7 as God's *Thirteen Attributes of Mercy*. The sages taught that God wrapped himself in a prayer shawl like a prayer leader and taught Moses these exact words, promising that whenever Israel sins, repents, and recites this proclamation in its proper order before him, he will forgive them. *(Babylonian Talmud, Rosh Hashanah 17b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the tradition of the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy in Rosh Hashanah 17b is one of the most famous passages in the Talmud -->
