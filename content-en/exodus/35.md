# Exodus 35 — Willing Hearts Offering Gifts for the Tabernacle

## Story

The covenant has been restored, and the two new stone tablets rest safely in Moses' hands. Now the time has come to begin the great project God revealed on the mountain: building the tabernacle so he can live among his people. Moses calls the entire Israelite community together—and remarkably, he opens not with a fundraising appeal, but with the Sabbath law: work may be done for six days, yet the seventh day is a holy day of complete rest to the LORD. Anyone who works on it must be put to death, and not even a fire may be lit in their homes on the Sabbath (35:1–3).

Only then does Moses share God's invitation to give. He states the governing principle right up front—no compulsion, no flat tax, just: "Let everyone whose heart is willing bring an offering to the LORD." The list of materials is rich and varied: gold, silver, and bronze; blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, fine linen, and goat hair; ram skins dyed red, fine leather, and acacia wood; olive oil for the lamps, spices for the anointing oil and fragrant incense; plus onyx stones and other gems to set on the priestly garments (35:4–9).

Materials alone will not raise the tent; skilled hands are needed too. Moses invites every gifted craftsman among the people to step forward and build everything the LORD has commanded—the upright frames, curtains, ark of the Testimony, table for the Bread of the Presence, lampstand, altar of incense, altar of burnt offering, bronze washing basin, courtyard hangings, tent pegs and ropes, right down to the sacred garments for Aaron and his sons (35:10–19).

The assembly disperses back to their tents, and then a remarkable scene unfolds. Everyone "whose heart stirred him and whose spirit prompted him" begins bringing gifts. Men and women alike stream forward with brooches, earrings, rings, necklaces, and gold jewelry of every kind. Whoever owns colored yarn, fine linen, goat hair, tanned hides, silver, bronze, or acacia wood brings it along. Skilled women sit and spin blue, purple, and scarlet yarn and fine linen by hand; other gifted women whose hearts are moved spin the goat hair. The community leaders contribute onyx stones, precious gems, lamp oil, and fragrant spices. Scripture sums it up simply: Israelite men and women alike, every single person whose heart prompted them, brought a freewill offering for the work God had commanded (35:20–29).

At the close of the chapter, Moses publicly names the two master builders God chose back in chapter 31: Bezalel of the tribe of Judah and Oholiab of the tribe of Dan. He reminds the people that God has filled Bezalel with his Spirit, granting him wisdom and skill to design works in gold, silver, and bronze, cut gemstones, and carve wood. And Moses adds one fresh detail not mentioned in chapter 31: God has also given both men "the ability to teach others," so they will not merely craft everything themselves, but train and guide fellow artisans in every kind of engraving, embroidery, weaving, and fine design (35:30–35).

## Key Takeaways

**Even building God's house cannot crowd out the day of rest.** Opening the tabernacle project with the Sabbath law—mirroring how chapter 31 closed the blueprints with the Sabbath—draws a bright line: Israel is no longer a nation of slaves forced to make bricks without a day off in Egypt. No matter how noble the work, God's people must know when to stop and rest in him.

**The very same gold, two completely different hearts.** Only chapters earlier, the people pulled off their gold earrings to cast a golden calf (32:2–3). Now those same kinds of earrings, rings, and necklaces are brought forward to build the tabernacle. Money and valuables are not evil in themselves; what matters is whether they serve anxious self-reliance or grateful devotion to God.

**God welcomes only willing gifts.** Phrases like *willing heart* and *stirred heart* echo throughout the chapter (35:5; 35:21–22; 35:26; 35:29). God could easily have levied a mandatory tax and had the tabernacle finished in record time, yet he refused. The home where he dwells among his people had to rise from eager hearts, never from grudging obligation.

**Everyone has a part to play in the shared work.** Wealthy leaders were not the only ones contributing gems; ordinary families gave wood, bronze, and thread; women spun goat hair and dyed yarn with their hands; craftspeople offered their labor; and the two chief artisans gave their "ability to teach others" to raise up a whole team. The tabernacle was never Moses' solo venture—it was a home built by the whole community.

## Deeper Meaning

**Freewill generosity carries straight into the New Testament.** When the apostle Paul invites the Corinthian church to take up a collection for struggling believers in Jerusalem, he echoes this chapter's exact spirit: each person should give what they have decided in their heart, not reluctantly or under pressure, "For God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7).

**Diverse gifts building up the body of Christ.** The picture of an entire community contributing side by side—spinning yarn, carving wood, mentoring apprentices to build God's dwelling—foreshadows the Church in the New Testament. Every believer receives a distinct gift from the same Spirit; nobody is unnecessary, and nobody can replace everyone else. Together they grow into a living temple where God dwells (1 Corinthians 12:4–7; Ephesians 4:11–16).

**The gift of teaching is also from God.** In chapter 31, Bezalel received craftsmanship; here, both he and Oholiab receive the ability to pass that craft on to others (35:34). In the New Testament, Paul similarly urges Timothy to entrust what he has learned to faithful people who will in turn teach others as well (2 Timothy 2:2). God's work endures through generous mentorship, never through a few gifted individuals guarding their skills for themselves.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why is lighting a fire forbidden on the Sabbath?** Verse 35:3 prohibits kindling a fire in any home on the Sabbath. Placed immediately before metalworking for the tabernacle, many commentators think the rule covers both routine daily cooking and, especially, firing up furnaces and smelters for metalwork on the day of rest. In traditional Judaism, this verse became the basis for preparing meals before Friday evening and refraining from lighting fires on the Sabbath.

**Where did recently freed slaves get all that gold, silver, and gemstones?** The answer reaches back to the night they left Egypt: God gave the Israelites such favor with their Egyptian neighbors that they asked for—and received—generous amounts of silver, gold, and fine clothing before setting out (12:35–36). The very wealth they carried away from the house of slavery now builds the sanctuary of the God who set them free.

**The prominent role of women in building the tabernacle.** Scripture deliberately highlights women's contributions in 35:22 and 35:25–26: they not only offered jewelry, but personally spun the richly colored yarn and goat hair that formed the tabernacle's magnificent curtains. In an ancient world where women's work on major public projects often went unrecorded, this candid recognition stands out.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, highlights the phrase "the ability to teach others" given to Bezalel and Oholiab (35:34).
