# Exodus 29 — Consecrating the Priests and the Daily Offerings

## Story

Chapter 28 gave the blueprint for the holy garments; now chapter 29 lays out the ceremony that ordains and sets apart Aaron and his sons to step officially into the priesthood. The ritual calls for one young bull, two unblemished rams, and a basket of unleavened breads, cakes, and wafers prepared with oil. First, Moses brings Aaron and his sons to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, washes them with water, dresses Aaron piece by piece in his holy vestments, places the turban and gold diadem on his head, and pours anointing oil over him. Next, Moses dresses Aaron's sons in their tunics, wraps their sashes, and ties on their headbands (29:1–9).

Three sacrifices follow in sequence, each carrying a distinct purpose. First comes the *sin offering*, using the young bull: Aaron and his sons press their hands onto its head, transferring their guilt to the animal. After slaughtering it, Moses dabs blood onto the altar's four horns and pours the rest at its base; the choice fat is burned on the altar, while the meat, hide, and dung must be hauled outside the camp and burned completely. Second comes the *burnt offering*, using the first ram: after hands are laid and blood is sprinkled on all sides of the altar, the entire ram is cut up, washed, and burned whole on the altar as a pleasing aroma to the LORD—a vivid picture of total surrender (29:10–18).

Third is the second ram, designated as the *ram for ordination*. Moses takes some of its blood and applies it to the right earlobes, right thumbs, and right big toes of Aaron and his sons, then sprinkles a mixture of blood and anointing oil over them and their garments. Selected fat, the right thigh, and samples of unleavened bread are placed in the priests' hands to be lifted as a wave offering before the LORD before being burned on the altar. The breast and thigh become the sacred portion assigned to Moses and the priestly family. Finally, Aaron and his sons boil the rest of the ordination ram and eat it together with the unleavened bread right at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting; no outsider may share in it, and anything left until morning must be burned. The entire ordination and altar-atonement ceremony spans seven full days, and Aaron's holy garments are to pass down to his successor to wear for seven days upon taking office (29:19–37).

Once altar and priests are consecrated, God establishes Israel's daily rhythm of worship: one year-old lamb offered every morning and a second lamb offered at twilight, each accompanied by a grain offering mixed with olive oil and a drink offering of wine. At that very entrance where the daily sacrifice rises, God promises to meet and speak with His people. The chapter reaches its climax in the core promise behind the entire tabernacle: "Then I will dwell among the Israelites and be their God. And they will know that I am the LORD their God, who brought them out of the land of Egypt so that I might dwell among them" (29:38–46).

## Key Takeaways

**Even those chosen as priests must first be washed and atoned for.** Aaron and his sons cannot march into holy service on their own merit or status. Before they can ever offer a single sacrifice on behalf of the people, they themselves must be washed, lay hands on a sin-offering bull, and be anointed and set apart (29:4; 29:10–14).

**Blood placed on ear, hand, and foot marks whole-person dedication.** The ear is set apart to hear God's word, the hand to carry out His sacred work, and the foot to walk in His ways (29:20). Consecration to God is never merely putting on a fine robe; it is handing over all our senses, actions, and steps to Him.

**Three sacrifices map out the basic sequence of true worship.** The sin offering deals with guilt (29:14); the burnt offering surrenders the whole life to God (29:18); and the shared meal from the ordination ram seals peaceful fellowship with God (29:31–33). Forgiven, surrendered, and welcomed to the table—that is the pathway into God's presence.

**The ultimate purpose of the exodus was not just escaping Egypt, but God living among His people.** Verses 29:45–46 lay bare the heartbeat of the whole book of Exodus: God did not break Israel's chains simply so they could wander wherever they pleased; He brought them out of Egypt *so that He might dwell among them*. The daily morning and evening sacrifices keep that relationship alive and unbroken day after day.

## Deeper Meaning

**The letter to the Hebrews sets Aaron's ordination directly against Christ's eternal priesthood.** Because Aaron was a fragile sinner, he first had to offer sacrifices for his own sins before offering for the people, and the priests stood repeating morning and evening sacrifices without end (29:10–14; 29:38–42). Jesus, utterly spotless, never needed an offering for Himself; He offered His own body once for all and sat down forever at the right hand of God (Hebrews 7:26–28; 10:11–14).

**Burning the sin-offering bull outside the camp becomes a prophetic glimpse of Golgotha.** In verse 29:14, the flesh and hide of the sin-offering bull were carried outside the camp to be burned as carrying sin's defilement. The letter to the Hebrews points straight back to this rule: in the very same way, Jesus suffered outside the gate of Jerusalem to sanctify His people through His own blood (Hebrews 13:11–12).

**The promise "I will dwell among the Israelites" (29:45) finds complete fulfillment in Jesus and in the new creation.** In John's Gospel, the Word became flesh and *pitched His tabernacle* among us, revealing God's glory (John 1:14). And on the closing pages of Scripture, the promise of Exodus 29:45 rings out one final time in full triumph: the dwelling place of God is now among humanity, He will dwell with them, and they will be His people (Revelation 21:3).

**Every New Testament believer is called into a holy priesthood.** The washing, clothing, anointing, and consecration of chapter 29 pave the way for the apostle Peter's declaration that the Church is a royal priesthood, offering spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ (1 Peter 2:5; 2:9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Laying hands on the animal's head (29:10; 29:15; 29:19) meant pressing down with real weight, not tapping lightly.** The Hebrew verb literally means *to lean upon*. When Aaron and his sons leaned their hands onto the head of the bull or ram, they visibly acknowledged that this creature was standing in for their own lives before God.

**The "wave offering" (29:24) pictured presenting a gift to God and receiving back the portion He gave His servants.** In this ancient gesture, portions of the offering were lifted and moved toward the altar and back, declaring that everything belongs to God while He graciously assigns a share to sustain those who serve Him.

**Saying "whatever touches the altar will be holy" (29:37) does not describe automatic magical goodness.** In Old Testament ritual language, becoming *holy* meant being claimed strictly as God's property and barred from ordinary everyday use—sometimes carrying real danger for anyone who touched it improperly. That is why everyone approaching the altar had to undergo careful preparation.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, interprets the dabbing of blood on Aaron's right ear, thumb, and big toe (29:20) as a practical reminder for everyone who serves God. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, underscores how Moses had to offer a sin offering for Aaron himself (29:10–14) before Aaron could serve as priest. God arranged it this way on purpose, Calvin notes, so Israel would never place ultimate trust in a mortal man like Aaron, but would lift their eyes in hope toward a spotless, perfect High Priest—Christ Himself. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: using Aaron's imperfection to point ahead to Christ aligns closely with Hebrews 7 and Calvin's exposition; exact original wording on chapter 29 not verified -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the celebrated 19th-century London preacher, often pointed to the two lambs offered faithfully every morning and evening (29:38–42) as a picture of the believer's daily reliance on the cross. He urged Christians to open every morning and close every evening looking up to the Lamb of God, since the human heart needs forgiving grace every day—not merely once in the distant past. *(Sermons on the Daily Offering — Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: linking the morning and evening sacrifices to daily prayer and fresh reliance on the cross is a familiar theme in Spurgeon's preaching; exact sermon title not recalled -->

**Brevard Childs**, the 20th-century American Old Testament scholar, regards the closing verses (29:43–46) as the theological peak of the entire tabernacle instruction block from chapters 25 through 29. Human rituals do not make the tabernacle holy on their own, he observes; it is God's own *glory* and living presence that consecrates the tent, the altar, and the priests. *(The Book of Exodus: A Critical, Theological Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: treating 29:43–46 as the theological climax of the tabernacle section where God's glory sanctifies the sanctuary is a notable point in Childs's commentary; exact page number not verified -->
