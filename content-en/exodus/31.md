# Exodus 31 — Spirit-Filled Artisans and the Sabbath

## Story

Moses has been on Mount Sinai for forty days and forty nights, wrapped in the cloud covering the summit (24:18). Throughout that time, God has shown him every detail of a sacred tent—the tabernacle—so that he may dwell right in the middle of his people (25:8–9). The ark of the covenant, the table, the lampstand, the curtains, the priestly garments, the altars, the anointing oil, the fragrant incense—everything has been laid out with precision. Chapter 31 brings those instructions to their close (31:1).

The blueprint is ready; now builders are needed. God says he has "called by name Bezalel," the son of Uri and grandson of Hur, from the tribe of Judah. This Hur is likely the same man who stood alongside Aaron holding up Moses' arms during the battle with the Amalekites (17:12). God fills Bezalel "with the Spirit of God," granting him skill, ability, and knowledge—yet not for preaching or prophesying. Those gifts are for designing artistic works, crafting in gold, silver, and bronze, cutting gemstones for settings, carving wood, and mastering every kind of handiwork (31:2–5).

Bezalel will not work alone. God assigns him an assistant: Oholiab, son of Ahisamach, from the tribe of Dan. God also places skill in the hearts of every gifted craftsman among the people, so that together they can build everything he has commanded (31:6).

Then God reviews the list of what they are to make: the Tent of Meeting, the ark of the Testimony with its mercy seat, the table, the pure gold lampstand, the altar of incense, the altar of burnt offering, the bronze basin for washing, the sacred garments for Aaron and his sons, the anointing oil, and the fragrant incense. The inventory closes with a clear charge: they are to make everything according to all that God has commanded (31:7–11).

At this point, the instructions might seem complete. Yet God saves one final command for last, and he delivers it with unmistakable weight. The Israelites must keep the Sabbath—the seventh day of rest—because it is "a sign between Me and you" for all generations to come. Through that sign, people will know that the LORD himself is the one who sanctifies them. Anyone who works on that day must be put to death, or cut off from among his people (31:12–14).

God repeats the familiar rhythm: six days for work, and the seventh day for complete rest, set apart as holy to the LORD. Israel must keep this day as a permanent covenant. Its reason reaches all the way back to creation: in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth, and on the seventh day he "rested and was refreshed" (31:15–17).

With that, God's words come to an end. He places into Moses' hands two stone tablets containing the law. Scripture points out that no human chisel carved them—they were "inscribed by the finger of God" (31:18). Holding the two tablets, Moses prepares to head down the mountain. He has no idea what the people are doing at its base.

## Key Takeaways

**God calls people by name, and equips those he calls.** God does not simply tell Moses to go hire good craftsmen. He names one specific man, right down to his father and grandfather, and then gives him the exact abilities the work requires. A task assigned by God never travels alone; it always comes paired with the strength to carry it out.

**Skilled craftsmanship is also a gift of the Spirit.** The very first person in the Bible described as filled with the Spirit of God is not a prophet or a priest, but an artisan. Casting gold, carving wood, and setting stones are sacred work when done for God. People who serve with their hands do not stand a tier below those who serve with words.

**Rest matters so much that God puts it last.** After chapter upon chapter of tabernacle plans, God ends with the Sabbath. The message is unmistakable: even building a house for God has to pause on the day of rest. No work, however holy it seems, is allowed to swallow up the time set aside for resting in God's presence.

**The Sabbath is a sign of relationship.** God calls it a "sign" between himself and his people. Like a wedding ring, resting on the seventh day does not make Israel belong to God, but it shows the watching world whose they are.

## Deeper Meaning

**Building the tabernacle as recreating the world.** Across chapters 25–31, the phrase "Then the LORD said to Moses" opens seven distinct speeches (25:1; 30:11; 30:17; 30:22; 30:34; 31:1; 31:12). The seventh speech is about the Sabbath rest, mirroring the seven days of creation that culminate in God's rest (Genesis 2:2–3). Earlier, the cloud had covered Mount Sinai for six days, and only on the seventh day did God call to Moses (24:16). At the end of the book, when the tabernacle is finished, Moses inspects all the work and blesses the people—just as God looked over all he had made and saw that it was very good (39:43; cf. Genesis 1:31). Many readers see a quiet pattern here: the tabernacle is a miniature world, a pocket of creation's original order restored in the middle of the wilderness.

**The very same wisdom.** The three Hebrew qualities granted to Bezalel—wisdom, understanding, and knowledge , rendered "skill, ability, and knowledge" in the BSB (31:3),—are the exact same three terms Proverbs uses to describe how God formed the universe (Proverbs 3:19–20). The craftsman building God's house works with the very same wisdom God used to build the world.

**One man from Judah and one from Dan.** Judah is the tribe from which Israel's kings will come. Dan was the son of Bilhah, a servant woman, and his tribe later earned a far less celebrated reputation (Genesis 30:5–6). God pairs two men from opposite ends of Israel's social map to work side by side. The New Testament describes the Church in the very same way: there are many different gifts, yet only one Spirit, and every gift is given for the common good (1 Corinthians 12:4–7).

**The Spirit is still building God's house.** Here, the Spirit of God fills artisans to construct a tent where God can dwell. In the New Testament, Paul says that believers themselves are the building being joined together, growing into a dwelling place for God in the Spirit (Ephesians 2:21–22).

**The finger of God.** The two stone tablets are written by the finger of God (31:18). Earlier, Egypt's magicians had been forced to admit that the plagues were "the finger of God" (8:19). Jesus likewise says he drives out demons by the finger of God (Luke 11:20). And Paul writes that God no longer writes his law on tablets of stone, but by his Spirit on human hearts (2 Corinthians 3:3).

**Sabbath rest and Jesus.** Ezekiel echoes 31:13 almost word for word: the Sabbath is a sign so the people will know that the LORD sets them apart as holy (Ezekiel 20:12). Jesus reminds his listeners that the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath (Mark 2:27). The letter to the Hebrews carries the theme even further: a Sabbath rest still remains for God's people, and whoever enters it rests from relying on his own works (Hebrews 4:9–10).

## Notes & Nuances

**The death penalty for working on the Sabbath.** Reading 31:14–15 can jolt modern readers. It helps to remember that here the Sabbath functions as the covenant sign, much like circumcision (Genesis 17:11). Deliberately casting off that sign was a public renunciation of the covenant with God. Scripture records one instance where this penalty was carried out, on a man caught gathering wood on the Sabbath (Numbers 15:32–36). The New Testament explains that festival days and Sabbaths were a shadow of things to come, while the reality belongs to Christ (Colossians 2:16–17), which is why Christians do not enforce this civil penalty.

**God does not get tired.** Verse 31:17 says that God "rested and was refreshed." Literally, the Hebrew word for "refreshed" carries the vivid image of exhaling or catching one's breath. This is human language used so that the people can picture what God means. Elsewhere Scripture states plainly that the Creator "will not grow tired or weary" (Isaiah 40:28). God rested not out of exhaustion, but like a master craftsman stepping back to delight in finished work.

**What does "cut off from among his people" mean?** Interpreters understand this phrase in different ways. Some take it to mean permanent banishment from the community. Others see it as divine judgment carried out directly by God—such as an untimely death or dying without descendants to carry on one's name. Because the text leaves it open, it is wise not to insist on a single explanation.

**How Christians observe a day of rest remains a lively question.** Some churches continue to keep the seventh day strictly according to the commandment. Most observe Sunday—the day Jesus rose from the dead—as their day of rest and worship. Still others view the command today primarily as a principle: maintaining a healthy rhythm of work and rest while finding ultimate rest in Jesus. Chapter 31 does not settle that debate; it simply reveals how deeply rest matters to God.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Geneva Reformer, points directly to Bezalel and Oholiab when discussing human talent. Since Scripture attributes their craftsmanship to the Spirit of God, Calvin argues that every admirable gift in art, science, and law—whether found in believers or unbelievers—comes from the Spirit as well. To despise such good gifts, he insists, is to insult the very Spirit who gave them. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, II.2.15–16)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Institutes II.2.16 cites Bezalel and Oholiab by name; the point about not despising truth wherever it appears is in II.2.15 and is very widely quoted -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, emphasizes that whenever God calls someone to a task, he also equips that person to carry it out. He also notices the chapter's placement of the Sabbath command: no matter how urgent or sacred the work of building the tabernacle may be, it is never allowed to encroach on the Sabbath rest. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls the two main ideas from his chapter 31 notes, though unsure of his exact wording -->

**Terence Fretheim**, an American Old Testament professor, reads the tabernacle as a miniature creation. In his view, the instructions for the tabernacle are deliberately arranged like the seven days of creation and crowned with the Sabbath. In the middle of a fractured world, God gives his people a sanctuary where creation's original order is rebuilt. *(Exodus, Interpretation)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: creation is a central theme throughout his commentary; not certain whether he discusses the seven speeches specifically in the chapter 31 section -->

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, draws attention to the word "sign" in 31:13. Across the first five books of the Bible, God establishes three covenant signs: the rainbow after the flood, circumcision for Abraham, and the Sabbath for Israel. Sarna also notes that ancient Jewish sages linked the Sabbath command directly to the tabernacle instructions to teach that even sacred construction had to halt on the day of rest. *(Exodus, JPS Torah Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident the three covenant signs observation is Sarna's, though unsure whether it appears in his Exodus commentary or in Exploring Exodus -->
