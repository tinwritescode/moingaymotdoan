# Exodus 28 — The Holy Garments of the Priests

## Story

The blueprints for the tabernacle and courtyard are finished; now God appoints the men who will minister inside. He selects Aaron—Moses' older brother—together with his four sons Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar to serve Him as priests. Before they begin their duties, skilled craftsmen filled with God's wisdom must make holy garments for Aaron "to give him glory and splendor" and consecrate him for service. The garments use the exact same precious materials as the tabernacle curtains: gold thread, blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and fine linen (28:1–5).

Most striking on the outside is the *ephod*—a sleeveless vest woven of gold thread and colored yarn, secured with a matching woven waistband. Mounted on the ephod's two shoulder pieces are two onyx stones set in gold filigree, engraved with the names of Israel's twelve tribes in order of birth, six on each side. Thus whenever Aaron steps into the Holy Place, he is to "bear their names on his two shoulders as a memorial before the LORD" (28:6–12).

Anchored snugly over the chest of the ephod with braided gold chains and a blue cord is the *breastpiece of judgment*, folded double into a square pouch. Across its front sit four rows of gemstones—twelve stones in all, each engraved with the name of one tribe of Israel. In this way, the names of the entire nation rest not only on Aaron's shoulders but right over his heart whenever he stands before God. Placed inside the breastpiece are the *Urim* and *Thummim*—two sacred objects used to seek the LORD's guidance and verdict on behalf of His people (28:13–30).

Beneath the ephod lies a robe woven entirely of blue cloth, with a reinforced collar opening so it will not tear. All around its lower hem hang alternating woven pomegranates in rich colors and tiny gold bells. Whenever Aaron enters or leaves the sanctuary, the ringing of those bells signals his solemn ministry so that he will not die. On Aaron's head rests a fine-linen turban, with a plate of pure gold tied to the front engraved with the words: "HOLY TO THE LORD." That gold plate rests permanently on his forehead as he bears the guilt of any flaws in Israel's sacred gifts, so that the people's offerings may be acceptable before the LORD (28:31–38).

Closest to Aaron's skin is a woven fine-linen tunic bound with an embroidered sash. Aaron's sons receive simpler yet still dignified attire—tunics, sashes, and headbands. In addition, both Aaron and his sons must wear linen undergarments reaching from waist to thigh to ensure strict modesty whenever they approach the altar or enter the Tent of Meeting, keeping them from incurring guilt and dying (28:39–43).

## Key Takeaways

**Priesthood is God's calling, never an office anyone claims on their own.** Aaron and his sons did not campaign for the role; God Himself called them by name and set them apart (28:1). Nor were the splendid robes meant to flatter Aaron personally—they reflected the majesty of the God he served.

**The priest carries the entire nation on his shoulders and over his heart.** The two stones on the shoulders speak of strength to carry a burden; the twelve gemstones over the chest speak of love and constant remembrance (28:12; 28:29). Standing before God, the priest never goes alone—he brings all twelve tribes with him, leaving not a single one behind.

**Even humanity's finest offerings still need to be made holy.** Verse 28:38 reveals a humbling truth: the gold plate on Aaron's forehead helps him bear *the iniquity of the holy things* Israel brings as gifts. Even in our highest moments of bringing offerings to God, human worship remains flawed and incomplete, needing a holy representative to stand for us so our gifts are accepted.

**Golden bells and modest undergarments teach deep reverence before God.** Aaron's robes echo the very colors and fabrics of the tabernacle curtains: entering God's house calls for harmony with the holiness of that space. From the bells chiming at his hem to the linen undergarments beneath, every detail signals that approaching the Holy One is a matter of life and death (28:35; 28:42–43).

## Deeper Meaning

**Aaron's garments are only a shadow of Christ, our perfect High Priest.** The letter to the Hebrews explains that Aaron's priestly line served only *a copy and shadow* of heavenly realities (Hebrews 8:4–5). Jesus is a far greater High Priest: unlike Aaron, He is neither weakened by sin nor cut short by death, but lives forever to intercede for everyone who draws near to God through Him (Hebrews 7:24–27). He carries His people's names on shoulders of almighty power and on a heart overflowing with mercy, able to sympathize with every weakness we feel (Hebrews 4:14–16).

**Aaron's borrowed splendor gives way to the true glory of the incarnate Word.** Aaron needed gold thread, gemstones, and dyed linen to wear "glory and splendor" (28:2). When Jesus *pitched His tabernacle* among us, He wore no jeweled vestments; His own life full of grace and truth radiated the glory of the one and only Son from the Father (John 1:14).

**The gold plate "HOLY TO THE LORD" reaches its full meaning in Christ and His Church.** Aaron wore those words on his forehead as an outward emblem; Christ actually is holy, innocent, and undefiled, uniquely qualified to offer Himself to God and sanctify His people completely (Hebrews 7:26; 10:10). By the time of the prophet Zechariah, that promise expands so dramatically that even horse bells and ordinary cooking pots in Jerusalem will carry the inscription *HOLY TO THE LORD* (Zechariah 14:20)—every corner of a believer's everyday life set apart for God.

**The twelve gemstones on Aaron's chest echo in the foundations of the New Jerusalem.** When Revelation depicts the holy city coming down from heaven, its twelve wall foundations shine with twelve precious stones echoing Aaron's breastpiece (Revelation 21:19–20). The people once carried over the high priest's heart become eternal jewels built into the city of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Scripture never tells us what the "Urim and Thummim" (28:30) looked like.** The two Hebrew words are commonly understood to mean *lights and perfections*. Many scholars suggest they were two small stones or sacred lots tucked inside the breastpiece pouch, used to receive clear *yes* or *no* guidance from God in major national decisions (cf. 1 Samuel 14:41; Numbers 27:21). Later, as prophets and written Scripture guided Israel, this method faded out of use.

**Several gemstone names in verses 28:17–20 are difficult to match with modern mineral names.** Ancient gem terminology rarely maps one-to-one onto modern geology; for instance, the stone translated "diamond" in verse 28:18 was more likely a clear semiprecious stone that a gem cutter could engrave. What matters is not identifying every mineral, but recognizing that every tribe had its own distinct, precious, irreplaceable stone.

**The golden bells around the hem (28:35) were not paired with a rope to drag out a dead priest.** A popular legend claims people tied a cord around the high priest's ankle in case he died inside the Most Holy Place and had to be pulled out. Scripture mentions nothing of the kind, and verse 28:35 states plainly why the bells were there: so the sound would be heard as he entered and left the sanctuary before the LORD, marking proper, reverent approach.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, lingers long over verse 28:38 and the gold plate inscribed *HOLY TO THE LORD*. Even our most fervent prayers and offerings, he observes, remain mingled with weakness and imperfection; we could never be accepted by God apart from Christ, our spotless High Priest, bearing away the flaws clinging even to our holiest worship. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 28)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reflecting on verse 28:38 that even our holy offerings need Christ to bear their imperfections is a famous point in Matthew Henry and Puritan exposition -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, explains why Aaron's attire was so elaborate and dazzling. Under the Old Testament, he writes, God used visible splendor like picture lessons for an infant people to teach them about the spiritual glory of the coming Mediator; now that Christ has appeared, true glory shines in the Gospel rather than in gold-trimmed robes and jewels. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: viewing Old Testament ceremonies and priestly garments as temporary visual aids pointing to Christ is a consistent theme in Calvin; exact wording on chapter 28 not verified -->

**Philo of Alexandria**, the 1st-century Jewish philosopher, reads the high priest's robes as a miniature portrait of the entire cosmos: the blue robe mirrors the sky, the four woven colors mirror the four elements of nature, and the twelve gemstones mirror the twelve constellations and twelve months of the year. When the high priest steps into the sanctuary wearing those garments, Philo suggests, he brings not only Israel but all of creation before the Creator in prayer. *(On the Life of Moses, Book II)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Philo's cosmic symbolism for the high priest's vestments in On the Life of Moses Book II is famous and widely cited by scholars -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, points out that Aaron's garments contain zero traces of the magical charms or shamanic regalia worn in neighboring pagan cults.
