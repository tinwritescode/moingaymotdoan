# Exodus 22 — Property Laws and Compassion for the Vulnerable

## Story

Chapter 22 continues Israel's civil code with rulings on property, theft, and restitution. If someone steals an ox or a sheep and slaughters or sells it, he must repay five oxen for an ox and four sheep for a sheep. If a thief is caught breaking in at night and is beaten to death, the homeowner is not guilty of bloodshed; if it happens after sunrise in broad daylight when everything can be seen clearly, the homeowner bears bloodguilt. A thief must make full restitution, and if he owns nothing, he must be sold into service to work off the debt. If the stolen animal is found still alive in his possession, he repays double (22:1–4).

Next come responsibilities for damage to fields and entrusted property. Whoever lets livestock wander into another person's field or vineyard must pay restitution from the very best of his own crop. Whoever starts a fire that spreads through thornbushes into stacked or standing grain must pay back the loss in full. When someone leaves money or goods with a neighbor for safekeeping and they are stolen, the thief—if caught—repays double; if the thief is not found, the homeowner must appear before God (or the judges) to swear he did not take the goods himself. Any dispute over lost property or entrusted livestock that dies or is injured with no witnesses is settled by a solemn oath before the LORD. If an entrusted animal is stolen, the caretaker repays it; if it was torn by wild beasts, bringing the carcass as evidence clears him. And anyone who borrows a neighbor's animal that is injured or dies while the owner is absent must repay it, unless the owner was present or the animal was rented for a fee (22:5–15).

From verse 16, the laws turn to family life, morality, and worship. A man who seduces and sleeps with an unpledged virgin must pay the bridal price and marry her; if her father firmly refuses to give her to him, the man must still pay an amount equal to the bridal price to secure her future. Three grave offenses carry death or utter destruction from among the people: practicing sorcery, lying with an animal, and sacrificing to any god other than the LORD alone (22:16–20).

The following section stands out with a passionate, personal tone quite different from the clipped legal style before it: laws defending society's most vulnerable people. Israel must never mistreat or oppress a foreign resident, because they themselves were once foreigners in Egypt. Nor may they exploit any widow or orphan; God delivers a stern warning that if they cry out to Him, He will surely hear, His anger will burn, and He will strike the oppressor with the sword until the oppressor's own wife becomes a widow and his children become fatherless (22:21–24).

If they lend money to a poor fellow Israelite, they must not act like harsh creditors or charge interest. If they take a neighbor's cloak as collateral, they must return it before sunset, because it is the only covering he has to sleep in at night; if he cries out to God, God will hear, because He declares, "I am compassionate" (22:25–27).

The chapter closes with reminders of reverence toward God. The people must not blaspheme God or curse the ruler of their nation. They must not hold back offerings from their firstfruits and wine vats, and they must set apart their firstborn sons and the firstborn of their cattle and sheep on the eighth day. Finally, they are to live as "My holy people," refusing to eat meat from any animal mauled by wild beasts in the field and throwing it to the dogs instead (22:28–31).

## Key Takeaways

**Biblical justice emphasizes restitution over brutal punishment.** A thief does not lose a hand or his life as in many other ancient codes; instead, he repays double, fourfold, or fivefold depending on the loss and what he did with the stolen property (22:1–4). Restitution both repairs the victim's loss and requires the offender to take responsibility through his own labor.

**Even self-defense carries moral boundaries.** A homeowner is cleared of guilt when striking down a burglar breaking in under cover of darkness, yet may not use the protection of property as an excuse to kill in full daylight when the situation is clear (22:2–3). Human life—even a thief's life—still weighs more than possessions.

**Memories of past suffering must grow into present compassion.** God tells Israel not to oppress foreign residents because they remember living as foreigners in Egypt (22:21). Having suffered under harsh hands is never a reason to take it out on others; it is a lesson in stepping into the shoes of the vulnerable.

**God Himself steps forward as defender of those with no advocate.** Foreigners, widows, orphans, and the poor without a cloak for the night had no clout at the ancient courtroom gate. God speaks up directly: if they cry out, He will listen and deal with their oppressors Himself (22:22–27). He does not merely legislate fairness; He reveals Himself by saying, "I am compassionate."

**Reverence for God shows up in ordinary daily choices.** Offering firstfruits reminds the people that every harvest comes from God's hand; refusing to eat mauled meat marks out a distinct way of life worthy of being called His holy people (22:29–31).

## Deeper Meaning

**Fourfold restitution in verse 22:1 becomes a measuring stick for genuine repentance.** When King David heard the prophet Nathan's story of a rich man seizing a poor man's only lamb, David flared up and declared the man must repay fourfold (2 Samuel 12:6). Centuries later, when the tax collector Zacchaeus met Jesus and repented, he volunteered to pay back four times over anyone he had cheated (Luke 19:8)—going well beyond what the law required for a voluntary confession to prove his heart had truly changed.

**The ban on charging interest to the poor paves the way for New Testament generosity.** Verse 22:25 protects struggling neighbors from slipping into a spiral of debt that strips away their land and freedom. In the New Testament, Jesus carries that spirit even further: lend without expecting anything in return, because our Father in heaven is kind even to the ungrateful (Luke 6:34–36).

**Care for widows and orphans becomes the hallmark of genuine faith.** The apostle James writes that pure and undefiled religion before God means caring for orphans and widows in their distress and keeping oneself unstained by the world (James 1:27). Those exact two threads—compassion for the helpless and a holy life—stand side by side at the end of Exodus 22.

**The apostle Paul quotes verse 22:28 directly while on trial before the Sanhedrin.** When the high priest Ananias ordered someone to strike Paul on the mouth, Paul reacted sharply—yet the moment he learned Ananias was the high priest, he apologized immediately and cited this very command against cursing a ruler (Acts 23:2–5). Even when treated unjustly, Paul honored the God-ordained office.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 22:18 regarding a "sorceress" belongs in the context of rejecting idolatry and occult magic.** Mosaic law strictly outlawed sorcery, divination, and necromancy because they sought power apart from God and pulled the whole community away from Him (Deuteronomy 18:10–12). Tragically, in medieval and early modern Europe, this verse was repeatedly abused to hunt down and condemn countless innocent women—a painful consequence of tearing ancient Israel's civil law out of its historical context.

**The bridal price in verses 22:16–17 was financial security for the young woman, not buying a human being.** In the ancient world, a young woman who lost her virginity outside marriage faced slim prospects of marrying and risked lifelong poverty without protection. The law forced the man either to take lifelong responsibility for her as his wife or, if her father judged him unfit, to pay the bridal price as an economic safeguard for her future.

**The command in verse 22:29 to "give Me the firstborn of your sons" never meant offering children as burnt sacrifices.** Scripture forbids child sacrifice in the strongest possible terms (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31). As chapter 13 already made plain, Israel's firstborn sons belonged to God and were to be redeemed with an offering (13:13); later, the entire tribe of Levi was chosen to serve in place of Israel's firstborn sons (Numbers 3:12–13).

**Appearing "before God" (translated "before the judges" in the BSB) in verses 22:8–9 can refer either to the sanctuary or to legal magistrates.** The Hebrew word *Elohim* normally means God, yet in courtroom contexts it can also refer to judges acting in God's name. Either way, the meaning is clear: when there are no human witnesses, the dispute is laid before God's supreme oversight through a solemn oath.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, notices the difference between repaying five oxen and four sheep in verse 22:1. He explains that an ox was both more expensive and essential for plowing the field, so losing one hurt the owner far more deeply; moreover, an ox was easier to watch than a sheep, meaning anyone who stole one had acted with greater boldness. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 22)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: this practical and economic explanation for the five-oxen / four-sheep ratio is a common and characteristic observation in Matthew Henry; exact original wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, approaches the ban on interest in verse 22:25 with careful discernment. He distinguishes between fair commercial lending and exploiting the poor in distress: what God fiercely condemns here is preying on a struggling neighbor's hardship to enrich oneself at the expense of another's misery. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's distinction between commercial lending and exploiting the poor is famous in theological and economic history and appears in his exposition of the lending laws -->

**Brevard Childs**, the 20th-century American Old Testament scholar, highlights the sudden shift in voice starting at verse 22:21: the cool courtroom *if someone does this, then do that* gives way to God speaking directly in the first person (*I*) to His people. In his view, crimes against the weak easily slip through the cracks of human courts, so God Himself steps in to enforce justice. *(The Book of Exodus: A Critical, Theological Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting the shift from casuistic case law to direct first-person divine speech in 22:21–27 is a familiar observation in Childs's commentary; exact wording not verified -->

**Christopher J. H. Wright**, the British Old Testament scholar, stresses that the laws protecting the poor and the foreigner in chapter 22 are not mere private charity, but social justice rooted in God's own character.
