# Exodus 6 — "I Am the LORD"

## Story

The LORD answers Moses's bitter cry: "Now you will see what I will do to Pharaoh." Because of My mighty hand he will let the people go—in fact, because of My strong hand he will drive them out of his land (6:1).

Then God declares: "I am the LORD." I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as God Almighty, yet by My name the LORD I did not make Myself known to them. I established My covenant with them to give them the land of Canaan, where they lived as foreigners. Now I have heard the groaning of the Israelites whom the Egyptians are enslaving, and I have remembered My covenant (6:2–5).

He sends Moses back to the people with a sweeping chain of promises: I am the LORD. I will bring you out from under the yoke of the Egyptians, deliver you from their bondage, and redeem you with an outstretched arm and mighty acts of judgment. I will take you as My own people, and I will be your God, and you will know that I am the LORD your God who brought you out. I will bring you into the land I swore to give to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and give it to you as a possession. "I am the LORD!" (6:6–8).

Moses carries these words to the Israelites, but because of their broken spirit and cruel bondage, they cannot bring themselves to listen (6:9).

The LORD tells Moses to go back to Pharaoh and demand that he let the Israelites leave his land. Moses protests: if the Israelites themselves won't listen to me, why would Pharaoh listen to someone as unskilled in speech as I am? Even so, the LORD gives Moses and Aaron a direct charge concerning both Israel and Pharaoh, to bring the Israelites out of Egypt (6:10–13).

Here the narrative pauses for a genealogy. It begins briefly with the sons of Reuben, Israel's firstborn, and then the sons of Simeon, giving each line only a few sentences (6:14–15).

When it reaches the line of Levi, however, the record slows down and fills in the details: Levi's sons Gershon, Kohath, and Merari, and their descendants. Amram, Kohath's son, marries his father's sister Jochebed, and she bears Aaron and Moses. The list continues with Aaron's wife and sons, down to his grandson Phinehas (6:16–25). The two men standing up to Pharaoh did not drop out of thin air; they have parents, clans, and real roots among their people.

The genealogy concludes with deliberate emphasis: this is the very Aaron and Moses to whom the LORD said, "Bring the Israelites out of the land of Egypt by their divisions"—the very ones who spoke to Pharaoh to lead Israel out (6:26–27).

Then the story picks up right where it paused. On the day the LORD spoke to Moses in Egypt, He told him: I am the LORD; tell Pharaoh everything I say to you. And Moses again objects: since I am unskilled in speech, why would Pharaoh listen to me? (6:28–30).

## Key Takeaways

**When human words run out, God stakes His own name on the outcome.** The chapter is framed by the commanding refrain, "I am the LORD" (6:2; 6:6; 6:8; 6:29). Facing Pharaoh's defiant question (5:2) and Moses's despair (5:22–23), God does not offer long explanations; He pledges that He Himself will act with a mighty hand (6:1).

**Seven *"I will"* promises lay out the full sweep of redemption.** In three dense verses (6:6–8), God stacks seven promises one on top of another: I will bring you out from under Egypt's yoke, deliver you from bondage, redeem you with an outstretched arm, take you as My own people, be your God, bring you into the Promised Land, and give it to you as a possession. Every step from rescue to belonging is initiated and finished by God Himself.

**Crushing hardship can numb the heart even to good news.** Moses brings those breathtaking promises to the Israelites, yet they cannot hear them through their broken spirits and brutal labor (6:9). God does not scold their exhaustion; He presses on with His plan to save them even while they lack the strength to believe.

**The genealogy anchors God's saving work in real history.** Right at a tense point in the plot, verses 6:14–25 stop to trace the family tree of Moses and Aaron through the tribe of Levi. The two men confronting Pharaoh are not mythical heroes from nowhere, but real flesh-and-blood sons of their community.

## Deeper Meaning

**The four cups of wine at Passover.** Later Jewish tradition drew on the first four saving verbs of 6:6–7—*bring you out*, *deliver you*, *redeem you*, and *take you as My own people*—to give meaning to the four cups of wine drunk at the annual Passover meal. It was at that very Passover table that Jesus lifted a cup and established the new covenant in His blood (Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25).

**God redeems His people to make them His own.** The word *redeem* in 6:6 originally described a close relative paying the price to buy back a kinsman sold into slavery (Leviticus 25:47–49). The New Testament picks up that exact picture to describe the work of Christ: He redeemed us not with silver or gold, but with His precious blood, to claim us as God's own people (1 Peter 1:18–19; Titus 2:14).

**The central promise of the entire Bible.** Verse 6:7—"I will take you as My own people, and I will be your God"—is the covenant heartbeat that echoes from the prophets (Jeremiah 31:33) all the way to the closing pages of Scripture, when the new heaven and new earth appear (Revelation 21:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 6:3 say God did not make Himself known by His name *the LORD* (*Yahweh*) when Genesis uses that name repeatedly?** Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob certainly knew and spoke the name *the LORD* (Genesis 15:7; 28:13). Verse 6:3 does not mean they had never heard the syllables; rather, they had known God primarily as *God Almighty*—the One who makes promises—without yet experiencing the full depth of the name *the LORD*, the faithful Redeemer who steps into history to rescue an entire nation and fulfill His oath.

**What lies behind the phrase *"unskilled in speech"* (6:12; 6:30)?** Literally, the Hebrew says *a man of uncircumcised lips*—a vivid metaphor for lips that feel sluggish, blocked, inadequate, or unworthy to carry God's holy words.

**Why does the genealogy in 6:14–25 list only the first three tribes and stop?** The writer is not trying to catalog all twelve tribes; he simply moves in birth order from Reuben and Simeon to the third son, Levi, in order to establish the exact lineage of Aaron and Moses—the two men stepping up to face Pharaoh (6:26–27).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explained 6:3 in terms of experiential depth: the patriarchs were not ignorant of the name *the LORD*, yet they tasted the promises only from afar, whereas Moses's generation was about to witness with their own eyes the saving power that unveiled the name's full meaning. **Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, called the promises of 6:6–8 a golden covenant handed to an exhausted people. He observed that even when Moses shrinks back again at the close of the chapter, haunted by his sense of being *unskilled in speech* (6:12; 6:30), God refuses to cast him aside and patiently leads him step by step into his calling. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus chapter 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: commenting on the chain of promises in 6:6–8 and God's patience with Moses is very characteristic of Matthew Henry; exact wording not verified -->
