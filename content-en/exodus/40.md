# Exodus 40 — Setting Up the Tabernacle and the Filling of Glory

## Story

Every piece of the tabernacle is finished, inspected, and blessed at the close of chapter 39. Now God names the day to raise it: the first day of the first month—Israel's New Year's Day. God walks Moses step by step through the assembly order from inside out: erect the tabernacle frame, set the ark of the Testimony inside and screen it off with the veil; bring in the table with its utensils and the lampstand with its lamps set up; position the gold altar of incense in front of the ark and hang the entrance curtain; out in front, set the altar of burnt offering and place the basin filled with water between the Tent of Meeting and the altar; finally, raise the surrounding courtyard and hang the curtain across its gate (40:1–8).

Once everything stands in place, it must be anointed with oil to consecrate it as holy: the tabernacle and all its furnishings, the altar of burnt offering, and the washing basin. Next, Moses is to bring Aaron and his sons to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, wash them with water, dress them in the sacred garments, and anoint them into a permanent priesthood for generations to come (40:9–15).

Right on schedule—on the first day of the first month of the second year after leaving Egypt—Moses raises the tabernacle, carrying out every single command. He lays down the silver bases, sets up the upright frames, slides the crossbars home, and spreads the tent coverings overhead. He places the stone tablets of the Testimony inside the ark, attaches the carrying poles, sets the mercy seat on top, carries the ark inside, and hangs the veil to shield it. Next he places the table along the north wall and arranges the bread on it; sets the lampstand opposite on the south wall and sets up the lamps; positions the gold altar before the veil and burns fragrant incense; and hangs the curtain at the tabernacle doorway. Stepping outside, he sets up the altar of burnt offering and offers the burnt offering and grain offering upon it; sets the basin filled with water in the space between, where Moses, Aaron, and Aaron's sons wash their hands and feet whenever they enter the Tent of Meeting or approach the altar. Last of all, he erects the surrounding courtyard and hangs its entrance curtain. Seven times through this sequence, the refrain sounds out—"just as the LORD had commanded him" (and "Moses" on the seventh)—before concluding with five simple words: "So Moses finished the work" (40:16–33).

The very instant the work is finished, heaven answers. "Then the cloud covered the Tent of Meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle." That glory settles so densely and overwhelmingly that even Moses—the man who spoke face to face with God atop Sinai—"was unable to enter the Tent of Meeting" (40:34–35).

Exodus closes with a sweeping portrait that carries Israel through every wilderness mile ahead: whenever the cloud lifted from above the tabernacle, the Israelites packed up and set out; whenever it remained, they stayed right where they were until the day it rose again. Throughout every stage of their journey, the cloud of the LORD hovered over the tabernacle by day, and fire glowed inside the cloud by night, clearly visible to the entire house of Israel (40:36–38).

## Key Takeaways

**Exodus does not end when Israel escapes slavery—it ends when God moves in among them.** If the goal of the exodus had only been political liberation, the book could have closed in triumph on the shore of the Red Sea (chapter 15). Instead, twenty-five more chapters build toward this single climax: God leaves the distant mountaintop to pitch his tent right in the heart of a flesh-and-blood community. True freedom is living in the presence of God.

**New Year's Day marks a fresh start with God.** The tabernacle goes up on the first day of the first month (40:2; 40:17), exactly one year after the month Israel's rescue from Egypt began (12:2). Once an enslaved workforce forced to build store cities for Pharaoh (1:11), they have become a free people building a home for the LORD.

**Wholehearted obedience clears the way for God's glory.** The refrain "just as the LORD had commanded" rings seven times through chapter 40, echoing its sevenfold beat in chapter 39. Human hands can never force God to show up, yet when his people faithfully finish the task he entrusts to them, he delights to step inside and fill the house with his glory.

**Even the person closest to God pauses before his holiness.** Moses being "unable to enter" (40:35) is no bleak ending; it is a moment of deep reverence. Even with the tabernacle sitting right in the center of camp, God never becomes someone humans can stroll up to and manage at will. That lingering final question—how can mortal people step inside to meet the holy God now living right next door?—swings open the door straight into Leviticus (Leviticus 1:1).

**The rhythm of God's people is moving and waiting at God's lead.** The daytime cloud and nighttime fire remind us that a life of faith does not run on restless human schedules. Sometimes obedience means pulling up stakes and marching forward; sometimes obedience means staying patiently put until the cloud lifts.

## Deeper Meaning

**The tabernacle is Eden restored.** Scholars hear unmistakable echoes of Genesis 1–2 throughout this finale: "So Moses finished the work" (40:33) directly mirrors God finishing the work of creation (Genesis 2:2); the sevenfold refrain of obedience recalls the seven days of creation; and raising the sanctuary on the first day of the first month recalls the exact calendar date when the earth dried out after Noah's flood (Genesis 8:13). Once again, God dwells in the midst of humanity as in the beginning.

**The same scene of glory unfolds again at Solomon's temple.** Centuries later, when King Solomon dedicates the stone temple in Jerusalem, this exact moment repeats itself: the cloud fills the LORD's house so completely that the priests cannot even stand to perform their service (1 Kings 8:10–11). Neither a stone temple nor a portable tent holds value on its own; its glory comes solely from the One who chooses to move in.

**The Word *pitched his tent* among us.** When John's Gospel declares, "The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14), the Greek verb literally means *pitched his tent*—or *tabernacled*—right among us, and the very next line adds, "We have seen His glory." Jesus himself is God's true tabernacle walking among humanity, bringing the very glory Moses could not enter in 40:35 near enough to touch and know.

**The closing vision of the entire Bible.** Scripture's final book ends with the exact scene Exodus 40 introduces, expanded now to fill a new heaven and a new earth: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man, and He will dwell with them. They will be His people" (Revelation 21:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Moses anoint Aaron during chapter 40?** God commands the anointing and ordination of Aaron and his sons in 40:9–15, yet the narrative of Moses carrying out the work in 40:16–33 describes only erecting the tabernacle and setting up its furnishings, without narrating Aaron's consecration yet. That solemn seven-day ordination ceremony is recorded in full detail a little later in Leviticus (Leviticus 8:1–36).

**Does Moses being unable to enter contradict chapter 33?** In 33:9–11, Moses walked freely into the temporary Tent of Meeting outside camp and spoke with God like a friend; here in 40:35, he cannot step inside the newly erected tabernacle. There is no contradiction: this is the decisive moment when God's glory descends in full, overwhelming intensity to take possession of his new dwelling. By the very first verse of Leviticus, the LORD calls to Moses from inside the Tent of Meeting and lays out the sacrificial system so human beings can draw near to him safely (Leviticus 1:1).

**Were the daytime cloud and nighttime fire two separate phenomena or one?** Both were a single pillar marking God's presence: under the bright desert sun it appeared as a shading cloud, while in the darkness of night the fiery glow of God's glory shone unmistakably through it so the entire camp could see (cf. 13:21–22; Numbers 9:15–16).

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, contrasts Moses standing barred outside the glory-filled tabernacle (40:35) with the bright confidence believers enjoy under the New Testament. **Umberto Cassuto**, a twentieth-century Jewish scholar, points out the remarkable symmetry tying the close of Exodus back to the opening of Genesis. In his reading, the sevenfold refrain "just as the LORD had commanded" and the summary line "So Moses finished the work" in 40:33 intentionally echo the seven days of creation—showing that the completed tabernacle is the crowning moment when God's creation finally regains a home for his presence among humankind. *(A Commentary on the Book of Exodus)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Cassuto is well known for highlighting the sevenfold structure and Genesis 1–2 echoes across Exodus 39–40; exact wording details uncertain -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer, reads the guiding cloud and fire at the end of the book (40:36–38) as a daily school of faith: Israel was never free to pick its own departure dates or camp wherever it pleased, but had to keep its eyes fixed upward on God's signal. For Calvin, the Church in every generation walks the very same road—finding real peace only by moving and pausing at the direction of God's word. *(Harmony of the Last Four Books of Moses, on Exodus chapter 40)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: aligns closely with Calvin's practical exposition of 40:36–38; exact original wording not checked -->

**Brevard Childs**, a twentieth-century American Old Testament scholar, observes that Exodus intentionally closes on an open-ended note: even with every piece finished, Moses remains standing outside, unable to enter.
