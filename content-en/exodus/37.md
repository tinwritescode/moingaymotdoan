# Exodus 37 — The Golden Furnishings of the Sanctuary

## Story

In the previous chapter, the craftsmen completed the shell of the tabernacle—its curtains, upright frames, and posts. Now come the sacred furnishings that will stand inside. Leading the work is Bezalel, the artisan God called by name and filled with his Spirit to master every craft (31:2–3). Nearly every sentence in this chapter echoes chapters 25 and 30, where God showed Moses the pattern on the mountain. Back then, the verb was a command: "you shall make." Now it turns into accomplished action: "he made."

First comes the ark, later known as the ark of the covenant. Built of acacia wood, it measures two and a half cubits long, a cubit and a half wide, and a cubit and a half high. Since a cubit is roughly a forearm's length—about 18 inches (half a meter)—the ark is about the size of a large chest. It is overlaid with pure gold inside and out, fitted with four gold rings at its four feet, and carried by two gold-overlaid poles slipped through the rings (37:1–5).

Resting atop the ark is a lid called the "mercy seat," crafted of pure gold to fit the top precisely. At its two ends rise two cherubim hammered from the very same piece of solid gold—one piece, no seams. The cherubim stand facing each other, gazing down toward the mercy seat with wings arched upward to overshadow it (37:6–9). God had already told Moses this is the exact spot where he will meet him: "above the mercy seat between the two cherubim" (25:22).

Next is the table, also made of acacia wood overlaid with pure gold, edged by a rim a handbreadth wide (about four fingers), with rings and poles for carrying. Alongside it, the artisans fashion pure gold plates, dishes, bowls, and pitchers for pouring drink offerings (37:10–16). Fresh bread will stay set out on this table before the LORD continually (25:30).

The most intricate masterpiece of all is the lampstand. It is hammered from a single block of pure gold—no soldering, no joined pieces. From its central shaft rise six curving branches, three on each side, shaped like a miniature tree. Every branch bears cups sculpted like almond blossoms complete with buds and petals, topped by seven lamps. The lampstand and its utensils alone use up an entire talent of pure gold—roughly seventy-five pounds (more than thirty kilograms) (37:17–24).

Then comes the altar of incense: compact and square, one cubit on each side and two cubits high, with a horn projecting from each of its four corners. Like the other inner pieces, it is overlaid with pure gold, circled with gold molding, and equipped with gold rings and carrying poles (37:25–28).

Finally, Bezalel prepares the sacred anointing oil and the pure, fragrant incense, using the art of a master perfumer (37:29). God had already prescribed both recipes—and strictly forbidden anyone in Israel from copying either blend for personal use (30:22–38).

## Key Takeaways

**Instructions have become reality.** Chapter 25 was the blueprint; chapter 37 is the finished room. Scripture patiently records it almost word for word so readers cannot miss the point: Israel carried out every instruction down to the letter, adding nothing and leaving nothing out.

**The closer to God, the richer the metal.** Every piece in this chapter is either pure gold or overlaid with gold, because it sits inside the enclosed rooms nearest God's presence. Out in the open courtyard, as the next chapter shows, everything shifts to bronze. Even the metals preach a quiet lesson: whatever draws closest to God is set apart as most holy.

**God dwells in the middle—and journeys with his people.** Every major furnishing has rings and carrying poles. This is no stone temple bolted permanently to one hilltop. It is the mobile dwelling of a God who walks right alongside his people through the wilderness.

**Skilled hands are a gift from God.** Scripture declares that Bezalel was filled with the Spirit of God—not to deliver prophecies or perform miracles, but to hammer gold, carve wood, and blend incense (35:30–31). A skilled worker taking careful pride in their craft is serving God just as surely as a preacher.

**At the very center lies the law, covered by mercy.** Inside the ark rest the two stone tablets of the law (25:16; 40:20). Resting directly over them is the mercy seat. God's moral demands sit underneath; the place of forgiveness and meeting sits right on top.

## Deeper Meaning

**The mercy seat and the cross.** Once every year, on the Day of Atonement, the high priest carried blood into the Most Holy Place and sprinkled it on the mercy seat (Leviticus 16:14–15). When Paul writes that God presented Jesus "as the atoning sacrifice" (Romans 3:25), he chooses the exact Greek word that the Greek Old Testament used for the mercy seat. The letter to the Hebrews likewise mentions "the mercy seat" overshadowed by the cherubim (Hebrews 9:5). What once stood hidden behind a heavy veil now stands wide open: "Let us then approach the throne of grace with confidence" (Hebrews 4:16).

**Cherubim from the garden of Eden.** The very first time cherubim appear in Scripture, they stand east of Eden with a flaming sword, blocking humanity's return to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24). Here they stand guard once again—yet now they frame the very place where God meets human beings. The way is still guarded by holiness, but a meeting place has been opened.

**A tree sculpted in gold.** With its trunk, branches, buds, and almond flowers, the golden lampstand looks remarkably like a blossoming almond tree. Many readers see here an echo of the tree of life in Eden (Genesis 2:9). The almond is the earliest tree to bloom in spring; God once showed Jeremiah an almond branch as a wordplay on his watchfulness over his promise (Jeremiah 1:11–12), and Aaron's staff likewise budded and produced ripe almonds (Numbers 17:8).

**Bread and light.** Twelve loaves sat on the golden table, replaced fresh every Sabbath (Leviticus 24:5–8)—widely understood as representing the twelve tribes living continually before God's face. Directly across from the table stood the lampstand, kept burning from evening until morning (27:20–21). In John's Gospel, Jesus declares himself both "the bread of life" (John 6:35) and "the light of the world" (John 8:12). And the book of Revelation pictures the churches themselves as golden lampstands, entrusted now with keeping that light shining in the world (Revelation 1:20).

**Incense and prayer.** Fragrant incense burned on the golden altar every morning and twilight (30:7–8). The psalmist prays that his prayer may rise before God "like incense" (Psalms 141:2). When Zechariah stepped inside the sanctuary to burn incense, the crowd outside was praying (Luke 1:9–10). And in Revelation, clouds of incense ascend before God's throne mingled with the prayers of all the saints (Revelation 8:3–4).

**Copy and shadow.** The letter to the Hebrews describes the earthly tabernacle as "a copy and shadow of what is in heaven," quoting God's charge to Moses to build everything according to the pattern shown on the mountain (Hebrews 8:5; cf. Exodus 25:40). The true sanctuary stands in heaven; the gleaming furnishings of this chapter are its shadow cast onto earth.

## Notes & Nuances

**Do not skim past the repetition.** Modern readers are often tempted to jump over this chapter since its details appeared earlier in chapter 25. In the ancient world, however, recounting every specification a second time was a way of honoring craftsmanship and obedience—much like reading out every completed milestone at a building's grand dedication.

**Do not force hidden meanings onto every ring and peg.** Over the centuries, some interpreters have tried to assign spiritual symbolism to every single detail, right down to the count of clasps and colors of thread. After naming the tabernacle's furnishings, the letter to the Hebrews itself pauses and says, "we cannot discuss these things in detail now" (Hebrews 9:5). It is wisest to hold firmly to the big connections Scripture itself draws and tread lightly with everything else.

**The cherubim are not idols for worship.** The second commandment forbids making carved images to bow down to (20:4–5), yet right atop the ark sit two golden cherubim. The crucial difference is that no one ever worshiped them, and they did not depict God himself—they were attendants bowing low before the place where the invisible God was enthroned. By contrast, Israel had called the golden calf in chapter 32 the gods who led them out of Egypt (32:4).

**Where did the altar of incense actually stand?** Exodus places this altar just outside the veil, directly in front of the Most Holy Place (30:6). Yet Hebrews 9:4 lists "the golden altar of incense" (using a Greek term that can also mean a golden censer) alongside the furnishings of the Most Holy Place. Two main explanations make sense of this. First, the Greek word in Hebrews may refer to the portable golden censer carried inside on the Day of Atonement rather than the stationary altar. Second, although the altar stood just outside the veil, its function was so tightly tied to the inner room that 1 Kings calls it the altar "that belonged to the inner sanctuary" (1 Kings 6:22).

**"Bezalel made" does not mean he worked solo.** He served as chief master craftsman, working alongside Oholiab and a full team of gifted artisans (36:1). Scripture simply credits the project under the name of the person leading it.

**Gregory of Nyssa**, a fourth-century bishop in Asia Minor, tells the story of Moses' life as the soul's ascent toward God. For Gregory, the true heavenly tabernacle Moses beheld on the mountain is Christ himself—the sanctuary "not made by hands"—while the earthly tent of fabric and gold is its shadow. **Umberto Cassuto**, an Italian Jewish scholar who later settled in Israel, explains why chapters 35–40 echo chapters 25–31 nearly verbatim.
