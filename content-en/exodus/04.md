# Exodus 4 — Moses Refuses, Then Sets Out

## Story

Moses still isn't convinced. "What if they do not believe me or listen to my voice?" he asks. "They may say, 'The LORD has not appeared to you.'" The LORD asks him, "What is that in your hand?" "A staff," Moses answers. "Throw it on the ground," says the LORD. Moses throws it down, it turns into a snake, and Moses runs from it (4:1–3).

"Stretch out your hand and grab it by the tail," the LORD commands. Moses reaches out, catches the snake, and it turns back into a staff in his hand. This sign is so the people will believe that the God of their fathers has truly appeared to him (4:4–5). An ordinary shepherd's crook is about to become "the staff of God."

God gives him a second sign. He tells Moses to put his hand inside his cloak; when he pulls it out, his hand is leprous, white as snow. He puts it back in and draws it out again, and it is completely restored, like the rest of his skin. If they won't believe the first sign, they may believe the second. And if they still refuse to believe both signs, he is to take water from the Nile and pour it onto dry ground, where it will turn to blood (4:6–9).

Moses tries another objection: "Please, Lord, I have never been eloquent, neither in the past nor since You have spoken to Your servant, for I am slow of speech and tongue." The LORD answers: "Who gave man his mouth? Or who makes the mute or the deaf, the sighted or the blind? Is it not I, the LORD? Now go! I will help you as you speak, and I will teach you what to say" (4:10–12).

At last Moses drops every excuse and speaks his true mind: "Please, Lord, send someone else" (4:13).

The LORD's anger burns against Moses. Even so, He gives him a helper: Aaron the Levite, his brother, who speaks well and is already on his way to meet him—and will be glad in his heart to see him. You will put the words in Aaron's mouth, and he will speak to the people as your spokesman. Now take this staff in your hand to perform the signs (4:14–17).

Moses returns to his father-in-law Jethro and asks leave to go back to his brothers in Egypt to see if they are still alive. "Go in peace," Jethro replies. The LORD had also told Moses in Midian to return, because all the men who wanted to kill him are dead. So Moses puts his wife and sons on a donkey, takes the staff of God in his hand, and heads back to Egypt (4:18–20).

Along the way, God warns him: perform before Pharaoh all the wonders I have put in your power, yet I will harden his heart so he will not let the people go. Then tell Pharaoh what the LORD says: "Israel is My firstborn son, and I told you to let My son go so that he may worship Me. But since you have refused to let him go, behold, I will kill your firstborn son!" (4:21–23).

At a lodging place along the road, the LORD meets Moses and is about to kill him. Zipporah quickly grabs a flint knife, cuts off her son's foreskin, touches it to Moses's feet, and declares, "Surely you are a bridegroom of blood to me!" Then the LORD lets him alone—and the text notes she said "bridegroom of blood" because of the circumcision (4:24–26). It remains one of the most puzzling passages in the entire book.

Meanwhile, the LORD tells Aaron to go meet Moses in the wilderness. Aaron meets his brother at the mountain of God and kisses him, and Moses tells him everything the LORD has commanded him to say and all the signs he is to perform (4:27–28).

The two brothers return to Egypt and assemble all the elders of Israel. Aaron speaks everything the LORD told Moses and performs the signs in full view of the people. The people believe. And when they hear that the LORD has attended to them and seen their affliction, they bow down and worship (4:29–31).

## Key Takeaways

**God uses the ordinary thing already in your hand.** God asks Moses what he is holding—just an everyday shepherd's staff (4:2). Yet once Moses obeys and lays it down, it becomes "the staff of God" (4:20), the instrument of mighty signs before Pharaoh.

**The Creator of the human mouth is more than able to cover our weaknesses.** Moses points to his halting speech to beg off (4:10). God doesn't debate his rhetorical skill; He simply reminds Moses who made the human mouth in the first place, and promises to be with him as he speaks (4:11–12).

**Excessive humility can cross the line into disobedience.** Behind one objection after another, Moses finally admits what he really wants: send somebody else (4:13). That is when God's anger flares (4:14)—because holding back after all of God's promises is no longer humility, but refusal to trust Him.

**Even in anger, God remains patient and gracious.** Instead of discarding Moses, the LORD gives him his eloquent brother Aaron to walk beside him and serve as his voice (4:14–16).

**The leader of the covenant people cannot neglect the covenant himself.** The startling scene at the roadside lodging (4:24–26) delivers a sobering reminder: Moses is about to carry God's message to Pharaoh and all Israel, yet his own son has not received circumcision—the sign of the covenant given to Abraham (Genesis 17:14).

## Deeper Meaning

**Israel is God's *firstborn son*.** In 4:22–23, God for the first time calls the entire nation of Israel His *firstborn son*, warning that if Pharaoh refuses to release God's son, God will strike Pharaoh's own firstborn. That title echoes through the prophet Hosea (Hosea 11:1) and reaches its fullest meaning in Jesus—God's beloved Son, the firstborn over all creation (Matthew 3:17; Colossians 1:15).

**Signs that authenticate the one God sends.** The three signs given to Moses—a staff turned snake, a hand struck with leprosy and healed, Nile water turned to blood—strike directly at Egypt's symbols of power: the royal cobra on Pharaoh's crown and the life-giving Nile (4:2–9). In the New Testament, Jesus's miracles carry the same purpose: bearing witness that the Father has sent Him (John 5:36; 20:30–31).

**God's power made complete in weakness.** Moses's tug-of-war with God in chapter 4 establishes a pattern running through all of Scripture: God routinely chooses people who feel unqualified so no one can boast before Him—just as we see later with Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1:6–8) and Paul (2 Corinthians 12:9).

## Notes & Nuances

**How should we understand the scene where the LORD *was about to kill* Moses at the lodging place (4:24–26)?** This is one of the most cryptic episodes in the Old Testament because the account is so brief and the Hebrew pronouns leave several details unspoken. The most widely held explanation is that Moses had neglected to circumcise his son as the covenant required (Genesis 17:14). When a sudden illness or judgment threatened Moses's life, Zipporah grasped the reason, quickly circumcised their son with a flint knife, and touched the foreskin to Moses's feet to spare her husband. Calling him a *bridegroom of blood* meant he had been bought back for her through the blood of circumcision.

**Does God hardening Pharaoh's heart (4:21) mean Pharaoh had no free choice?** Across chapters 5 through 14, Scripture speaks both ways: several times Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15; 8:32; 9:34), and several times the LORD hardens it (9:12; 10:1). Here God prepares Moses for a fierce struggle; Pharaoh's hardness is a tangle of the king's own stubborn refusal and God's judicial act of handing him deeper into the path he chose (Romans 1:24; 9:17–18).

**Did Moses have a speech impediment, or simply lack confidence?** Verse 4:10 says he was "slow of speech and tongue." Some think he literally stuttered; others suggest that after forty years in Midian, he no longer felt fluent in polished Egyptian court language. Either way, Acts 7:22 tells us Moses was once "powerful in speech and action," suggesting that the deeper barrier was fear of returning to the land that had tried to execute him.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, traced Moses's five objections across chapters 3 and 4 to show the thin line between humility and unbelief: recognizing our weakness is right, yet refusing to go after God has promised His presence is disobedience. On the lodging scene (4:24–26), Calvin held that God disciplined Moses severely as an example—the man about to deliver God's law to an entire nation could not treat the sign of the covenant lightly in his own home. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, Exodus chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin comments on the disobedience behind Moses's excuses and explains 4:24–26 through his neglect of circumcision; exact wording not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, turned God's question in 4:2 into a practical encouragement: God does not demand what we do not have, but uses the plain shepherd's staff already in our hand when we surrender it to Him. He also lingered on the joyful reunion of Aaron and Moses at the mountain of God (4:27) as a tender comfort God gives His servants for the road ahead. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: drawing a practical lesson from the staff in Moses's hand is a very familiar application in Matthew Henry; exact wording not verified -->

**Brevard Childs**, twentieth-century American Old Testament scholar, acknowledged that 4:24–26 contains archaic details we cannot fully decode, yet noted its crucial role where it stands in Exodus: right before Moses confronts Pharaoh with the threat against Egypt's firstborn (4:23), Moses's own household must be redeemed through covenant blood. *(The Book of Exodus, 1974)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Childs connects 4:24–26 with the foretold plague on the firstborn in 4:22–23 and the later Passover; exact wording not verified -->
