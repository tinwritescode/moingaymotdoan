# Exodus 20 — The Ten Commandments

## Story

Israel stands gathered at the base of Mount Sinai amid thunder, lightning, and billowing smoke rising from the peak (19:16–18). From the mountain, God Himself speaks aloud to the entire nation. Before issuing a single command, He introduces Himself: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (20:1–2).

The first four commandments govern Israel's relationship with God. First, they must have no other gods before Him. Second, they must never craft an idol shaped like anything in heaven, on earth, or in the waters beneath to bow down to or worship; He is "a jealous God," visiting the consequences of sin down to the third and fourth generations of those who hate Him, yet pouring out steadfast love to a thousand generations of those who love Him and keep His commandments. Third, they must never take the LORD's name "in vain"—using His name carelessly, flippantly, or falsely. Fourth, they must remember the seventh-day Sabbath to keep it holy: do all their work in six days, and on the seventh let the entire household—children, servants, resident foreigners, and even livestock—rest, just as the LORD created heaven and earth in six days and rested on the seventh (20:3–11).

The remaining six commandments govern how people live with one another. Fifth, honor your father and mother so your days may be long in the land God is giving you. Sixth, do not murder. Seventh, do not commit adultery. Eighth, do not steal. Ninth, do not bear false witness against your neighbor. Tenth, do not covet your neighbor's house, wife, servants, livestock, or anything else that belongs to your neighbor (20:12–17).

Hearing the thunder and the blast of the ram's horn, watching lightning flash and the mountain smoke, the people tremble and pull back to stand far away. They beg Moses to listen to God on their behalf and relay His words, terrified that hearing God speak directly will kill them. Moses reassures them: do not be afraid; God has come to test them so that the fear of Him will stay before them and keep them from sinning. So the people stay back at a distance, while Moses steps alone into the thick darkness where God is (20:18–21).

Out of that cloud, the LORD adds His first instructions about worship. Because Israel witnessed Him speaking from heaven with no visible form, they must never make silver or gold gods alongside Him. When they build an altar for burnt offerings and peace offerings, they should make it simply out of earth—or if they use stones, leave them uncut and unshaped, since swinging an iron chisel over them will defile the altar. Nor should they climb up to the altar on high steps, lest they expose themselves immodestly. And God makes a promise: wherever He causes His name to be remembered, He will come to His people and bless them (20:22–26).

## Key Takeaways

**Grace comes before law.** God does not open the Ten Commandments with a cold list of prohibitions. First He reminds Israel of what He has already done: He rescued them out of slavery in Egypt (20:2). Israel does not obey the Law to buy their freedom; they obey because God has already set them free and claimed them as His own.

**Loving God and loving people belong together.** The first four commandments reach upward toward God; the last six reach outward toward our neighbors. Meticulous religious observance paired with murder, theft, lies, or envy is counterfeit worship. Conversely, how we treat our parents and neighbors grows straight out of whether God truly holds first place in our hearts.

**God's Law searches the heart, not merely outward behavior.** Commandments six through nine—murder, adultery, theft, false testimony—are outward acts a human courtroom can prosecute. Yet the tenth commandment, "You shall not covet," cuts straight into hidden desires inside the heart (20:17). God sees the quiet envy simmering long before it ever turns into action.

**Healthy reverence for God produces healthy living.** Moses tells a shaking crowd: do not be afraid, because God came so that the fear of Him would stay before you (20:20). That sounds like a paradox until we recognize two very different kinds of fear: terror that makes people run away from God, and deep, awestruck reverence that keeps people from slipping into sin.

**True worship lives in an obedient heart, not shiny statues or ornate altars.** No silver or gold idols are allowed, and the altar is just heaped earth or rough, unchiselled stone (20:23–25). Majesty never comes from human craftsmanship; it comes from the living presence of God in the place He chooses.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus sums up all Ten Commandments in two commands of love.** When a teacher of the Law asks which commandment is greatest, Jesus answers: love the Lord your God with all your heart, and love your neighbor as yourself (Matthew 22:36–40). He also reveals that the spirit of these commandments goes far deeper than the bare letter: nursing anger against a brother already plants the seed of murder, and looking lustfully at someone already commits adultery in the heart (Matthew 5:21–28).

**The Ten Commandments reveal both God's holiness and human helplessness.** The apostle Paul confesses that the tenth commandment—do not covet—was the very mirror that exposed the sinful heart inside him (Romans 7:7–12). The Law is holy and good, yet on its own it lacks the power to transform us; instead, it leads us to realize how deeply we need Christ, who kept the Law completely and bore its penalty in our place (Galatians 3:24).

**The fifth commandment becomes the first New Testament promise for families.** Paul repeats the call for children to honor their parents alongside its promise of blessing and long life on the earth (Ephesians 6:1–3). He also gathers all the neighbor-facing commandments into a single line: whoever loves their neighbor has fulfilled the Law (Romans 13:8–10).

**Trembling at Sinai spotlights our need for a Mediator.** Unable to bear hearing God's voice directly, the people beg Moses to stand between them and God (20:19). The letter to the Hebrews recalls that terrifying scene of fire, thunder, and smoke and sets it beside Mount Zion in the new covenant: through Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, believers draw near to God not in terror, but in grace (Hebrews 12:18–24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Different traditions number the Ten Commandments differently.** The biblical text itself puts no numbers beside the commands. Protestant and Eastern Orthodox traditions split *no other gods* (20:3) and *no idols* (20:4–6) into commandments 1 and 2, keeping *do not covet* together as commandment 10. Catholic and Lutheran traditions combine *no other gods* and *no idols* into commandment 1, then split verse 20:17 into commandments 9 and 10. Jewish tradition counts the opening declaration in verse 20:2 as the first word. However they are numbered, the text itself remains completely unchanged.

**Visiting iniquity to the third and fourth generation does not mean children are punished for their parents' sins.** In verse 20:5, the phrase *of those who hate Me* is tied directly to the warning, and elsewhere Scripture explicitly declares that children will not be put to death for their parents' sins (Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20). When parents turn away from God, destructive habits and their fallout naturally ripple across generations if children keep walking down the same path. By contrast, God's steadfast love for those who love Him stretches to *a thousand generations*—vastly outweighing judgment (20:6).

**"You shall not murder" refers specifically to unlawful killing, not every taking of life.** The Hebrew verb in verse 20:13 refers to unlawful homicide or killing an innocent person—which is why modern translations like the BSB render it "murder" rather than simply "kill." In the very next chapter, the Law of Moses itself prescribes capital punishment for certain grave crimes and draws a clear line between premeditated murder and accidental killing (21:12–14).

**Calling God "a jealous God" can easily be misunderstood by modern readers.** In the second commandment, the LORD describes Himself as "a jealous God" (20:5). This is not petty, selfish human jealousy, but the fierce devotion that guards a covenant marriage: God refuses to share His people's loyalty while they chase after rival gods.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the 4th–5th century bishop of Hippo, divided the Ten Commandments across two stone tablets: the first tablet holds the commands calling us to love God, and the second holds the commands calling us to love our neighbor. **Martin Luther**, the 16th-century German Reformer, insisted that the First Commandment is the bedrock supporting all nine that follow. In his famous explanation, *having a god* simply means whatever or whomever your heart leans on for all good and runs to in every trouble; once a person truly fears, loves, and trusts the living God above all else, obedience to the remaining commandments flows naturally from it. *(Large Catechism, on the First Commandment)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's famous definition of the First Commandment and placing one's trust in God appears at the very start of his exposition of the Ten Commandments in the Large Catechism -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, taught that every single commandment reaches far wider than its bare wording: whenever God forbids an evil, He simultaneously commands the opposite good. For instance, forbidding murder does not merely mean keeping your hands off a weapon; it calls us actively to protect human life and seek our neighbor's well-being. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book II, Chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Interpreting each commandment both negatively (what it forbids) and positively (what it requires) is Calvin's classic rule in Book II, Chapter 8 -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, reflected on God's opening words in verse 20:2, noting that the LORD does not merely assert His supreme authority as Sovereign, but points straight to His tenderest act of rescue.
