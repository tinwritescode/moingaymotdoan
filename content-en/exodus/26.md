# Exodus 26 — The Structure of the Tabernacle and the Dividing Veil

## Story

In chapter 25, God revealed to Moses the precious furnishings to be placed inside the tabernacle: the ark of the covenant, the table for the Bread of the Presence, and the golden lampstand. Now in chapter 26, He gives instructions for building the portable tent itself that will house those sacred pieces. The structure is engineered in neat modular parts so Israel can easily dismantle it, carry it across the wilderness, and raise it again at every camp (26:1–30).

The roof and walls of the tabernacle are covered in four stacked layers, moving from dazzling beauty within to rugged durability outside. The innermost layer consists of ten large curtains woven of finely spun linen with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, with cherubim skillfully worked into them; they are joined into two sets and linked together with gold clasps so that the tabernacle becomes a single unit. Over that goes a second layer of thicker goat-hair curtains joined with bronze clasps, hanging wider over both sides and the rear to shield the delicate fabric beneath. Topping everything are two weatherproof coverings to withstand sun and storm: a layer of ram skins dyed red, and on the very outside a tough covering of fine leather (26:1–14).

Supporting all four coverings is a sturdy yet detachable wooden framework. The south, north, and west walls consist of upright acacia wood frames overlaid with pure gold. At the bottom of each frame, two tenons slot snugly into heavy silver bases resting on the ground. Running horizontally along the outside of the frames are acacia crossbars overlaid with gold, threaded through gold rings; the center crossbar stretches continuously from one end to the other to lock the whole wall straight and rigid. Once again God reminds Moses, "So you are to set up the tabernacle according to the pattern shown you on the mountain" (26:15–30).

The interior of the tabernacle is split into two rooms by a thick veil hung on four gold-overlaid acacia posts standing in silver bases. Like the inner curtains, this veil is woven from finely spun linen with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn and worked with cherubim. Its purpose is stated unmistakably: "the veil will separate the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place." Behind the veil lies the Most Holy Place, housing only the ark of the Testimony with the mercy seat atop it. Outside the veil lies the Holy Place, with the table for the Bread of the Presence against the north wall and the golden lampstand directly opposite on the south. Finally, the east entrance of the tent is screened by another embroidered curtain hung on five gold-overlaid posts set in bronze bases (26:31–37).

## Key Takeaways

**The tabernacle is radiant on the inside yet plain on the outside.** Anyone passing through the desert sees only dark weather-beaten animal hides shielding the tent from sun and wind; step inside, however, and you enter a world of pure gold, fine linen, rich crimson and blue, and finely woven cherubim (26:1–14). The beauty of God's house is not put on display to impress casual onlookers, but reserved for those who enter His presence.

**The materials grow more precious the closer you move to God's dwelling.** The post bases at the entrance are bronze, the frame bases inside are silver, and the frames, crossbars, and innermost clasps are pure gold (26:6; 26:19; 26:37). Every step nearer to the Most Holy Place steps up in richness, reminding the people of the majesty of the One living in their midst.

**The veil both invites nearness and guards a boundary of holiness.** God chooses to pitch His tent right in the heart of Israel's camp, yet sinful human beings cannot simply barge into His presence. The veil embroidered with cherubim recalls the cherubim stationed at the entrance of Eden long ago (Genesis 3:24): it declares that God is right on the other side, yet the doorway back in still remains closed (26:31–33).

**The tabernacle must be joined together as a single unit.** The phrase *a unit* repeats deliberately in the instructions for linking the curtains and frames (26:6; 26:11). Scores of separate frames and panels, held firm by gold clasps and continuous crossbars, come together into one unified home for God.

**Humanity is not free to edit God's blueprint.** Verse 26:30 holds Moses strictly to the pattern revealed on the mountain. The tabernacle is far more than a clever feat of engineering; it is a scale model of a heavenly reality that no human mind could invent on its own.

## Deeper Meaning

**The tabernacle foreshadows Jesus Himself coming to dwell among humanity.** John's Gospel opens with an echo straight out of Exodus: the Word became flesh and *made His dwelling among us*, full of grace and truth (John 1:14). In the original Greek, the verb *made His dwelling* literally means *pitched His tabernacle* among us. Outwardly Jesus wore ordinary human flesh like the weathered hides covering the tent, yet within Him dwelt the fullness of God's glory.

**The letter to the Hebrews explains that the earthly tabernacle was a shadow of the true sanctuary in heaven.** Its author quotes God's warning to Moses to follow the mountain pattern in order to show that a tent of wood and linen was only a temporary copy (Hebrews 8:2–5). Christ serves as High Priest in the greater, more perfect tabernacle in heaven, not built by human hands (Hebrews 9:11).

**The veil shielding the Most Holy Place points to Christ's body torn on the cross.** Under the old covenant, the veil of verse 26:33 signaled that the way into the Most Holy Place was not yet open to everyone (Hebrews 9:8). At the very instant Jesus breathed His last on the cross, the temple curtain tore in two from top to bottom (Matthew 27:51). Hebrews makes the connection explicit: by the blood of Jesus, believers now enter the Most Holy Place with confidence through the new and living way He opened through the curtain—that is, His own body (Hebrews 10:19–20).

**The frames joined into one dwelling also picture the Church.** In the New Testament, God no longer lives in a linen tent or stone temple; instead, believers joined together in Christ are built into a holy temple, a dwelling in which God lives by His Spirit (Ephesians 2:20–22; 1 Corinthians 3:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Embroidered cherubim on the curtains do not break the second commandment.** The command in chapter 20 forbids carving images to bow down to and worship (20:4–5). The cherubim on the veil and on the mercy seat were never idols for people to venerate, but symbols of the heavenly attendants surrounding God's throne, evoking the atmosphere of heaven where God dwells.

**The wooden walls may have been solid planks or open lattice-like frames.** Traditional translations often render the Hebrew word in verse 26:15 as *boards* or *planks*, leading many readers to imagine heavy solid slabs of timber. Many modern scholars (followed by the BSB's *upright frames*) believe the term describes open rectangular frames shaped somewhat like ladders—both far lighter to haul across the wilderness and open enough to let the richly embroidered inner cherubim curtains show through.

**Do not over-allegorize every dye color or gold clasp.** Blue, purple, and scarlet yarns woven into fine linen were simply the finest luxury fabrics reserved for ancient royal palaces. Reading the big picture—that this is the mobile palace of the King of the universe camping with His people—is far healthier and truer to Scripture than inventing secret doctrines for fifty gold clasps or eleven goat-hair curtains.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, delights in the image of the tabernacle's four stacked coverings moving from outside to inside. He compares it to both Christ and the Church: from the world's outside vantage point they appear plain and unimpressive, like weather-worn skins enduring sun and wind, yet anyone who steps inside discovers all the beauty and glory God has placed within. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 26)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: comparing the plain exterior and glorious interior of the tabernacle to Christ and the Church is very typical of Matthew Henry on chapter 26; exact original wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, focuses on the veil separating the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place (26:31–33). In his view, God deliberately veiled the ark behind that curtain to instill holy reverence in Israel, while also teaching them that under the law full communion with God was not yet thrown wide open until Christ came and tore the curtain apart. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: seeing the veil as both guarding reverence and foreshadowing Christ opening access to the Most Holy Place fits Calvin's theology closely; exact location on chapter 26 not verified -->

**Gregory of Nyssa**, the 4th-century bishop in Asia Minor, reads the entire structure of the tabernacle as a portrait of the Church and the believer's life. He notes how the upright frames are bound firmly together by crossbars and gold rings: many distinct individuals united into one single dwelling place for God through shared love and faith. *(The Life of Moses, Book II)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Gregory of Nyssa interprets the pillars, frames, and curtains of the tabernacle spiritually in reference to the Church in Book II; exact detail assigned to the crossbars not verified -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, interprets the architecture of the tabernacle as both a *miniature Garden of Eden* and a *portable Mount Sinai*.
