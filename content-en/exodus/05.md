# Exodus 5 — Bricks Without Straw

## Story

Moses and Aaron go in to Pharaoh and deliver God's message: "This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: 'Let My people go, so that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness'" (5:1).

Pharaoh's reply drips with contempt: "Who is the LORD that I should obey His voice and let Israel go? I do not know the LORD, and I will not let Israel go" (5:2). That question is about to be answered over the chapters ahead, in ways Pharaoh cannot imagine.

The two brothers plead with him: the God of the Hebrews has met with us; please let us go a three-day journey into the wilderness to sacrifice to Him, or He may strike us with plagues or the sword. The king snaps back: Moses and Aaron, why are you distracting the people from their work? Get back to your labor! The people of the land are already numerous, and now you want to give them a break from their work (5:3–5).

That very same day, Pharaoh issues orders to the taskmasters and foremen: stop supplying the people with straw for making bricks; let them go gather straw for themselves. Yet keep their daily brick quota exactly the same—do not reduce it by a single brick. They are lazy, which is why they keep crying out to go sacrifice to their God. Make the work harder on the men so they stay busy and pay no attention to these lies (5:6–9).

The order goes out. The Israelites scatter across the entire land of Egypt searching for stubble to use as straw, while the taskmasters hound them to deliver the same daily quota as before (5:10–13).

The Israelite foremen appointed by Pharaoh's taskmasters are beaten and interrogated: why haven't you met your brick quota yesterday or today as you did before? (5:14).

The Israelite foremen go straight to Pharaoh to appeal: why are you treating your servants this way? No straw is given to us, yet we are told to make bricks—and your servants are being beaten when the fault lies with your own people! "You are slackers!" Pharaoh shoots back. "Slackers! That is why you keep saying, 'Let us go and sacrifice to the LORD.' Now get to work! You will get no straw, yet you must deliver the full quota of bricks" (5:15–18).

The Israelite foremen realize they are in deep trouble. Leaving Pharaoh's presence, they run straight into Moses and Aaron waiting outside and lash out: "May the LORD look upon you and judge you! You have made us a stench before Pharaoh and his officials; you have placed in their hand a sword to kill us!" (5:19–21).

Moses turns back to the LORD and pours out his anguish: "Lord, why have You brought trouble upon this people? Is this why You sent me? Ever since I went to Pharaoh to speak in Your name, he has brought trouble on this people, and You have not delivered Your people in any way" (5:22–23). His very first act of obedience has made everything worse.

## Key Takeaways

**The real showdown comes down to one question: who is Lord?** When Moses and Aaron ask to let Israel go worship, Pharaoh fires back arrogantly, "Who is the LORD that I should obey His voice?" (5:2). The entire sequence of the ten plagues that follows is God's step-by-step answer to that boastful challenge.

**Oppressive power responds by tightening the screws.** Pharaoh dismisses the longing to worship God as laziness, cuts off the straw, and refuses to lower the daily quota (5:7–9; 5:17). Tyranny always fears giving the oppressed space to breathe and listen to a voice higher than the state.

**Obeying God sometimes makes life harder before it gets better.** The moment Moses and Aaron step out in obedience, no miracles happen; instead, the people are beaten harder and their own leaders curse them (5:14; 5:20–21). The road to freedom rarely runs smoothly without fierce pushback.

**Bring your raw disappointment straight to God.** Moses does not hide his confusion or put on a brave pious mask. He carries his sharpest, most painful questions right into God's presence: why did You send me if it only brought harm, and why haven't You rescued them? (5:22–23). Honest lament before God is always better than quietly walking away.

## Deeper Meaning

**Liberation is not merely escape from slavery, but freedom to worship God.** God's demand is never just "Let My people go"—it always carries a clear purpose: so they may hold a feast to Him in the wilderness (5:1; 5:3). Biblical freedom is not belonging to no one; it is being freed from a cruel master so we can serve our Creator (Romans 6:17–18).

**Tyranny turns victims against one another.** Egyptian taskmasters install Israelite foremen over their own people and then beat those foremen when the quota falls short, driving them to blame Moses instead of their oppressor (5:14; 5:21). It is an ancient playbook of dark power: divide God's people from the inside.

**The darkest hour comes right before the dawn.** Moses's utter helplessness at the close of chapter 5 sets the stage for God's mighty intervention beginning in chapter 6. When human effort hits a dead end, all the glory of deliverance belongs undeniably to God (2 Corinthians 1:8–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Moses ask only for a *three-day journey* into the wilderness (5:3) instead of permanent release?** In ancient Near Eastern diplomacy, starting with a modest request could expose a ruler's true posture: if Pharaoh refused even a brief three-day religious pilgrimage, his hard-hearted tyranny would be unmistakable. It may also have pointed to Mount Sinai as their first destination before continuing on to Canaan.

**Why did brickmakers need straw?** Mixing chopped straw into Nile clay kept sun-dried mudbricks bound together so they would not crack as they dried. Without supplied straw, the Israelites had to roam the countryside gathering dry harvest stubble—doubling their workload while the daily quota stayed untouched (5:12–13).

**What does *"made us a stench"* (5:21) mean?** It is a vivid Hebrew idiom (see Genesis 34:30) meaning you have made us despised, suspect, and in mortal danger in Pharaoh's eyes.

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 5 as a clash between two rival systems: Pharaoh's relentless economy of endless production with zero rest, and the LORD's call to set slaves free for rest and worship.
