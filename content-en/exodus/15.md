# Exodus 15 — The Song by the Sea and the Bitter Water of Marah

## Story

Standing on the far shore of the Red Sea and looking back at what just happened, Moses and the Israelites burst into a soaring song of praise to the LORD. It opens with ringing triumph: "I will sing to the LORD, for He is highly exalted. The horse and rider He has thrown into the sea. The LORD is my strength and my song, and He has become my salvation" (15:1–2).

The song replays the night's victory in vivid strokes: the LORD is a mighty warrior who hurled Pharaoh's chariots and elite officers into the Red Sea, sending them plunging to the bottom like a stone, sinking like lead. The enemy had boasted of chasing them down, drawing the sword, and dividing the plunder, yet one blast of God's breath piled the waters up like a wall and brought them crashing back down to bury them. Among all the gods, none is like the LORD—majestic in holiness, awesome in praises, working wonders (15:3–12).

The second half of the song turns its gaze toward the future. In steadfast love, the LORD will guide the people He has redeemed all the way to His holy dwelling. News of this triumph will send dread through every surrounding nation—Philistia, Edom, Moab, and Canaan—leaving them trembling and frozen like stone until God's people pass safely by. God will plant Israel on the mountain of His inheritance, and the song closes with an everlasting declaration: "The LORD will reign forever and ever!" (15:13–18).

After a brief narrative recap reminding us that Egypt's army was swallowed by the sea while Israel walked across on dry ground, the prophetess Miriam—sister of Moses and Aaron—steps out with a tambourine in her hand. All the women of Israel follow her with tambourines and dancing as she leads them in the refrain: "Sing to the LORD, for He is highly exalted; the horse and rider He has thrown into the sea" (15:19–21).

Yet the echoes of singing have barely died away before the harsh reality of the wilderness hits. Moses leads Israel away from the Red Sea into the Desert of Shur. For three straight days they hike under sun and wind without finding a drop of water. When they finally reach Marah, hope surges—only to collapse when they taste the water and find it too bitter to drink, which is why the place was named Marah, meaning *bitter*. Immediately the people turn on Moses, grumbling, "What are we to drink?" (15:22–24).

Moses cries out to the LORD. God shows him a piece of wood—a log; Moses throws it into the water, and at once the bitter water turns sweet. Right there the LORD gives them a statute and tests them, promising that if they listen carefully to His voice, do what is right, and keep His commands, He will never inflict on them any of the diseases He brought on Egypt, "For I am the LORD who heals you." From there they march on to Elim, a lush oasis with twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees, and they set up camp beside the water (15:25–27).

## Key Takeaways

**Worship is the instinctive response to being rescued.** Before Israel ever receives the Law or builds the Tabernacle, they have a song. Men and women alike, Moses and Miriam together, lift their voices, strike tambourines, and dance (15:1; 15:20–21). Living faith never stays locked inside the head; it spills out in song before God's grace.

**It takes only three days for singing to turn into grumbling.** On the shore of the Red Sea they sang praise at the top of their lungs, yet just three thirsty days into the Desert of Shur they are already complaining against Moses (15:22–24). How quickly human hearts forget mercy; the first test of freedom was not a charging army, but ordinary thirst in the desert.

**God does not erase the wilderness, but He turns bitter into sweet.** God could easily have steered Israel straight to the oasis of Elim, yet He led them through Marah first to test their hearts and teach them trust (15:25). When Moses cries out, God does not drop rain from the sky; He points to a log that transforms the very bitter water in front of them into something sweet.

**God is the Healer.** In Egypt, the LORD revealed Himself as Judge through ten devastating plagues. At Marah, He introduces Himself with a tender name: "the LORD who heals you" (15:26). Obeying Him is not a crushing chore, but the pathway to wholeness and well-being.

**After Marah comes Elim.** Twelve springs for twelve tribes, and seventy palm trees spreading cool shade (15:27). God knows how much His people can take; He does not lead them only through parched tests, but also prepares timely oases of rest along the road.

## Deeper Meaning

**The Song of Moses rings out again in heaven in Revelation.** Near the end of the Bible, those who have conquered the beast stand beside a sea of glass and sing "the song of God's servant Moses and of the Lamb" (Revelation 15:3–4). That first victory hymn on the banks of the Red Sea becomes the pattern for the final triumph song of all God's people once evil is defeated forever.

**Verse 15:2 becomes a confession echoed across the Old Testament.** The lyric "The LORD is my strength and my song, and He has become my salvation" (15:2) reappears almost word for word in the Psalms (Psalms 118:14) and in the prophet Isaiah (Isaiah 12:2). Century after century, whenever God rescued His people, they reached back for that very first melody from the Red Sea shore.

**The Healer revealed in Jesus.** God's self-disclosure at Marah as the LORD who heals (15:26) comes alive most vividly when Jesus walks the earth, healing every sickness and affliction among the crowds—restoring not only broken bodies, but hearts wounded and embittered by sin (Matthew 8:16–17).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was Miriam Aaron and Moses' older sister or younger sister?** Verse 15:20 simply calls Miriam "Aaron's sister," since Hebrew uses a single word for both older and younger sisters. Looking back to chapter 2, when newborn Moses floated in a basket on the Nile, his sister was already old enough to keep watch and negotiate shrewdly with Pharaoh's daughter (2:4–8). So Miriam was older than Moses, and by tradition she was the eldest of all three siblings. She is also the first woman in the Bible to be given the title *prophetess*.

**The Song of the Sea is one of the oldest poems in Scripture.** Many Hebrew scholars point out that the vocabulary, imagery, and rhythm of this song (15:1–18)—especially Miriam's crisp refrain (15:21)—preserve remarkably archaic grammatical forms. This suggests Israel passed this hymn down from the very earliest days after the crossing.

**Does calling the LORD "a warrior" (15:3) glorify violence?** Calling God a warrior here is not an endorsement of aggression or conquest. In chapter 14, Israel never even drew a sword; God stepped forward alone to stop Pharaoh's oppressive army and shield defenseless slaves. In Scripture, the warrior image first and foremost speaks of a God who stands up for the oppressed when they cannot save themselves.

**Did the wood thrown into the water at Marah have natural purifying properties?** Some travelers in the Sinai Peninsula have looked for native shrubs whose sap or bark might sweeten brackish water. Yet the text names no species at all; it says simply that "the LORD showed him a log" (15:25). The point of the story lies not in chemistry hidden inside a branch, but in God's power to turn bitterness sweet when His people call on Him.

## What Commentators Say

**Gregory of Nyssa**, the 4th-century bishop in Asia Minor, saw in the wood cast into the bitter waters of Marah (15:25) a symbol of Christ's cross. **Matthew Henry**, the 17th–18th century English pastor, drew out the vivid contrast between Marah and Elim (15:23–27). Life for God's people on earth, he noted, always alternates between bitter stops like Marah and refreshing oases like Elim: at Marah we should never despair because God can sweeten the water, and at Elim we must remember it is only a temporary camp on the road home, not our permanent country. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry certainly draws the lesson about life alternating between Marah's trials and Elim's consolations at the end of chapter 15; exact wording not yet verified -->

**Brevard Childs**, Old Testament professor at Yale University, highlighted the structural role of the Song of the Sea: it does not merely celebrate past deliverance in verses 15:1–12, but sweeps forward from the Red Sea all the way to Sinai and Canaan in verses 15:13–18.
