# Exodus 21 — Laws for Servants and Protecting Human Life

## Story

After giving the Ten Commandments, God hands down to Moses a series of concrete laws to bring those commandments into Israel's everyday life. Scholars often call chapters 21 through 23 the *Book of the Covenant* (24:7). It opens with laws concerning Hebrew servants—poor people who had to sell their labor to pay off debts (21:1–2).

A male Hebrew servant is to work for his master for six years; in the seventh year, he goes free without paying anything. If he entered service alone, he leaves alone; if he already had a wife, his wife leaves with him. If the master gave him a wife during his years of service, the woman and her children still belong to the master. Yet if the servant loves his master, his wife, and his children and chooses freely to stay, the master brings him before God, stands him against the door or doorpost, and pierces his ear with an awl as a mark; from then on, he belongs to the master's household for life (21:2–6).

Next come laws protecting a daughter sold by her father as a maidservant—a form of arranged marriage for poor families in the ancient world. If the master intended her for himself and then changed his mind, he must allow her family to redeem her; he has no right to sell her to foreigners. If he chooses her for his son, he must treat her like his own daughter. If he takes another wife, he may not cut back the food, clothing, or marital rights of the first. If he fails to provide these three things, she goes free without paying any money (21:7–11).

The next section deals with murder and violence. Anyone who deliberately strikes and kills another person must be put to death. But if the killing was accidental and unpremeditated, God will appoint a place of refuge where the person who acted unintentionally can flee while awaiting trial. By contrast, someone who schemes to murder his neighbor must be dragged away and executed even if he clings to God's altar. Three other grave crimes carry the death penalty: striking one's father or mother, kidnapping someone to sell into slavery, and cursing one's father or mother (21:12–17).

If two people fight and one leaves the other bedridden, yet the injured person later gets up and walks outside with a staff, the attacker does not face a murder charge but must compensate the victim for lost work and see that he is completely healed. A master who beats a servant to death on the spot must be punished; if the servant survives a day or two before dying, the master does not receive the death penalty, since the servant's labor is property the master paid for. Yet if a master strikes a servant and blinds an eye or knocks out a tooth, he must let the servant go free immediately (21:18–21; 21:26–27).

In the middle of these rulings sits a case where two men fighting accidentally strike a pregnant woman. If no further serious injury occurs, the offender must pay a fine demanded by the husband and approved by the court. If serious injury follows, the law sets the principle of proportionate compensation: "a life for a life—eye for eye, tooth for tooth," extending to hand, foot, burn, and wound (21:22–25).

The chapter closes with laws on liability for dangerous livestock and hazardous property. An ox that gores someone to death must be stoned and its meat may not be eaten, while the owner is not held guilty. But if the ox already had a habit of goring, and its owner had been warned yet still failed to restrain it, both the ox and its owner face death—though the victim's family may allow the owner to pay a ransom to redeem his life. The exact same rule applies if a son or daughter is gored; if the victim is a servant, the ox's owner pays thirty shekels of silver to the servant's master and the ox is stoned. Anyone who digs a pit and leaves it uncovered so another person's ox or donkey falls in and dies must make restitution. If one man's ox kills his neighbor's ox, they sell the live ox and split both the proceeds and the dead animal; if the owner knew his ox had a habit of goring and failed to pen it up, he must repay a live ox for the dead one (21:28–36).

## Key Takeaways

**God's law begins by protecting the most vulnerable.** Remarkably, right after the Ten Commandments, the very first civil statute is not about kings or landowners, but about the Hebrew servant (21:2). A nation that once lived as slaves in Egypt is forbidden from turning its own struggling neighbors into lifelong slaves; service is capped at six years.

**Human life is worth more than any property or status.** The law draws a sharp line between intentional murder and accidental killing, shielding the unintentional offender with a place of refuge while refusing mercy to the scheming killer even at the altar (21:12–14). Even an animal that gores a person to death is dealt with severely, driving home that nothing may cheapen human life (21:28–29).

**Masters themselves are bound by accountability and real limits.** In the ancient world, slave owners routinely held absolute power of life and death over their servants. Here the law draws clear boundaries: beating a servant to death brings punishment, and destroying a single eye or tooth costs the master all authority and sets the servant free at once (21:20; 21:26–27). A servant is a human being with dignity, not an object for a master's rage.

**The principle of "eye for eye" was designed to curb runaway revenge.** Verses 21:23–25 sound harsh to modern ears, yet in an ancient world where a single slap could ignite a blood feud between entire clans, equal retaliation served as a fence restraining violence: punishment could never exceed the actual harm, and justice belonged in the hands of judges rather than private vendettas.

**Carelessness that harms others is still moral guilt.** You do not have to swing a club on purpose to be at fault; knowing an ox is dangerous and leaving it loose, or digging a pit along a path without covering it, makes you answerable to the community (21:29; 21:33–34). Living together as God's people means actively guarding your neighbor's safety.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus calls His followers to rise above the courtroom's minimum standard of justice.** In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus quotes the rule of *eye for eye, tooth for tooth* to warn believers not to twist a judge's courtroom guideline into an excuse for personal revenge (Matthew 5:38–42). He teaches His disciples to meet insult with generosity and forgiveness rather than insisting on settling every score.

**The servant's price of thirty shekels of silver echoes in the betrayal of Jesus.** In verse 21:32, the compensation for a servant gored to death by an ox is thirty shekels of silver. Centuries later, the prophet Zechariah speaks of thirty pieces of silver as the insulting price placed on God's shepherd (Zechariah 11:12–13), and Judas betrayed Jesus for that exact sum of thirty pieces of silver (Matthew 26:15). The Son of God willingly accepted the price of a servant to redeem humanity.

**The servant who voluntarily has his ear pierced recalls Christ's lifelong obedience.** Psalm 40:6 speaks of one whose ears God has opened to listen and delight in doing His will; the letter to the Hebrews quotes those words and places them on the lips of Christ as He enters the world to offer Himself as a sacrifice (Hebrews 10:5–7). Christ serves not out of compulsion, but out of love for the Father and for His people.

**The place of refuge for unintentional killing paves the way for the cities of refuge.** The promise in verse 21:13 is later fleshed out in Numbers 35 and Joshua 20: six cities spread across the land where someone who accidentally took a life could flee from blind vengeance. The letter to the Hebrews borrows that picture of fleeing to a place of refuge to describe believers taking hold of God's unshakeable promise (Hebrews 6:18).

## Notes & Nuances

**Scripture does not promote slavery; it imposes humane limits inside an ancient society.** The servitude described here was very different from modern chattel slavery: most servants were people in debt selling their labor for a fixed six-year term to pay what they owed, while kidnapping someone to sell into slavery carried an immediate death sentence (21:16). Mosaic law did not abolish the ancient economic structure overnight, yet it planted protections for human dignity unmatched in surrounding law codes.

**Verses 21:20–21 on beating a servant remain troubling to modern readers.** The law does not give a master permission to kill a servant, but distinguishes between death on the spot (indicating intent to kill) and death after a day or two (treated as unintended, with the master already bearing the financial loss). Commentators recognize this as early legislation adapted to an ancient setting, long before the New Testament calls masters and servants to treat one another as beloved brothers in Christ (Philemon 1:15–16).

**Verse 21:22 about the pregnant woman is understood in two different ways.** The Hebrew wording can be rendered either as a *miscarriage* (as many older translations take it) or as *giving birth prematurely* (as the BSB translates it). On the second reading, if mother and child both come through unharmed, only a fine applies, while serious injury to either invokes a life for a life; on the first reading, losing the unborn child brings a fine while severe injury to the mother invokes the stricter penalty. Under either view, the law places special protection around a pregnant woman and the life she carries.

**Verses 21:15 and 21:17 on the death penalty for striking or cursing parents address grave rebellion by adults.** This is not talking about a young child talking back in a moment of temper, but a grown child violently assaulting or repudiating parents—undermining the very foundation of family and community order. In Israel's actual legal practice, such cases had to be thoroughly tried before the town elders; parents had no right to execute a child on their own (Deuteronomy 21:18–21).

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Geneva Reformer, explains the law of *eye for eye, tooth for tooth* as a rule for impartial judges rather than a license for private vengeance. **Matthew Henry**, the English minister who died in 1714, draws attention to the fact that God places laws protecting servants at the very opening of the civil code. He observes that God shows no partiality toward rank or wealth; He cares as deeply for the rights of a poor laborer as for a wealthy master, and reminds Israel of their own past slavery so they will practice mercy. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 21)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting that the civil laws open with servants is very characteristic of Matthew Henry on the start of chapter 21; original wording not verified -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, compares the laws of Exodus 21 with the ancient Babylonian Code of Hammurabi. He highlights a striking contrast: under Hammurabi, a nobleman who destroys the eye of a commoner or slave simply pays a fine, whereas under biblical law, a master who blinds an eye or knocks out a single tooth of his servant must set that servant free at once. Biblical law thus places the worth of a human body above financial profit. *(Exploring Exodus)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: comparing the goring-ox and servant laws of Exodus 21 with the Code of Hammurabi is a hallmark of Sarna's work; unsure whether this appears in Exploring Exodus or the JPS Torah Commentary -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the celebrated 19th-century London preacher, preached on the servant who voluntarily has his ear pierced in verses 21:5–6 as a vivid portrait of love for the Lord.
