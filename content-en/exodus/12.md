# Exodus 12 — The Passover and the Night Out of Egypt

## Story

Before the night of judgment falls, the LORD gives Moses and Aaron a decree that resets Israel's entire sense of time: this month is to become the first month of their year. On the tenth day of the month, every household must choose a year-old male lamb or young goat without blemish. Any family too small to eat a whole animal is to share one with their nearest neighbor. They must keep the animal until twilight on the fourteenth day, when the whole community slaughters it (12:1–6).

They are to take some of the blood and brush it onto the two side posts and the top beam of the doorframe of the house where they eat the meal. That night they must eat the meat roasted whole over fire—never raw, never boiled in water—alongside unleavened bread and bitter herbs. Nothing may be left until morning; anything remaining must be burned. Even the way they eat matters: "fully dressed for travel, with your sandals on your feet and your staff in your hand. You are to eat in haste; it is the LORD's Passover" (12:7–11).

God spells out what will happen that night: He will pass through Egypt, strike down every firstborn of both people and livestock, and execute judgment against all the gods of Egypt. Yet the blood on the doorframes will serve as a sign; when the LORD sees the blood, He will pass over them, and no destructive plague will touch their homes. That day is to be remembered forever, followed by a seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread. For all seven days no leaven may remain anywhere in their houses, with sacred assemblies and rest from labor on the first and seventh days. Anyone who eats leavened bread must be cut off from the community of Israel (12:12–20).

Moses summons the elders of Israel and passes along the exact instructions: dip a cluster of hyssop into the blood in the basin, brush it across the top and sides of the doorframe, and let no one step outside the house until morning. Seeing the blood, the LORD will pass over the doorway and keep the destroyer from entering. Moses adds that later, once they settle in the promised land and their children ask, "What does this service mean to you?", parents must tell the story of how the LORD passed over the Israelite homes and spared their families. Hearing this, the people bow down and worship, then go and do everything just as God commanded (12:21–28).

At midnight, the unthinkable happens. The LORD strikes down every firstborn male across Egypt, from Pharaoh's heir on the throne to the firstborn of the prisoner in the dungeon, as well as the firstborn of all the livestock. Pharaoh, his officials, and every Egyptian bolt awake in the dark, and loud wailing erupts across the country, "for there was no house without someone dead" (12:29–30).

That very night Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron and surrenders completely: get up, take the Israelites, go worship the LORD, and take all your flocks and herds just as you asked—then he blurts out an astonishing plea: "And bless me also." The Egyptians press Israel to leave at once, terrified that otherwise they will all die. The Israelites quickly wrap their kneading bowls of unleavened dough in their cloaks and hoist them onto their shoulders, and they ask the Egyptians for articles of silver, gold, and clothing. The LORD grants them favor, so the Egyptians gladly hand everything over (12:31–36).

Israel sets out from Rameses toward Succoth—about six hundred thousand men on foot, not counting women and children—accompanied by a large mixed multitude of non-Israelites and huge droves of livestock. Because they were rushed out so suddenly without time to prepare provisions, they bake unleavened loaves from the dough they brought along. Israel's stay in Egypt lasted four hundred and thirty years to the very day; as soon as those years were complete, all the LORD's divisions marched out of the land. That night becomes a sacred vigil kept for the LORD through every generation to come (12:37–42).

At the close of the chapter, God gives regulations on who may eat the Passover meal. No outsider or hired hand may eat it, yet any purchased slave or resident foreigner who undergoes circumcision is welcomed to celebrate it on completely equal footing with the native-born. The lamb must be eaten inside one house, no meat may be carried outdoors, and "you may not break any of the bones." The whole assembly obeys every instruction, and on that very day the LORD brings His people out of Egypt—opening the next leg of their journey in chapter 13 (12:43–51).

## Key Takeaways

**Redemption starts life over from day one.** God declares that this month will be the beginning of Israel's year (12:2). They are not merely changing addresses; they are receiving a brand-new history. From now on, their calendar no longer revolves around Pharaoh's reign, but around the day God rescued them.

**Even Israel needed the mark of the blood to be spared.** The final plague did not automatically skip Israelite homes simply because they were descendants of Abraham. They too had to slaughter an unblemished lamb, apply its blood to their doorframes, and stay inside (12:7; 12:22). Salvation comes through grace and substitution, received through trusting obedience.

**Eat ready to move.** Belt fastened, sandals strapped on, staff in hand, eating in haste (12:11)—this was no leisurely feast meant for settling comfortably into slavery, but a meal for people poised to walk out the moment God calls. Trusting God's promise of rescue shows up in packing your bags while it is still dark.

**Storytelling keeps faith alive across generations.** Before Israel even sets foot outside Egypt, God is already thinking of children not yet born who will sit around the table and ask, "What does this service mean to you?" (12:26). Passover turns the history of salvation into a meal you can taste—the sting of bitter herbs, the plainness of unleavened bread, the smell of roasted lamb—so every generation experiences that rescue as their own.

**The door of salvation stands open to outsiders.** A mixed multitude of foreigners walked out of Egypt alongside Israel (12:38), and the law closing this chapter makes clear that any foreigner who enters the covenant shares Passover on equal terms with the native-born (12:48–49). From the very beginning, God's people were never meant to be a closed club based on bloodline alone.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus is the Passover Lamb for the whole world.** This is the deepest thread linking the Old Testament to the New. When John the Baptist sees Jesus approaching, he points and says, "Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). The apostle Paul puts it directly: "For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7). And Peter reminds believers that we were redeemed "with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Peter 1:18–19).

**Not one of His bones was broken.** A detail that might look minor in verse 12:46—never breaking any bone of the Passover lamb—is quoted directly by John's Gospel at the foot of the cross. When Roman soldiers broke the legs of the two criminals crucified beside Jesus, they saw that Jesus was already dead and left His legs unbroken, fulfilling Scripture: "Not one of His bones will be broken" (John 19:36).

**The Lord's Supper was born at the Passover table.** On the night before His death, Jesus sat down with His disciples to celebrate Passover. Taking the unleavened bread and the cup from that thousand-year-old meal, He filled them with their fullest meaning: His body given and His blood of the new covenant poured out for many (Luke 22:15; 22:19–20).

**Unleavened bread becomes a picture of a cleansed life.** When Paul urges the church in Corinth to sweep out old sin, he reaches straight for the seven-day ban on leaven in chapter 12: clear out the old leaven of malice and wickedness so you can live with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth (1 Corinthians 5:7–8).

**Moses' faith on the night of the blood.** The letter to the Hebrews lists Moses' keeping of the Passover and applying the blood as a landmark act of faith (Hebrews 11:28). Painting blood across a doorway in the middle of a national crisis took real trust that God's word was stronger than every visible power around them.

## Notes & Nuances

**The figure of "about 600,000 men on foot" (12:37)—roughly two million people.** Adding women and children to six hundred thousand adult men yields a population of around two million. Bible scholars debate this number lively: how could a crowd that vast cross the Sinai desert and survive forty years, and could the Hebrew word translated *thousand* (*eleph*) also mean *clan* or *military unit* (making six hundred units, or tens of thousands of people)? Others read the number literally, seeing the sheer scale as underscoring the miracle of God sustaining them through the wilderness. Either way, the core point stands clear: from the seventy members of Jacob's household who entered Egypt, God multiplied them into a great nation just as He promised Abraham.

**The 430 years in verse 12:40 and the 400 years in Genesis 15:13.** The four hundred years God mentioned to Abraham is a round number, whereas four hundred and thirty here gives a more exact count. Notably, the ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) adds the phrase *and in the land of Canaan* to verse 12:40, counting the full 430 years from Abraham's arrival in Canaan to the Exodus—a reckoning that also matches Paul's statement in Galatians (Galatians 3:17). The two calculations simply differ in their starting point and do not contradict the passage's message.

**The meaning of *Passover*.** The Hebrew word *Pesach* carries both the sense of *passing over* (sparing a house from judgment) and a tender nuance of *hovering over to protect*, like a mother bird shielding her nest (cf. Isaiah 31:5). Both meanings weave together beautifully: that night the LORD did not merely skip past Israel's homes, He stood guard at their thresholds so the destroyer could not enter (12:23).

**Bitter herbs and lamb roasted whole.** The bitter herbs reminded Israel of the bitter years of forced labor in Egypt (1:14). Roasting the lamb whole over fire—without chopping it up or boiling it elaborately—both allowed a quick meal on an urgent night and preserved the picture of a family gathered around one undivided sacrifice.

**Melito of Sardis**, a 2nd-century bishop in Asia Minor, wrote one of Christianity's oldest surviving Easter sermons directly on chapter 12. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, emphasized that the lamb's blood on the doorframe possessed no magical power of its own to ward off the destroying angel. Its effectiveness came entirely from God's promise and command. When the Israelites dipped hyssop into the blood, brushed their doorframes, and stayed safely indoors, they were resting their faith in God's word rather than in an outward ritual—and that sign pointed straight to the blood of Christ. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin certainly explains sacraments and the sign of the blood as effective through God's promise and faith rather than any inherent magic; exact wording on chapter 12 not yet verified -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the 19th-century London preacher, delivered a beloved sermon on verse 12:13: "when I see the blood, I will pass over you." Comforting believers anxious about how weak their faith felt, he pointed out that God did not say *when you see the blood*, but *when I see the blood*. People shut safely inside their house could not even see the blood outside on the doorframe, yet God outside saw it unmistakably—a believer's security rests on God's view of Christ's sacrifice, not on our shifting emotions. *(New Park Street Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's sermon The Blood on Exodus 12 verse 13, emphasizing that God sees the blood rather than our shifting feelings, is one of his most famous sermons -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, highlighted how bold the Passover ritual was in its ancient setting: in Egypt, the ram was a sacred animal linked with the major deity Amun. For every Israelite household to openly set aside a male lamb four days in advance, slaughter it at twilight, and smear its blood right on the front doorframe was a public act of defiance against Egypt's idols—fulfilling God's declaration in verse 12:12 that He would execute judgment on all Egypt's gods. *(Exploring Exodus, 1986)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Recalled that Sarna and Jewish tradition explain the open slaughter of the lamb and marking of the doorframes as a repudiation of Egyptian idolatry; not certain whether he names Amun specifically in this book -->
