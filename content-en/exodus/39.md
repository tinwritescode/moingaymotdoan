# Exodus 39 — The Priestly Garments and the Finished Work

## Story

The tabernacle and all its furnishings are finished; now come the sacred garments for the men who will minister inside. Using blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, the artisans weave the holy garments for Aaron the high priest. First is the ephod—a richly worked vest with two shoulder pieces and a woven waistband. To blend gold right into the cloth, craftsmen hammer gold into paper-thin sheets and slice them into delicate threads (39:1–5).

On the two shoulder pieces, they set two onyx stones inside gold filigree mounts, engraved like signet seals with the names of Israel's tribes. These serve as "memorial stones": every time Aaron stands before God, he carries the names of the entire nation across his shoulders (39:6–7; cf. 28:12).

Next comes the breastpiece, folded double into a square measuring one span on each side. Mounted across its front sit twelve precious gemstones arranged in four rows of three, each stone engraved with the name of one of Israel's tribes (39:8–14). Braided gold chains, gold rings, and a blue cord anchor the breastpiece snugly to the ephod so it "would not swing out from the ephod" (39:15–21).

Beneath the ephod goes a full robe woven entirely of blue cloth, with a reinforced collar around the neck opening so it will not tear. All around the bottom hem, the artisans attach woven pomegranates of blue, purple, and scarlet alternating with bells of pure gold—a pomegranate, then a bell, circling the entire hem (39:22–26). God had earlier instructed that the ringing of those bells must be heard whenever Aaron enters or leaves the sanctuary (28:35).

They also weave fine linen tunics, a turban, ornate headbands, linen undergarments, and an embroidered sash for Aaron and his sons (39:27–29). Last of all, they fashion a plate of pure gold, engrave it with the words "HOLY TO THE LORD," and fasten it with a blue cord to the front of Aaron's turban (39:30–31).

At that moment Scripture declares: "So all the work for the tabernacle, the Tent of Meeting, was completed" (39:32). The Israelites carry every single piece to Moses for review—the tent and all its fittings, the coverings and curtains, the ark of the Testimony, the table, the lampstand, both altars, the washing basin, the courtyard hangings, and the sacred priestly garments (39:33–41).

Moses carefully inspects everything they have made, sees that they have accomplished it all just as the LORD commanded, and blesses them (39:42–43).

## Key Takeaways

**"Just as the LORD had commanded Moses."** That refrain chimes seven times across the garment section like a steady chorus closing each completed piece (39:1; 39:5; 39:7; 39:21; 39:26; 39:29; 39:31). Just a few chapters back, the people took matters into their own hands and forged a golden calf (32:4). Now they craft every single item strictly according to God's word. This chapter captures a people who have learned obedience.

**The priest carries the people on his shoulders and over his heart.** The names of the twelve tribes appear twice: once across Aaron's shoulders and once over his chest. Shoulders speak of strength and bearing burdens; the heart speaks of love and care. God's servant never walks into God's presence alone—he carries the entire people right along with him (28:29).

**Holy to the LORD, worn openly on the forehead.** The final—and most visible—piece is that gleaming gold plate across Aaron's turban. Anyone meeting Aaron reads those words first. Every person serving God ought to wear an unmistakable badge of identity: this life belongs wholly to the LORD.

**God cares about beauty.** God ordered these garments made to give Aaron "glory and splendor" (28:2). Gold thread, jewels, richly dyed yarn, woven pomegranates, tiny bells—none of it was tossed together casually. True worship does not require extravagance, yet it never looks down on beauty or careful craft.

**Careful inspection followed by blessing.** Moses did not just glance at the pile and sign off. He examined every piece closely, and only then spoke a blessing over the workers. Diligent work deserves to be seen, and good leaders know how to bless the people who poured themselves into it.

## Deeper Meaning

**A new week of creation.** Set the end of chapter 39 next to Genesis 1–2, and you hear the exact same cadence. There, "the heavens and the earth were completed" (Genesis 2:1); here, "all the work for the tabernacle... was completed" (39:32). There, God looked upon all he had made and saw that it was very good (Genesis 1:31); here, Moses inspects every piece and sees that they have done it faithfully. There, God blessed the seventh day (Genesis 2:3); here, Moses blesses the people (39:43). Many scholars see deliberate artistry in this parallel: finishing the tabernacle is like building a miniature world where God once again lives among humanity.

**Our great high priest.** The letter to the Hebrews calls Jesus "a great high priest" (Hebrews 4:14). Just as Aaron carried Israel's names on his shoulders and over his heart, Jesus carries his people continually before God, because he "always lives to intercede" for them (Hebrews 7:25).

**From one priest's forehead to the bells on everyday horses.** Here, only one man—the high priest—wears "HOLY TO THE LORD" across his brow. Yet the prophet Zechariah foresees a coming day when those very words will be engraved even on the bells of horses, and ordinary cooking pots will be as sacred as the bowls before the altar (Zechariah 14:20–21). On the closing page of Scripture, God's name shines on the forehead of every single servant who belongs to him (Revelation 22:4). Holiness will no longer be fenced into one corner of life.

**The whole nation called to be priests.** Right before giving the law at Sinai, God promised that Israel would become "a kingdom of priests" (19:6). Peter picks up that exact title for the Church, calling believers "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9). Aaron's radiant garments offer a glimpse of what God longs to do for all who belong to him: clothing them in splendor, setting them apart, and drawing them close.

**God clothes humanity himself.** The very first time God made clothing for human beings was in Eden after their fall (Genesis 3:21). Now he provides garments for the priest so he can stand safely in his presence. The New Testament tells believers that they have "clothed yourselves with Christ" (Galatians 3:27). Nobody can stitch together a robe worthy of standing before God; it has to be given by God himself.

## Notes & Nuances

**The exact identities of the twelve gemstones are uncertain.** Today no one knows for certain which modern minerals match all twelve ancient Hebrew stone names on the breastpiece. English translations vary noticeably, and modern stone names remain educated estimates. It is unwise to build elaborate spiritual theories on the name of any individual stone.

**The bells were not a magical charm.** Exodus 28:35 says Aaron's bells must be heard when he enters and exits "so that he will not die." That does not mean the bells held superstitious protective power. The real point is that Aaron had to enter holy space in the exact manner God prescribed, wearing the full garments God appointed—approaching God on God's terms, never casually on his own.

**Why are the Urim and Thummim not mentioned here?** Back in chapter 28, God instructed Moses to place the Urim and Thummim inside the breastpiece (28:30)—two sacred objects used to discern God's guidance, though no one today knows their exact appearance or how they worked. Chapter 39 records only what the craftsmen manufactured, which is why they are omitted here; Moses places them into the breastpiece later when ordaining Aaron (Leviticus 8:8).

**Sacred garments and clergy vestments today.** Some Christian traditions draw on this chapter to design liturgical vestments for priests and pastors. Others conclude that Aaron's priesthood reached its fulfillment in Jesus, so special vestments are no longer necessary. Chapter 39 does not settle that disagreement on its own; what it unmistakably insists on is that anyone serving God must live in harmony with the words written across Aaron's forehead.

**Terence Fretheim**, an American Old Testament professor, reads the whole tabernacle account through the lens of Genesis 1–2. To him, the tabernacle stands as a microcosm of the universe, and Israel's construction of it right after the golden calf represents God partnering with human beings to renew creation. **Gregory of Nyssa**, a fourth-century bishop in Asia Minor, reads the high priest's garments as an emblem of a virtuous life. He pauses over the pomegranates stitched along the hem: tough and bitter on the outside, yet sweet within. A life of virtue feels just like that, Gregory observes—demanding at the start, yet yielding sweetness as it matures.
