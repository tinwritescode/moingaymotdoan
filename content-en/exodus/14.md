# Exodus 14 — Crossing the Red Sea

## Story

Still following the pillar of cloud and fire from the end of chapter 13, the Israelites receive an unusual order from the LORD: turn back and camp right by the sea, in front of Pi-hahiroth, opposite Baal-zephon. From the outside, it looks like walking straight into a trap. God lets Moses in on His plan beforehand: Pharaoh will assume Israel is wandering lost and hemmed in by the desert, his heart will harden again, and he will chase after them—and right there God will display His glory so that the Egyptians will know He is the LORD (14:1–4).

Everything unfolds just as God said. When word reaches Egypt that the people have fled, Pharaoh and his officials suddenly regret letting their entire slave workforce slip away. The king readies his chariot at once, mobilizing six hundred of Egypt's finest chariots along with every other chariot and officer in the land. They thunder after Israel and overtake them camped right beside the sea (14:5–9).

Looking up to see clouds of dust and Pharaoh's chariot force bearing down on them, the Israelites panic and cry out to the LORD, then round bitterly on Moses: were there not enough graves in Egypt that he dragged them into the desert to die? Better to have stayed behind serving the Egyptians than to perish in the wilderness! Standing steady in the middle of a terrified crowd, Moses gives them words that echo down the centuries: "Do not be afraid. Stand firm and you will see the LORD's salvation, which He will accomplish for you today; for the Egyptians you see today, you will never see again. The LORD will fight for you; you need only to be still" (14:10–14).

Then the LORD speaks to Moses: why are you still crying out to Me? Tell the Israelites to move forward, and lift your staff, stretch your hand over the sea, and divide it so they can walk straight across on dry ground. At that very moment, the angel of God and the pillar of cloud that had been leading the column move to the rear, standing like a shield between the two camps. To Egypt the cloud brings pitch darkness, while to Israel it lights up the night, keeping the Egyptian army from getting near them all night long (14:15–20).

Moses stretches out his hand over the sea. All night long the LORD drives the sea back with a fierce east wind, parting the waters and turning the seabed into dry land. The Israelites step down into the sea and walk across on dry ground, with a wall of water towering on their right and on their left. Recklessly, the Egyptians surge in after them—all of Pharaoh's horses, chariots, and horsemen plunging right into the middle of the sea (14:21–23).

During the last watch before dawn, the LORD looks down on the Egyptian army from the pillar of fire and cloud and throws their entire force into chaos. Chariot wheels jam and wobble, making it nearly impossible to drive. Realizing what is happening, the Egyptians shout to retreat—the LORD Himself is fighting for Israel! God tells Moses to stretch out his hand over the sea once more. At daybreak, the sea crashes back into place, cutting off their escape and swallowing every chariot, horseman, and soldier in Pharaoh's pursuing army; not a single one survives (14:24–28).

Israel, meanwhile, reaches the far shore safely. That day the LORD saved them from the grip of Egypt; standing on the bank looking at Egyptian bodies washed up on the shore, the people saw the mighty power of the LORD, revered Him, and put their trust in Him and in His servant Moses (14:29–31).

## Key Takeaways

**God sometimes leads us into what looks like a dead end so His power can shine.** Israel did not stumble onto the shore of the Red Sea by accident; God deliberately told them to turn back and camp there (14:2). Deep water ahead, chariots behind, desert on both sides. Only when every human escape route slams shut does it become unmistakable that deliverance comes solely from God's hand.

**Fear rewrites the memory of slavery.** Only days earlier they were groaning under whips and bitter labor, yet the instant danger appears they insist it would have been better to stay slaves in Egypt (14:12). In panic, familiar chains suddenly start looking safer than unfamiliar freedom.

**Sometimes faith stands still, and sometimes faith steps forward.** Moses tells the people to be still and watch the LORD fight (14:13–14), and in the very next verse God tells Moses to get them moving forward (14:15). There is no contradiction: be still in your heart instead of panicking or thrashing around in self-reliance, yet move your feet in obedience into the sea once God parts the way.

**God's own presence is the wall that guards His people.** The pillar of cloud shifts from the front to the rear, stepping directly between Israel and the Egyptian army (14:19–20). That same cloud throws darkness over the enemy while lighting the night for God's people. The God who goes ahead to guide also stands behind to defend.

**Complete rescue produces genuine faith.** Earlier the people believed when they heard promises, then crumbled the moment trouble hit. After the night at the Red Sea, seeing God's mighty hand with their own eyes, the whole nation stands in awe of the LORD, trusts Him, and trusts the leader He chose (14:31).

## Deeper Meaning

**Crossing the Red Sea pictures Christian baptism in the New Testament.** Looking back on this night, the apostle Paul writes that Israel's ancestors were all under the cloud, passed through the sea, and "were all baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:1–2). Just as the Red Sea severed Israel permanently from slavery in Egypt and opened into a new life of freedom, baptism marks a believer's death to the old slavery of sin and entrance into new life with Christ.

**Recorded in Scripture's roll call of faith.** The letter to the Hebrews remembers this crossing explicitly: "By faith the people passed through the Red Sea as on dry land; but when the Egyptians tried to follow, they were drowned" (Hebrews 11:29). Both armies stepped onto the exact same seabed, yet those who walked in faith lived, while those who charged ahead in defiance perished.

**The Red Sea becomes the gold standard for every rescue to come.** Throughout the Old Testament, whenever Israel faces hopeless odds, prophets and psalmists point back to the God who split the sea and carved a path through surging waters (Psalms 77:19; Isaiah 43:16). This event stands as permanent proof that no power on earth—neither nature nor empire—can derail God's saving purpose.

## Notes & Nuances

**Red Sea or *Sea of Reeds*?** In Hebrew, this body of water is called *Yam Suph*, literally *Sea of Reeds*—likely pointing to the shallow, reed-lined lakes along Egypt's northeastern frontier near today's Suez Canal, or to a northern arm connected to the Red Sea. The ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) rendered the name as the *Red Sea*, and that familiar title carried over into almost every English Bible, including the BSB. Whichever arm of water it was, the narrative clearly stresses that the water was deep enough to stand in twin walls and drown an entire chariot force (14:22; 14:28).

**A miracle working through a natural east wind.** Verse 14:21 explicitly says the LORD used a strong east wind blowing all night to push back the water and expose dry ground. That does not diminish the miracle in the slightest; on the contrary, it shows that God commands the forces of nature itself, timing wind and water to the exact hour His people need a crossing and releasing the sea the moment the Egyptian army is trapped in the middle.

**Did Pharaoh himself die in the Red Sea?** Chapter 14 states that the returning waters swallowed all the chariots, horsemen, and every soldier of Pharaoh's army who followed Israel into the sea (14:28), without explicitly specifying whether Pharaoh rode into the seabed or commanded from the shore. One line in Psalms 136:15 celebrates God sweeping "Pharaoh and his army into the Red Sea," using triumphant poetic language. Whether Pharaoh drowned there or limped home stripped of his army, his grip over Israel ended forever that morning.

## What Commentators Say

**Gregory of Nyssa**, the 4th-century bishop in Asia Minor, read the Red Sea crossing as a cleansing picture of baptism, building on Paul's words in 1 Corinthians 10. In his reading, the pursuing Egyptians, horses, and chariots represent the old passions, vices, and habits of a sinful life; when a believer passes through the waters of baptism, those pursuing forces are drowned behind them so they can step onto the far shore in new freedom. *(The Life of Moses, Part II)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Reading the Red Sea as baptism drowning sinful passions in Part II of The Life of Moses is a classic and widely quoted passage of Gregory of Nyssa -->

**Matthew Henry**, the 17th–18th century English pastor, paused over God's question to Moses in verse 14:15: "Why are you crying out to Me? Tell the Israelites to go forward." He noticed that Moses must have been praying silently in his heart even though the text records no spoken prayer—and God's reply offers a down-to-earth lesson: prayer is vital, yet there comes a moment to stop crying out and step forward in obedience to God's command. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Exodus 14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry certainly comments on Moses' silent prayer and the call to action in verse 14:15; exact wording not yet verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, reflected on Israel's panic and bitter complaints by the shoreline (14:10–12). To him, the scene exposes human frailty: after witnessing miracle after miracle in Egypt, one sudden crisis is enough to send faith reeling. Even so, God does not abandon them in their weakness; He still steps in to rescue them, making His patient mercy shine all the brighter. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: The core point about human frailty and God's patient mercy certainly aligns with Calvin on chapter 14; exact wording not yet verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, viewed the Red Sea deliverance as the defining story of Israel's identity.
