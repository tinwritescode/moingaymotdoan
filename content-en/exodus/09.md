# Exodus 9 — Livestock Plague, Boils, and Hail

## Story

The LORD sends Moses back to Pharaoh with the familiar demand to let His people go. If the king keeps restraining them, God's hand will bring a severe plague on Egypt's livestock in the field—horses, donkeys, camels, herds, and flocks. Once again, God announces a clear distinction: not a single animal belonging to the Israelites will die. The very next day, Egyptian livestock die across the country while Israel's animals remain completely unharmed. Pharaoh sends officials to check, sees it is true, yet his heart stays hard and he refuses to let the people go (9:1–7).

The sixth plague strikes human flesh directly, with no warning beforehand. God tells Moses and Aaron to scoop up handfuls of soot from a furnace, and right in front of Pharaoh, Moses tosses it into the air. It spreads as fine dust across Egypt, breaking out in festering boils on people and animals alike. Even the magicians are covered with boils so badly they cannot stand before Moses anymore. At this point, the text states plainly for the first time that the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart so he would not listen, just as the LORD had told Moses (9:8–12).

The seventh plague opens with the longest, most solemn warning yet. Rising early to confront Pharaoh, Moses declares that this time God is sending the full weight of His plagues directly against Pharaoh's heart, his officials, and his people, so he will know there is no one like the LORD in all the earth. By now God could have struck Pharaoh and Egypt down and wiped them off the map—yet He raised Pharaoh up for this very purpose: to display His power and cause His name to be proclaimed throughout the earth (9:13–16).

Tomorrow at this exact hour, God warns, the worst hailstorm in Egypt's history will fall. Yet woven right into the judgment is an offer of mercy: send word now to bring your servants and livestock in from the fields, because anything left outside will die. Among Pharaoh's own officials, those who fear the word of the LORD hurry to shelter their servants and herds; those who brush it off leave them exposed in the open (9:17–21).

The next day, Moses stretches his staff toward heaven. Thunder roars, hail pours down, and lightning flashes across the ground—a storm of fire and ice unlike anything Egypt had ever seen since becoming a nation. Everything left out in the fields—people, animals, crops, and trees—is battered and destroyed. Only the land of Goshen, where the Israelites live, sees not a single hailstone (9:22–26).

Shaken, Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron and speaks words that sound remarkably like repentance: "This time I have sinned. The LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked." He begs them to pray for the thunder and hail to stop and promises to let Israel go immediately. Moses agrees to go outside the city and lift his hands in prayer so Pharaoh will know the earth belongs to the LORD—yet he tells the king straight to his face that he knows Pharaoh and his officials still do not truly fear God. The storm has ruined the flax and barley, which were in bloom and ripe, while the later-growing wheat and spelt survive. Moses leaves the city and prays, and the thunder, hail, and rain immediately stop. Seeing the skies clear, Pharaoh and his officials sin again and harden their hearts, refusing to let Israel go (9:27–35).

## Key Takeaways

**The plagues escalate steadily—from property, to flesh, to life itself.** Chapter 8 brought nuisance and revulsion; chapter 9 brings crushing economic loss, searing physical pain, and lethal danger falling from the sky. Stubbornness never stays in one place; it drags heavier and heavier consequences behind it.

**Even in the middle of judgment, God leaves a door open for anyone who listens.** Before the hail falls, God sends a clear warning so anyone who takes Him at His word can bring servants and livestock under shelter (9:19–20). Some Egyptian officials actually heed that warning and save their households. Salvation is never restricted by bloodline; it opens to anyone who trusts God's word.

**Confessing sin with the lips is not the same as repenting in the heart.** Pharaoh's words sound textbook-perfect: God is righteous, and I am wicked (9:27). On the surface it looks like a breakthrough. Yet Moses sees right through it: being terrified of thunder is not the fear of the LORD (9:30). As soon as the storm clears, the promise evaporates.

**Furnace soot becomes an emblem of poetic justice.** Israel had slaved bitterly over Egyptian kilns and brick furnaces. Now handfuls of soot from a furnace float across the land and erupt in painful boils on their oppressors (9:8–10). The suffering inflicted on the powerless has a way of returning upon the oppressor's own skin.

**God's mighty deeds aim wider than defeating Pharaoh—they aim to make His name known everywhere.** God explains why He did not wipe Pharaoh out immediately: so His power would be unmistakably displayed and His name proclaimed across the whole earth (9:15–16). Decades later, even Rahab far away in Jericho has heard what God did in Egypt and puts her faith in Him (Joshua 2:9–11).

## Deeper Meaning

**The apostle Paul quotes 9:16 when reflecting on God's sovereignty.** In Romans, while unpacking God's mercy and sovereign purposes, Paul cites God's exact words to Pharaoh—that He raised him up to display His power and make His name known throughout the earth (Romans 9:17). Pharaoh's story becomes Scripture's great reminder that no human ruler can overturn God's saving purpose.

**Boils and hail return in the bowls of wrath in Revelation.** In the Bible's final vision, painful sores break out on those who worship the beast (Revelation 16:2), and massive hailstones rain from heaven on unrepentant humanity (Revelation 16:21). Both scenes draw straight from chapter 9, showing that rebellion against God echoes across history—and that God's justice will prevail in the end.

**A line drawn by faith begins to emerge.** In the fifth plague, the boundary ran neatly between Israel and Egypt (9:4). By the seventh plague, the dividing line cuts right through Pharaoh's own court: those who fear God's word live, while those who ignore it suffer loss (9:20–21). It foreshadows a truth woven through all of Scripture: what ultimately saves a person is not nationality, but trusting and obeying the word of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**If "all the livestock" died in 9:6, why are there still livestock in the fields in 9:19?** Verse 9:3 specifies that the plague struck livestock *in the field*, meaning animals kept in stalls or barns survived. Moreover, in ancient storytelling, "all" often means "every kind" or "vast numbers—virtually wiped out," rather than a modern statistical headcount of every individual animal.

**This is the first time the text explicitly says the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart.** Across the first five plagues, Scripture notes that Pharaoh hardened his own heart or that his heart remained hard (7:13; 7:22; 8:15; 8:19; 8:32; 9:7). Only at the sixth plague does the wording shift to "the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart" (9:12). Many readers see a chilling turning point here: when someone stubbornly rejects the light again and again, that very hardness becomes part of God's judgment, confirming them in what they chose.

**The magicians vanish from the story from here on.** In the third plague they hit their limit (8:18); in the sixth they are covered in boils and cannot even stand before Moses (9:11). They never appear again. In Egyptian religion, priests had to maintain strict bodily purity to serve at the altars; festering boils disqualified them from serving their own gods.

**Why include the small note about flax, barley, wheat, and spelt (9:31–32)?** Those two verses are far from filler. They pinpoint the season of the year—roughly late January or February, before the wheat harvest—and, even more importantly, explain why crops are still standing in chapter 10 for the locusts to devour.

God does not inject evil into a good heart, Augustine argued; Pharaoh was already proud and cruel.
