# Exodus 7 — The Nile Turns to Blood

## Story

At the close of the previous chapter, Moses complains once more that he is unskilled in speech and Pharaoh will never listen to him (6:30). The LORD answers with a startling statement: "See, I have made you like God to Pharaoh," and your brother Aaron will be your prophet—the one who speaks on your behalf (7:1). Moses will pass on everything God commands, and Aaron will speak it before the king (7:2).

God also warns both brothers that the road ahead will not be easy. He will harden Pharaoh's heart, and the king will refuse to listen. Yet God will lay His hand on Egypt, multiply His signs and wonders, and bring His people out—and then the Egyptians will know who He is (7:3–5). Moses and Aaron do just as the LORD commanded. By this time Moses is eighty years old and Aaron is eighty-three (7:6–7).

The LORD tells them in advance that Pharaoh will demand a miracle—and sure enough, he does. Aaron throws down his staff before Pharaoh and his officials, and it turns into a serpent. Pharaoh summons his wise men, sorcerers, and magicians, and they do the same by their secret arts: each throws down his staff, and it becomes a serpent. Yet "Aaron's staff swallowed up the other staffs" (7:8–12). Even so, Pharaoh's heart stays hard and he refuses to listen, just as the LORD had said (7:13).

Next, the LORD sends Moses out early in the morning to meet Pharaoh on the bank of the Nile, holding the same staff that turned into a snake. Moses is to repeat God's command—"Let My people go"—and warn him that because he has refused to listen, God will strike the Nile: the water will turn to blood, the fish will die, the river will stink, and the Egyptians will be unable to drink from it (7:14–18).

It happens just as God said. At the LORD's command, Aaron raises his staff over all the waters of Egypt—rivers, canals, ponds, reservoirs, and even water stored in vessels of wood and stone. Right before Pharaoh and his officials, Aaron strikes the Nile, and all the water turns to blood. The fish die, the river reeks, and blood is everywhere throughout Egypt (7:19–21).

Yet Egypt's magicians manage to replicate the feat by their secret arts, giving Pharaoh an excuse to dig in. He turns around, walks back into his palace, and "did not take any of this to heart" (7:22–23). Meanwhile, all the Egyptians have to dig along the banks of the Nile searching for drinkable water. Seven full days pass this way (7:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**God doesn't replace the weak servant—He sends a partner.** Moses insists he cannot speak well. God doesn't go hunting for a smoother orator; He entrusts His message to Moses and places Aaron beside him as his mouthpiece. Moses's weakness never derails God's work—it simply proves the work doesn't rest on Moses's talent.

**The goal is for people to know the LORD.** Twice in this chapter God says that through what is coming, Egypt and Pharaoh will know that He is the LORD (7:5; 7:17). The plagues are not mere punishment; they are a dramatic revelation of who truly rules the world.

**Everything unfolds just as God spoke beforehand.** The refrain "just as the LORD had said" repeats through this chapter (7:13; 7:22). Even Pharaoh's stubborn refusal never catches God off guard. Knowing this ahead of time keeps Moses from losing heart when doors slam shut.

**A hardened heart can stare at a miracle and walk away unmoved.** An entire river turns to blood, yet Pharaoh simply turns on his heel, goes inside his palace, and doesn't give it a second thought (7:23). People often say, "If God would just show me a miracle, I'd believe." Pharaoh proves how uncertain that really is.

**Eighty years old is not too late.** Moses and Aaron step into the greatest assignment of their lives past the age of eighty (7:7). With God, advanced age is never a reason to be put on the shelf.

## Deeper Meaning

**The answer to "Who is the LORD?"** At their first meeting, Pharaoh scoffed: Who is the LORD that I should obey Him? I do not know the LORD (5:2). Starting in this chapter, the plagues deliver the answer. "By this you will know that I am the LORD," God declares (7:17). Because Pharaoh refused to know God through His word, he will come to know Him through His deeds.

**The river that swallowed babies.** Earlier, Pharaoh ordered every Hebrew baby boy to be thrown into the Nile (1:22). Now that very river turns to blood. Scripture never explicitly connects the two scenes, yet a thoughtful reader can hardly miss the echo: innocent blood was poured into the Nile, and now the Nile gives back blood.

**The serpent and the sea monster.** The Hebrew word translated *serpent* here is different from the word for *snake* back in chapter 4 (4:3); it often refers to a great sea monster or crocodile. Later, the prophet Ezekiel calls Pharaoh the great monster sprawling among his streams, boasting that the Nile belongs to him (Ezekiel 29:3). When Aaron's staff swallows the magicians' staffs, ancient readers would have caught a vivid preview: God's power is about to swallow Egypt's proud symbols whole.

**Moses's first public sign and Jesus's first sign.** Moses's first public sign before Pharaoh turns water into blood, bringing death. Jesus's first sign turns water into wine at a wedding feast, bringing joy (John 2:1–11). Many readers have set the two side by side: the law came through Moses, while grace and truth came through Jesus Christ (John 1:17).

**The unnamed magicians later given names.** Exodus never names Pharaoh's magicians, yet the apostle Paul, drawing on Jewish tradition from his day, calls them Jannes and Jambres and compares them to false teachers who oppose the truth (2 Timothy 3:8). Being able to imitate God's works never means standing on God's side.

**Water turning to blood at the end of the Bible.** Revelation revives this plague: God's two witnesses have authority to turn water into blood (Revelation 11:6), and the bowls of wrath turn the sea, rivers, and springs to blood (Revelation 16:3–4). What happened in Egypt becomes the pattern for God's final judgment on a rebellious world.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Like God" does not mean Moses became divine.** Earlier God told Moses it would be "as if you were God" to Aaron, with Aaron serving as his mouth (4:16). The point is representation: Moses receives God's words and carries God's authority before Pharaoh, while Aaron speaks them aloud. Pharaoh claimed to be a god; the LORD sends an eighty-year-old man who struggles with words to stand toe-to-toe with him.

**Who hardened Pharaoh's heart?** This chapter uses both angles. God announces that He will harden Pharaoh's heart (7:3), yet the narrative also reports that Pharaoh's heart was hardened and unyielding (7:13–14). Believers weigh these statements differently—some stressing God's sovereign control, others stressing that God only deepened a hardness Pharaoh had already chosen for himself (5:2). What is unmistakable is that Scripture never treats Pharaoh as an innocent puppet; he remains morally responsible for his choices.

**Did the magicians really turn staffs into serpents?** Readers generally take one of three views: clever sleight of hand (like Egyptian snake charmers who could paralyze cobras until they went rigid like rods), real occult power from dark spiritual forces, or God permitting them to succeed briefly so Pharaoh's stubbornness would be exposed. Either way, they clearly lose: their staffs are swallowed, and they can only add to the plague of blood without ever reversing it. Note also that Pharaoh calls Egypt's "wise men and sorcerers and magicians" (7:11)—not mere street entertainers, but learned royal sages trained in Egypt's highest court sciences.

**Literal blood or reddened water?** Some suggest an unusually heavy summer flood carrying red silt and toxic algae turned the Nile blood-red, killing the fish and fouling the water. Others read it as literal blood. Scripture does not pause to explain the mechanics; what it emphasizes is the exact timing and total scope—happening the instant Moses speaks, right before Pharaoh's eyes, and reaching every container across the land.

**Where did the magicians find water to turn into blood?** If blood was everywhere across Egypt, where did they get clean water to copy the sign? Likely from wells dug near the riverbank (7:24). The story isn't trying to answer every logistical question; it keeps our eyes fixed on the main point.
