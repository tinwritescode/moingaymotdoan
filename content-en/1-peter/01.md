# 1 Peter 1 — A Living Hope in a Season of Exile

## Story

Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ, writes to believers scattered like temporary residents across five provinces of Asia Minor: Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia. He greets them as God's elect—chosen according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, sanctified by the Spirit, for obedience to Jesus Christ and sprinkling with His blood—and wishes them abundant grace and peace (1:1–2). Within two opening verses, he anchors their identity in the work of Father, Son, and Spirit.

He breaks into praise to God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. In His great mercy, He has given us new birth into "a living hope" through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead. That hope is an inheritance that is "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading," kept securely in heaven for them. And they themselves are being shielded by God's power through faith, waiting for the full salvation ready to be revealed in the last time (1:3–5).

In this they greatly rejoice, even though for a little while they must endure grief in various trials. Peter compares tested faith to gold refined in fire: even perishable gold is tested by fire, and their faith—far more precious than gold—is refined so that it may result in praise, glory, and honor when Jesus Christ is revealed. Though they have never seen Him, they love Him; though they do not see Him now, they believe in Him and rejoice with an inexpressible and glorious joy, because they are receiving the goal of their faith, the salvation of their souls (1:6–9).

This very salvation is what the ancient prophets searched and inquired about intently when they prophesied of the grace that would come to them. They sought to discover the time and circumstances to which the Spirit of Christ within them was pointing when He predicted the sufferings of Christ and the glories that would follow. God revealed to those prophets that they were serving not themselves, but believers today—those who have now heard the gospel through preachers empowered by the Holy Spirit sent from heaven. Even angels long to look into these things (1:10–12).

On that foundation, Peter turns to daily living. He tells them to prepare their minds for action—literally, to gird up the loins of their minds like travelers ready for the road—to be sober-minded, and to set their hope fully on the grace to be brought to them when Jesus Christ is revealed. As obedient children, they must no longer conform to the evil desires they followed in their former ignorance. Just as the One who called them is holy, so they must be holy in all they do, for it is written: "Be holy, because I am holy" (1:13–16; Leviticus 11:44).

Since they call on a Father who judges each person's work impartially, they should live out their time as temporary residents on earth in reverent fear. For they know they were not redeemed from the empty way of life handed down by their ancestors with perishable things like silver or gold, but with "the precious blood of Christ," like that of a lamb without blemish or spot. He was foreknown before the foundation of the world, yet revealed in these last times for their sake; through Him they believe in God, who raised Him from the dead and glorified Him, so that their faith and hope rest in God (1:17–21).

Now that they have purified their souls by obeying the truth so that they have sincere brotherly love, they must love one another deeply from the heart. For they have been born again not of perishable seed, but of imperishable seed, through the living and enduring word of God. Peter quotes the prophet Isaiah: all flesh is like grass, and all its glory like the flowers of the field; the grass withers and the flowers fall, but "the word of the Lord stands forever." And that word is the very gospel that was preached to them (1:22–25; Isaiah 40:6–8).

## Key Takeaways

**Christian joy does not wait for suffering to disappear.** Peter never minimizes the real sorrow of trials, yet he places that brief grief beside an indestructible inheritance guarded in heaven. Just as fire burns away only impurities from gold without touching its worth, trials refine faith so it will shine when Christ returns (1:3–7).

**Believers today live in the very age the ancient prophets longed to see.** The gospel these readers heard is no disconnected novelty, but the climax of the entire Old Testament story. The prophets foresaw Christ's two-beat pattern—suffering first, glory afterward—and that same rhythm explains the church's present path (1:10–12).

**People bought at so steep a price cannot live casually.** Peter does not rely on raw terror to enforce holiness; he reminds believers that their ransom was the precious blood of Christ. Knowing we belong to a holy Father and are only passing through this world naturally moves us to leave old habits behind and pursue purity and genuine love (1:14–22).

**New life grows from the living, enduring word of God.** Everything in this passing age fades like wildflowers in a field, but the gospel planted in believers' hearts lasts forever. Born from that same imperishable seed, they become one family with strength to love one another far more deeply than the fragile affections of the world (1:23–25).

## Deeper Meaning

**The identity of "exiles" links believers to the journey of Abraham and Israel.** Calling Christians scattered exiles (1:1) and referring to their "time as foreigners" (1:17), Peter draws directly on Old Testament language: Abraham confessed himself a stranger and foreigner in the land of Canaan (Genesis 23:4), and Israel ate the Passover with cloaks tucked into their belts ready to depart (Exodus 12:11). The literal image of girding up the mind in verse 1:13 recalls that very Exodus night.

**The spotless lamb and the sprinkling of covenant blood.** The phrase "sprinkling with His blood" (1:2) recalls the ceremony at Mount Sinai, where Moses sprinkled sacrificial blood on the people to seal the covenant after they pledged obedience (Exodus 24:7–8). And "a lamb without blemish or spot" (1:19) connects straight to the Passover lamb on the night of rescue from Egypt (Exodus 12:5) and the Suffering Servant led like a lamb to the slaughter in Isaiah (Isaiah 53:7).

**The call to holiness from Leviticus.** The quotation "Be holy, because I am holy" (1:16) comes from Leviticus (Leviticus 11:44; 19:2), where God summoned Israel to live distinctly from surrounding nations because He was their God. Peter extends that exact calling to largely Gentile believers across Asia Minor: now they too are God's own household.

**Withering grass and the word that stands forever.** When Peter cites Isaiah at the close of the chapter (1:24–25; Isaiah 40:6–8), he recalls its original setting—comforting exiles in Babylon: no matter how mighty an empire looks, it withers like grass, while God's promise of rescue stands forever. What God promised through Isaiah has now come true in the gospel.

## Notes & Nuances

**"New birth" here is tightly bound to Jesus' resurrection.** Many readers think first of new birth from Jesus' conversation with Nicodemus (John 3:3). Peter adds a concrete anchor: new birth is not merely an inward emotional shift, but springs from the historical event of Jesus rising from the dead (1:3) and from the proclaimed word of the gospel (1:23).

**Verse 1:11 says "the Spirit of Christ" was already at work in the Old Testament prophets.** That phrasing is striking, since those prophets lived centuries before Jesus was born in Bethlehem. Peter affirms that Christ did not first begin to exist in the first century; His Spirit was already guiding ancient prophets to announce His coming sufferings and glory.

**Reverent fear of the Father in verse 1:17 is not the terror of a slave.** Believers call God "Father" while knowing He judges impartially. Such fear is the earnest reverence of a child who hates to grieve their Father and refuses to treat lightly the costly blood that redeemed them (1:17–19).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observed that Peter places praise before every command in chapter 1. When people are weighed down by trials, hearing bare moral commands only discourages them further; first they need to lift their eyes to their secure heavenly inheritance so they gain strength to stand firm. *(Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, on 1 Peter chapter 1 verses 3–6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's point that Peter leads with comfort and hope before issuing exhortations is clear in his commentary on 1 Peter -->

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, treasured 1 Peter as one of the noblest and clearest books on the gospel in the New Testament. Preaching on verses 1:18–19, he stressed that being ransomed by Christ's precious blood sweeps away every illusion that people can purchase salvation with silver, gold, or their own merits. *(Sermons on 1 Peter, on chapter 1 verses 18–19)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther famously esteemed 1 Peter among the foremost New Testament books and preached forcefully on Christ's blood as ransom in chapter 1 -->

**Karen H. Jobes**, American New Testament scholar, examines the geography of the five Asia Minor provinces listed in verse 1:1, noting that much of this region lay north of the Taurus Mountains, where Roman policies had displaced and resettled populations. Thus the language of "exiles" and "foreigners" carried not only spiritual meaning, but resonated directly with the social displacement of the first readers. *(1 Peter, Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament, 2005, on chapter 1 verse 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jobes's thesis on Roman resettlement and the literal social resonance of exile across the five provinces is a prominent contribution of her BECNT volume -->

**Edmund Clowney**, twentieth-century American theologian, linked verse 1:11 directly with how the risen Jesus interpreted the Old Testament Scriptures on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:25–27).
