# Nehemiah 6 — Completing the Wall Amid Traps

## Story

Having resolved the internal debt crisis in chapter 5, Jerusalem's rebuilding enters its home stretch. Once Sanballat, Tobiah, Geshem the Arab, and the rest of the enemies hear that the wall stands completely repaired without a single breach left—though the gate doors have not yet been hung—they realize collective ridicule and military threats have both failed. Immediately they aim directly at Nehemiah himself. Sanballat and Geshem send a seemingly cordial invitation: "Come, let us meet together in one of the villages on the plain of Ono." Seeing straight through their plot to harm him, Nehemiah sends messengers back with a crisp, resolute reply: "I am doing a great work and cannot come down. Why should the work stop while I leave it to go down to you?" Four times running they send the exact same invitation, and four times Nehemiah returns the exact same refusal (6:1–4).

When lures fail, Sanballat tries a nastier tactic on the fifth round. He dispatches his servant carrying "an open letter in his hand"—deliberately unsealed so bystanders along the road can freely read and spread rumors. Quoting alleged gossip circulating among surrounding nations and naming Geshem (Gashmu) as corroborating witness, the letter charges that Nehemiah and the Jews are plotting revolt, building the wall so he can crown himself king. It even fabricates claims that Nehemiah appointed prophets right in Jerusalem to proclaim, "There is a king in Judah!" Feigning concern that reports like this are bound to reach the Persian emperor, Sanballat urges him to come confer immediately. Unshaken, Nehemiah sends back a flat denial: "Nothing like what you say is happening; you are just making it up out of your own head." He recognizes the entire stunt is designed purely to terrify the Jews so their hands drop and the work stalls. Right inside his memoir he breathes a short, earnest prayer: "Now, O God, strengthen my hands!" (6:5–9).

Unable to crack him from outside, the enemies plant a snare right inside God's own community. One day Nehemiah visits the home of Shemaiah son of Delaiah, grandson of Mehetabel, who is "confined to his home." Lowering his voice like a prophet delivering an urgent oracle, Shemaiah warns that assassins are coming tonight to kill him, urging both of them to meet inside the house of God—right inside the temple sanctuary—and bolt the doors to survive. Without blinking, Nehemiah fires back two steel-edged questions: "Should a man like me run away? Should one like me go into the temple to save his own life? I will not go!" (6:10–11).

Instantly Nehemiah discerns that God never sent Shemaiah, because the prophecy ran straight against him and against God's law: Tobiah and Sanballat had bribed him. Beneath pretended concern for his safety lurked a double trap—scaring him into violating the sacred sanctuary restricted to priests, thereby drawing him into sin so they could ruin his reputation and disgrace him before the whole nation. Rather than taking personal revenge, Nehemiah hands every plotter over to God: remember Tobiah and Sanballat according to their deeds, along with the prophetess Noadiah and the rest of the prophets who conspired to intimidate him (6:12–14).

Every scheme—summit invitations, slanderous open letter, hired fake prophecy—shatters completely. On the twenty-fifth day of Elul, Jerusalem's wall stands finished in just fifty-two days. When news spreads, all their enemies and surrounding nations are filled with dread, stripped of arrogance and thoroughly deflated, "for they realized that this work had been accomplished by our God" (6:15–16).

Even so, the chapter does not end in seamless peace. In those very days after the wall is completed, a hidden crack continues smoldering inside Jerusalem itself. Numerous nobles of Judah keep exchanging frequent letters with Tobiah. The root lies in tangled marriage alliances and sworn oaths: Tobiah is son-in-law to Shecaniah of the family of Arah, and his son Jehohanan married the daughter of Meshullam son of Berechiah. Bound by family ties and shared interests, these nobles keep praising Tobiah's good deeds right in front of Nehemiah while leaking everything Nehemiah says straight back to Tobiah. Meanwhile, Tobiah keeps firing off letters with one relentless aim: to intimidate Nehemiah (6:17–19).

## Key Takeaways

**When open attacks fail, enemies switch to targeted traps against leaders.** In chapter 4, Sanballat and company mocked and threatened the whole workforce. By chapter 6, with the wall nearly sealed, they stop attacking head-on and focus on neutralizing Nehemiah personally through bogus meetings, political slander, and religious snares (6:1–13). As worthy work nears completion, tests often grow subtler rather than lighter.

**Finishing well requires saying a firm *no* to distractions.** Four times Sanballat invites him down to the plain of Ono under a polite cloak of dialogue, and Nehemiah refuses to budge even once (6:2–4). He understands that stepping off the wall for phony talks halts the vital work entrusted to him. Not every invitation merits acceptance, and not every argument deserves our time.

**Fear is the enemy's favorite tool to paralyze hands and push people into sin.** Throughout chapter 6, one hidden objective behind the open letter, hired prophecy, and steady stream of mail echoes repeatedly: to frighten Nehemiah and the builders so their hands weaken and he stumbles into sin (6:9; 6:13; 6:14; 6:19). Fear tempts people to abandon duty or seize crooked shortcuts for self-preservation. Nehemiah's response stays simple and steady: call a lie by its true name, and look up asking God to strengthen his hands (6:8–9).

**True discernment tests advice by God's Word, never by religious appearances.** Shemaiah belongs to the community, appears devoutly secluded, and speaks in prophetic cadence as though guarding Nehemiah's life (6:10). Unfooled by titles or pious posture, Nehemiah checks the counsel straight against God's law and recognizes that any voice urging cowardly flight and sacrilege cannot come from God (6:11–13). No matter how spiritual or caring it sounds, counsel contradicting God's Word is always a trap.

**Work accomplished beyond human strength forces even enemies to recognize God's hand.** Surrounded by enemies, building with swords at their sides, weathering economic turmoil and insider traps, Judah completes the entire circuit in fifty-two days (6:15). That outcome defies purely human explanation, compelling even hostile neighbors to deflate and acknowledge God stood behind the work (6:16).

## Deeper Meaning

**The treason accusation replays the exact script that once halted Jerusalem.** Sanballat's open letter alleging that Judah is fortifying the city to revolt and crown Nehemiah king (6:6–7) carried lethal weight. Back in Ezra, that exact tactic worked decisively: hostile officials wrote to this very King Artaxerxes accusing Jerusalem of being a "rebellious city," triggering a royal armed halt to wall construction (Ezra 4:12–16; 4:21–24). The very day Nehemiah first arrived, Sanballat had already hurled the treason label (2:19). This time Nehemiah refuses to let old fears freeze him, calmly refuting the lie and staying locked onto the task sanctioned by both God and king.

**Guarding the sanctuary's sacred boundary: Torah law and King Uzziah's tragedy.** Nehemiah's sharp retort in 6:11—"Should one like me go into the temple to save his own life?"—stands squarely on Mosaic law. God strictly ordained that only Aaronic priests might enter the inner sanctuary; any unauthorized outsider encroaching faced death (Numbers 3:10; 18:7). In Judah's history, even mighty King Uzziah was struck immediately with leprosy on his forehead when he barged into the temple to burn incense, living quarantined until death (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). As a layman, Nehemiah flatly refuses to buy physical safety at the price of trampling God's holiness.

**The test distinguishing true prophets from false spans both Testaments.** Nehemiah's sharp discernment toward Shemaiah and the prophetess Noadiah (6:12–14) applies the exact rule laid down in Deuteronomy: anyone claiming prophetic authority who steers God's people against God's commands is certainly not sent by the LORD and need not be feared (Deuteronomy 13:1–3; 18:20–22). In the New Testament, that watchfulness grows sharper still: Jesus warns His followers to beware false prophets arriving in sheep's clothing who inwardly are ravenous wolves (Matthew 7:15), and John instructs believers not to believe every spirit, but to test the spirits to see whether they are from God (1 John 4:1).

**Foreshadowing the One who refused to flee death threats or false royal charges.** Standing steady amid chapter 6's traps, Nehemiah foreshadows Jesus centuries later. When Pharisees tried scaring Jesus into leaving by warning that Herod wanted to kill Him, Jesus refused to run, marching steadily onward until His work was completed at Jerusalem (Luke 13:31–33). And just as Sanballat framed Nehemiah as a self-proclaimed king to alarm Persia (6:6–7), Jesus's accusers later aimed that exact political charge before Governor Pilate—alleging He stirred revolt and claimed to be a king opposing Caesar (Luke 23:2; John 19:12).

**Finishing a stone wall never finishes the battle for a community's heart.** The moment surrounding nations lose heart recognizing God's hand (6:16) echoes Canaan melting in dread before the LORD's mighty works (Joshua 2:9–11) and the exiles' song declaring even the nations must say the LORD has done great things for them (Psalms 126:2–3). Yet the chapter's closing three verses (6:17–19) strike a sobering minor chord. One of the two families intermarried with Tobiah is Meshullam son of Berechiah—the very man who eagerly repaired two sections of the wall earlier (3:4; 3:30). Those cozy family and business ties erupt even worse later, when priest Eliashib hands Tobiah a spacious temple storeroom and his grandson marries Sanballat's daughter (13:4–9; 13:28–29). Rebuilding a stone wall takes fifty-two days; guarding a community's heart from compromise takes a lifetime.

## Notes & Nuances

**Where was the plain of Ono, and why was meeting there so perilous?** Ono lay northwest of Judah near the coastal plain of Sharon, roughly forty kilometers from Jerusalem—more than a day's walk each way. Sitting along the border zone outside Judah's secure control, it leaned straight into enemy territory. Agreeing to go would leave the unfinished wall leaderless at the pivotal hour before the gates were hung, while walking Nehemiah straight into an ambush far from help (6:1–2).

**Why was an "open letter" in verse 5 especially sinister?** Ancient official correspondence was tightly rolled, tied, and stamped with wax or clay seals for confidentiality. Sending a servant carrying an unsealed letter straight into Jerusalem was calculated psychological warfare: every literate courier and onlooker along the route could read the treason charge against Artaxerxes and spread panic across the city. Sanballat aimed to exploit Judah's dread of Persian reprisal so citizens would panic and abandon Nehemiah.

**How should we understand Shemaiah being "confined to his home" in verse 10 and the prophetess Noadiah in verse 14?** The Hebrew participle rendered "confined" (or "shut up") in 6:10 indicates being restricted or voluntarily secluded indoors. Commentators offer two likely reasons: Shemaiah may have staged theatrical hiding to make his assassination rumor feel urgent, or cited ritual impurity or a vow to lure Nehemiah into a private meeting at his house. As for Noadiah in 6:14, Scripture honors numerous faithful prophetesses—Miriam, Deborah, Huldah, and Anna (Exodus 15:20; Judges 4:4; 2 Kings 22:14; Luke 2:36). Noadiah siding with the opposition does not diminish women's calling; it shows that prophetic office—whether held by man or woman—can be corrupted when sold out for money and political intrigue.

**Was finishing the entire wall in fifty-two days truly realistic?** That timeframe in 6:15 surprised even the first-century Jewish historian Josephus, who expanded it to two years and four months in his retelling. Most modern Old Testament scholars, however, find fifty-two days wholly credible. Nehemiah was not quarrying a brand-new fortification from scratch; he reused intact lower courses and existing tumbled stones around a much smaller post-exilic perimeter, patching breaches and raising heights with dozens of crews working simultaneously from sunrise to starlight. The twenty-fifth of Elul fell around late September or early October 445 BC—barely half a year after Nehemiah lifted the cup before Artaxerxes in Nisan (2:1).

**Is Nehemiah's prayer in verse 14 a vindictive curse?** Nehemiah asks God to remember Tobiah, Sanballat, Noadiah, and the hired prophets according to their deeds (6:14). Like his prayer in chapter 4 (4:4–5), its sharp tone contrasts with Jesus's call to love our enemies (Matthew 5:44). Reading fairly, note that Nehemiah holds real gubernatorial authority yet refuses vigilante retaliation against slanderers and false prophets, placing righteous judgment squarely in God's hands—a principle echoed in the New Testament (Romans 12:19). At the same time, it remains an Old Covenant prayer capturing the raw pressure and loneliness of a leader surrounded by betrayal, not a license for New Covenant believers to nurse grudges.
