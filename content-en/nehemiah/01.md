# Nehemiah 1 — Grief and Prayer in the Citadel of Susa

## Story

The book of Nehemiah opens in the first person, reading like the private memoir of an exile holding high office in the Persian court. It is the month of Chislev—late autumn or early winter—in the twentieth year of King Artaxerxes, around 445 BC. Nehemiah son of Hacaliah is stationed in the citadel of Susa, the Persian empire's royal winter residence (1:1).

Amid that palace splendor over a thousand kilometers from Jerusalem, one of his brothers, Hanani, arrives with several men from Judah. Right away Nehemiah asks about two concerns weighing on his heart: how the surviving Jewish remnant who escaped exile are faring, and what condition the city of Jerusalem is in (1:2).

The report they bring is heartbreaking. The survivors back in the province of Judah are "in great trouble and disgrace," while "the wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates have been burned with fire" (1:3). An ancient city stripped of walls and gates stood like a house with no doors in a hostile neighborhood—wide open to raiders, bullies, and public humiliation.

Hearing this news, Nehemiah does not rush off to pull strings or draft strategies. Instead, "I sat down and wept. For days I mourned and fasted and prayed before the God of heaven" (1:4). Not until the month of Nisan—roughly four months later—will an opening come to speak with the king (2:1). Throughout a long winter, Jerusalem's pain lodges inside his heart as he brings it day and night before God.

Nehemiah's prayer begins by fixing his gaze on God Himself. He addresses Him as "the LORD, God of heaven, the great and awesome God, who keeps His covenant of loving devotion" with those who love Him and obey His commandments (1:5). He begs God to keep His eyes open and His ears attentive to his day-and-night plea for Israel. Remarkably, he does not stand apart blaming others. He confesses the collective sin of the whole nation, saying plainly, "both I and my father's house have sinned." He admits God's people have acted very corruptly against Him, failing to keep the commands, statutes, and ordinances He gave through Moses (1:6–7).

After confession, Nehemiah appeals straight to the very promise God spoke through Moses. God had warned that if Israel proved unfaithful, He would scatter them among the nations. Yet He also promised that if they returned to Him and kept His commands, even if their exiles lay "at the farthest horizon," He would gather them back and bring them to the place He chose as a dwelling for His Name (1:8–9). Nehemiah reminds God that these people are still His servants and His people, whom He redeemed by His great power and mighty hand (1:10).

Only at the end of his prayer does he mention the step ahead of him. He asks God to hear the prayers of His servants—showing he is not praying alone, but joining many others who revere God's Name. Then he asks God to grant him success today and "grant him mercy before this man." Only in the closing line does Nehemiah drop a brief yet decisive disclosure: "Now I was cupbearer to the king" (1:11).

## Key Takeaways

**Living in comfort does not excuse forgetting the suffering of our brothers and sisters.** Nehemiah enjoys high standing and security in the Susa palace, months of travel from his ancestral homeland—a city he may never even have seen in person. Still, he proactively asks after Jerusalem, and when bad news arrives, he sits down and weeps as if grief had struck his own household (1:2–4). Love for God's people is measured not by physical proximity, but by whether their affliction reaches our hearts.

**Great undertakings begin on our knees.** Nehemiah will soon prove a crisp organizer and decisive leader. Yet his first instinct is not to sketch blueprints, but to weep, fast, and pray across months (1:4; 2:1). He recognizes that restoring a ruined city is never merely a matter of stones and royal permits; first and foremost, it is a matter between God's people and God Himself.

**Confessing communal sin with *we* instead of *they*.** Though Nehemiah never lived during the pre-exilic idolatry that brought Jerusalem down, he does not pose as an innocent critic pointing fingers at past generations. He steps right inside his people's shame: we have sinned, "both I and my father's house have sinned" (1:6–7). Anyone truly called to mend a broken community stops standing outside judging and steps inside sharing responsibility.

**Praying on the solid ground of God's character and promises.** Nehemiah never bargains with God on the basis of personal merit or Judah's worthiness. He anchors his appeal on two immovable rocks: God keeps His covenant of steadfast love (1:5), and God bound Himself to the promise to regather His people when they turn back (1:8–9). Prayer is not arm-twisting a reluctant Deity, but bringing His own promises back to Him in trust.

**Before God, earth's mightiest emperor is simply *this man*.** The world hailed Artaxerxes as King of Kings, ruler over an empire stretching from India to Egypt. Yet after speaking with "the great and awesome God" (1:5), Nehemiah refers to the emperor merely as "this man" (1:11). Whoever truly sees God's greatness loses paralyzing dread before human authority.

## Deeper Meaning

**Steeped in the covenant promises of Deuteronomy.** Nehemiah's prayer in verses 5 through 10 breathes the language of Deuteronomy. Opening by praising God as the One who keeps His covenant of steadfast love echoes Moses instructing Israel before entering the Promised Land (Deuteronomy 7:9). Recalling dispersion among the nations followed by regathering from the farthest horizon connects straight to the warnings and restoration pledges in Moses's farewell address (Deuteronomy 28:64; 30:1–5). And appealing to Israel as the people God redeemed by His great power and mighty hand mirrors Moses interceding for Israel after the golden calf crisis (Deuteronomy 9:26–29). Nehemiah does not read Scripture merely as ancient history; he brings God's Word straight into his hour of crisis.

**Echoing Solomon's temple dedication prayer.** Asking God to keep His eyes open and ears attentive to prayers offered day and night, and pleading for mercy before foreign rulers (1:6; 1:11), Nehemiah retraces Solomon's prayer from centuries earlier. When dedicating the temple, Solomon foresaw Israel sinning, being exiled to distant lands, repenting wholeheartedly toward God's chosen city, and needing God to forgive and grant them compassion before their captors (1 Kings 8:28–30; 8:46–50). From exile in Susa, Nehemiah steps directly into that historic prayer.

**Sharing the post-exilic rhythm of repentance.** Nehemiah's confession stands alongside two other great prayers from the same era: Daniel in Babylon reading Jeremiah's prophecy (Daniel 9:3–5) and Ezra in Jerusalem grieving over the returned community's sin (Ezra 9:5–6). All three are deeply godly men, yet all three fast, weep, and confess in the first-person plural *we*. Scripture shows that every authentic renewal of God's people begins with brokenhearted repentance.

**Positioned inside the palace for a mission of rescue.** The closing sentence reveals Nehemiah serving as the Persian king's cupbearer (1:11)—an intimate, high-trust post in ancient Near Eastern courts, much like Pharaoh's cupbearer in Joseph's day (Genesis 40:1–2). Just as God positioned Joseph in Egypt and Esther right there in the citadel of Susa (Esther 1:2), He stationed His servant in the exact place needed long before the crisis landed. Nehemiah did not flee secular work to serve God; his palace office became the very bridge enabling Jerusalem's rebuilding.

**Tears over Jerusalem pointing forward to the New Testament.** Nehemiah sitting down to weep over broken Jerusalem opens a moving thread reaching into the New Testament. Over four centuries later, Jesus Himself wept over Jerusalem as He approached the city, foreseeing its siege and ruin because it missed the hour of God's visitation (Luke 19:41–44). The apostle Paul likewise carried unceasing sorrow in his heart for his fellow Israelites (Romans 9:2–3). The closer a person lives to God's heart, the more readily they shed tears over the brokenness of God's people.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did news of ruined walls shock Nehemiah so deeply?** Babylon had burned Jerusalem back in 586 BC—over a hundred and forty years before Nehemiah's time. Hearing about century-old devastation would hardly have stunned him into sitting down to weep as if tragedy had just occurred. The book of Ezra supplies the missing link: earlier during Artaxerxes's own reign, a group of Judeans tried to rebuild Jerusalem's wall, only for local officials to accuse them of plotting rebellion, prompting the king to halt the project by armed force (Ezra 4:11–16; 4:21–23). Almost certainly those opponents exploited that royal decree to smash the newly raised stonework and torch the gates. Hanani's report in Susa brought word of that fresh setback.

**Being cupbearer meant far more than pouring wine.** Modern ears hear *cupbearer* and picture a minor waiter. In ancient Persian royal courts, however, poisoning was an ever-present danger, meaning the official who tasted and handed the king his cup had to be extraordinarily loyal, discerning, and deeply trusted. That office gave Nehemiah daily face-to-face access to the monarch, substantial court influence, and considerable personal wealth—as confirmed later when he funded the entire governor's table out of his own pocket.

**When was the month of Chislev in the twentieth year?** On the Babylonian-influenced Jewish calendar, Chislev fell around November–December. The twentieth year of Artaxerxes I (reigned 465–424 BC) generally falls in late 446 or late 445 BC. Since the month of Nisan at the start of chapter 2 is also dated to the twentieth year (2:1), many scholars note that Nehemiah counted regnal years on an autumn-to-autumn calendar (starting in Tishri, September–October), placing Chislev earlier in the same regnal year than Nisan.

**Was Hanani Nehemiah's biological brother or simply a fellow Judean?** In verse 2, the Hebrew phrase "one of my brothers" can denote either a blood brother or a kinsman and compatriot. Yet in chapter 7, Nehemiah explicitly calls him "my brother Hanani" when entrusting him with charge over Jerusalem (7:2). Most commentators therefore conclude Hanani was indeed Nehemiah's actual brother.
