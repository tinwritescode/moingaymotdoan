# Nehemiah 3 — Rebuilding the Wall Side by Side

## Story

The moment the people rally and Nehemiah brushes aside enemy ridicule at the end of chapter 2, work on Jerusalem's wall begins immediately. Chapter 3 guides the reader on a complete counterclockwise circuit around the perimeter, starting at the northeast corner. Setting the very first stone is Eliashib the high priest together with his fellow priests. They rebuild the Sheep Gate, hang its doors, and "dedicated it," carrying the work as far as the Tower of the Hundred and the Tower of Hananel (3:1).

Moving westward along the northern wall, work links up stretch by stretch to the steady refrain "next to them" and "next to him." Men of Jericho and Zaccur build right beside the priests. The sons of Hassenaah tackle the Fish Gate, laying its beams and setting its doors, bolts, and bars. Next come the teams of Meremoth, Meshullam, and Zadok. Right behind them, townsfolk from Tekoa lend a hand, yet the record pauses for one blunt sour note: "their nobles would not put their shoulders to the work under their supervisors"—or the work of their Lord (3:2–5).

Swinging down the western side, Joiada and Meshullam repair the Old Gate—also called the Jeshanah Gate—laying beams and fitting doors, bolts, and bars. Beside them work volunteers from Gibeon and Mizpah, towns under the jurisdiction of the Trans-Euphrates governor. Then come hands trained for delicate crafts rather than hauling stone and mixing mortar: Uzziel of the goldsmiths and Hananiah of the perfumers labor side by side restoring Jerusalem as far as the Broad Wall. Next follow Rephaiah, ruler of half the district of Jerusalem, Jedaiah repairing right opposite his own house, Hattush, and Malchijah with Hasshub repairing another section plus the Tower of the Ovens. Joining them is Shallum, ruler of the other half-district of Jerusalem—and notably, standing on the wall working right alongside him are his daughters (3:6–12).

Rounding the southwest corner toward the southern tip—the exact crumbled stretch Nehemiah quietly surveyed at midnight—Hanun and the residents of Zanoah repair the Valley Gate and build a thousand cubits of wall all the way to the Dung Gate. Malchijah son of Rechab, ruler of the Beth-haccherem district, rebuilds the Dung Gate with its doors, bolts, and bars. Next, Shallun, ruler of the Mizpah district, restores the Fountain Gate, roofs it over, and rebuilds the wall by the Pool of Siloam near the King's Garden up to the steps descending from the City of David (3:13–15).

Turning up the eastern flank northward, the wall passes familiar landmarks of the ancient capital. Nehemiah son of Azbuk—ruler of half the district of Beth-zur, sharing the governor's name—repairs opposite the tombs of David, the artificial pool, and the House of the Mighty Men. Behind him come Levite crews led by Rehum, Hashabiah, and Bavvai, alongside rulers of both halves of the Keilah district and Ezer, ruler of Mizpah, working near the ascent to the armory at the angle in the wall. Amid dozens of names flowing steadily past, one man earns special praise: Baruch son of Zabbai "zealously repaired" from the angle to the doorway of high priest Eliashib's house. Right behind him, Meremoth steps up for a second shift, repairing along the length of Eliashib's house (3:16–21).

Climbing higher along the northeastern slope, priests from the surrounding plain pitch in; beside them Benjamin, Hasshub, and Azariah repair right next to their own homes. Binnui builds on to the angle, while Palal and Pedaiah repair opposite the projecting upper tower of the royal palace beside the court of the guard. The temple servants—Hebrew *Nethinim*—residing on the hill of Ophel take responsibility opposite the Water Gate on the east. Right here, the ordinary people of Tekoa return for a second assignment, quietly taking on another stretch up to the wall of Ophel as if making up for their own nobles' apathy (3:22–27).

The final stretch closes the full ring along the temple's northeastern side. Priests work above the Horse Gate, each tending the exact section opposite his own home. Next come Zadok, Shemaiah keeper of the East Gate, Hananiah, Hanun, and Meshullam son of Berechiah taking a second shift directly across from his living quarters. At last, Malchijah of the goldsmiths along with fellow goldsmiths and merchants repair from the Inspection Gate around the corner all the way back to the Sheep Gate—joining seamlessly where the high priest laid the very first stone (3:28–32).

## Key Takeaways

**God's great works require the shoulders of the entire community.** More than forty teams labor simultaneously around the perimeter in chapter 3. No lone superhero rebuilds Jerusalem by himself; Governor Nehemiah's own name never even appears once on the roster. The refrain "next to them" and "next to him" repeats nearly thirty times, proving real strength lies in each person holding their assigned piece and locking tightly into their neighbor's.

**Spiritual leaders must step up first by example.** High priest Eliashib does not fold his arms and pronounce blessings from the sidelines. Together with fellow priests, he rolls up his sleeves to rebuild the Sheep Gate first and dedicates the work to God (3:1). When the highest office lifts stones and mortar first, manual toil turns into worship and the whole community gains courage to follow.

**Everyone has a place on the wall regardless of trade or status.** Standing shoulder to shoulder are priests, Levites, district rulers, goldsmiths, perfumers, merchants, temple servants, neighbors from Jericho, Gibeon, and Mizpah, and even Shallum's daughters (3:12). Nobody pleads soft hands or lack of masonry credentials to stay home.

**Thoughtful organization inspires wholehearted effort.** Nehemiah assigns sections strategically. Many families and priests rebuild the exact strip of wall facing their own front doors (3:10; 3:23; 3:28–30). Everyone knows a solid wall in front of their house keeps their own spouse and children sleeping safely—fostering careful workmanship, shorter commutes, and quick mutual defense if trouble hits.

**God remembers plainly who worked eagerly and who hung back.** A seemingly routine roster preserves two starkly contrasting attitudes. Tekoa's nobles refused to stoop their necks to their Lord's work, leaving a permanent stain in Scripture (3:5). Meanwhile, ordinary townspeople from Tekoa took on two whole sections (3:5; 3:27), and Baruch is singled out by name for special zeal (3:20). Apathy and wholehearted devotion in shared ministry never go unnoticed before God.

## Deeper Meaning

**The rising wall fulfills ancient prophetic promises.** During the darkest hour right before Babylon leveled Jerusalem, Jeremiah relayed the LORD's promise that the city would be rebuilt for Him—from the Tower of Hananel to the Corner Gate, extending out to the Horse Gate on the east, all set apart as holy to the LORD (Jeremiah 31:38–40). In Nehemiah 3, those exact landmarks—the Tower of Hananel, the Horse Gate, and dedicating the wall as holy—spring back to life (3:1; 3:28). Every stone laid also answers the psalmist's old prayer asking God to do good to Zion and rebuild the walls of Jerusalem (Psalms 51:18).

**Physical labor becomes an act of genuine worship.** Standing right by the temple, the Sheep Gate was where sacrificial lambs entered. The priests dedicating the Sheep Gate and opening section as holy reveals that in Scripture's eyes no barrier divides "sacred" devotion from "ordinary" work (3:1). Raising stones, hanging doors, and bolting iron bars to protect where God's people dwell is itself ministry offered to God—echoing Mosaic provisions for dedicating a new home (Deuteronomy 20:5).

**An early portrait of the church as one body with many members.** Seeing dozens of groups across every vocation, rank, and gender standing shoulder to shoulder on one shared wall anticipates Paul's famous picture of the church in the New Testament. He likens believers to one body composed of diverse members, each placed by God, none permitted to look down on another, all joined closely together to build up the whole body in love (1 Corinthians 12:14–21; Ephesians 4:15–16). Peter likewise uses construction imagery, calling believers living stones being built into a spiritual house for God (1 Peter 2:5).

**From Jerusalem's stone gates to Jesus and the New Jerusalem.** Several landmarks named here reappear over four hundred years later in John's Gospel. Right beside the Sheep Gate lay the Pool of Bethesda, where Jesus healed a paralyzed man who had waited thirty-eight years (3:1; cf. John 5:2). The Pool of Siloam near the Fountain Gate is where He sent a man born blind to wash and receive sight (3:15; cf. John 9:7). Farther still, on Scripture's closing pages, the wall and twelve gates of the New Jerusalem inscribed with the twelve tribes and twelve apostles shine as the eternal climax of every building work of God's people across history (Revelation 21:12–14).

**A strong start never guarantees a faithful finish.** Reading all of Nehemiah uncovers a sobering twist tucked inside this honor roll. High priest Eliashib—who pioneered the Sheep Gate in verse 1—later allies with the enemy Tobiah and clears out a storeroom inside the temple courts for him while Nehemiah is away (13:4–7). Likewise, Meshullam son of Berechiah—who zealously repaired two sections here—later marries his daughter to Tobiah's son (3:4; 3:30; cf. 6:18). Today's zeal never replaces lifelong vigilance and faithfulness.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does chapter 3 consist solely of names and gates?** Modern readers often skim past lists like administrative spreadsheets. Yet the author intentionally kept this roster as a collective tribute: rebuilding Jerusalem was never one governor's solo triumph, but a shared community achievement. Many scholars also note that the voice switches abruptly from first-person "I" (chapters 2 and 4) to third-person here, suggesting Nehemiah incorporated an official construction archive directly into his memoir.

**The Nehemiah in verse 16 is not the book's author.** Verse 3:16 mentions Nehemiah son of Azbuk, ruler of half the Beth-zur district. Governor Nehemiah—the book's narrator—is the son of Hacaliah (1:1). They simply share a common name, just as Malchijah and Meshullam appear multiple times for distinct individuals in this same chapter.

**Who were the temple servants (*Nethinim*) in verse 26?** The Hebrew term *Nethinim* literally means *the given ones*. They assisted the Levites with daily temple duties, an order originally appointed by David and Israel's leaders (Ezra 2:43; 8:20). In Nehemiah's day they lived in their own quarter on the hill of Ophel just south of the temple mount, naturally repairing the eastern wall directly outside their neighborhood.

**The people of Tekoa stand out for a sharp contrast inside their own town.** Mentioned in both 3:5 and 3:27, Tekoa was a town south of Jerusalem—the ancient hometown of the prophet Amos. Remarkably, even though their nobles refused to bend their shoulders to the work (3:5), Tekoa's ordinary citizens poured themselves in so completely that after finishing one stretch on the northern wall, they stepped up to repair a second section along the eastern wall near Ophel (3:27).

**What do archaeologists observe about Nehemiah's wall line?** Tracing the ten gates named across chapter 3—Sheep, Fish, Old, Valley, Dung, Fountain, Water, Horse, East, and Inspection—alongside archaeological excavations in Jerusalem, most scholars conclude Nehemiah's wall enclosed a narrower footprint than the expansive pre-exilic city destroyed by Babylon. Along the steep eastern slope above the Kidron Valley, where pre-exilic fortifications had collapsed heavily, Nehemiah shifted the line higher along the ridge to build faster and defend more effectively with a small population.

To him, the high priest building and dedicating the Sheep Gate pointed first to Christ as the door of the sheepfold, while the neighboring Fish Gate symbolized the apostles preaching the Gospel as fishers of men. **Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, lingered over the steady refrain "next to them" and "next to him": when tasks are neatly divided and everyone links hands harmoniously, even monumental burdens grow light. He praised high priest Eliashib for pitching into manual labor first, while underscoring the lasting reproach on Tekoa's nobles who stood aloof while their poorer neighbors did double duty. **Derek Kidner**, British Old Testament scholar, observed that while chapter 3 looks like a dry ledger at a quick glance, it actually delivers a vibrant documentary of a community coming alive.
