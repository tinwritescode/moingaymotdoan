# Job 19 — Forsaken by Everyone, Yet My Redeemer Lives

## Story

Job answers Bildad with a cry weighed down by exhaustion and grief. He asks his friends: "How long will you torment me," and "Ten times now you have reproached me"—time after time—without a shred of shame? Even supposing he had made a mistake, his error stays with him and does them no harm. If they insist on magnifying themselves over him and using his disgrace as proof against him, they ought to recognize that "it is God who has wronged me" and drawn His net around him (19:1–6).

Job turns to describe what it feels like to be treated by God as an enemy. He cries out "Violence!" yet gets no answer; he calls for help, yet finds no justice. God has blocked his path, shrouded his road in darkness, stripped away his honor, removed the crown from his head, torn him down on every side, and "uprooted my hope like a tree." To Job's eyes right now, God acts like a besieging general marching troops to build ramps around his tent (19:7–12).

His anguish deepens as he catalogues complete human abandonment. God "has removed my brothers from me"; acquaintances treat him like a stranger, relatives stand aloof, and close friends forget him. Guests and maidservants regard him as an outsider; his servant ignores his call even when he begs. His wife finds his breath repulsive, his own brothers recoil, and even young children mock him. "All my best friends despise me," while his wasted frame leaves him escaped "only by the skin of my teeth." Out of this desolate pit, Job utters a rare, raw appeal to his friends: "Have pity on me, my friends, have pity!"—stop pursuing him the way God does (19:13–22).

Suddenly, Job's voice flares with a fierce longing to preserve his innocence for posterity. He cries, "Oh, that my words were recorded" in a scroll, or "inscribed with an iron stylus on lead, or chiseled in rock forever!" so future generations would know he was no evildoer (19:23–24).

Directly out of that wish for an enduring monument, Job's faith soars to the most celebrated pinnacle in the entire book: "But I know that my Redeemer lives, and in the end He will stand upon the earth." Even after his skin has been destroyed, he remains convinced: "yet in my flesh"—or apart from his flesh—"I will see God." He underscores it twice: "I will see Him for myself; my eyes will behold Him, and not as a stranger," until his heart faints with longing inside him (19:25–27).

Finally, Job turns back with a solemn warning for his friends: if they keep scheming to hound him and hunting for grounds to accuse him, they should fear the sword of punishment, "that you may know there is judgment" (19:28–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Often the keenest agony of suffering is being abandoned and misunderstood.** The heart of chapter 19 traces circle after circle of relationships pulling away from Job—brothers, kin, household staff, wife, intimate friends, even children (19:13–19). His plea "Have pity on me" (19:21) reminds us that hurting people need compassion, not chilly lectures.

**Real faith can ignite straight out of the pit of despair.** Only moments earlier Job felt God marching armies against him (19:12), yet a few verses later he confesses a living Redeemer who will rise to defend him (19:25). In Job, faith is not unbroken emotional calm, but holding fast to a Vindicator while every visible sign stays pitch-dark.

**Job's closing warning reverses the courtroom roles.** From the start, the three friends sat on the judge's bench condemning Job. In 19:28–29, Job warns them that hunting down an innocent sufferer is what truly deserves to fear God's judgment.

## Deeper Meaning

**The confession "I know that my Redeemer lives" (19:25) opens the horizon toward New Testament resurrection hope.** Although Job in his Old Testament setting did not yet possess full revelation concerning the resurrection, his yearning to behold God with his own eyes beyond the decay of his body touches the living seed of resurrection hope. In the New Testament, that hope becomes concrete certainty in Jesus, who rose as the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep (1 Corinthians 15:20–23), the Redeemer whom the church expects to see face to face as He truly is (1 John 3:2; Revelation 22:4).

**Job's path of universal rejection foreshadows the Suffering Servant and Jesus.** A righteous man shunned by loved ones, scorned publicly, and feeling struck by God (19:13–21) echoes the prophetic portrait of the Servant despised and rejected by mankind (Isaiah 53:3–4), fulfilled in Jesus when His disciples deserted Him on the night of His arrest (Matthew 26:56; Psalms 69:8).

## Notes & Nuances

**The term *Redeemer* (*go'el*) in 19:25 carries rich legal resonance and has prompted varied readings.** This is one of the most challenging passages in ancient Hebrew to translate. Under Old Testament law, the *go'el* was a kinsman-redeemer obligated to buy back family land, ransom an enslaved relative, or secure justice for a wronged family member (Leviticus 25:25; Ruth 3:12; Numbers 35:19). Many commentators understand Job to be trusting God Himself to stand as his *go'el* and clear his name—continuing the arc of his heavenly Witness (16:19) and Guarantor (17:3). Other modern scholars think Job imagines a distinct heavenly defender pleading his case before God.

**Verses 19:26–27 also allow two primary readings: *bodily resurrection* or *vindication/vision of God*.** The Hebrew phrase *mibbesari* in 19:26 can be translated either *apart from my flesh*—suggesting the soul beholding God after bodily decay—or *in my flesh* (as rendered by the Berean Standard Bible)—suggesting either bodily resurrection or seeing God while still alive in his wasted body. Longstanding Christian tradition—shaped by the Church Fathers and the Latin Vulgate—read this text as an early witness to bodily resurrection. Many modern scholars, noting Job's skepticism about afterlife return in chapters 14 and 17, read 19:25–27 as an expectant hope for a decisive vindication or encounter with God—either before death or as posthumous exoneration—which is partly realized when God appears to Job in chapters 38–42 and Job declares his own eyes have seen Him (42:5). Under either view, the anchor of Job's faith stands firm: death and injustice do not hold the last word, because his Vindicator lives.

**The phrase "ten times" (19:3) is an idiomatic round number for *again and again*.** In Scripture, the number ten commonly conveys repeated exasperation to the breaking point, as elsewhere in the Pentateuch (Genesis 31:7; Numbers 14:22), rather than an exact tally of ten speeches.
