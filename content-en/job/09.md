# Job 9 — Job Answers Bildad: If Only There Were a Mediator Between God and Me

## Story

Chapters 9 and 10 record Job's answer to Bildad. Job opens with a wry concession that he knows Bildad's broad generalizations are true, yet insists the real question lies elsewhere: "But how can a mortal be righteous before God?" If anyone wished to argue a case against God in court, he could not answer one of His questions out of a thousand. God is infinitely wise and overwhelmingly mighty; who has ever resisted Him and come out unscathed? (9:1–4).

To underscore the staggering mismatch between himself and his Creator, Job launches into a majestic yet chilling hymn to God's cosmic power. He moves mountains in anger, shakes the earth from its place, commands the sun not to rise, and seals off the stars. He alone stretches out the heavens, treads upon the waves of the sea, forms the constellations—the Bear, Orion, the Pleiades—and performs countless wonders beyond searching out. Yet this Almighty One moves invisibly and irresistibly: He passes by and Job does not see Him; He snatches away what He wills, and who dares ask Him what He is doing? Even the chaotic helpers of Rahab crouch beneath His feet (9:5–13).

Facing so overwhelming an opponent, Job sees every legal effort as hopeless. Even though he is righteous, he could never debate God—he could only plead for mercy from his Judge. Even if he called out and God answered, he would scarcely believe God truly listened, for God crushes him with a tempest and multiplies his wounds without cause, denying him even a moment to catch his breath. In strength, God is unstoppable; in justice, who could ever summon Him to court? Even though Job is innocent, his own trembling mouth would end up condemning him (9:14–20).

In his impasse, Job utters a daring claim that cuts straight across his friends' theology: he cares no longer for his own life, because it seems God destroys the blameless and the wicked alike. When sudden calamity strikes, the earth is handed over to the wicked and the faces of its judges are blindfolded—if it is not the Ruler of the universe who permits this, then who is it? His days race faster than a runner, skimming past like papyrus boats or an eagle swooping on prey. Even if he tried to force a smile and forget his grief, it would do no good, knowing God still will not hold him innocent; though he washed himself with snow and cleansed his hands with lye, God would plunge him into a slime pit until his own clothes detested him (9:21–31).

At the close of the chapter, Job names the deepest root of his tragedy: "For He is not a man like me, that I can answer Him, that we can take each other to court." He sighs with burning longing: "Nor is there a mediator between us, to lay his hand upon us both." If only God would lift His rod away and remove His terror, then Job could speak freely and present his case without dread—yet as things stand, no such advocate exists for him (9:32–35).

## Key Takeaways

**Job never denies God's power; what torments him is the sense that sheer power is drowning out justice.** His hymn to the One who moves mountains and shapes the constellations (9:5–10) reveals a reverence for God's majesty no less deep than his friends'. Yet precisely because God is vast and mortals are tiny, a fair courtroom hearing between the two feels impossible (9:14–19).

**One of the Bible's most fundamental questions rings out in 9:2.** When Job asks how a mortal can be righteous before God, he lifts the debate far beyond personal loss or illness, touching humanity's deepest dilemma before the Holy One.

**Job's yearning for a *mediator* in 9:33 marks an emotional and theological high point of the book.** Job longs for someone able to place one hand on God's shoulder and one hand on a human shoulder, bridging the infinite chasm between them so an innocent sufferer can be heard fairly.

## Deeper Meaning

**The sigh for a *mediator* in 9:33 finds its complete answer in Jesus Christ.** Job laments that no one stands on equal footing with God while standing close to humanity so as to lay a hand upon both (9:32–33). The New Testament announces the greatest good news in history: "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5). Because He is truly God and truly human, He is the Mediator of a new covenant who lays His hand on both God and humanity to reconcile the two (Hebrews 8:6; 9:15; 2 Corinthians 5:18–19).

**The question *How can a mortal be righteous before God?* (9:2) opens the path to the Gospel of justification by faith.** Job sees clearly that even if a person washes with snow and cleanses his hands with lye, human effort can never stand clean before God's holiness (9:30–31). The apostle Paul answers that exact question in Romans: no one is declared righteous before God by works of the law, yet God freely bestows righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ (Romans 3:20–24).

**The picture of God *treading upon the waves of the sea* (9:8) echoes in the miracle of Jesus.** When Jesus walks across the Sea of Galilee in the night storm and calms His terrified disciples (Mark 6:48–50; Matthew 14:25–27), He reveals Himself as the mighty Creator Job praised—yet no longer striding past in terrifying distance, but stepping right into the storm to save human beings.

## Notes & Nuances

**Courtroom and legal language surges to the forefront starting in chapter 9.** Terms such as *argue a case*, *Judge*, *summon*, *condemn*, and *mediator* show Job beginning to frame his fraught relationship with God as a lawsuit. This *lawsuit* metaphor becomes the structural backbone of Job's speeches all the way to his final oath in chapter 31.

**The name *Rahab* in 9:13 is not Rahab of Jericho from the book of Joshua.** In Old Testament poetry (26:12; Psalms 89:10; Isaiah 51:9), *Rahab* is a symbolic name for the mythic sea monster of chaos subdued by God, underscoring that no proud power in the cosmos can withstand His sovereignty.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, saw in 9:2 and 9:20 a penetrating confession of human helplessness before the absolute holiness of God. Calvin explained that while Job stood innocent of his friends' slanders, he still understood that before God's supreme tribunal no mortal can rely on self-righteousness, and can only appeal to mercy (9:15). *(Sermons on Job, section on chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic Reformed theological reading of Calvin on Job 9:2 and 9:15–20, without verifying the specific sermon -->

**Matthew Henry**, eighteenth-century English commentator, read 9:33 in bright New Testament light. He observed that Job's longing for an umpire to lay his hand upon both parties voices the natural cry of the human conscience across the gulf separating us from the Almighty, and that Jesus Christ Himself has come to fill the very breach Job feared could never be bridged. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Job 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking Job 9:33 to Christ's mediatorship in 1 Timothy 2:5 is the classic and best-known point in Matthew Henry's notes on Job 9:33 -->

**Gregory the Great**, sixth-century church father, likewise treated 9:33 as a longing prophecy pointing toward the incarnate Mediator. In his reading, because humanity cannot meet God on equal terms, the Savior took on both divinity and humanity so that He could truly place one hand on the Father and one hand on frail mortals. *(Morals on the Book of Job, book 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Gregory's christological reading of Job 9:32–33 in book 9 of Morals on the Book of Job is well known, though exact section numbers were not verified -->

**Francis I. Andersen**, twentieth-century Australian Old Testament scholar, drew attention to the boldness—yet not unbelief—of 9:22–24.
