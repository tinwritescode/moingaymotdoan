# Job 25 — Bildad's Brief Finale: Mortals Are Like Worms Before God

## Story

Bildad the Shuhite speaks for the third and final time in the book, and his speech is strikingly brief—a mere six verses. He makes no further effort to debate the fate of the wicked or answer Job's probing questions about social injustice in chapter 24; instead, he simply restates God's awe-inspiring transcendence. "Dominion and awe belong to God"; He establishes peace in His high places, His heavenly armies are beyond numbering, and "Upon whom does His light not rise?" (25:1–3).

From that grandeur, Bildad reprises the old question Eliphaz already posed twice before (4:17; 15:14): "How then can a man be just before God?" How can anyone born of woman be pure? If even "the moon does not shine, and the stars are not pure in His sight," how much less "man, who is but a maggot, and the son of man, who is only a worm!" (25:4–6).

## Key Takeaways

**The brevity of chapter 25 reveals that the three friends have run out of arguments.** Across the three debate cycles, their speeches shrink until they fall silent—Eliphaz speaks thirty verses in chapter 22, Bildad manages just six verses in chapter 25, and Zophar never speaks again. Bildad does not touch the real-world injustices Job raised in chapters 21 and 24, retreating instead to a generic theological slogan.

**Exalting God's holiness by degrading humanity distorts the truth.** God is indeed infinitely majestic and holy, yet Bildad wields that majesty to smother every cry for justice from Job, dismissing human beings as worthless maggots and worms unworthy of God's hearing (25:6).

**The question "How then can a man be just before God?" (25:4) is the right question, yet Bildad has no answer.** Looking solely at the gulf between the Almighty and flawed mortals leaves humanity completely deadlocked. The answer lies neither in self-defense nor in self-degradation, but in God's own justifying grace.

## Deeper Meaning

**Viewing humanity as mere worms (25:6) must be balanced alongside the dignity God bestows on human beings throughout Scripture.** The Bible candidly acknowledges human frailty and sinfulness (Psalms 22:6; Isaiah 41:14), yet at the same time affirms that humans are created in the image of God (Genesis 1:26–27) and crowned with glory and honor (Psalms 8:4–6). Humanity is never greater than God, yet neither are we worthless vermin in His eyes.

**Bildad's question in 25:4 opens straight onto the heart of the New Testament Gospel.** How can a mortal stand righteous before a holy God? The apostle Paul answers decisively in Romans: no one will be justified in His sight by works of the law, yet all who believe in Jesus are justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus (Romans 3:20–24; 2 Corinthians 5:21).

## Notes & Nuances

**Bildad's speech is preserved to show the friends' stalemate, not as God-approved theology.** At the end of the book, God rebukes the three companions for failing to speak rightly about Him as Job did (42:7). Chapter 25 is a textbook example of uttering words that sound reverent toward God while using them to silence a hurting person.

**Chapter 25 is the shortest chapter in the entire book of Job.** Some commentators have suspected that part of Bildad's speech was lost or merged into chapter 26, yet many scholars recognize the six-verse length as deliberate literary artistry: battered by Job's incisive rebuttals, the friends have truly run dry, muttering a few recycled lines before lapsing into silence.
