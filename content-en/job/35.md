# Job 35 — Do Human Sin or Righteousness Affect God?

## Story

Elihu delivers his third speech—the briefest of his four addresses—speaking directly to Job and the listening friends. He challenges what he believes is Job's posture: claiming to be more righteous than God and asking what personal advantage there is in avoiding sin (35:1–4).

To answer, Elihu tells Job to gaze up at the skies and look at the clouds towering high above him. God dwells loftily beyond mortal reach. If Job sins—even if his offenses multiply—what harm does that inflict on God? Conversely, if Job is righteous, what gift does he add to God? Human wickedness harms only fellow mortals, and human righteousness benefits only other human beings (35:5–8).

Elihu next explains why so many cries of distress seem to go unanswered by heaven. Crushed beneath the arm of the mighty, people cry out loudly for relief from oppression. Yet they cry out purely because of pain, never genuinely seeking "God my Maker, who gives songs in the night," who teaches humans more than the beasts of the earth and makes us wiser than the birds of the air. When people shout in empty pride without turning to Him, the Almighty does not answer (35:9–13).

Elihu turns that diagnosis straight onto Job: Job keeps saying he cannot see God, yet his case rests right before God, and he must wait patiently for Him. He ends sharply by remarking that only because God's anger has not yet struck harder does Job keep opening his mouth to multiply empty, uninformed talk (35:14–16).

## Key Takeaways

**God is wholly self-sufficient and never depends on human beings.** Our sin does not diminish God's holiness, nor do our good deeds enrich Him or put Him in our debt (35:6–7). Virtue is never a business deal forcing God to pay wages.

**There is a difference between groaning under pain and genuinely seeking God.** Elihu touches on a real insight: many oppressed people only want relief from discomfort rather than turning their hearts to their Maker—the One who "gives songs in the night" (35:9–10).

**Pushing God's transcendence too far risks painting a detached, unfeeling Deity.** Elihu's claim that neither human sin nor righteousness touches God (35:6–7) is true in the sense that God does not depend on us to exist, yet it can easily imply that God sits coldly aloof from human grief and loyalty.

## Deeper Meaning

**God needs nothing from human hands, yet cares deeply about human beings.** Elihu's logic in 35:7 resonates with Paul's proclamation in Athens that God is not served by human hands as though He needed anything (Acts 17:25), and his rhetorical question: who has ever given to God that God should repay him (Romans 11:35)? Yet the Gospel reveals that the One exalted above the clouds is never cold or detached: He grieves over sin and rejoices over a single sinner who repents (Luke 15:7; Ephesians 4:30).

**Songs in the night mark genuine faith.** The image of the Maker who "gives songs in the night" (35:10) comes vibrantly alive in the New Testament when Paul and Silas—beaten and fastened in stocks inside a Philippian prison—pray and sing hymns to God at midnight (Acts 16:25).

## Notes & Nuances

**Once again, Elihu misreads the heart of Job's question.** In 35:2–3, Elihu faults Job as though Job were arguing that righteous living has no payoff. Back in chapter 1 (1:9–11), it was actually Satan who questioned whether Job feared God for nothing; Job has held onto integrity even after losing every blessing. Job's real question is not *does goodness pay?*, but *why is an innocent person being treated like a criminal?*

**God Himself will soon reuse the sky imagery—yet to a very different effect.** Elihu tells Job to gaze up at the clouds to grasp how remote and unaffected God is (35:5–7). In chapter 38, God Himself invites Job to lift his eyes to the skies and cosmos, not to prove His aloofness, but to display His intimate wisdom and tender care over creation.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, agreed with Elihu that mortals can never turn good works into a claim putting God in their debt, since every good thing we hold already comes from Him. **Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, lingered lovingly over 35:10 on God who gives songs in the night.
