# Job 37 — The Thunder of the Almighty

## Story

Elihu delivers the finale of his fourth speech—and his final words in the entire book. Awe surges unmistakably as the storm appears to roll directly overhead: his heart pounds and leaps from its place. He urges everyone to listen closely to the thunderous roar and rumbling voice rolling out of God's mouth. Lightning flashes to the ends of the earth, followed by His majestic roaring voice—God performs mighty wonders far beyond human comprehension (37:1–5).

Elihu continues celebrating God's sovereignty across every season of the year. He commands the snow to fall upon the earth and sends pouring showers and heavy rainstorms. Winter tempests bring every human hand to a standstill so all mortals recognize His work, while wild animals retreat into their dens and lairs. Whirlwinds sweep out of the south and biting cold arrives from the north; by the mere breath of God, broad waters freeze solid into ice (37:6–10).

He loads thick clouds with moisture and scatters His lightning through them. Guided by His design, the clouds swirl across the whole earth to execute every command He assigns them—whether "for correction, or for His land, or for loving devotion" (37:11–13).

Here Elihu addresses Job by name once more and urges him: "Stand still and consider the wonders of God." He fires off a string of unanswerable questions—an unmistakable prelude to God's own whirlwind interrogation in chapter 38: Does Job know how God directs the clouds and makes His lightning flash? Does he grasp how rain-laden clouds hang poised in midair—marvels of the One perfect in knowledge? Does he know why his clothes grow stifling hot when the south wind hushes the earth? Can he join God in spreading out the skies, hard as a mirror of cast bronze? (37:14–18).

With gentle irony, Elihu says if Job is so confident, let him teach us what to say to God, since mortals groping in darkness cannot even marshal a proper case before Him—would anyone dare demand an audience to dispute with God without fearing being swallowed up? (37:19–20).

Closing his speech, Elihu lifts his eyes skyward: blinding brightness hidden behind the clouds breaks through once a passing wind clears the sky, and "out of the north He comes in golden splendor," announcing God's awesome majesty. He seals his address with a solemn confession of the Almighty: we cannot reach Him; He is exalted in power, rich in justice and righteousness, and never oppresses. Therefore mortals revere Him, for "He does not regard any who are wise in heart" (37:21–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Standing still to behold God's works restores us to our proper scale.** Elihu's counsel—"Stand still and consider the wonders of God" (37:14)—ranks among his loveliest lines. Stepping back from heated argument to contemplate sky, clouds, snow, and storm naturally cools human self-importance.

**The exact same natural event can serve very different purposes in God's hands.** Elihu recognizes that God sends storm clouds "whether for correction, or for His land, or for loving devotion" (37:13). Outwardly it looks like the very same storm, reminding us not to jump to conclusions about God's motives by sight alone.

**Reverence and humility remain the only fitting posture before the Almighty.** Humans cannot fully fathom God, yet we can trust that He unites absolute power with spotless justice and never oppresses (37:23–24).

## Deeper Meaning

**Storm and blazing radiance are classic signs of God's coming.** Thunder, lightning, fierce wind, and golden splendor at the end of chapter 37 lead directly into the LORD speaking out of the whirlwind in 38:1—paralleling His appearance at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19:16–19) and Ezekiel's vision of a great storm wind roaring out of the north wrapped in brilliant light (Ezekiel 1:4).

**God resists the self-styled wise and shows grace to the humble.** Elihu's final line—that God "does not regard any who are wise in heart" (37:24)—echoes across Scripture, from Proverbs 3:34 to Jesus thanking the Father for hiding these things from the wise and learned while revealing them to little children (Matthew 11:25), and the New Testament teaching that God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5).

## Notes & Nuances

**The line "Out of the north He comes in golden splendor" (37:22) resonates with ancient Near Eastern imagery.** In ancient literature, the far north (*Mount Zaphon*) symbolized the majestic dwelling place of deity—imagery the Bible poetically applies to Mount Zion as well (Psalms 48:2). Here, dazzling golden light breaking from the north heralds the LORD's imminent arrival in the storm.

**A balanced portrait of Elihu as he quietly exits the stage.** At the close of chapter 37, Elihu disappears from the book without a single mention in chapter 42. Looking back over chapters 32–37, two sides stand out clearly: he remains young, brash, at times self-assured and short on empathy for Job's pain; yet he also advances beyond the three older friends by recognizing suffering as gracious discipline—and especially in chapters 36–37, lifting everyone's eyes away from human wrangling toward the Creator just before God breaks His silence.
