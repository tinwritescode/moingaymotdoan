# Job 6 — Job Answers Eliphaz: Unbearable Pain and Dried-Up Friendship

## Story

Chapters 6 and 7 form Job's first reply to Eliphaz. In chapter 6, Job directs his words squarely at his friends. First, he defends the fierce words he poured out in chapter 3. He wishes his anguish and calamities could be placed on a balance scale, for they would outweigh the sands of the sea; it is that crushing weight that made his words wild and unguarded. These are not random misfortunes—he feels as though the poisoned arrows of the Almighty are embedded in his body, while God's terrors draw up in battle array against him. A wild donkey does not bray when it has grass, nor an ox bellow over its fodder; likewise, Job does not groan without reason. The bland counsel of his friends tastes like unsalted food or flavorless egg white, and his soul cannot stomach it (6:1–7).

Job returns to the sole wish he has left: he begs God to grant his request and crush him to death. If he could die soon, he would still carry one comfort amid unrelenting agony—that he has never denied the words of the Holy One. He asks where he could possibly find the strength to wait for the bright restoration Eliphaz just painted: "Is my strength like stone, or my flesh made of bronze?" There is no power left inside him to rescue himself (6:8–13).

From verse 14 onward, Job pours out deep disappointment toward the very friends sitting in front of him. He recalls a basic moral duty: a despairing person deserves kindness and loyalty from friends, lest he forsake the fear of the Almighty. Yet his friends have betrayed that trust, proving as unreliable as seasonal desert streams. In winter, swollen with melting snow and ice, the wadis run dark and full; yet in scorching summer, when caravans from Tema and Sheba turn aside searching for water, the streambeds evaporate dry, leaving the travelers ashamed and dying of thirst. Job's friends are just the same: the moment they see his terrifying calamity, they recoil in fear and offer no real help (6:14–21).

At the close of the chapter, Job challenges his friends to speak plainly and fairly. He never asked them for money, bribes, or deliverance from an enemy's hand. He is ready to sit quiet and listen if they will show him specifically where he has gone wrong. Honest words carry real force, whereas their veiled reproofs prove nothing and merely pick apart the wind-blown speech of a desperate man. In deep bitterness, he compares them to men who cast lots over an orphan and barter away their own friend. He pleads with them to look him straight in the face and reconsider, to stop acting unjustly, for his cause remains righteous and his tongue knows how to discern malice (6:22–30).

## Key Takeaways

**Real suffering cannot be weighed on scales of dry moralizing.** Job defends a sufferer's right to cry out: his protest is not baseless rebellion, but the honest response of a human being made of flesh and blood, not stone or bronze (6:5; 6:12).

**A lack of mercy from friends can hurt nearly as sharply as the original blow.** The picture of a desert stream running dry in summer heat (6:15–20) is one of Scripture's keenest metaphors for fair-weather friendship: overflowing when life is smooth, yet vanishing out of fear and judgment at the exact moment a friend needs water to survive.

**Even at the bottom of despair, Job clings tightly to faithfulness toward God.** His reason for wanting an early death in 6:9–10 is not to escape God, but the fear that if agony drags past his breaking point he might stumble and deny the words of the Holy One. That alone provides living proof that Satan's accusation in chapters 1–2 was false.

## Deeper Meaning

**Human fragility is understood from the inside by our Savior Himself.** Job's question—whether his strength is stone or his flesh bronze (6:12)—voices the cry of every finite human pressed beneath affliction. The New Testament tells us Jesus did not stand aloof like Job's friends to lecture from afar, but truly shared in our flesh and blood, endured testing and suffering, and therefore sympathizes completely with our weaknesses (Hebrews 2:14–18; 4:15).

**Mercy is the foremost measure of how we treat the despairing.** The principle Job reminds his friends of in 6:14—that a person in despair needs loyal kindness from friends—connects directly to New Testament commands to bear one another's burdens and so fulfill the law of Christ (Galatians 6:2), and to help the weak rather than nitpicking their words when they lose heart (1 Thessalonians 5:14).

## Notes & Nuances

**Job's words in 6:4 show that he views God as the direct source of his calamity.** Unaware of the heavenly exchange between God and Satan in chapters 1–2, Job perceives only the Almighty's arrows piercing him. His inward tension runs so deep precisely because he believes everything rests in God's hands while knowing he has committed no sin worthy of such wrath.

**The desert streambed (*wadi*) imagery in 6:15–20 reflects ancient Near Eastern geography.** Across the arid regions around Arabia and Edom, many gullies rush with water during winter snowmelt and rains, then bake bone-dry in summer. Trade caravans from Tema and Sheba—major Arabian commercial centers—depended on these watering places to stay alive; finding a dry bed meant mortal crisis.

**Several verses here—6:7, 6:10, 6:14, and 6:30—contain compressed or idiomatic Hebrew phrasing.** In 6:30, for instance, the Hebrew imagery refers to Job's palate still being able to taste calamity or malice—meaning his conscience and moral discernment have not been numbed by pain.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, sympathized deeply with 6:26, where Job reproaches his friends for nitpicking the words of a desperate man.
