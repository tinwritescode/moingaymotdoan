# Job 14 — Job Reflects on Life's Brevity and the Question of Resurrection

## Story

Chapter 14 closes Job's extended reply and brings the entire first cycle of debates to an end. Job is still addressing God directly, yet his tone shifts from fierce courtroom challenge to quiet, aching meditation on the fragile lot of all humankind. He opens with famous lines: "Man, who is born of woman, is short of days and full of trouble." Mortals spring up like a flower and wither away, fleeting like a shadow that never stays. Why would God fix His eyes in strict judgment on a creature so frail and inherently flawed—for who can bring what is clean out of what is unclean? Since human days are already numbered and bounded by limits no one can cross, Job asks God to look away from them so they may enjoy a little rest until their hired day is done (14:1–6).

From life's brevity, Job draws a poignant contrast between a tree and a human being. Even a cut-down tree still has hope: though its roots grow old in the ground and its stump seems dead in the dust, at the mere scent of water it buds again and puts forth fresh shoots like a sapling. By contrast, when a mortal dies and breathes his last, where is he? Like water vanishing from a lake or a riverbed drying up, a human lies down and does not rise; until the heavens are no more, people do not awake or rouse from their sleep (14:7–12).

Yet right when darkness seems thickest, a breathtaking spark of longing flashes inside Job's prayer. He cries out: "If only You would hide me in Sheol and conceal me until Your anger has passed! If only You would appoint a time for me and then remember me!" Then he voices one of life's supreme questions: "When a man dies, will he live again?" If such a day truly existed, he would gladly wait through every hard day of his service until his relief came; then God would call and Job would answer, and God would long once more for the creature His own hands made (14:13–15).

No sooner does that beam of light appear than present reality clouds it over again. Job returns to the weight pressing on him now: God still counts every step he takes, keeps watch over his sins, and seals his offenses in a bag like stored evidence. Just as mountains erode and crumble, rocks shift from their places, flowing water wears away stone, and torrents wash away soil, so God destroys human hope. God overpowers mortals and sends them away; once gone, they never know whether their children come to honor or sink into disgrace—all that remains is the lonely ache of their own flesh and the mourning of their own soul (14:16–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Human frailty and brevity form the most natural plea for God's mercy.** Job does not invoke human weakness to excuse wickedness, but to beg the Almighty not to deal relentlessly with a creature as fleeting as a blossom or a passing shadow (14:1–6).

**Even in deep despair, faith plants the seed of a question that reaches beyond the grave.** Verses 14:13–15 show Job beginning to imagine a possibility never glimpsed in earlier chapters: Sheol might not be a permanent dead end, but a temporary shelter until an appointed hour when God remembers him and calls his name.

**Job refuses hollow optimism, oscillating honestly between a flicker of hope and heavy reality.** Unlike his friends, who always close their speeches with slick happy endings, Job lets the bright hope of 14:13–15 sit right next to the gloom of 14:18–22. That push and pull mirrors the lived experience of believers still walking through dark valleys.

## Deeper Meaning

**The question *When a man dies, will he live again?* (14:14) receives its decisive answer in the resurrection of Jesus Christ.** In the Old Testament, Job could only wish through the mist for an appointed hour when God would call and he would answer (14:13–15). In the New Testament, Jesus stands before the tomb of Lazarus and declares plainly: I am the resurrection and the life; whoever believes in Me will live, even though he dies (John 11:25–26). Christ truly rose as the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep, guaranteeing that death never gets the last word (1 Corinthians 15:20–22; 15:54–57).

**The vision *You will call and I will answer* (14:15) reaches complete fulfillment on the day Christ returns.** Job longed for the day God would yearn for the work of His hands and summon him out of the grave. The New Testament confirms that exact moment is coming when the trumpet of God sounds and the dead in Christ rise first to meet the Lord (John 5:28–29; 1 Thessalonians 4:16–17). Even Job's phrase *until the heavens are no more* in 14:12 unwittingly brushes against the final horizon of a new heaven and a new earth (2 Peter 3:10–13; Revelation 21:1).

**Human helplessness before the question *Who can bring what is clean out of what is unclean?* (14:4) is resolved only by Christ.** Humanity cannot generate purity out of a fallen nature (Romans 3:23). Only through the Holy Spirit and the blood of Christ does God make sinners new creations, clean in His sight (John 3:5–6; Titus 3:5).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 14 closes the first cycle of debates (chapters 4–14).** All three friends—Eliphaz in chapters 4–5, Bildad in chapter 8, and Zophar in chapter 11—have laid out the same rigid retribution doctrine, yet none has convinced or comforted Job. Instead, the more they argue, the further they drive Job away from themselves and straight toward God with his questions and longings.

**The metaphor of a felled tree sprouting at the scent of water (14:7–9) reflects the familiar olive tree of the Mediterranean and Near East.** Even cut down to a gnarled, centuries-old stump, an olive root can send up vibrant green shoots when rain arrives. Job chooses this everyday sight to sharpen humanity's anguish over the seeming finality of death.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, observed that 14:1–4 offers an unvarnished summary of both the brevity and the fallen imperfection of human life. Turning to 14:13–15, Calvin saw that although clouds of affliction still obscured Job's faith and pulled him back into sorrow at chapter's end, his longing to be remembered by God beyond death revealed a spark of grace planted by God in the believing heart. *(Sermons on Job, section on chapter 14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: fits Calvin's Reformed reading of 14:1–4 and the tension of faith in 14:13–22, without checking the specific sermon -->

**Gregory the Great**, sixth-century church father, read the contrast between a sprouting tree and a dying mortal (14:7–12) as a reminder of how empty earthly pride is. In his reading, when Job asked to be hidden in Sheol until God's wrath subsided (14:13), he voiced the deep yearning of Old Testament saints awaiting the day Christ would descend and deliver those waiting for Him. *(Morals on the Book of Job, book 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: aligns with Gregory's christological reading of Job 14 in book 12 of Morals on the Book of Job, though exact section numbers were not verified -->
