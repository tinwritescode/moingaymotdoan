# Job 20 — Zophar Insists the Joy of the Wicked Is Brief

## Story

Zophar the Naamathite rises to answer Job, delivering his second and final speech in the book. Stung by Job's warning about judgment at the close of chapter 19, Zophar says his disturbed thoughts drive him to reply at once because he has heard a rebuke that insults him (20:1–3).

Zophar appeals to ancient consensus stretching back "ever since man was placed on the earth": "the mirth of the wicked is brief," and the joy of the godless lasts only a moment. "Though his arrogance reaches the heavens" and his head brushes the clouds, he will still "perish forever, like his own dung." He vanishes like a dream or a fleeting night vision so completely that acquaintances can no longer spot a trace of him; his children must appease the poor, and the youthful vigor in his bones lies down with him in the dust (20:4–11).

Zophar crafts a vivid metaphor of greed and its aftermath. At first, "evil is sweet in his mouth," and he holds it lovingly beneath his tongue, unwilling to let it go; yet once inside his stomach, that delicacy turns into "the venom of cobras within him." He "swallows wealth, only to vomit it up," because God drives ill-gotten gain straight back out of his belly. Viper poison kills him, and he never gets to gaze upon streams flowing with honey and curds (20:12–17).

Zophar states the reason for this downfall bluntly: the man must return the fruit of his labor without enjoying it because "he has oppressed and abandoned the poor" and seized houses he did not build. Insatiable greed denies him peace; "in the midst of his plenty, he will be distressed," and the full force of misery overtakes him (20:18–22).

The closing section portrays God's direct fury crashing down on the evildoer right as he fills his belly. Fleeing an iron weapon, he is pierced by a bronze arrow; pulling the shaft from his back brings sheer terror upon him. Total darkness waits like a stored treasure for his wealth; "an unfanned fire will consume him" and devour everything left in his tent. Both heaven and earth rise up to expose his guilt, and a flood sweeps his house away on the day of God's wrath. Zophar seals his speech with a crisp verdict: "This is the wicked man's portion from God" (20:23–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Defending bruised pride quickly turns dialogue into an assault.** Zophar begins from personal offense (20:2–3), so instead of hearing Job's plea for pity in chapter 19, he fires off the harshest tirade yet on the punishment of greedy oppressors.

**Sin tastes sweet at first yet ends in bitter poison.** The picture of evil lingering sweetly under the tongue before turning to cobra venom inside the stomach (20:12–16) is a sharp psychological and moral insight into greed. Zophar's grave sin is pinning that portrait of an exploiter onto Job without a shred of proof.

**The fleeting nature of unjust gain cannot explain every tragedy.** Zophar assumes that anyone stripped of wealth with fire consuming his tent (20:26; 20:28)—chillingly mirroring Job's disasters in chapter 1—must be a wicked oppressor under divine punishment. That one-way logic is exactly what Job demolishes in chapter 21.

## Deeper Meaning

**Scripture affirms the fragility of unjust wealth elsewhere, yet always in light of final judgment.** In the parable of the rich fool, Jesus likewise warns that one who hoards solely for himself can lose everything on a single fateful night (Luke 12:16–21), and James warns rich oppressors of coming judgment for defrauding laborers (James 5:1–6). Zophar's flaw is not believing in God's justice, but demanding that God settle every account instantaneously in this life.

**Wealth turning from sweetness to poison illustrates the self-destructive pull of greed.** The New Testament teaches that the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil, piercing those who chase it with many griefs (1 Timothy 6:9–10). Lasting joy never comes from hoarded possessions, but from godliness with contentment.

## Notes & Nuances

**Zophar's speech is not God's endorsed explanation for Job's suffering.** At the end of the book, God rebukes the three friends for failing to speak rightly about Him (42:7). Chapter 20 displays their deadlock vividly: rather than grappling with innocent suffering, Zophar merely replays the refrain that evildoers perish—only louder and harsher.

**This is Zophar's final speech in the book of Job.** In the third debate cycle (chapters 22–27), Eliphaz speaks in chapter 22, Bildad manages only a tiny speech in chapter 25, and Zophar says nothing at all. Many commentators take his silence as a sign that the friends have run out of ammunition against the real-world evidence Job presents.

**The phrase "an unfanned fire" (20:26) points metaphorically to fire sent from heaven.** In chapter 1, fire from God fell from the sky and burned up Job's sheep and servants (1:16). When Zophar invokes fire not kindled by human hands and wealth swept away, his imagery slashes straight at Job's rawest losses.
