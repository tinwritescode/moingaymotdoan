# Job 32 — Elihu Steps Forward

## Story

The narrator returns to open a new phase of the book. Job's three friends have stopped answering because Job remained righteous in his own eyes. At that moment, a younger bystander who has been listening silently all along steps forward: Elihu son of Barakel the Buzite, from the clan of Ram. His anger burns against both sides—against Job for justifying himself rather than God, and against the three friends because they found no convincing answer yet went right on condemning Job. Elihu had waited patiently because all of them were older than he; seeing the three elders out of words, however, his anger finally boils over (32:1–5).

Elihu speaks up, addressing the three older men first. He explains that his youth held him back in deference, assuming that advancing years naturally brought wisdom. Yet he has come to see that true insight comes not from sheer length of years, but from "a spirit in a man, the breath of the Almighty, that gives him understanding"—even the great and the aged do not automatically discern what is right. Therefore he asks them to hear him out so he too may state what he knows (32:6–10).

Elihu reminds them that he waited attentively through their whole debate, yet not one of them refuted Job or answered his arguments. He warns them not to boast that they found wisdom and then wash their hands of the matter by saying only God can defeat Job. Since Job has not directed his words at Elihu, Elihu promises not to answer Job using their worn-out speeches (32:11–14).

Turning as if addressing onlookers or musing aloud in the heavy silence, Elihu remarks that the three friends stand dismayed and speechless. He asks how he can keep waiting when they have nothing left to add. Now it is his turn to speak, for he is bursting with words, swollen inside "like unvented wine" ready to burst new wineskins. He must open his lips to find relief. Finally, Elihu vows to speak candidly without showing partiality or flattering anyone—for if he dared flatter, his Maker would swiftly sweep him away (32:15–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Age and status do not automatically guarantee wisdom.** Elihu honored social deference by listening silently first, yet he asserts a vital principle: true understanding springs from the breath of the Almighty, not merely from accumulating birthdays (32:6–9).

**Both sides of the debate had genuine blind spots worth confronting.** The three friends condemned Job without proof; meanwhile Job, pushed by intense pain, pressed his self-defense perilously close to elevating his own righteousness above God's (32:2–3). Both extremes called for examination.

**Zeal for truth can easily tangle with brash self-importance.** Elihu has fair reason to be frustrated with both sides, yet he spends an entire twenty-two-verse chapter mostly announcing that he is about to speak and marveling at how full of insight he is (32:18–20). Passionate youth can easily trip over its own eagerness.

## Deeper Meaning

**True wisdom comes from the Spirit of God.** Elihu's insight that "the breath of the Almighty... gives him understanding" (32:8) echoes the scene of God breathing the breath of life into humanity at creation (Genesis 2:7). In the New Testament, the apostle Paul unfolds this truth further: no one comprehends the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God, and believers receive the Holy Spirit so they may understand what God has freely given (1 Corinthians 2:11–12).

**Younger believers can still speak genuine truth when they keep an upright heart.** Elihu's boldness in speaking up despite being the youngest in the room recalls Paul's encouragement to Timothy: let no one despise your youth, but set an example in speech, conduct, love, faith, and purity (1 Timothy 4:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**Commentators differ sharply in how they evaluate Elihu.** Appearing only across chapters 32–37, he is never mentioned in the prologue (chapters 1–2) and receives neither praise nor rebuke from God in the epilogue (chapter 42). Three broad viewpoints have emerged. A positive reading sees Elihu as a wiser messenger than the three older friends, preparing Job's heart before God speaks from the whirlwind—and notes that God spares him the rebuke aimed at the other three in chapter 42. A critical reading views him as a pompous, long-winded youth who boasts of his insight only to recycle the same retribution mold as the older friends, explaining why God ignores him completely in chapters 38 and 42. A middle reading acknowledges that Elihu spots genuine errors in both Job and the friends and contributes the idea that God can use suffering as loving discipline rather than sheer punishment, yet still lacks tender compassion and falls short of the ultimate answer that only God Himself can deliver.

**New wineskins were a familiar sight in the ancient world.** Wine was stored in sewn goatskin pouches; as fresh wine fermented, expanding gas stretched the leather and could rupture old or unvented skins—the same everyday image Jesus later used when speaking of new wine and fresh wineskins (Matthew 9:17). Elihu borrows the metaphor to describe the pressure of words bursting to get out of him (32:19).
