# Job 34 — God Never Acts Unjustly

## Story

Job makes no reply, so Elihu launches into his second speech. This time he calls upon "you wise men" and "you men of learning" present—the three older friends and any onlookers—to weigh together what is right, reminding them that the ear tests words just as the palate tastes food (34:1–4).

Elihu cites what he considers Job's most outrageous statements: Job claimed he is righteous yet God took away his justice, making him look like a liar and inflicting an incurable wound despite his innocence. Elihu goes so far as to accuse Job of drinking up scoffing like water and keeping company with evildoers by claiming a person gains nothing from trying to please God (34:5–9).

From there, Elihu proclaims the centerpiece of the entire chapter: "Far be it from God to do wrong, and from the Almighty to act unjustly." God repays each person according to their deeds. To back this up, Elihu offers two lines of reasoning. First, God governs the entire earth on His own supreme authority—no one appointed Him or handed Him His throne. If He simply withdrew His spirit and breath to Himself, all flesh would perish instantly and return to dust. A Sovereign who generously sustains every living creature cannot possibly hate justice (34:10–17).

Second, God is a completely impartial Judge. People scarcely dare call earthly kings scoundrels or nobles wicked—how much less God, who shows no partiality to princes and does not favor the rich over the poor, for all alike are the work of His hands! He can shatter mighty oppressors in a single moment at midnight without lifting a human hand. His eyes watch every human step, and no deep shadow can hide wrongdoers. Because He knows every deed completely, He needs no drawn-out trial to overthrow tyrants and hear the cry of the crushed poor (34:18–28).

Elihu emphasizes God's absolute freedom: when He remains quiet or grants peace, who can condemn Him? When He hides His face, who can behold Him? He governs both nations and individuals alike so godless rulers do not ensnare the people (34:29–30).

Finally, Elihu prescribes what Job ought to say to God: instead of protesting, a sufferer should humbly accept discipline, ask God to teach what he cannot see, and vow to do evil no more. He asks whether God must tailor justice to suit Job's preferences, then asserts confidently that every wise listener will agree Job speaks without knowledge. Carried away by zeal, Elihu even wishes Job "were tried to the utmost" for answering like wicked men and adding rebellion to his sin (34:31–37).

## Key Takeaways

**Justice is inseparable from God's very nature.** The declaration "Far be it from God to do wrong" (34:10) forms a bedrock conviction of biblical faith. Elihu stands on solid ground defending God's character: the One who freely sustains the breath of every creature cannot be an unjust tyrant (34:14–15).

**God judges fairly without favoring wealth or status.** Every human being—from princes to the destitute—is equally the handiwork of God (34:19). He sees through every cover of darkness and hears the cry of the oppressed (34:22; 34:28).

**Defending truth without mercy swiftly turns cruel.** Sound as Elihu's theology of divine justice sounds, his ending exposes his gravest flaw: wishing that Job "were tried to the utmost" (34:36). When zeal for an abstract doctrine overshadows the bleeding human being sitting right in front of us, even right statements turn ice-cold.

## Deeper Meaning

**God sustains all life and shows zero partiality.** Elihu's confession that every creature lives by God's breath and returns to dust if He withdraws it (34:14–15) echoes Psalms 104:29 and Paul's speech in Athens proclaiming that God "gives everyone life and breath and everything else" (Acts 17:25). The truth that He does not favor the rich over the poor (34:19) likewise anchors New Testament church ethics (Romans 2:11; Ephesians 6:9).

**Paul revisits the exact question *Is God unjust?* in Romans.** Wrestling with God's unfathomable sovereignty, Paul confronts that same question and answers decisively: "Certainly not!" (Romans 9:14). Unlike Elihu, however, who expects tidy instant payback here and now, the New Testament reveals God's full justice supremely at the cross and on the final day of judgment (Romans 3:25–26).

## Notes & Nuances

**Elihu pins a saying on Job that Job never claimed as his own.** In 34:9, Elihu charges Job with saying it profits a person nothing to take delight in God. In reality, back in 21:15, Job was *quoting the defiant boast of the wicked* to show that evildoers often go unpunished. Pulling that line out of context and attributing it directly to Job makes Elihu's indictment unfair.

**In chapter 34, Elihu's argument begins sliding back toward the three older friends.** In chapter 33 he introduced a fresh angle on suffering as preventive discipline. Here, however, asserting that God "repays a man according to his deeds" (34:11) and accusing Job of piling rebellion onto his sin (34:37), he drifts close to the rigid retribution mold of Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar.
