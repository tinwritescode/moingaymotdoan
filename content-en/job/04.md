# Job 4 — Eliphaz Begins: You Reap What You Sow

## Story

After Job curses the day of his birth in chapter 3, the first cycle of debates gets underway. The first to speak is Eliphaz the Temanite, likely the eldest and most measured of the three friends. He opens cautiously, asking leave to speak because he can no longer hold back his words. First, he recalls Job's honorable past: Job had instructed many, strengthened weak hands, and steadied faltering knees. Yet now that trouble touches him personally, Job grows dismayed and loses his footing. Eliphaz urges him to draw confidence from his fear of God and the integrity of his ways (4:1–6).

From gentle encouragement, Eliphaz pivots to the core premise of his argument: an airtight law of moral retribution. He poses two rhetorical questions: "Consider now: Who, being innocent, has ever perished? Where were the upright ever destroyed?" By his own observation, those who plow iniquity and sow trouble reap the very same. Even fierce evildoers who roar like a pride of lions eventually have their teeth broken by the breath of God's anger, run out of prey, and scatter (4:7–11).

To lend extra gravity to his point, Eliphaz recounts a haunting nighttime encounter. While deep sleep fell on mortals, a spirit glided past his face, making his bones tremble and the hair on his body stand on end. From a shadowy form he could not make out came a whispered voice: "Can a mortal be more righteous than God, or a man more pure than his Maker?" (4:12–17).

Eliphaz concludes from this vision that if God detects fault even in His heavenly angels, how much more in fragile mortals who dwell in houses of clay founded on dust. Between dawn and dusk they can be crushed like moths, perishing unnoticed and dying without wisdom. His implication is plain: before a holy God, no human being—not even Job—has any right to claim innocence (4:18–21).

## Key Takeaways

**For the first time in the book, a rigid philosophy of *retribution* is placed on the table.** Eliphaz trusts a formula that feels self-evident: innocent people never perish, and whoever suffers disaster must have sown wickedness (4:7–8). As a general proverb of life, sowing and reaping often holds true; yet turned into an ironclad law explaining every tragedy, it immediately puts the sufferer in the dock.

**True statements about God can still wound when misused.** The vision's question—whether any mortal can be more righteous than his Maker (4:17)—sounds devout and is broadly accurate in general theology. Yet Eliphaz wields that truth to crush Job, insinuating that because all humans are imperfect, Job deserves every blow now raining down on him.

**Empathy quickly yields to the urge to defend one's belief system.** In chapter 2, Eliphaz wept with Job and sat silent for seven days (2:12–13). Once Job's lament in chapter 3 threatens to shake Eliphaz's orderly worldview, however, he can no longer stay quiet; he rushes to lecture so his neat picture of a fair universe will not crumble (4:2–5).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus directly overturns Eliphaz's formula that *calamity proves sin*.** When people asked Him about Galileans slaughtered by Pilate or eighteen killed when the tower of Siloam collapsed, Jesus affirmed they were no more sinful than anyone else (Luke 13:1–5). Likewise, before a man born blind, He answered decisively that neither this man nor his parents had sinned to cause his blindness (John 9:1–3). Scripture affirms a general principle of sowing and reaping (Galatians 6:7), yet firmly refuses to turn it into a gavel condemning every victim of affliction.

**The cross completely inverts Eliphaz's challenge in 4:7.** Eliphaz asks when an innocent person has ever perished (4:7). Readers of Job already know from chapter 1 that Job suffers *without cause* (1:9; 2:3). In the New Testament, the answer reaches deeper still: the only truly innocent and wholly upright One, Jesus Christ, was crucified among transgressors to bear the penalty in place of sinners (2 Corinthians 5:21; 1 Peter 3:18).

## Notes & Nuances

**Eliphaz's words in chapters 4 and 5 are accurately recorded by Scripture, yet not endorsed by God.** At the close of the book, the LORD tells Eliphaz directly that he and his two friends have not spoken rightly about Him as Job has (42:7). Readers should therefore avoid plucking pious-sounding lines from Eliphaz's mouth and citing them as unqualified biblical truth.

**The origin of the nighttime vision in 4:12–17 sparks much debate.** Eliphaz never clarifies whether the *spirit* that visited him was a messenger from God or simply a terrifying subjective experience. Strikingly, its message stresses only human baseness and frailty, entirely devoid of God's grace or compassion.

**Hebrew poetic parallelism shines throughout this speech.** In Old Testament poetry, each verse typically pairs two lines that echo or intensify one another, such as *plow iniquity / sow trouble* (4:8), or the cluster of five distinct Hebrew words for lions in 4:10–11 to underscore how even fierce power eventually melts away.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, observed that Eliphaz sets forth doctrine that is sound in principle yet misapplies it to a specific case.
