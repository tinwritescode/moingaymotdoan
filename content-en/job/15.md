# Job 15 — Eliphaz Condemns Job a Second Time

## Story

The second cycle of debates opens, and once again Eliphaz the Temanite speaks first. Whereas his opening speech still carried some tact, this time his tone turns harsh from the very first sentence. He dismisses Job's replies as empty wind, mocking him for "filling his belly with the east wind"—blistering yet worthless talk. Worse still, he accuses Job of "undermining the fear of God" and hindering meditation before Him. In Eliphaz's judgment, inner iniquity is teaching Job's mouth what to say, so "Your own mouth condemns you, not mine" (15:1–6).

Eliphaz shifts to mocking Job's confidence. He asks sarcastically whether Job was the very first mortal born before the hills were formed, or whether he listened in on God's secret council to claim a monopoly on wisdom. He reminds Job that "the gray-haired and the aged are on our side"—men even older than Job's father. In his eyes, their earlier counsel was nothing less than "God's consolations" and gentle words, yet Job brushed them aside and let his spirit boil over in rebellion against God (15:7–13).

Moving from personal reproach, Eliphaz reprises an earlier argument while pushing it further: mortals born of woman are flawed by nature; if God does not trust even His holy ones and the heavens themselves are not pure in His eyes, how much worse is corrupt humanity, "who drinks injustice like water" (15:14–16).

The bulk of his speech unfolds a grim portrait of the wicked person's doom, which Eliphaz insists comes straight from ancient ancestral tradition untouched by foreign corruption. He declares that "a wicked man writhes in pain all his days," haunted constantly by terrifying sounds in his ears, dreading the sword, wandering about for bread, and sensing the day of darkness closing in. Why? Because he dared shake his fist at the Almighty and charge defiantly against God with smug, bloated arrogance (15:17–27).

Inevitably, Eliphaz says, total ruin follows. The wicked man will dwell in ruined cities and crumbling houses; his wealth will not endure, his shoots will wither in flame, and his unripe fruit will drop like a blighted vine or an olive tree shedding its blossoms. Fire will consume the tents of bribery, for such people "conceive trouble and give birth to evil," their hearts continually hatching deceit (15:28–35).

## Key Takeaways

**As an argument drags on, rigid assumptions quickly slide from counsel into condemnation.** In chapters 4–5, Eliphaz still remembered Job as someone who had strengthened the weak; by chapter 15, he no longer mentions Job's upright past at all. Simply pushing back gets Job branded an impious rebel who "undermines the fear of God" (15:4).

**Hiding behind age and tradition does not guarantee truth.** Eliphaz invokes gray hair and ancestral heritage to silence Job's protest (15:10; 15:18). Wisdom handed down by elders is valuable, yet forged into a rigid mold to judge a specific person's suffering, it turns cruel.

**A true observation about human sinfulness can still be weaponized wrongly.** It is indeed true that all humanity is frail and stained before a holy God (15:14–16). Yet Eliphaz leaps from that universal reality straight to accusing Job of hidden crimes, erasing the real distinction between a God-fearing sufferer and a brazen rebel.

**Comfort stripped of empathy sounds like an indictment.** Eliphaz congratulates himself on offering "God's consolations" and gentle words (15:11), yet spends the whole second half of his speech painting a lurid picture of the wicked being tormented—aimed unmistakably at Job. Invoking God's name to pressure a hurting person into confessing nonexistent guilt only deepens the wound.

## Deeper Meaning

**The metaphor of the wicked conceiving evil and giving birth to ruin echoes across Scripture.** The closing verse of chapter 15 declares that evildoers "conceive trouble and give birth to evil" (15:35), recalling the psalmist's picture of the wicked pregnant with mischief (Psalms 7:14). In the New Testament, James uses the exact same imagery of birth to trace the trajectory of sin: desire conceives and gives birth to sin, and sin when full-grown gives birth to death (James 1:15).

**The question of how mortals can stand righteous before God finds its complete answer only in Christ.** Eliphaz asks a piercing question: what is man that he could be pure and righteous before God (15:14)? Paul affirms in the New Testament that no one is righteous by works of the law (Romans 3:10; 3:20). Unlike Eliphaz, however, who uses human frailty merely to crush Job, the Gospel proclaims that God Himself gives righteousness as a gift of grace through Jesus Christ (Romans 3:22–24).

**Human tradition can never substitute for true wisdom from God.** Eliphaz leans on ancestral lore to condemn an innocent man (15:18). Centuries later, Jesus likewise confronted people who clung so tightly to human traditions that they set aside God's mercy and justice (Mark 7:8–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Eliphaz's speech is faithfully preserved in Scripture, yet never endorsed by God.** Whenever reading Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar, keep the book's final verdict in mind: God directly rebukes Eliphaz and his two friends for not speaking accurately about Him as Job did (42:7). Many verses in chapter 15 sound dignified and proverbial, yet applied to Job they miss the truth completely.

**The *east wind* and *unmixed ancestral land* reflect ancient Near Eastern imagery.** Across desert lands, the scorching east wind blows off the wilderness, parching vegetation and bringing no rain—so "filling his belly with the east wind" (15:2) describes speech that is both hot-tempered and barren. Mentioning ancestors dwelling in a land where no foreigner passed through (15:19) underscores Eliphaz's claim that his retribution theology is pure, uncorrupted ancient orthodoxy.

**Hebrew poetic parallelism heightens the emotional weight.** Many verses pair two parallel images stating the same thought—such as a vine shedding unripe grapes beside an olive tree dropping its blossoms (15:33). Rather than adding fresh facts, the pairing drives home the sense of doom Eliphaz tries to pin onto the wicked.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, noted in his sermons on Job that Eliphaz says much that is accurate regarding human corruption and the bitter fruit of sin, yet errs gravely by applying it mechanically to Job.
