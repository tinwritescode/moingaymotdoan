# Job 1 — Calamity Strikes a Blameless Man

## Story

The narrator opens with a portrait of a wealthy, God-fearing man in the land of Uz named Job. He is introduced with four striking qualities: he was "blameless and upright; he feared God and shunned evil." Job had seven sons and three daughters, along with vast flocks and herds—seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred yoke of oxen, five hundred female donkeys, and a great number of servants—making him the greatest of all the people of the East (1:1–3).

His sons regularly took turns hosting feasts in their homes, inviting their three sisters to eat and drink with them. Whenever a round of feasting concluded, Job would rise early in the morning, send for his children to purify them, and offer a burnt offering for each one of them. He did this faithfully out of quiet concern that his children might have sinned and cursed God in their hearts (1:4–5).

The scene shifts abruptly from earth to the heavenly court. One day, the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD, and Satan also came with them. The LORD asked Satan where he had come from, and Satan answered that he had been roaming throughout the earth, going back and forth on it. It was the LORD Himself who first brought up Job, calling him His servant and praising him as unmatched on earth—a blameless and upright man who fears God and shuns evil (1:6–8).

Satan immediately countered with a sharp, cynical question: "Does Job fear God for nothing?" He claimed Job was loyal only because God had placed a hedge of protection around him, his household, and everything he owned, blessing the work of his hands. Satan challenged God that if He stretched out His hand and struck everything Job had, Job would surely curse Him to His face. The LORD permitted Satan to touch all that Job owned, while setting a clear boundary: he must not lay a finger on Job himself (1:9–12).

Disaster struck in relentless succession on a single day, while Job's children were eating and drinking in their oldest brother's house. Four messengers ran in one after another, each arriving before the previous one had finished speaking, and every report ended with the refrain, "and I alone have escaped to tell you." The Sabeans raided and carried off the oxen and donkeys and killed the servants; fire from heaven burned up the sheep and the shepherds; three raiding parties of Chaldeans swept away the camels and killed the servants; finally, a mighty wind from the desert collapsed the four corners of the oldest brother's house, crushing all ten of Job's children to death (1:13–19).

Hearing this rapid barrage of news that left him penniless and childless, Job stood up, tore his robe, and shaved his head in deep mourning, then fell to the ground in worship. He spoke the famous words: "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked I will return. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the name of the LORD." The narrator concludes that in all this, Job did not sin or charge God with wrongdoing (1:20–22).

## Key Takeaways

**The central question of the entire book is whether human beings love God for who He is.** Satan does not deny that Job lives an upright life; he attacks Job's motives. In the accuser's cynical view, piety is nothing more than a transaction to secure blessing and comfort (1:9–11).

**Job's suffering is not caused by hidden sin.** From the very first verse, the narrator declares him "blameless and upright," and God Himself confirms that verdict before the heavenly assembly (1:1; 1:8). This dismantles from the outset the simplistic assumption that anyone struck by catastrophe must have done something wicked.

**Satan's power is always bounded by God's sovereignty.** Satan cannot act independently; he must obtain permission and abide by the limit God sets—not laying a hand on Job's own body (1:12). Yet God's permission also opens a painful mystery that Job down on earth knows nothing about.

**True faith is not numbness to pain, but worship in the midst of pain.** Job tears his robe and shaves his head, entering fully into grief rather than pretending to feel nothing (1:20). Yet he does not turn his back on God; he acknowledges that everything he ever held was a temporary gift from God's hand (1:21).

## Deeper Meaning

**The heavenly curtain lifts to reveal the spiritual battle behind earthly suffering.** The reader knows what Job does not—much like in the New Testament, when Jesus warns Peter that Satan has demanded to sift the disciples like wheat, yet Jesus has prayed that Peter's faith may not fail (Luke 22:31–32). In Revelation, Satan likewise appears in this exact role as the accuser of our brothers and sisters, who accuses them day and night before our God (Revelation 12:10).

**Job's faithfulness echoes New Testament teaching on perseverance and contentment.** James points directly to Job as an example of steadfast endurance in affliction, highlighting the Lord's compassionate and merciful outcome (James 5:11). When Paul urges believers toward contentment, he nearly repeats Job's very words: we brought nothing into the world, and we can take nothing out of it (1 Timothy 6:7).

**Job acts as a priest for his children, foreshadowing a more complete Mediator.** He offers sacrifices repeatedly in case his children have sinned in their hearts, yet those offerings must be repeated endlessly without ever putting his anxiety fully to rest (1:5). The New Testament points to Christ, who offered Himself once for all and always lives to intercede for those who draw near to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25–27).

## Notes & Nuances

**The word *blameless* does not mean sinless perfection.** In verse 1:1, the term describes a person of genuine integrity—wholehearted and consistent, not double-minded—rather than someone who has never made a mistake. Job himself later acknowledges the sins of his youth (13:26).

**The title *Satan* carries the definite article in Hebrew, meaning *the adversary* or *the accuser*.** In this heavenly courtroom scene, the figure functions like a prosecuting official who patrols the earth and probes human loyalty before God, much as he appears in Zechariah (Zechariah 3:1). Readers should avoid importing every later theological detail about the devil here at the expense of seeing his specific role in Job's storyline.

**Job knows nothing of the conversation in heaven.** The entire long cycle of debates from chapter 3 through chapter 37 unfolds in the darkness of that ignorance. Keeping this detail in mind helps readers sympathize with Job's bitter outcries later on, rather than judging him hastily from the vantage point of someone who has already read chapter 1.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, emphasized from Job 1 that Satan possesses no independent power equal to God, but can act only within the limits God allows and as an instrument under His control. According to Calvin, the story teaches believers both to recognize the real malice of the enemy and to rest assured that every event ultimately remains in God's sovereign hand. *(Sermons on Job, section on chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: God's sovereignty over Satan is a well-known centerpiece of Calvin's Sermons on Job -->

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, drew attention to Job's spiritual care for his children in 1:5. He observed that families given to feasting easily face inward temptations, so Job models how parents ought to intercede and guard the holiness of their households before God, while praising Job's confession in 1:21 as a pattern of submission in loss. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Job 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Matthew Henry's familiar exposition of Job 1, summarized from memory without checking the exact text on ccel.org -->
