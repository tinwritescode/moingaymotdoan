# Job 39 — God's Wisdom Among the Wild Creatures

## Story

God continues His first whirlwind speech, moving Job from skies and oceans into the vibrant realm of wild animals completely beyond human control. First, He asks whether Job knows the season when mountain goats give birth or watches the deer bear their fawns on steep crags. Job neither counts the months they carry their young nor assists them in labor; yet their offspring thrive in the open wild and set out on their own (39:1–4).

Next comes the untamed wild donkey. God asks who let it roam free and gave it the wasteland and salt flats as its home. It scorns the noise of the city, never listens to a driver's shout, and ranges across the hills hunting for green pasture (39:5–8).

Then God points to the mighty wild ox. Will it agree to serve Job, spend the night beside his manger, wear a yoke to plow furrows, or haul grain home to his threshing floor? Obviously not—there are wild powers on earth never made to act as human servants (39:9–12).

A touch of playful humor enters the poem as God turns to the ostrich. She flaps her wings joyfully yet clumsily, abandoning her eggs in the warm sand unconcerned that a foot or wild beast might crush them, treating her young harshly as though they were not hers. God openly remarks that He deprived her of wisdom and understanding—yet the instant she spreads her wings to run, she laughs at both horse and rider with her astonishing speed (39:13–18).

God devotes the chapter's most stirring poetry to the warhorse. Did Job give it strength and clothe its neck with a flowing mane, making it leap like a locust with a terrifying snort? It paws fiercely in the valley, fearless before flashing sword and rattling quiver; at the blast of the trumpet it shouts "Aha!", catching the scent of battle from afar and charging headlong into the fray (39:19–25).

Finally, God lifts Job's gaze skyward where hawk and eagle soar. Not by Job's insight does the hawk spread its wings southward with the seasons, nor at Job's command does the eagle build its nest high on sheer, unreachable cliffs—spotting prey from far away to feed its fledglings (39:26–30).

## Key Takeaways

**Wild creation exists for God's delight and wisdom, not solely to serve humankind.** From mountain goats on high ledges to desert donkeys and untamable wild oxen, every animal in chapter 39 shares one trait: it belongs outside human mastery and resists domestication, yet God cares for it and delights in its fierce freedom (39:1–12).

**God's order is far wider and richer than tidy human expectations.** Even traits that appear quirky or alarming—the scatterbrained yet blazingly fast ostrich, the fearless warhorse, the blood-hunting eagle—belong inside the colorful tapestry of creation under God's rule (39:13–30).

**If mortals cannot govern even a handful of wild creatures, how can we presume to supervise God's rule of the universe?** These questions are never meant to crush Job, but to help him feel the vast span between tiny human comprehension and the Creator's all-embracing wisdom.

## Deeper Meaning

**God watches tenderly over even the smallest and wildest lives.** The portrait of God tending every mountain goat's birth and every eagle's nest on inaccessible peaks (39:1–4; 39:27–30) leads straight to Jesus's assurance in the New Testament: not a single sparrow falls to the ground apart from your Father, who feeds the birds of the air and clothes the lilies of the field (Matthew 6:26; 10:29).

**Every creature showcases the glory and wisdom of its Maker.** Beholding creation across chapters 38 and 39 anticipates the truth Paul articulates in Romans: God's invisible attributes—His eternal power and divine nature—have been clearly seen in the things He has made (Romans 1:20).

## Notes & Nuances

**Two of these creatures reflect striking ancient Hebrew terminology.** The *wild ox* (39:9) translates Hebrew *re'em*—the massive, long-horned wild ox (aurochs) of the ancient Near East, now extinct, which older English versions sometimes rendered "unicorn." The *ostrich* (39:13) was nicknamed the "camel-bird" in several ancient tongues because of its long neck and tall, striding legs.

**Verse 39:30 contains a vivid proverb Jesus later echoed.** The closing observation about birds of prey—"wherever the slain are, there it is"—was a well-known ancient saying for sharp sight and unmistakable certainty, later echoed by Jesus when describing how unmistakable the coming of the Son of Man will be (Matthew 24:28).
