# Job 22 — Eliphaz Invents Crimes Against Job and Urges Peace with God

## Story

The third cycle of debates opens with the final speech of Eliphaz the Temanite. He begins with a rhetorical question: "Can a man be of benefit to God?" A wise or righteous person benefits only himself and adds nothing to the Almighty. Therefore, God certainly does not punish Job out of fear or envy of his piety (22:1–4).

From that premise, Eliphaz crosses a line none of the friends dared cross before: dropping all hints, he fabricates a detailed indictment directly against Job. He asserts confidently: "Is not your wickedness great? Are not your iniquities endless?" Then he recites charges as if he were an eyewitness: Job "needlessly demanded security from your brothers," stripped the naked of their clothing, "gave no water to the weary," withheld bread from the hungry, seized land by brute power, "sent widows away empty-handed," and crushed the arms of the fatherless. In Eliphaz's mind, heinous crimes like these explain why snares, sudden terror, and floods have crashed down on Job's head (22:5–11).

Next, Eliphaz puts godless words into Job's mouth, accusing him of thinking like the wicked who assume God sits too high behind thick clouds to notice anything down on earth: "What does God know?" He warns Job not to tread the ancient path of wicked men swept away by a flood—people who told the Almighty to depart even while He filled their homes with good things, and whose downfall makes the righteous rejoice (22:12–20).

Unexpectedly, the final third of the speech shifts to a warm, lyrical appeal for repentance. Eliphaz urges Job: "Reconcile now and be at peace with Him; thereby good will come to you. Receive instruction from His mouth." If Job returns to the Almighty, puts away wrongdoing, and casts his fine gold into the dust, "then the Almighty will be your gold." Then he will delight in God, find his prayers answered, see light shine on his path, and even guilty people will be "delivered through the cleanness of your hands" (22:21–30).

## Key Takeaways

**Clinging to a false assumption tempts people to invent evidence to save it.** Eliphaz is dead certain that Job's suffering stems from colossal sin; finding no real offenses, he simply manufactures a catalogue of cruelty toward the poor (22:5–9). This marks the low point of injustice committed by friends convinced they are defending God.

**Even the finest counsel misses the mark when aimed at the wrong target.** Verses 22:21–28 contain moving calls to return to God and prize the Almighty above gold (22:25). Yet because Eliphaz wields them to coerce an innocent man into confessing fabricated crimes, his advice brings no healing and only deepens the injustice.

**Eliphaz's closing line unwittingly becomes an ironic prophecy about himself.** In 22:30, Eliphaz promises that if Job repents, even guilty people will be rescued through Job's clean hands. At the close of the book (42:7–9), that exact scene unfolds—only the guilty ones needing Job's intercession turn out to be Eliphaz and his two friends!

## Deeper Meaning

**The call to make God our supreme treasure (22:24–25) echoes genuine biblical truth even on the lips of a misguided accuser.** In the Psalms, believers declare the Lord to be their portion and heart's sole treasure (Psalms 73:25–26). In the New Testament, Paul counts every former gain as loss to gain Christ as his supreme treasure (Philippians 3:7–8), and Jesus teaches us to seek first God's kingdom rather than hoarding earthly riches (Matthew 6:19–21).

**Eliphaz's argument that God gains nothing from human righteousness (22:2–3) is only half true.** It is true that God is completely self-sufficient and lacks nothing that mortals could supply (Acts 17:24–25). Yet Eliphaz errs by inferring that God stands coldly indifferent to His people's faithfulness; on the contrary, God Himself boasted joyfully about Job at the very start of the book (1:8), and Scripture testifies that the Lord delights in those who fear Him and rejoice in fellowship with Him (Psalms 147:11; Zephaniah 3:17).

## Notes & Nuances

**The charges laid out in chapter 22 are Eliphaz's slanders, utterly unendorsed by God.** From the opening chapter, God attested that Job was blameless and upright (1:8); Job will refute each of these charges of exploiting the poor in chapters 29 and 31; and in the end God Himself rebukes Eliphaz and his companions for failing to speak rightly about Him (42:7).

**Distinguish between the spiritual beauty of 22:21–28 and Eliphaz's misapplication of it.** Many believers rightly love phrases like "Reconcile now and be at peace with Him" (22:21) and "the Almighty will be your gold" (22:25). Those sentiments harmonize thoroughly with Scripture's broader call to repentance, yet within the storyline of Job we must remember that Eliphaz is prescribing genuine medicine to the wrong patient.

**The flood sweeping away the wicked in 22:16 likely alludes to the Great Flood.** Speaking of the ancient path of wicked men whose foundations were washed away by a torrent recalls Noah's wicked generation destroyed in the deluge of Genesis 6–7—a vivid emblem of judgment in ancient memory.
