# Job 40 — Job Covers His Mouth and the Portrait of Behemoth

## Story

Wrapping up His first speech, the LORD pauses and invites Job to respond: will the one who contends with the Almighty correct Him? Let the one who accuses God give an answer (40:1–2).

For the first time after chapters of lengthy self-defense, Job humbles himself completely. He replies briefly to the LORD that he is small and unworthy—how could he possibly answer God? "I lay my hand over my mouth." He spoke once—even twice—before, yet now he will stop and say no more (40:3–5).

Still, the LORD is not finished. Job's silence at this point is merely submission before overwhelming power; it has not yet reached the root of Job's charge against God's justice. So out of the whirlwind the LORD speaks a second time, again commanding Job to brace himself like a man and answer. He goes straight to the core issue: "Would you really annul My justice? Would you condemn Me to justify yourself?" (40:6–8).

The LORD issues an awe-inspiring challenge: if Job thinks he could run the world more fairly than God, let him step into God's shoes for a day! Does he have an arm like God's, or can his voice thunder like His? Let him adorn himself with majesty, splendor, glory, and honor; let him unleash the fury of his wrath, humble every proud person with a single glance, crush the wicked on the spot, and bury them all in the dust. If Job can pull that off, the LORD Himself will praise him and acknowledge that his own right hand can save him (40:9–14).

To illustrate mortal helplessness before massive powers within creation, the LORD directs Job's gaze to a colossal creature named *Behemoth* in Hebrew. God emphasizes right away: "Look at Behemoth, which I made along with you." It eats grass like an ox, yet packs staggering might in its loins and belly muscles; its tail sways stiff as a cedar, its thigh sinews are tightly knit, its bones are tubes of bronze, and its limbs like rods of iron. It ranks foremost among the works of God, and only its Maker can approach it with a sword (40:15–19).

The hills yield grass for it while wild beasts play nearby. It lies calmly beneath lotus plants, hidden among reeds and marshy willows. Though a river rages furiously—even if the Jordan surges right up to its mouth—it does not flinch. The LORD closes with two pointed questions: can anyone capture it by its eyes or pierce its nose with a snare to tame it? (40:20–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Standing face to face before God silences every human self-defense.** Job had once vowed to march before God like a prince to present every step of his life (31:37). Actually confronting the Almighty, all he can do is place his hand over his mouth and confess his smallness (40:4–5).

**We cannot vindicate our own innocence by putting God in the dock.** Verse 40:8 puts a finger directly on where Job slipped: in his pain, trying to defend his innocence, he slid into condemning God's governance as unjust. God never accuses Job of hidden wickedness, yet He firmly corrects that attitude.

**Governing the world justly lies far beyond human capacity.** God's challenge in 40:10–14 demonstrates that eradicating evil and pride across creation is infinitely more complex than mortals suppose. A human being unable to subdue even Behemoth lacks the stature to audit God's administration of cosmic justice.

## Deeper Meaning

**Before God's majesty, every human mouth falls silent.** Job placing his hand over his mouth (40:4) captures the rightful posture of finite mortals before the Holy One—echoed by Paul in Romans when he says the law speaks so that "every mouth may be silenced and the whole world held accountable to God" (Romans 3:19).

**God alone holds power to bring down the proud and save humanity.** Saying in 40:14 that if Job can humble every proud rebel his own right hand will save him underscores that human beings cannot save themselves. In the New Testament, God exercises His own mighty arm in Christ to scatter the proud, rescue sinners, and establish true righteousness (Luke 1:51–52; Ephesians 2:8–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Interpreters hold three main views regarding *Behemoth* in 40:15–24.** The Hebrew word *behemot* is an intensive plural of *beast*—meaning *the great beast* or *super-beast*. Commentators read this creature in three major ways. First, *a real wild animal*: most traditional commentaries identify it as the *hippopotamus* (some also propose the *elephant* or *water buffalo*)—a plant-eater inhabiting marshes and reeds, immensely powerful and unbothered by surging rivers. Second, *imagery echoing ancient Near Eastern myth*: many modern scholars point to heightened poetic traits like a cedar-like tail and bronze-and-iron bones, paired alongside *Leviathan* in chapter 41—a famous ancient literary duo symbolizing untamed cosmic powers far beyond human reach. Third, *a symbol of chaos that remains solely God's creature*: whether rooted in a poetically magnified real beast or ancient chaos imagery, the decisive theological anchor stands right in 40:15: "which I made along with you." Behemoth is no rival god challenging the LORD, but simply a fellow creature He fashioned and rules effortlessly.
