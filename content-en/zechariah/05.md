# Zechariah 5 — The Flying Scroll and the Basket Carrying Wickedness to Shinar

## Story

After two encouraging visions lifting up High Priest Joshua and Governor Zerubbabel in chapters 3 and 4, Zechariah's night of visions shifts abruptly to a solemn tone in the sixth and seventh visions—two striking pictures of moral cleansing across the newly restored land. Lifting his eyes to the sky, Zechariah beholds a huge "flying scroll" unrolled in midair, "twenty cubits long and ten cubits wide"—roughly thirty feet by fifteen feet (5:1–2).

The angel at once explains the strange scroll's meaning: "This is the curse that is going out over the face of all the land." Written on both sides like a public warrant no one can hide from, one side sentences "everyone who steals" to be cut off, and the other sentences "everyone who swears falsely" in the LORD's name to be cut off. The LORD of Hosts Himself dispatches that scroll; it flies straight into the thief's house and the perjurer's house, lodges right inside, and "consumes it, both its timber and its stones" (5:3–4).

No sooner does the sixth vision close than the angel steps out and tells Zechariah to lift his eyes to an even stranger seventh vision: a large measuring basket—an *ephah*, the biggest standard grain measure in ancient times—moves into view. The angel declares that this represents the iniquity of the people throughout the entire land (5:5–6). Immediately, a heavy circular "lead cover" on the basket's mouth lifts up, revealing a woman huddled inside. The angel identifies her flatly: "This is Wickedness!" Before she can climb out, the angel shoves her right back down into the basket and slams the heavy lead weight back down over its opening (5:7–8).

Zechariah looks up again and sees two other women appearing, wind filling their broad wings "like those of a stork." They lift the basket holding Wickedness right up between earth and sky. Curious at this bizarre spectacle, Zechariah asks the angel where they are taking the basket. The angel answers decisively: "To build a house for it in the land of Shinar. And when it is prepared, the basket will be set there on its pedestal" (5:9–11).

## Key Takeaways

**Rebuilding a stone temple is not enough; God's community must also be cleansed of everyday sin.** Chapters 3 and 4 overflowed with grace for rebuilding the temple, yet chapter 5 brings an immediate sobering check: people cannot live in the land where God dwells while still practicing theft and perjury (5:3–4). God's forgiving grace is never permission for His people to tolerate deceit.

**God's law examines both our relationship with Him and our treatment of our neighbors.** The two sides of the flying scroll name two representative sins: "swearing falsely" by God's name directly dishonors the Holy One, while "stealing" violates a neighbor's rights (5:3–4). Those two crimes stand for both tablets of the Ten Commandments. Once dishonesty burrows into a household, judgment does not stop at the doorstep—it devours "both its timber and its stones."

**Wickedness does not vanish through human willpower; God Himself must overpower and banish it.** Inside the measuring basket, Wickedness refuses to stay put—the angel must thrust her back down, clamp the heavy lead cover shut, and ship her completely out of the Promised Land (5:7–11). Because an *ephah* was a commercial grain measure, stuffing Wickedness inside it also subtly unmasks the commercial cheating and materialism returning exiles risked dragging back from Babylon.

**Sin holds no permanent home in God's land.** The seventh vision ends with genuine relief: Judah is swept clean of evil down to its root, while Wickedness is deported back to "the land of Shinar"—the territory of Babylon and the Tower of Babel, where humanity first built an arrogant civilization defying God (5:11).

## Deeper Meaning

**The scroll's dimensions echo the Tabernacle and Temple, while its two sides echo the Ten Commandments.** Measuring "twenty cubits long and ten cubits wide" in 5:2 is no random figure: those were the exact dimensions of the Holy Place inside Moses's Tabernacle and the front portico of Solomon's Temple (1 Kings 6:3). The very blueprint of true worship now flies overhead as a scroll of God's law measuring the whole land! The two offenses written on its two sides come directly from the two halves of the Ten Commandments: the third commandment forbidding misuse of the LORD's name or false oaths (Exodus 20:7), and the eighth forbidding theft (Exodus 20:15). And a house consumed "both its timber and its stones" (5:4) recalls the law requiring total demolition of a house infected with persistent rot or mildew in Leviticus (Leviticus 14:45).

**"The land of Shinar" connects the Tower of Babel in Genesis to "Babylon the Great" in Revelation.** When the angel states that Wickedness is carried off to have a temple built for it in "the land of Shinar" (5:11), Bible readers immediately recognize that ancient name: it was on "a plain in the land of Shinar" that arrogant humanity banded together to erect the city and Tower of Babel to make a name for themselves (Genesis 11:2). Later, Shinar became another name for Babylon (Daniel 1:2). By the Bible's closing book, the picture of Wickedness housed in its own shrine at Babylon echoes directly into the announcement of "Babylon the Great"—a dwelling place for demons and every foul thing before its final overthrow (Revelation 18:2).

**God removing sin from His land anticipates Christ's finished work.** While chapter 3 showed God stripping guilt from one representative individual—High Priest Joshua—in a single day (3:4; 3:9), chapter 5 widens the lens to God uprooting "Wickedness" itself from the entire land. What the vision of the basket sketches through symbolic deportation, Jesus later completed decisively at the cross—taking away the sin of the world and opening the promise of a new heaven and new earth where nothing impure will ever enter again.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why is "Wickedness" pictured as a woman sitting inside the basket (5:7–8)?** Read hastily, this detail can easily be misunderstood as casting women as symbols of evil. In reality, Hebrew grammar explains it clearly: the abstract noun "wickedness" (*rish'ah*) is grammatically **feminine**—just as "wisdom" in Proverbs (Proverbs 8–9) is feminine and personified as Lady Wisdom! On top of that, many scholars note that the wording "build a house [temple] for it" and "set it on its pedestal" (5:11) deliberately evokes the statue of a pagan goddess (such as Ishtar of Babylon) being hauled out of the holy land, sealed up, and shipped back to her own idol shrine in Babylon. The vision is never targeting womanhood; it uses natural Hebrew gender and anti-idolatry satire to reject paganism and sin.

**The "wings of a stork" (5:9) and the heavy "lead cover" (5:7–8).** Under the Law of Moses (Leviticus 11:19), the stork was classified among unclean birds forbidden as food—making two beings with broad, powerful stork wings hoisting the basket of Wickedness through the air an apt fit for the unclean cargo being expelled from the holy land! Meanwhile, the round lid slammed down in 5:7–8 is a heavy disk of *lead* (literally a circular "talent" of lead), emphasizing that Wickedness is sealed down with crushing weight so it cannot break loose.

**A small textual variant in 5:6 ("their appearance" or "their iniquity").** The standard Hebrew Masoretic Text reads *einam*, literally "their eye" or "their appearance" across all the land. However, the ancient Greek Septuagint and Syriac differ by a single letter and read *awonam*, meaning "their iniquity" across all the land—matching 5:8 ("This is Wickedness!") seamlessly. Many modern English Bibles, including the BSB, follow "their iniquity," though both readings lead straight to the vision's same clear meaning.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, notes that the flying scroll's massive size—twenty cubits by ten cubits (5:2)—matches the Holy Place of the ancient tabernacle, showing that God's verdict issues straight from His holy dwelling. On Wickedness sealed inside the basket and hauled off to Shinar (5:6–11), Jerome explains that sin and deceit cannot coexist forever inside the holy land and are therefore banished to Babylon, the emblem of confusion and rebellion against God. *(Commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Jerome linking the flying scroll's dimensions to the tabernacle in chapter 5 verse 2 and explaining the removal of Wickedness to Shinar/Babylon in verses 6–11; original Latin not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, reflects incisively on 5:3–4 to explain why the flying scroll singles out only theft and false swearing. In Calvin's view, those two offenses compactly represent both tablets of the Ten Commandments—irreverence toward God on one side and injustice toward neighbors on the other—while the scroll lodging inside the offender's home to consume both timber and stone shows that God's curse clings tightly to ill-gotten gain no matter how cleverly people hide it. On the basket vision (5:5–11), Calvin emphasizes that the heavy lead cover slammed down and the flight to Shinar announce an inescapable verdict upon those who stubbornly cling to evil. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's exposition of theft and perjury representing both tablets of the Ten Commandments in chapter 5 verses 3–4 is a very well-known, signature insight in his Zechariah lectures -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws a practical warning from the unrolled flying scroll (5:1–2): it is not rolled shut and hidden away, but spread wide open across the sky so everyone can read it clearly and no one can plead ignorance. Pausing on 5:4 over thieves and false swearers, he remarks that many imagine a little cheating will help build up a wealthier household, yet that very dishonest profit invites a curse straight indoors that eats away both the timber and stone of the estate. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Zechariah chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral points on the open, readable scroll in verses 1–2 and the curse devouring dishonest estates in verse 4; original text on ccel.org not checked -->

**Joyce G. Baldwin**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, stressed that chapter 5's twin visions are not gloomy despair, but good news of purification for a community rebuilding the temple. She observed that the measuring basket (a commercial tool) sealing Wickedness inside before flying off to "build a house" and set her on a pedestal in Shinar (5:6–11) paints a biting parody of a false idol temple in Babylon—standing in deliberate contrast to the holy temple of the LORD rising in Jerusalem in chapters 4 and 6. *(Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, Tyndale Old Testament Commentaries, 1972)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Joyce Baldwin's reading of chapter 5 as good news of cleansing and her contrast between the counterfeit temple in Shinar and the true temple in Jerusalem in the Tyndale series; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Mark J. Boda**, the Canadian Old Testament scholar, highlights the symmetrical architecture of the eight night visions in Zechariah 1–6: right after the central summit (chapters 3–4) restores priest and governor to rebuild the temple, chapter 5 tackles the flip side of restoration—purging unrepentant sinners (vision 6) and uprooting sin and idolatry at their core (vision 7) to ship them back to Babylon.
