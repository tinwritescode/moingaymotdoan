# Zechariah 2 — The City Without Stone Walls

## Story

Still within that same night of visions, right after the four horns and four craftsmen (1:18–21), Zechariah lifts his eyes again and enters the third vision. He sees a man walking along with a measuring line in his hand (2:1). Zechariah asks, "Where are you going?" The man replies, "To measure Jerusalem, to see how wide and how long it is" (2:2). With the city still devastated and its walls broken down (cf. Nehemiah 1:3), surveying the perimeter to rebuild defensive walls sounded completely sensible and urgent.

Yet right at that instant, an urgent order rings out. As the angel talking with Zechariah walks forward, another angel rushes out to meet him and cries: "Run and tell that young man: 'Jerusalem will be a city without walls because of the multitude of men and livestock within it'" (2:3–4). Every human measuring line is far too cramped for God's plan. Instead of stone fortifications hemming the city in, the LORD declares that He Himself will serve as its defense: "For I will be a wall of fire around it, declares the LORD, and I will be the glory within it" (2:5).

Out of that vision, God's word shifts into an urgent summons aimed at Jews still lingering in exile. The LORD shouts: "Get up! Get up! Flee from the land of the north, declares the LORD, for I have scattered you like the four winds of heaven" (2:6). He addresses them by name: "Escape, O Zion, you who dwell with the Daughter of Babylon!" (2:7). Although the exile had officially ended nearly twenty years earlier, many families had grown settled in homes and businesses in Babylon and had little desire to return to a ruined homeland. God urges them to leave at once, because judgment is about to fall on the nations that plundered them.

The explanation that follows combines awesome solemnity with astonishing tenderness. The LORD of Hosts sends the Speaker against the nations that looted Judah, "for whoever touches you touches the apple of His eye" (2:8). The Speaker declares that He need only wave His hand over them, and those oppressors will become plunder for their own former servants; then God's people will know that "the LORD of Hosts has sent Me" (2:9).

Then the voice softens into an invitation to sing, addressed to those already back in Jerusalem: "Shout for joy and be glad, O Daughter of Zion, for I am coming to dwell among you" (2:10). Nor is that joy restricted to Judah alone. On that day, "many nations will join themselves to the LORD, and they will become My people. I will dwell among you," and once more He confirms Zion will know that the LORD of Hosts has sent Him (2:11). The LORD will inherit Judah as His portion in the "holy land," and He will "choose Jerusalem once again" (2:12).

The entire chapter—closing the first cluster of three visions—ends with a cry that hushes the whole world: "Be silent before the LORD, all people, for He has roused Himself from His holy dwelling" (2:13).

## Key Takeaways

**God's plans always outgrow human measuring lines.** The man with the measuring cord meant well: he wanted to map Jerusalem's boundaries and wall in the modest remnant that survived (2:1–2). Yet an angel had to sprint out and halt him. Humans tend to measure the future by fear and small current headcounts, while God promises growth so abundant that no stone wall can contain it (2:4).

**The surest security is not tall walls, but God's own presence.** In the ancient world, an unwalled city was open prey for raiders. Yet God declares Jerusalem will thrive safely as "a city without walls," because outside stands His "wall of fire" and inside shines His "glory" (2:4–5). That fire keeps enemies back from afar while warming and illuminating everyone within.

**Lingering in Babylon's comfort when God calls you home is dangerous.** Babylon had started as a place of captivity, yet after seventy years it had become a familiar, comfortable place to prosper—far easier than impoverished Judah. God had to cry out twice to "flee" and "escape" (2:6–7) to jolt awake those who had stopped missing Zion. Growing cozy inside a world system under judgment risks being swept up in its collapse.

**God feels harm done to His people as pain against His own body.** The pupil ("apple") of the eye is the most delicate, sensitive, fiercely guarded spot on the human body. When God says, "whoever touches you touches the apple of His eye" (2:8), He shows that His people are no distant possession He visits occasionally; He holds them right beside Himself, and striking them pokes straight into the eye of the Almighty.

**God's presence turns a small people into a gathering place for all nations.** God comes to dwell in Zion not to lock His blessings behind closed gates, but so that "many nations will join themselves to the LORD" and become His people (2:10–11). When God truly rises and acts, the only fitting response for all humanity is hushed awe before Him (2:13).

## Deeper Meaning

**The man with a measuring line reaches all the way to Revelation.** Before Zechariah, Ezekiel had seen a man shining like bronze holding a linen cord and measuring rod to survey the new temple (Ezekiel 40:3). Later in Revelation, the apostle John receives a reed to measure the temple and watches an angel with a golden rod measure the New Jerusalem (Revelation 11:1; 21:15–17). While measuring in Ezekiel and Revelation highlights God's preservation and perfection, halting the surveyor in Zechariah 2 stresses the complementary truth: the vitality of God's city cannot be boxed inside cramped human limits.

**Wall of fire and indwelling glory recall the Exodus.** When Israel left Egypt, the LORD traveled with them in a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night to guard and guide them (Exodus 13:21–22). Once the tabernacle stood completed, the LORD's glory filled its interior (Exodus 40:34–35). The promise of 2:5 braids both memories back together for restored Jerusalem: once more God shields His people with fire and dwells among them in glory as at the beginning.

**"Apple of His eye" and the call to flee Babylon.** Calling God's people the apple of His eye first appeared in the Song of Moses recalling how the LORD guarded Israel in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 32:10) and in King David's prayer (Psalms 17:8). Meanwhile, the cry to flee Babylon (2:6–7) echoes earlier calls from Isaiah and Jeremiah (Isaiah 48:20; Jeremiah 51:6), and centuries later rings out again in Revelation summoning God's people out of the rebellious world system (Revelation 18:4).

**The One sent by the LORD is Himself the LORD dwelling among humanity.** In 2:8–11, the Speaker both speaks as the LORD ("I am coming to dwell among you") and states that "the LORD of Hosts has sent Me." That remarkable phrasing opens a doorway toward the New Testament, where the Word—who is Himself God—is sent by the Father, becomes flesh, and dwells among us full of grace and truth (John 1:14). Through Him, the promise that many nations will join themselves to the LORD and become His people (2:11; cf. Isaiah 19:24–25; 56:6–7) comes alive in the church, where believers become the living temple in whom God walks and dwells (2 Corinthians 6:16), reaching its consummation in the New Jerusalem where God's dwelling is with humanity (Revelation 21:3).

**Solemn silence before God acts.** The call "Be silent before the LORD, all people" (2:13) joins the chorus of earlier prophets Habakkuk and Zephaniah (Habakkuk 2:20; Zephaniah 1:7). Humans clamor and empires boast of strength, yet when the LORD rouses Himself from His holy dwelling, every noise on earth must fall silent.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does the promise "without walls" contradict Nehemiah rebuilding walls later?** About seventy-five years after this vision, Nehemiah returned from Persia and led the people in rebuilding Jerusalem's walls (Nehemiah 2:17). There is no contradiction. The vision in 2:4–5 is not a practical ban on building defensive walls, but a prophetic picture using an unwalled village to affirm two truths: future population will overflow all expectations, and Jerusalem's real security always rests in God Himself rather than stone masonry.

**Who is "that young man" in 2:4?** Following the storyline of 2:1–4, many readers understand "that young man" to be the surveyor with the measuring line introduced in verse 1. Other commentators think it refers to Zechariah himself—still a young prophet at the time—to whom the angel races with a message to relay to the people. The first reading tracks the scene most smoothly, since the man about to measure the city is called off; either way, the main point stands: Jerusalem's future cannot be measured down and boxed in.

**Why is Babylon called "the land of the north" when it lay to the east?** Geographically, Babylon sat east of Judah, yet the vast Arabian and Syrian Desert blocked direct travel between them. Armies and caravans alike marched northwest along the Euphrates River before descending into Israel from the north. Consequently, Scripture routinely refers to Babylon and the land of exile as "the land of the north" (2:6).

**The phrase "After glory" in 2:8 is difficult to translate.** The Hebrew reads literally "After glory He sent me to the nations" (rendered in the BSB as "After His Glory has sent Me against the nations"). Commentators take it along two main lines: either *after* displaying His glory in Jerusalem, He sends His messenger to judge the nations, or *for the sake of* His glory, He sends His messenger against them. Either way, the reason for divine intervention shines in the very next line: touching His people touches the apple of His eye.

**The sole occurrence of "holy land" in the Old Testament.** People today casually refer to Israel as the "Holy Land," yet 2:12 is actually the only verse in the entire Old Testament that applies the exact phrase "holy land" to Judah's territory. The land is holy not because its soil holds intrinsic magic, but because God chooses to dwell there in the midst of His people (2:10; 2:12).

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, zeroes in on the striking language of verses 8–11 where the Speaker is both the LORD and sent by the LORD. He points out that the One who says "I am coming to dwell among you" speaks with the LORD's own divine name while declaring that the LORD of Hosts has sent Him. For Luther, this is a clear Old Testament glimpse of two Persons within the one Godhead—God the Father sending God the Son to dwell among His people and gather the Gentiles. *(Lectures on the Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Luther highlighting "the LORD sending the LORD" in Zechariah chapter 2 verses 8–11 as pointing to Christ sent by the Father; original text not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, lingers over the call to "flee from the land of the north" in verses 6 and 7 to uncover a very real human temptation: many Jews had grown comfortable and prosperous in Babylon and dreaded returning to a desolate homeland to start over. Explaining verse 8, Calvin remarks that "the apple of His eye" reveals God's supreme tenderness—He experiences every blow struck at His people as an assault on the most sensitive part of Himself. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's two characteristic comments on attachment to Babylon in verses 6–7 and the apple of God's eye in verse 8; original text not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, delights in both halves of verse 5: God serves both as "a wall of fire" around the perimeter—keeping enemies at bay while leaving those inside unharmed—and as "the glory" at the center, illuminating the city from within. At verse 13, he observes that summoning all flesh to silence calls humans to quiet every complaint, doubt, and boast while waiting to watch the wonders God is about to perform. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Zechariah chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's practical exposition of both wall of fire and indwelling glory in verse 5 and reverent silence in verse 13; original wording not checked -->

**Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael**, an ancient rabbinic commentary, cites verse 8 as one of the most famous examples of reverent scribal emendations (*Tiqqunei Soferim*). According to this tradition, the line originally stood in the first person—whoever touches you touches the apple of *My* eye—yet ancient scribes shifted the pronoun to "His eye" out of reverence, hesitating to write of humans poking straight into the eye of the Most High.
