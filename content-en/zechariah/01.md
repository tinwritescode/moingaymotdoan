# Zechariah 1 — The Horsemen Among the Myrtle Trees

## Story

In the eighth month of King Darius's second year—barely a month after Judah had heard Haggai's encouragement and begun rebuilding the temple while still struggling with discouragement (cf. Haggai 2:1–3)—the word of the LORD came to the prophet Zechariah son of Berechiah, the son of Iddo (1:1). Unlike Haggai's urgent push to lay stone upon stone, God's opening word through Zechariah aims straight at the heart. The LORD reminds them how fiercely angry He had been with their ancestors, then calls directly to the generation standing before Him: "Return to Me, declares the LORD of Hosts, and I will return to you" (1:2–3).

God warns them not to repeat their forefathers' mistakes. Earlier prophets had cried out to their ancestors to turn from evil ways and wicked deeds, yet they refused to listen or pay attention (1:4). Then God asks two quiet, haunting questions: "Where are your fathers now? And the prophets, do they live forever?" (1:5). Both the hearers and the preachers have passed from the scene; only God's words and statutes did not die—they overtook those ancestors. In the end, when exile struck, those very forefathers repented and confessed that the LORD had dealt with them exactly as He had purposed according to their ways and deeds (1:6).

Three months later, on the night of the twenty-fourth day of the eleventh month—the month of Shebat, in late winter—the word of the LORD came again to Zechariah in a rapid sequence of visions all in one night (1:7). In the first vision, Zechariah sees a man riding a red horse standing among myrtle trees in a deep hollow; behind him stand red, sorrel, and white horses (1:8). Puzzled by the strange scene, Zechariah asks the angel talking with him what these horses mean, and the angel promises to show him (1:9).

At that moment, the man standing among the myrtle trees speaks up himself: these are the ones the LORD has sent to patrol the earth (1:10). The riders immediately report back to the angel of the LORD standing among the myrtle trees: "We have patrolled the earth, and behold, all the earth is at rest and quiet" (1:11). That seemingly peaceful report actually stung Judah's heart: the Persian Empire and surrounding nations were sitting comfortable, secure, and untouched—with no sign of the great shaking Haggai had announced two months earlier (cf. Haggai 2:6–7)—while Jerusalem still lay in ruins.

Hearing that, the angel of the LORD cries out directly to God in aching intercession: "O LORD of Hosts, how long will You withhold mercy from Jerusalem and the cities of Judah, with which You have been angry these seventy years?" (1:12). The LORD immediately answers the interpreting angel with "kind and comforting words" (1:13).

The angel at once tells Zechariah to proclaim that reply aloud for all to hear. The LORD declares that He is "exceedingly jealous for Jerusalem and Zion," and deeply angry with the nations resting at ease—because while He was only "a little angry" with His people, those nations went far too far and added to the disaster (1:14–15). Therefore, God proclaims, "I will return to Jerusalem with mercy": His house will be rebuilt, and a builder's measuring line will be stretched out across Jerusalem (1:16). The angel urges Zechariah to shout out a second promise overflowing with hope: God's cities will once again overflow with prosperity, and the LORD will comfort Zion and again choose Jerusalem (1:17).

Right away, the second vision unfolds. Zechariah lifts his eyes and sees four towering horns (1:18). Asking the interpreting angel what they are, he learns these are the horns that scattered Judah, Israel, and Jerusalem (1:19). Next, the LORD shows him four craftsmen stepping forward (1:20). When Zechariah asks what they have come to do, God explains: those horns scattered Judah "so that no one could raise his head," yet these four craftsmen have arrived to terrify them and cast down the horns of the nations that lifted their power to devastate the land of Judah (1:21).

## Key Takeaways

**Rebuilding the heart must come before rebuilding stone walls.** Before revealing a single glowing vision of Jerusalem's future, God stops the people with one brief call: "Return to Me... and I will return to you" (1:3). The exiles had walked out of Babylon with their feet and laid hands on temple stones, yet if their hearts stayed as stubborn as their ancestors', every outward structure would be hollow.

**Human beings pass away, while God's word endures and overtakes every generation.** The two questions in 1:5–6 sound like the quiet sigh of history: both the rebellious generation and the prophets who warned them are gone. Only one thing remains—the word of God. Just as surely as His word came true in stern judgment when their fathers turned away, it will come true just as surely when He promises mercy to those who return.

**When the whole world looks eerily quiet, God is still watching and caring for His people.** The patrol report—"all the earth is at rest and quiet" (1:11)—captures what discourages believers most: empires and wrongdoers sit undisturbed while faithful people struggle among ruins. Yet the vision proves God's eyes are wide open. His scouts still range across the whole earth, and right down in a low hollow among humble myrtle trees, the Sovereign stands listening to intercession for His people (1:10–13).

**God never excuses those who exploit His discipline to brutalize others.** God had used foreign empires as instruments to discipline Israel, yet He took no pleasure in seeing His people cruelly trampled. When those nations overstepped their bounds in smug cruelty beyond what God intended, God Himself turns back to hold them accountable (1:15).

**For every power raised against God's people, God already has an answer ready to shatter it.** Four fierce horns symbolize overwhelming might that crushed Judah until no one could lift a head (1:19–21). Yet instantly four craftsmen step onto the stage—four against four, not one short. However fearsome earthly powers appear, they remain mere objects God's hammers can smash when His hour strikes.

## Deeper Meaning

**The call to return resounds from the Old Testament into the New.** The invitation "Return to Me... and I will return to you" (1:3) not only opens Zechariah, but is echoed nearly a century later by Malachi (Malachi 3:7). In the New Testament, James strikes the exact same note when urging believers to draw near to God, promising He will draw near to them (James 4:8). God never slams the door after we stumble; He always stands ready to move toward anyone willing to turn back.

**God's word outlasts every human generation.** Zechariah's reminder in 1:5–6—fathers pass away and prophets pass away, yet God's word stands—links directly with Isaiah's celebrated declaration: grass withers and flowers fade, but the word of our God stands forever (Isaiah 40:8). Likewise, God's indictment of nations who acted mercilessly when He was only mildly angry (1:15) echoes His earlier charge against Babylon in Isaiah: God handed His people over, yet Babylon showed no mercy and laid a crushing yoke even on the elderly (Isaiah 47:6). And the promise that He will "comfort Zion" (1:17) immediately recalls the tender opening of Isaiah 40 (Isaiah 40:1–2).

**The seventy years have run their course.** The plea "these seventy years" in 1:12 points straight to Jeremiah's prophecy that Judah would serve Babylon for seventy years before God visited them and brought them home (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). Counting from 586 BC—when Jerusalem's temple was burned—to 519 BC when Zechariah received this vision, nearly seventy full years had elapsed. Asking "how long?" is not baseless complaining; it is gripping tightly to God's own spoken promise.

**The Angel standing to intercede for God's people.** In the first vision, the "angel of the LORD" stands among the myrtle trees pleading for Jerusalem before the LORD of Hosts (1:11–12). Generations of readers have naturally thought of the New Testament Mediator: Jesus Christ, who stands at God's right hand and always lives to intercede for those who belong to Him (Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25).

**Colored horses and horns open up apocalyptic language.** Horses of different colors dispatched across the earth (1:8–11) return in chapter 6 of Zechariah, and centuries later ride forth majestically in Revelation as the four horsemen on white, red, black, and pale horses (Revelation 6:2–8). Meanwhile, horns symbolizing arrogant imperial might (1:18–21) are familiar both from the Psalms' praise of God cutting off the horns of the wicked (Psalms 75:4–5; 75:10) and from Daniel's visions of empires (Daniel 8:3–4).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who was Zechariah, and how did he relate to Haggai?** Zechariah is introduced as "son of Berechiah, the son of Iddo" (1:1), while Ezra simply calls him "son of Iddo" (Ezra 5:1; 6:14), because ancient Hebrew frequently used "son" for a grandson when the grandfather was a prominent clan head. According to the list of returning priests, Iddo headed a priestly family and Zechariah succeeded him (Nehemiah 12:16), making Zechariah both priest and prophet. Ezra shows him standing side by side with Haggai encouraging Zerubbabel and Joshua to rebuild the temple (Ezra 5:1–2). He should not be confused with the earlier Zechariah murdered between the temple and altar in King Joash's reign centuries prior, even though Jesus refers in Matthew to "Zechariah son of Berechiah" (Matthew 23:35)—a detail widely discussed by scholars.

**How many angels appear in the first vision?** Reading 1:8–14 rapidly, it is easy to get confused among "a man riding a red horse," "the angel who was speaking with me," and "the angel of the LORD." Reading carefully reveals two main figures acting together: first, the interpreting angel beside Zechariah who guides him and explains the vision (1:9; 1:13–14); second, the "man riding a red horse" standing among the myrtle trees—also called "the angel of the LORD"—who receives the scouts' report and intercedes before the LORD of Hosts (1:8; 1:10–12). This design spotlights a unique Angel standing in a mediating role between God and His people.

**Do the horse colors carry hidden meanings?** Unlike Revelation, where each horse color clearly signifies a specific calamity (Revelation 6:2–8), in Zechariah 1 the angel never explains any individual symbolism for red, sorrel, or white (1:8–10). The sole point emphasized is that they form a full patrol sent by God across the entire earth. Readers should therefore avoid forcing intricate color symbolism onto details the text leaves unexplained.

**Who are the four horns and four craftsmen?** Scripture does not name four specific empires or kings. Some commentators list four great powers that successively dominated Israel, such as Assyria, Babylon, Persia, and Greece (or Assyria, Egypt, Babylon, and Persia). Many other scholars view the number "four" as representing the four compass directions—north, south, east, and west—meaning every hostile force on every side that ever tore Israel and Judah apart. Either way, the core message is unmistakable: for every power that ever battered God's people, God raises an exact match of craftsmen to bring it down.

**Note the chapter numbering difference across Bibles.** In the original Hebrew text, the final four verses of chapter 1 (the vision of the four horns and four craftsmen) are placed at the start of chapter 2, becoming 2:1–4. English Bibles (including the BSB) keep them at the end of chapter 1 (1:18–21). Knowing this small detail prevents confusion when older commentaries cite the four craftsmen under chapter 2.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the late fourth- and early fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Vulgate, reads the rider on the red horse standing among myrtle trees in a deep hollow as a figure of Christ: He does not stand aloof on a distant peak, but humbles Himself right in the midst of God's small, embattled community in the world. As for the four horns at the chapter's end, Jerome understands them as hostile powers from the four quarters of the earth that scattered God's people, and the four craftsmen as God's instruments sent to shatter that pride. *(Commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Jerome's christological reading of the myrtle-hollow vision and four-directions explanation of the four horns; original text not checked -->

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, pauses closely over the angel of the LORD standing among the myrtle trees and interceding for Jerusalem in verse 12. For Luther, this figure is the pre-incarnate Son of God: He stands right amid the frail church, guarding His people while surrounding nations sit seemingly untouched, and He Himself relays the Father's comforting words to the despondent. *(Lectures on the Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Luther identifying the interceding Angel among the myrtles in verse 12 as Christ; exact wording not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, emphasizes the sharp lesson of verses 5 and 6: people easily assume that because times have changed and older generations are gone, God's word has lost its force; Calvin counters that fathers and prophets alike are mortal, whereas God's word lives forever and overtakes every generation. At verse 12, along with many Reformation interpreters, he recognizes in the interceding angel the figure of Christ our Mediator pleading for His people in distress. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's two central points on the enduring word of God in verses 5–6 and the Mediator in verse 12 in his Minor Prophets commentaries; original text not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws deep encouragement from the symmetry closing the chapter: whenever God's people look up and see four fierce horns aimed at them, God promptly shows four craftsmen stepping forth to match them. However many schemes and forces enemies muster, God always has just as many instruments and servants ready to terrify and cast them down. He also notes that myrtle trees in a low valley vividly picture the church—often lowly in the world's eyes, yet the very spot where the Lord Himself stands in the center. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Zechariah chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's familiar observations on the lowly myrtle trees and the exact balance of four horns and four craftsmen; original text not checked -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the ancient collection of Jewish law and commentary, includes a celebrated discussion of the four craftsmen in verse 20 (chapter 2 verse 3 in Hebrew numbering). Reading the vision messianically, the rabbis name four figures who will arise to redeem Israel: Messiah son of David, Messiah son of Joseph, the prophet Elijah, and the Righteous Priest. In another tractate, the sages interpret the myrtle trees in the deep hollow in verse 8 as righteous figures living amid the Babylonian exile, through whom God's wrath was appeased. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah, folio 52b; Sanhedrin, folio 93a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the list of four craftsmen in Sukkah 52b and the explanation of the myrtles in Sanhedrin 93a are two very well-known Talmudic passages on Zechariah chapter 1 -->
