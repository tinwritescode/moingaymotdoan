# Zechariah 11 — The Rejected Shepherd and Thirty Pieces of Silver

## Story

After two luminous chapters celebrating the humble King and the joyful return of God's people (9:9–10; 10:6–12), chapter 11 pivots sharply into a dark lament. The oracle commands Lebanon to throw open its doors so fire may devour its towering cedars (11:1). Cypress trees must wail because the cedar has fallen and majestic trees lie ruined; the oaks of Bashan must wail because the dense forest has been felled (11:2). Everywhere rings out the wailing of shepherds whose rich pastures are wrecked, and the roaring of young lions because "the pride of the Jordan"—the lush thickets along the Jordan River—has been laid waste (11:3).

Amid that bleak backdrop, the LORD God assigns Zechariah a strange prophetic drama: go "pasture the flock marked for slaughter" (11:4). The flock's plight is heartbreaking: buyers slaughter them without feeling any guilt; sellers smugly praise God, saying, "Blessed be the LORD, for I have become rich!"; and none of their own shepherds feel a shred of pity for them (11:5). The LORD declares He will no longer pity the land's inhabitants, handing each person over to his neighbor and his king to crush the land without rescue (11:6). Obeying God, the prophet takes up shepherding the flock doomed to slaughter—especially the most oppressed of the sheep—choosing two staffs, naming one "Favor" and the other "Union," and begins tending them (11:7).

Yet the shepherd's service ends quickly. Within a single month, he removes "three shepherds," because he has grown weary of them and they detest him (11:8). Faced with the flock's stubbornness, the shepherd resigns: "I will not be your shepherd. Let the dying die, and the perishing perish; and let those who remain devour one another's flesh" (11:9). Then he snaps the staff named "Favor" in two, annulling the covenant God had made with all peoples to protect the flock (11:10). Broken that very day, the act proves to the oppressed sheep watching closely that everything happening is indeed the word of the LORD (11:11).

Before walking off, the shepherd tells the sheep-traders to pay him his wages if they think it right—or leave it if not. They weigh out exactly "thirty pieces of silver" (11:12). The LORD speaks to the prophet with biting sarcasm: "Throw it to the potter—the handsome price at which they valued Me!" So the prophet takes the thirty pieces of silver and throws them to the potter right inside the house of the LORD (11:13). Next he breaks the second staff, "Union," severing the brotherhood between Judah and Israel (11:14).

The prophetic drama adds a second act equally grim. The LORD instructs Zechariah to pick up "the equipment of a foolish shepherd" (11:15). God warns He is about to raise up a ruthless shepherd in the land: he will not care for the lost, seek the young, heal the injured, or feed the healthy, instead devouring the flesh of the fattest sheep and tearing off their very hooves (11:16). Even so, that tyrant will not escape judgment: "Woe to the worthless shepherd who deserts the flock!" May the sword strike his arm and his right eye, leaving his arm completely withered and his right eye totally blind (11:17).

## Key Takeaways

**Greed wearing religious language is the ugliest form of hypocrisy.** The sheep-traders exploit vulnerable people to the slaughter and calmly bless the LORD for making them rich (11:5). Using God's name to sanctify wealth built on other people's suffering signals a society rotten at the root.

**Rejecting the true Shepherd severs both our protection and our unity.** The two staffs, "Favor" and "Union," stand for two supreme blessings God provides: shielding from outside threats and brotherly harmony within (11:7; 11:10; 11:14). Once the people grow sick of the true Shepherd, both staffs snap: peace vanishes outside, and people turn to devouring one another inside (11:9).

**Thirty pieces of silver exposes how cheaply humanity can value God.** After all God's faithful shepherding, they pay Him the exact compensation fixed for a slave gored by an ox. God's razor-edged irony—calling it "the handsome price at which they valued Me" and tossing it aside (11:13)—shows He has zero need for human coins; what grieves and angers Him is contempt for the One who sustains us.

**Sometimes the harshest judgment is God handing people over to the leaders they chose.** Refusing a gracious shepherd never leads to real freedom; it drops people straight into the grip of "a foolish shepherd" who fattens himself on the flock (11:15–16). Pushing away God's gentle rule always opens the door to cruel substitutes.

**Even in the darkest hour, humble sheep still recognize God's voice.** Amid greedy shepherds and heartless merchants, 11:11 still highlights "the afflicted of the flock" who pay attention and recognize the word of the LORD. God never lumps everyone blindly together; He sees the poor and faithful standing firm amid chaos.

## Deeper Meaning

**Thirty pieces of silver and the betrayal of Jesus.** More than five centuries later, the eerie prophetic scene of 11:12–13 played out down to painful detail during Passion Week. Judas Iscariot went to the chief priests asking what they would pay to hand Jesus over, and they counted out exactly "thirty pieces of silver" (Matthew 26:15)—the exact legal compensation under Moses's law for a servant gored by an ox (Exodus 21:32). Overwhelmed with remorse, Judas flung the thirty silver coins into the temple, and the priests used that money to buy the potter's field as a burial ground for foreigners (Matthew 27:3–10). Zechariah's true Shepherd appraised at a slave's price came to full, piercing life in Jesus—the Good Shepherd rejected by His own nation's religious establishment (John 10:11–13).

**Two broken staffs reverse Ezekiel's sign of reunion.** Ezekiel had once taken two wooden sticks labeled Judah and Joseph and joined them into one single stick to foretell north and south uniting again (Ezekiel 37:16–19). Zechariah 11 runs that drama in reverse: breaking "Union" snaps brotherhood between Judah and Israel (11:14), while breaking "Favor" revokes the covenant of peace like that promised through Hosea (Hosea 2:18). Chapter 11 warns that earlier restoration promises are never a blank check: if God's people reject Him all over again, ancient fractures and disasters can return.

**The "foolish shepherd" echoes Ezekiel's indictment of bad rulers.** Describing a shepherd who ignores the lost, leaves the wounded unhealed, fails to feed the healthy, and devours the choice sheep (11:16) follows almost line for line the LORD's rebuke of Israel's selfish rulers in Ezekiel (Ezekiel 34:2–4). Likewise, lions roaring over ruined Jordan thickets in 11:3 echoes Jeremiah's familiar warning before Jerusalem's fall (Jeremiah 12:5; 49:19). The repetition reveals that Israel's tragedy was no isolated fluke, but a recurring disease whenever power casts off the fear of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who are the "three shepherds" removed in one month in 11:8?** This is one of the most puzzling verses in the entire Old Testament; over two millennia, interpreters have proposed dozens of candidates. Some view them as Israel's three leadership offices (king, priest, prophet); others link them to three specific Persian- or Greek-era monarchs or high priests; still others treat "three" and "one month" symbolically for swift, complete removal of corrupt leadership. Honesty calls us to admit we do not know their exact historical names while holding fast to the verse's clear message: the rupture between the true Shepherd and the era's leaders had passed the point of repair.

**"Potter" or "treasury" in the house of the LORD (11:13)?** In Hebrew, the words for "potter" (*yotser*) and "treasury" or "treasurer" (*otsar*) differ by a single letter. The ancient Syriac translation and a few modern versions read "throw it into the treasury" in the temple, while the traditional Hebrew Masoretic Text—followed by the BSB—reads "throw it to the potter." The near-identical sound forms a sharp pun: silver that might have gone into the temple treasury is contemptuously tossed aside to a lowly potter, underscoring disgust at such an insulting wage. It is precisely this wording ("potter") that Matthew weaves into the purchase of the potter's field (Matthew 27:7–10).

**Why does Matthew 27:9 attribute this quotation to "Jeremiah the prophet"?** Careful readers of Matthew 27:9–10 are often surprised: "thirty pieces of silver" and "the potter" clearly come from Zechariah 11:12–13, so why does Matthew name Jeremiah? Scholars offer three main explanations. The most widespread is a "composite quotation": Matthew blends Zechariah 11's thirty pieces of silver with Jeremiah's potter's jar in the Valley of Hinnom alongside innocent blood (Jeremiah 19:1–2) and Jeremiah's purchase of a field (Jeremiah 32:6–9), naming only the major prophet Jeremiah according to ancient Jewish custom. Another notes that in some early Jewish scroll orderings, Jeremiah headed the Prophets division, so his name could refer to the prophetic collection as a whole. Other commentators—such as John Calvin—candidly view Jeremiah's name as an early memory slip or scribal error that in no way diminishes the truth of the event.

**Harsh verses on abandoning the flock and devouring one another (11:6; 11:9; 11:16).** Hearing God say He will no longer pity the land, leaving sheep to "devour one another's flesh," and raising up a cruel shepherd who tears off hooves naturally feels chilling. Yet the chapter never calls God cruel or approves of the ruthless shepherd—proved immediately by the closing curse striking that worthless shepherd down (11:17). Scripture is stating a sobering reality: when humanity stubbornly pushes away God's protective hand, He honors that choice by pulling back. The resulting horrors of civil strife and predatory rulers are not things God delights in, but the devastating fallout when a society cuts its own covenant ties with Him.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, carefully examines Matthew 27:9's attribution of Zechariah 11's thirty-silver-pieces prophecy to Jeremiah. He observes that even if the Evangelist's mind turned to Jeremiah's name, it harms faith not at all; rather, it reveals the deep unity of the prophets, because one and the same Spirit spoke through them all, and Jeremiah too speaks of both a potter and buying a field. *(The Harmony of the Gospels, Book 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Augustine's argument in Book 3 of The Harmony of the Gospels regarding Matthew chapter 27 verse 9 and Zechariah chapter 11; exact section number not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, highlights God's bitter irony in 11:13 when calling thirty pieces of silver a "handsome price": it indicts the people's ingratitude for pricing God's saving care at the level of a slave. Turning to Matthew 27:9 citing this text under Jeremiah's name, Calvin stays remarkably candid: he states plainly that the prophecy belongs to Zechariah, admits it is unclear how Jeremiah's name entered the text or whether it was an early slip, and refuses to twist the facts with strained excuses. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 11; Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Matthew chapter 27)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's candid admission on Jeremiah's name in Matthew chapter 27 verse 9 and his sarcastic exposition of Zechariah chapter 11 verse 13 are well known; original text not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses on 11:5 to expose the dangerous hypocrisy of the sheep-dealers: they grow wealthy by sending others to slaughter yet casually thank God as though ill-gotten profits were blessings from heaven. Moving to the chapter's second half, he draws a solemn lesson: when a people reject Christ the Good Shepherd, the gravest judgment God allows is leaving them under the rule of foolish, predatory shepherds. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Zechariah chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's remarks on hypocrisy in verse 5 and the judgment of the foolish shepherd at the end of chapter 11; original text not checked -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the ancient collection of Jewish law and commentary, preserves a dramatic story tied to 11:1. It relates that forty years before Roman troops burned the Second Temple in AD 70, the great temple doors swung open all by themselves. Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai rebuked the sanctuary and quoted Zechariah 11:1 straight at it: "Open your doors, O Lebanon, that the fire may consume your cedars!"—for in Jewish interpretive tradition, "Lebanon" stood symbolically for the temple built of Lebanon cedar, foretelling the day flames would devour it. *(Babylonian Talmud, Yoma, folio 39b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the account of Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai quoting Zechariah chapter 11 verse 1 before the self-opening temple doors in Yoma 39b is famous and widely cited -->
