# Zechariah 13 — The Cleansing Fountain and the Stricken Shepherd

## Story

Chapter 13 flows straight without pause from Jerusalem weeping in repentance before the Pierced One at the close of chapter 12. Opening with "On that day," tears of contrition immediately meet God's stream of cleansing grace: "On that day a fountain will be opened to the house of David and the people of Jerusalem, to cleanse them from sin and impurity" (13:1).

That fountain does far more than wash away yesterday's guilt; it launches a sweeping purification across the whole land. The LORD of Hosts declares He will erase the very names of idols from the earth so they are never remembered again, and He will banish false prophets and "the unclean spirit" from the land (13:2). Zeal for truth will burn so fiercely in that day that if anyone still dares to prophesy lies in the LORD's name, his own "father and mother who gave birth to him" will refuse to shield him, condemning him and piercing him through (13:3).

Under that cleansing wave, false prophecy collapses in total disgrace. Every false prophet will feel ashamed of his fabricated visions and stop wearing "a hairy cloak" to masquerade as a man of God. Confronted, he will quickly deny it: "I am not a prophet; I work the land, for I was bought as a servant in my youth." Even when pressed about telltale scars on his body—"What are these wounds on your chest [literally, between your hands]?"—he will scramble for an awkward cover story: "These are the wounds I received in the house of my friends" (13:4–6).

Starting at verse 7, the chapter abruptly shifts into a brief poem that shakes all redemption history. The LORD of Hosts Himself issues a command to the sword: "Awake, O sword, against My Shepherd, against the man who is My Companion, declares the LORD of Hosts. Strike the Shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered, and I will turn My hand against the little ones" (13:7). In the immense upheaval following the Shepherd's striking, two-thirds of the land will be cut off and perish, leaving one-third behind. Yet that surviving third is not abandoned; God Himself guides them through refining fire: "I will bring this third through the fire; I will refine them like silver and test them like gold." Fire serves not to destroy, but to burn off impurities until the warm covenant exchange closing the chapter blossoms anew: "They will call on My name, and I will answer them. I will say, 'They are My people,' and they will say, 'The LORD is our God'" (13:8–9).

## Key Takeaways

**True sorrow over sin always opens the road to a fountain of cleansing.** At chapter 12's close, people's eyes open to the One they pierced and they weep bitterly; in the very first verse of chapter 13, God has "a fountain... opened" waiting to wash away both sin and impurity (13:1). God never exposes human guilt to crush us in despair, but to lead us straight to the cleansing stream.

**Real forgiveness always brings a clean break with idols and deceit.** Flowing from 13:1, verses 13:2–6 show that a community cleansed by God no longer tolerates idols or impostors using God's name to mislead people. Religious hypocrisy that once strutted boldly in a prophet's cloak retreats in shame from the life of God's people.

**Redemption passes right through the wounding of God's own "Shepherd" and "Companion."** Verse 13:7 holds a breathtaking mystery: the sword is not merely swung by earthly enemies—the LORD of Hosts Himself summons it against "My Shepherd" and "the man who is My Companion." Behind the agonizing scattering of the flock stands a saving purpose ordained in heaven.

**Trials work like a refiner's fire to produce a pure covenant people.** The flames of 13:9 never aim to incinerate the surviving third, but to burn away dross until living fellowship revives in full: they call, God answers, and both exchange the dearest covenant vows—"They are My people" and "The LORD is our God" (13:9).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus Himself quotes 13:7 on the night of His arrest in Gethsemane.** On the night of the Last Supper, walking toward the Mount of Olives, Jesus foretold what was coming to the eleven disciples using Zechariah 13:7 verbatim: "This very night you will all fall away on account of Me. For it is written: 'I will strike the Shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered'" (Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27). By stepping into the titles "My Shepherd" and "the man who is My Companion"—a potent Hebrew word denoting *an equal associate standing side by side*—Jesus simultaneously affirmed His divine equality with the Father and His willing sacrifice on the cross within the Father's redemptive plan.

**The cleansing fountain (13:1) connects to the blood and water from Jesus's side.** In 12:10, the people look upon the One they pierced—the very line John quotes (John 19:37) immediately after the soldier's spear opened Jesus's side and "blood and water flowed out" (John 19:34). Across centuries, believers have naturally seen in 13:1—"a fountain... opened" for "sin and impurity"—a radiant foreshadowing of Christ's blood purifying us from every sin (1 John 1:7). Not ceremonial washings of the old temple, but the fountain of grace opened from the pierced Shepherd Himself cleanses the human conscience.

**Faith tested by fire and the echo of Hosea's covenant.** God refining the surviving remnant "like silver and... like gold" (13:9) is echoed by the apostle Peter comforting suffering Christians: "so that the proven character of your faith—more precious than gold, which perishes even though refined by fire—may result in praise, glory, and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 1:7). Meanwhile, the tender covenant refrain ending chapter 13 ("They are My people"—"The LORD is our God") mirrors the restoration pledges of Hosea 2:23 and Jeremiah 31:33.

## Notes & Nuances

**Parents piercing their own child for false prophecy (13:3) is very hard to read.** Modern readers rightly recoil from 13:3 ("his father and mother who gave birth to him will pierce him through when he prophesies"). Two historical and literary points clarify what is happening. First, this directly echoes the stringent covenant statute of Deuteronomy 13:6–9 forbidding even closest family members from shielding someone enticing Israel after false gods. Second, reading 13:3 together with 13:4–6 shows the prophet's rhetorical point: hatred of religious fraud in the renewed community will run so deep that not even blood ties will protect impostors exploiting God's name—and indeed, in 13:4–6, false prophets strip off their hairy cloaks in embarrassment and quit before any bloodshed ever occurs. It is fierce Old Testament imagery for uncompromising loyalty to truth, never an endorsement of family violence today.

**Does 13:6 ("What are these wounds on your chest [between your hands]?") refer to false prophets or to Jesus?** This verse carries a fascinating split history of interpretation. **The direct contextual reading (most modern scholars and key Reformers):** verses 13:2–6 form one continuous portrait of ashamed false prophets disowning their profession; the scars "between your hands" (across chest or back) were self-inflicted gashes cut during pagan ecstatic rituals like those of Baal's prophets (1 Kings 18:28). Caught with incriminating marks of his former trade, the impostor invents a lame excuse about getting hurt at a friend's house! **The older devotional Christian reading:** many historic preachers detached 13:6 and linked it with 13:7 directly following to meditate on the nail wounds in Jesus's hands—wounded in the house of His own "friends," Israel. Knowing both readings respects the natural flow of 13:2–6 while illuminating why this verse moved so many hymns and sermons.

**Is "turn My hand against the little ones" in 13:7 negative or protective?** In Hebrew, the idiom *turn one's hand upon/against someone* carries two opposite senses: either raising a hand in judgment—fitting the two-thirds cut off (13:8)—or turning a caring hand to shelter "the little ones," the vulnerable flock scattered when their shepherd is struck, fitting the preservation of the surviving third (13:8–9). Both shades illuminate the flock's path: they pass through painful scattering yet remain within God's refining and sustaining hand.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, immediately ties the opening fountain for "sin and impurity" (13:1) to the waters of baptism and cleansing blood from Jesus's side, and explains 13:7 directly through Jesus's citation in Matthew 26:31 on the night of His arrest when the disciples fled. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, displays exemplary exegetical discipline across 13:6 and 13:7. First, Calvin firmly rejects forcing 13:6 ("wounds between your hands") onto Jesus's nail prints: he shows plainly that 13:2–6 speaks entirely of embarrassed false prophets lying to escape exposure, so applying that line to Christ strains the text. Immediately afterward, however, at 13:7 ("Awake, O sword, against My Shepherd, against the man who is My Companion"), Calvin champions this as an authentic, glorious prophecy of Christ's Passion—One sharing the Father's divine essence ("My Companion") who nonetheless submits to the sword of justice to redeem His flock. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 13)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's sharp distinction—rejecting a christological reading of chapter 13 verse 6 while strongly affirming verse 7 of Christ's deity and Passion—is one of his best-known comments on Zechariah -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, reflects warmly on 13:1: the fountain washing away sin is no locked cistern, but an "opened" spring flowing freely for both David's royal house and every resident of Jerusalem through Christ's blood. Coming to the silver-and-gold furnace of 13:9, Henry comforts believers that God sits right beside the crucible like an attentive refiner: He never lets the flames burn too hot or damage His precious metal, burning away only the dross so His people's faith gleams brighter. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Zechariah chapter 13)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral points on the open fountain in chapter 13 verse 1 and the careful silversmith watching the fire in verse 9; original text on ccel.org not checked -->

**Joyce G. Baldwin**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, highlights the tight thread joining three figures across chapters 11–13: the good shepherd rejected for thirty silver coins (chapter 11), the Pierced One mourned across the land (chapter 12), and "My Shepherd"—"the man who is My Companion"—struck by God's own sword (13:7).
