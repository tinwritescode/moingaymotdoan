# Zechariah 7 — A Question About Fasting and Hearts as Hard as Flint

## Story

Nearly two years after the night of visions in chapters 1–6, Zechariah enters its second section with a clear date stamp: "In the fourth year of King Darius... on the fourth day of the ninth month, the month of Chislev"—around December of 518 BC, halfway through rebuilding the temple in Jerusalem. A delegation from Bethel—Sharezer and Regem-melech along with their attendants—travels up to Jerusalem to seek the LORD's favor and ask the priests and prophets a very practical question: "Should I weep and fast in the fifth month, as I have done these many years?" For seventy years, the fifth month had been observed with weeping and fasting to mourn the day Babylonian troops burned Solomon's temple; now that the new temple was taking shape, did they still need to keep that day of mourning? (7:1–3).

Instead of giving a quick "yes" or "no" on the calendar ritual, the LORD of Hosts directs Zechariah to widen the answer to all the people and the priests, cutting straight to the hidden motives of their hearts. God asks two piercing counter-questions: "When you fasted and mourned in the fifth and seventh months for these seventy years, was it really for Me that you fasted? And when you were eating and drinking, were you not doing so simply for yourselves?" Across seventy years of observing their self-appointed fasts in the fifth and seventh months, they had really been mourning their own losses rather than turning toward God—just as their feasting had served only their own appetites. God then reminds them of the very words He had proclaimed through the earlier prophets back when Jerusalem and its surrounding towns still thrived in peace (7:4–7).

The word of the LORD comes again to Zechariah, distilling the core message those earlier prophets had faithfully taught: "Administer true justice. Show loving devotion and compassion to one another. Do not oppress the widow or the fatherless, the foreigner or the poor. And do not plot evil in your hearts against one another" (7:8–10).

Yet how had their ancestors responded? Four vivid portraits expose their stubbornness: they refused to pay attention, "turned a stubborn shoulder" like an unruly ox shaking off its yoke, "stopped up their ears so they could not hear," and even "made their hearts like flint" against the law and the words the LORD of Hosts had sent by His Spirit through the earlier prophets. The fallout was fitting and bitter: "Just as I called and they would not listen, so when they called I would not listen." Fierce wrath from the LORD of Hosts scattered them like a whirlwind among unfamiliar nations, turning "the pleasant land into a wasteland" with no one passing to and fro (7:11–14).

## Key Takeaways

**God examines the motive behind worship before looking at outward form.** The delegation from Bethel asked a straightforward ritual question about the calendar (7:3), yet God probed their hearts instead: "was it really for Me that you fasted?" (7:5). Seventy years of fasting, prayer, or religious habit still ring hollow if we practice them merely to nurse self-pity or keep up routine without turning our hearts toward God.

**Worship that truly pleases God shows up in justice, mercy, and protecting the vulnerable.** Verses 7:9–10 lay down an unmistakable standard: practice true justice, show loyal love and compassion to one another, refuse to oppress ancient society's four most vulnerable groups (widows, orphans, foreigners, and the poor), and keep the heart clear of scheming harm against neighbors. True faith is measured not by how many days we skip meals, but by how we treat the people right beside us.

**People do not turn hard-hearted by accident; repeatedly shutting out God's word hardens the heart into stone.** Verse 7:12 uses an active, sobering verb: "They made their hearts like flint." Spiritual numbness is not blind fate; it grows from choice after choice of shrugging off God's yoke and plugging our ears to the Spirit's voice in Scripture (7:11–12).

**Old historical lessons remain live warnings for a new generation.** Returning exiles busy rebuilding the temple could easily assume that raising fresh walls and keeping proper fast days would automatically spare them their ancestors' tragedy. Chapter 7 confronts them with plain truth: the land did not become a wasteland for lack of ceremonies, but because they refused to listen when God called for justice and mercy (7:13–14).

## Deeper Meaning

**The fifth- and seventh-month fasts commemorated the wounds of 586 BC.** The Law of Moses actually mandated only one annual fast day, the Day of Atonement in the seventh month; yet during seventy years of exile, the Jews added four extra fasts mourning Jerusalem's downfall (8:19). Among them, the **fifth-month** fast memorialized the day Nebuzaradan, captain of Babylon's guard, burned down the LORD's temple and the city (2 Kings 25:8–9), while the **seventh-month** fast mourned the assassination of Governor Gedaliah at Mizpah, which shattered the last fragile remnant in Judah (2 Kings 25:25; Jeremiah 41:1–2). Not until the close of chapter 8 does God finally answer their calendar question directly with a breathtaking promise: He will turn all four gloomy fasts into "joy and gladness and cheerful feasts for the house of Judah" (8:19).

**The "fast God chooses" runs straight from Isaiah 58 to Matthew's Gospel.** Exposing self-centered fasting in Zechariah 7:5–10 harmonizes seamlessly with Isaiah's message: God takes no pleasure in bowing heads and lying on ashes while oppressing workers; the fast He chooses is loosening chains of injustice, sharing bread with the hungry, and sheltering the poor (Isaiah 58:3–7). In the New Testament, Jesus carries that exact spirit forward, teaching His disciples not to parade gloomy faces for human applause when fasting, but to direct their hearts quietly to the Father in secret (Matthew 6:16–18), putting mercy and justice ahead of petty ritualism.

**Prophetic words sent "by His Spirit" anchor the doctrine of Scripture.** One compact clause in 7:12 carries extraordinary theological weight: "the words that the LORD of Hosts had sent by His Spirit through the earlier prophets." This is one of the clearest Old Testament statements confirming that prophetic Scripture did not arise from private human ideas, but was breathed and carried along by the Holy Spirit—connecting directly to the apostle Peter's testimony in the New Testament that no prophecy ever came by human will, but people spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit (2 Peter 1:21).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who in Bethel sent the delegation in 7:2?** Bethel lay roughly twelve miles north of Jerusalem; once a center of the northern kingdom's golden-calf worship, it had been resettled by returning Judeans after the exile (Ezra 2:28). Because the Hebrew syntax of 7:2 is quite terse, English Bibles handle it in two ways: many (including the BSB) read "the people of Bethel had sent Sharezer and Regem-melech," while others take *Bethel-sharezer* as a compound Babylonian personal name of an exilic Jewish official who sent Regem-melech to inquire. Either way, figures bearing Babylonian names (Sharezer is Babylonian) were coming to Jerusalem to seek God's guidance at the rising temple.

**Why does 7:5 ask twice, "was it really for Me that you fasted?"** Repeating "for Me" captures sharp Hebrew emphasis. The Jews had instituted the fifth- and seventh-month mourning fasts on their own initiative without any command in the Law of Moses, and for seventy years they grieved endlessly over losing their nation, temple, and prestige while overlooking *why* disaster had fallen. God reminds them that mourning the consequences without repenting of the causes—injustice and hard hearts—is not true fasting unto the LORD.

**Hearts "as hard as flint" (7:12) and the reciprocity of 7:13.** The Hebrew word *shamir* (rendered "flint" in the BSB and "adamant" or "diamond" in older translations) refers to an ultra-hard abrasive stone used to engrave ordinary rock—the same image found in Jeremiah 17:1 and Ezekiel 3:9. When people willfully harden a supple heart into flint, God's word simply ricochets off it. Meanwhile, 7:13 ("Just as I called and they would not listen, so when they called I would not listen") shows God's fitting justice—yet never a permanent rejection of anyone who truly repents, as chapter 8 immediately proves with an outpouring of grace for the returned people.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, clearly explains the historical background of the fifth-month fast in 7:3 and the seventh-month fast in 7:5, linking them respectively to the burning of the temple and the murder of Governor Gedaliah. He observes that God's response in 7:5–10 teaches Jews and Christians alike an enduring truth: depriving the body of food carries zero worth before God unless people also abstain from injustice, falsehood, and oppressing widows and orphans. *(Commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Jerome linking the fifth- and seventh-month fasts to the events of 586 BC and prioritizing ethical justice over mere abstinence in Zechariah chapter 7; original Latin not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, zeroes in on the razor-sharp question in 7:5–6 ("was it really for Me that you fasted?"). Calvin unmasks the subtle hypocrisy of formalistic religion: people readily invent self-made austere rituals outside God's law and congratulate themselves for piety, while brushing aside what God actually commands—justice and compassion (7:9–10). For Calvin, what 7:11–12 demands is open-eared, soft-hearted obedience to the Spirit speaking through Scripture. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Zechariah chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's critique of self-invented fasting rituals versus genuine obedience to God's word in Zechariah chapter 7 verses 5–10 is a signature, widely known feature of his lectures -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws a searching test of self-examination from 7:5–6: in every act of worship—whether fasting in sorrow or feasting in joy—the question deciding its worth before God is simply, "Am I doing this for the Lord, or for myself?" Henry also pauses over the "stubborn shoulder" and "heart like flint" (7:11–12) to warn that refusing to hear God's voice while He calls gradually leads toward the bleakest tragedy in 7:13: crying out in trouble only to meet silent skies. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Zechariah chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's two characteristic pastoral themes on "for God or for self" in verses 5–6 and progressive heart-hardening in verses 11–13; original text on ccel.org not checked -->
