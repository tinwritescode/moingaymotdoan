# Joel 1 — The Locust Plague and the Call to Fast

## Story

The book opens tersely, giving neither king's reign nor date: "This is the word of the LORD that came to Joel son of Pethuel" (1:1). Instantly the prophet summons the elders and every inhabitant of the land to pay close attention. A catastrophe unprecedented in their lifetime—and unheard of in all their ancestors' days—has just struck Judah. The crisis is so massive they must tell it to their children, and their children to the next generation, passing the memory down across ages. Wave upon wave of insects have stripped everything bare: "What the chewing locust has left, the swarming locust has eaten; what the swarming locust has left, the young locust has eaten; and what the young locust has left, the destroying locust has eaten" (1:2–4).

From the nation as a whole, Joel calls out specific groups one by one—starting with drunkards and wine drinkers. He tells them to wake up and wail, because sweet wine has been snatched right from their lips. The reason is chilling: "a nation has invaded My land, powerful and without number"—a locust swarm dense as an invading army. Its teeth are like a lion's teeth, with the fangs of a lioness; it has ruined the vines, splintered the fig trees, stripped off their bark and tossed it away, leaving bare branches bleached white (1:5–7).

Joel turns to the whole land, urging it to lament like a young virgin dressed in sackcloth mourning the bridegroom of her youth. The disaster has not merely emptied cupboards; it has struck straight inside the house of the LORD: grain offerings and drink offerings have vanished from daily worship, throwing the priests into mourning. Fields lie wasted, the ground grieves, grain is ruined, new wine has dried up, and olive oil fails (1:8–10).

The summons to wail rolls on to farmers and vinedressers. They stand dismayed and mourn because the wheat and barley harvest of the field has perished. Every fruit tree sustaining Judah's vitality—vine, fig, pomegranate, palm, apple, and every tree of the field—has withered away. And with the land lying lifeless, "surely the joy of mankind has dried up!" (1:11–12).

Next, Joel directly addresses the priests who minister day and night at the altar: put on sackcloth and weep—even spend the entire night lying in sackcloth—because grain offerings and drink offerings are withheld from the house of your God. They must not stand by silently; they must act at once: "Consecrate a fast; call a sacred assembly! Gather the elders and all the residents of the land to the house of the LORD your God, and cry out to the LORD!" (1:13–14).

Right inside that urgent call, the prophet cries out in alarm: "Alas for the day! For the Day of the LORD is near, and it will come as destruction from the Almighty." Before their very eyes food has been cut off, and joy and gladness have vanished from God's house. Seeds lie shriveled beneath parched clods; storehouses stand in ruins and granaries collapse because the grain has dried up. Even the animals share the agony: cattle groan, herds wander aimlessly with no pasture, and flocks of sheep suffer bitterly (1:15–18).

At the close of the chapter, Joel does not merely stand back directing others; he drops to his own knees to intercede for the whole land: "To You, O LORD, I call!" Scorching drought like fire has devoured the open pastures and burned up every tree in the field. Even the wild beasts pant up toward God, because every stream of water has dried up (1:19–20).

## Key Takeaways

**Catastrophe shakes both everyday livelihoods and sacred worship.** The locust invasion and drought spared nobody in Judah: revelers lost their wine, farmers lost their crops, and priests lost offerings for the altar (1:5; 1:9; 1:11). For Joel, the sharpest wound is not simply empty stomachs, but the interruption of daily fellowship and worship at God's house.

**Honest grief must drive us directly into God's presence.** Joel never tells the people to put on brave faces or try to power through crisis on their own. He calls them to weep like a young bride mourning her lost husband (1:8), then channels that weeping straight to the temple in fasting, solemn assembly, and prayer (1:14). Grief kept apart from God breeds despair; grief brought before God opens a doorway to hope.

**Present shockwaves serve as wake-up calls to a greater Day.** Gazing at fields stripped bare by locusts, Joel refuses to shrug it off as bad luck or strange weather. He recognizes inside it the shadow of "the Day of the LORD" drawing near (1:15). Sudden disruptions in life act as alarm bells waking us from spiritual sleep and reminding us how fragile we stand before the Almighty.

**All creation groans together when the land suffers.** Few chapters in Scripture listen so closely to animal suffering: cattle bewildered, flocks distressed, wild creatures panting upward beside bone-dry streambeds (1:18; 1:20). Humanity and the earth are bound together; brokenness on earth drags innocent creatures into shared pain.

**God's messenger is also the first intercessor.** For eighteen verses, Joel urges elders, farmers, and priests to weep and cry out. In the final two verses, he does not stand aloof pointing fingers; he enters his people's pain and cries out first: "To You, O LORD, I call!" (1:19).

## Deeper Meaning

**Chilling echoes of the plague in Egypt.** Joel's opening—a disaster unmatched since their ancestors' days that must be retold across generations (1:2–3)—directly recalls the locust plague poured out on Egypt long before (Exodus 10:2; 10:14–15). What startles Israel is the reversal: once God sent locusts to judge Pharaoh and free His people, yet now that very plague sweeps across the land He gave them.

**Covenant discipline and the path of prayer Solomon opened.** Back in Moses' day, God warned in the covenant that if Israel drifted away, locusts and worms would devour their wheat, vineyards, and olive groves (Deuteronomy 28:38–42). Yet when dedicating the temple, King Solomon also prayed open a door of mercy: whenever famine, drought, locust, or caterpillar strikes the land, if people recognize the plague in their own hearts and pray toward this temple, hear from heaven and forgive (1 Kings 8:37–40). By summoning the whole land to God's house to cry out (1:14), Joel leads Judah down the exact road Solomon prayed into place.

**The daily rhythm of sacrifice broken off.** Under Mosaic law, morning and evening every day at the temple required a lamb accompanied by a grain offering of fine flour mixed with oil and a drink offering of wine (Exodus 29:38–41). That steady pulse signified God's abiding presence and blessing among His people. Repeating twice the grief that grain and drink offerings have been cut off from the LORD's house (1:9; 1:13) told ancient listeners instantly: disaster has pierced straight to the heart of the covenant.

**"The Day of the LORD" uniting the prophets right into Revelation.** Verse 1:15 sounds nearly identical to Isaiah's announcement of judgment day (Isaiah 13:6), and shares the sobering edge of Amos's warning not to presume the Day of the LORD brings easy triumph, for it is darkness rather than light (Amos 5:18–20). Centuries later, the terrifying swarm with "teeth like lions' teeth" in 1:6 reappears vividly in the end-time judgment vision of Revelation (Revelation 9:7–8).

**Creation's groaning and the promise of repayment ahead.** The Hebrew verb for wild animals "panting" for God in 1:20 is the exact verb used of a deer panting for flowing streams in the Psalms (Psalms 42:1). In the New Testament, Paul widens that cry to all creation: the whole created order groans together in labor pains, waiting eagerly for liberation alongside God's children (Romans 8:20–22). And right inside Joel itself, those four dreaded locust names in 1:4 do not get the final word: in the very next chapter, God names all four back again while promising to repay every year they devoured (2:25).

## Notes & Nuances

**We cannot pinpoint Joel's exact historical era.** Unlike many prophetic books dated by kings' reigns, 1:1 gives only Joel's name and his father Pethuel. Scholars lean toward two primary dates. One group places Joel very early, in the ninth century BC during young King Joash's minority, when elders and priests managed affairs—explaining why no king is mentioned. A larger majority today places Joel in the post-exilic era, around the fifth or fourth century BC, noting that the temple stands rebuilt, Judah has no king, and chapter 3 speaks of God's people already scattered among the nations. Precisely because Joel is unmoored from a single reign, his words speak readily across every generation.

**Are the four locust terms in 1:4 different species or four stages of one swarm?** Hebrew uses four distinct terms, rendered in the Berean Standard Bible as *chewing locust*, *swarming locust*, *young locust*, and *destroying locust*. Some scholars view them as four developmental stages of the desert locust—from wingless hatchling to full flying swarm; others read them as four poetic synonyms or successive insect waves stripping every crop bare. Under either view, the rhetorical punch is unmistakable: wave after wave leaves not a single green blade alive.

**Are the locusts real insects or a metaphor for human invaders?** Reading chapter 1 closely, they eat vines, strip fig bark, accompany drought that dries up streams and shrivels seed under clods (1:4; 1:7; 1:17; 1:20)—every clue points to a literal, devastating locust plague striking Judah. Yet because Joel compares them to "a nation... powerful and without number" with lions' teeth (1:6), and in chapter 2 portrays them as an army scaling walls, many ancient commentators allegorized the four locusts as four successive empires invading Israel. Most modern scholars hold both levels together: starting from a real historical locust swarm, the prophet lifts his people's eyes to the far greater judgment of the Day of the LORD.

**The covenant triad of grain, new wine, and olive oil in 1:10.** Verse 1:10 deliberately lists all three staple crops of Judah—grain, new wine, and olive oil (though a few older translations accidentally conflated "new wine" and "oil"). Read alongside the "sweet wine" cut off in 1:5 and the withered vines of 1:12, this classic covenant triad signals the total collapse of every pillar sustaining life and worship in the land.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the early-fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Bible, reports two readings of 1:4 right inside his commentary. Alongside the literal sense of an actual locust plague devastating Judah, he records the traditional allegorical reading shared by Jewish sages and many early Christians: the four locust waves symbolize four successive empires invading God's people—Assyria-Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, flatly rejected reading the four locust terms of 1:4 allegorically as four foreign empires. In his view, forcing an allegory blunts the passage's real edge: Almighty God needs no mighty armies—just sending tiny insects like locusts suffices to bring an arrogant nation to its knees. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, notices how Joel calls class after class to lament—drunkards, priests, farmers—showing that calamity touches every corner of life. Like Calvin, he remarks on 1:9 and 1:13 that true love for God grieves more over empty temple altars than empty private barns. **Douglas Stuart**, the American Old Testament scholar, frames all of chapter 1 within the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28: locusts and drought are never freak accidents, but covenant discipline designed to pull Judah back to God.
