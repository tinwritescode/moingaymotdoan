# Romans 1 — Not Ashamed of the Gospel

## Story

Paul opens his letter by introducing himself: he is a servant of Christ Jesus, called to be an apostle and set apart for the gospel of God. This gospel is no recent invention; God promised it beforehand through his prophets in the Holy Scriptures. Its core content is a Person: God's Son, born from David's royal line according to the flesh, and declared with power to be the Son of God by his resurrection from the dead. Through him Paul received grace and apostleship to bring about the obedience of faith among all the Gentiles. He writes to all in Rome who are loved by God and called to be saints, wishing them grace and peace (1:1–7). Paul has never set foot in Rome; this church was not founded by him. So he introduces himself first through the gospel itself—the way someone introduces themselves by what they live for.

First, Paul gives thanks to God because their faith is spoken of throughout the entire world. Calling God as his witness, he tells them how continually he remembers them in prayer, asking for an opportunity at last to visit them. He longs to see them so he can share some spiritual gift to strengthen them—then immediately softens that humbly: really, so that both sides may be mutually encouraged by each other's faith, yours and mine. Many times he planned to come yet was prevented. Feeling obligated to everyone—Greeks and non-Greeks, wise and foolish alike—he is eager to preach the gospel to those in Rome as well (1:8–15).

Then arrive the thesis verses of the entire letter: "For I am not ashamed of the gospel, because it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, first to the Jew, then to the Greek." In the gospel, the righteousness of God is revealed from faith to faith, just as it is written: "The righteous will live by faith" (1:16–17; Habakkuk 2:4). Everything Paul writes across the next fifteen chapters unpacks those two sentences.

Before unpacking the good news, however, Paul lays bare the bad news. God's wrath is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of people who suppress the truth through their wickedness. Nobody can plead ignorance. What can be known about God sits plainly before their eyes: ever since the creation of the world, his invisible qualities—his eternal power and divine nature—have been clearly perceived through what he has made. Therefore humanity stands completely without excuse (1:18–20).

The problem is never a lack of evidence, but deliberate refusal. Though they knew God, they neither glorified him as God nor gave thanks to him. Their thinking turned futile, and their foolish hearts grew dark. Claiming to be wise, they became fools, exchanging the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal humans, birds, four-footed animals, and reptiles (1:21–23). In Paul's eyes, that is the root sin beneath all others: trading the Creator for created things.

Three times Paul writes that God "gave them over." First, God gave them over to the impure desires of their hearts, because they traded God's truth for a lie and worshiped created things rather than the Creator. Second, God gave them over to shameful passions: women exchanged natural relations for unnatural ones, and men likewise abandoned natural relations with women and burned with passion for one another, receiving within themselves the due penalty for their error (1:24–27). Wrath here is not lightning bolts from the sky; it is God letting humanity walk straight down the road it insists on choosing.

Third, since they saw no value in retaining the knowledge of God, God gave them over to a depraved mind to do what ought never to be done. Paul lists a sweeping catalog: unrighteousness, evil, greed, envy, murder, strife, deceit, slander, arrogance, boasting, inventing evil, disobeying parents, breaking faith, lacking natural affection, showing no mercy. Though fully aware of God's righteous decree that those practicing such things deserve death, they not only keep doing them, they enthusiastically applaud others who do them too (1:28–32). Inside that catalog, sexual sin is just one entry among many—gossip and disrespecting parents stand right beside murder.

## Key Takeaways

**The gospel is not self-help moral advice, but God's active power to save.** Paul feels zero embarrassment carrying the message of a crucified Savior straight into the empire's mightiest capital. The gospel does more than offer inspiring stories; it unleashes God's real power to rescue everyone who believes, Jew and Gentile alike (1:16–17).

**Human sin begins with ingratitude and replacing the Creator.** For Paul, moral breakdown stems not from a shortage of clues about God, but from willful rejection. People see God's handiwork across creation yet refuse to honor or thank him, pouring their devotion into finite substitutes instead of the One who made them (1:20–25).

**God's wrath here shows itself through letting go.** Three times Paul repeats that God "gave them over" to what their hearts demanded (1:24; 1:26; 1:28). The most sobering judgment is not fire from heaven, but God lifting his restraining hand and allowing humanity to taste the full harvest of its chosen rebellion.

**The gospel dismantles every ladder of cultural and intellectual pride.** Paul calls himself a debtor to Greeks and outsiders, educated thinkers and ordinary folks alike (1:14). Standing before the gospel, nobody gets to look down on anyone else as inferior, because everyone needs the exact same rescue.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus is the promised royal son of David foretold a millennium earlier.** Right in his opening greeting, Paul stresses that this gospel is no brand-new religion, but the fulfillment of God's ancient promise through the prophets concerning David's royal line (1:2–4; 2 Samuel 7:12–14). Rising from the dead establishes him unmistakably as the Son of God in power.

**"The righteous will live by faith" forms the crimson thread linking Habakkuk to all of Romans.** Paul borrows the prophet Habakkuk's declaration spoken amid rampant injustice, when the faithful could stand firm only by trusting God (1:17; Habakkuk 2:4). For Paul, that line captures the sole path to standing right with God.

**Creation itself serves as an open testimony to its Maker.** Stating that God's eternal power and divine nature shine plainly through the created world (1:19–20), Paul echoes the familiar song of the psalmist: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalms 19:1). The universe is not silent; human hearts simply clamp their ears shut.

**Trading God's glory for animal images recalls Israel's own darkest stain.** When Paul writes that humanity exchanged God's glory for images of birds, animals, and creeping things (1:23), readers steeped in Scripture instantly recall the psalm describing the golden calf at Sinai: "They exchanged their Glory for the image of an ox that eats grass" (Psalms 106:20). Subtly, Paul hints that Israel is no innocent bystander in this portrait of human rebellion.

**Bringing all Gentiles to the obedience of faith fulfills God's covenant oath to Abraham.** Paul's mission targets every nation on earth (1:5), bringing to fruition God's opening vow in Genesis that every family of the earth would be blessed through Abraham's offspring (Genesis 12:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**The phrase "the righteousness of God" in 1:17 carries two major readings.** Since the Reformation, many understand it as the righteous standing God freely credits to believers. Numerous modern scholars highlight its Old Testament covenant root: God's own faithfulness to keep his promises and deliver his people. Both senses complement rather than compete with one another throughout Romans (1:17).

**Verses 1:26–27 on same-sex relations remain among the most debated passages in the New Testament.** The traditional reading understands Paul to treat all same-sex sexual behavior as departing from God's original creation design of male and female. Some contemporary scholars argue Paul specifically targeted exploitative practices, unrestrained excess, and idol-linked abuses widespread in Greco-Roman society rather than committed modern relationships. Whatever view one examines, Paul's overarching rhetorical trajectory is crucial: this paragraph is never meant to perch readers on a high pedestal condemning one specific group; at the very opening of chapter 2, Paul spins straight around and points straight at anyone passing judgment (2:1).

**God's wrath is not a volatile temper tantrum.** In 1:18, divine wrath denotes the steady, settled opposition of a holy God against evil and the willful suppression of truth. That explains why a letter overflowing with grace and love starts with wrath: without honest bad news, good news has nobody left to save.

**The closing list of sins spares nobody.** Paul places universally condemned crimes like murder side by side with everyday habits like gossip, bragging, and disrespect toward parents (1:29–31). Rather than sketching a fringe criminal profile, he draws a mirror holding all humanity accountable.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, recounted how he once dreaded and even resented the phrase "the righteousness of God" in 1:17, assuming it meant strict penal justice condemning sinners. Pondering the verse until he realized it describes the righteousness God freely gives to those who trust him, he felt as though the very gates of paradise swung wide open before him. *(Preface to the Complete Edition of Luther's Latin Writings, 1545)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's transformative breakthrough on Romans 1:17 is famously recounted in his own 1545 autobiographical Preface -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, explains why Paul stresses being "not ashamed of the gospel" when writing to Rome. **Karl Barth**, twentieth-century Swiss theologian, reads 1:18–32 not merely as an indictment of irreligious pagans, but as God's verdict against human religion itself whenever humanity attempts to reshape God in its own image. His 1922 commentary on Romans shook European theology after World War I. *(The Epistle to the Romans — Der Römerbrief, 1922)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: critiquing human religion that turns God into an idol lies at the heart of Barth's landmark 1922 Der Römerbrief -->

**Paula Fredriksen**, American scholar of ancient Judaism, points out that linking idolatry directly to moral decay in 1:18–32 followed a standard motif in Second Temple Jewish literature. **Douglas Moo**, American New Testament scholar, stresses that the triple refrain "God gave them over" does not picture God standing idly by while things drift.
