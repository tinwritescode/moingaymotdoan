# Romans 7 — The Law and the Sin Within Me

## Story

In chapter 6 Paul declared that believers are not under law. Now he explains what that means using an illustration anyone acquainted with law will recognize: law binds a person only while that person lives. A married woman is bound by law to her husband as long as he is alive. If she marries another man while her husband lives, she is called an adulteress; but if her husband dies, she is released from that law and is not an adulteress if she marries someone else (7:1–3).

So too with you, Paul writes: through the body of Christ you have died to the law, so that you might belong to another—to him who was raised from the dead—in order that we may bear fruit for God. When we lived in the realm of the flesh, sinful passions aroused by the law were at work in our bodies, bearing fruit for death. Now, however, having died to what once held us captive, we have been released from the law so that we serve in the new way of the Spirit, not in the old way of the written code (7:4–6). The analogy does not match detail for detail: in the illustration the husband dies, while in the application the wife dies. Yet the point is unmistakable: a death has closed one marriage to open another.

Does that mean the law itself is sin? "Certainly not!" Indeed, Paul would not have known what sin was except through the law. He would not have known what coveting really is if the law had not said, "You shall not covet." Yet sin seized the opportunity afforded by the commandment and stirred up every kind of covetous desire in him; apart from the law, sin lies dead. Once he lived apart from the law, but when the commandment came, sin sprang to life and he died. The very commandment meant to bring life brought him death, because sin used the commandment to deceive him and kill him (7:7–11; Exodus 20:17). The episode sounds remarkably like Adam in Eden: a command, a deceiver, and a death.

So then, the law is holy, and the commandment is holy, righteous, and good. Did something good then become death to him? No. Sin produced death in him through what was good, exposing its own true nature. Through the commandment, sin became utterly sinful (7:12–13). Paul defends the law to the very end. The fault lies not in the law, but in the human being who receives it.

Then Paul shifts into the present tense, launching one of the most fiercely debated passages in the letter. We know the law is spiritual, he writes, but I am unspiritual, sold as a slave to sin. "I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do, I do not do. But what I hate, I do." If I do what I do not want to do, I agree that the law is good. As it is, it is no longer I who do it, but sin living in me (7:14–17).

He knows that nothing good dwells in him—that is, in his flesh. The desire to do good is right there, but the power to carry it out is missing. The good he wants to do he does not do; the evil he does not want to do he keeps doing. He discovers a law at work: whenever he wants to do good, evil sits right beside him. In his inner being he delights in God's law, yet he sees another law at work in his body's members, waging war against the law of his mind and taking him prisoner to the law of sin (7:18–23). Readers still debate who this "I" is—Paul before conversion, Paul as a Christian, or humanity under the law in general—yet almost everyone who reads it recognizes that voice.

The inner tug-of-war culminates in a cry: "What a wretched man I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?" The answer arrives immediately: "Thanks be to God, through Jesus Christ our Lord!" Paul then sums up the divided state: with his mind he serves the law of God, while with his flesh he serves the law of sin (7:24–25). The question asks *who*, not *what*. The full answer waits right across the threshold in chapter 8.

## Key Takeaways

**Release from the law is not a license for lawlessness, but freedom to belong to Christ and bear fruit for God.** Through the illustration of a marriage ended by death, Paul explains that believers have died to the law through Christ's body so they can serve God "in the new way of the Spirit, and not in the old way of the written code" (7:4–6).

**The law is not the culprit behind sin; sin hijacked a good gift to deceive humanity.** Paul states plainly that "the law is holy, and the commandment is holy, righteous, and good" (7:12). The flaw sits not in the commandment, but in the power of sin already lurking inside people, using the command as a springboard and revealing how truly deadly it is (7:7–13).

**Knowing what is right and wanting to do good cannot overcome the inner pull of sin.** Verses 14–23 sketch an agonizing struggle: the mind affirms the law's goodness and desires what is right, yet conduct slides toward the very evil it hates (7:15; 7:18–19). Unaided human willpower cannot defeat the "law of sin" holding it captive (7:23).

**The way out of inner deadlock is not more rules, but a Rescuer.** The cry "What a wretched man I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?" asks not for a method or a program, but for a person—and finds its answer in thanksgiving to God through Jesus Christ (7:24–25).

## Deeper Meaning

**The contrast between "the new way of the Spirit" and "the old way of the written code" marks the arrival of the new covenant.** Paul is describing more than two personal habits; he is contrasting two eras of salvation history (7:6). He made the same distinction to the Corinthians: the new covenant belongs to the Spirit, "for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (2 Corinthians 3:6).

**The story of the commandment arriving and sin "deceiving" echoes the fall in Eden.** The verb "deceived" in 7:11 matches Eve's confession to God in Genesis: "The serpent deceived me, and I ate" (Genesis 3:13). Human experience under the law reenacts Adam and Eve's tragedy beneath the forbidden tree.

**The tenth commandment—"You shall not covet"—reaches straight into the hidden recesses of the heart.** Of the Ten Commandments, the first nine largely involve visible outward behavior, whereas the tenth targets inner craving (7:7; Exodus 20:17). Paul selects this exact command because it proves nobody can satisfy the law by external compliance alone.

**The conflict at the end of chapter 7 resembles, yet differs sharply from, the battle described in Galatians.** In Galatians 5:17, flesh and Spirit oppose each other so that believers do not do whatever they want, yet the Spirit is actively present to lead them into victory (Galatians 5:17). In Romans 7:14–25, by contrast, the Holy Spirit is not mentioned once, underscoring human helplessness under the law alone—just before the Spirit bursts onto the scene at the start of chapter 8.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who the "I" is in 7:14–25 remains one of the most contested questions in all of Romans.** Two main readings stand out. The first (held by the later Augustine, Luther, and Calvin) sees this as the real experience of a genuine Christian still wrestling with remaining sin throughout life, pointing to the present tense and the "inner being" that delights in God's law (7:22; 7:25). The second (held by Chrysostom, the younger Augustine, and many modern scholars) views it as a portrait of life under the law without the Spirit—whether Paul prior to Christ, Israel under Torah, or humanity in Adam seen in hindsight through the gospel—because phrases like "sold as a slave to sin" (7:14) and "prisoner of the law of sin" (7:23) contrast starkly with the liberated believer portrayed in chapters 6 and 8.

**Paul never belittles or rejects the goodness of the Mosaic law.** Facing any accusation that he slanders the law, Paul affirms without hesitation that the law is holy, righteous, good, and spiritual (7:12; 7:14). The defect belongs not to the law, but to weak human flesh gripped by sin.

**The marriage analogy in 7:1–3 is not intended as detailed legislation on marriage and divorce.** Paul borrows marital law solely to illustrate one straightforward principle: death dissolves an old bond and makes room for a new union (7:1–4). Pressing every detail of the illustration into a one-to-one correspondence stretches it past Paul's intent.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, famously reversed his own interpretation of 7:14–25. As a younger thinker he read the "I" as someone under the law prior to receiving grace; later, during his controversy with Pelagians over whether sinless perfection is attainable in this life, he concluded that Paul was describing the lifelong struggle of a true believer. *(Retractations, Books 1 and 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's shift in interpreting Romans 7:14–25 is recorded by himself in Retractations and is famous in the history of exegesis -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, treated 7:14–25 as the clearest illustration of his well-known insight that the Christian on earth is always "simultaneously righteous and a sinner." By grace the believer's heart delights in God's law, yet the flesh remains, keeping the believer dependent on Jesus every single day. *(Lectures on Romans, 1515–1516, on chapter 7 verses 14–25)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther leaned on Romans 7:14–25 as a primary foundation for his "simul iustus et peccator" formula in his 1515–1516 lectures -->

**Krister Stendahl**, twentieth-century Swedish New Testament scholar, wrote a landmark 1963 essay challenging the Western habit of reading Romans 7 as Paul's diary of a tormented conscience. He argued that Paul actually possessed a robust conscience before his conversion and that in Romans 7 Paul is not lamenting private guilt feelings, but defending the goodness of the law. *(The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West, 1963)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the critique of the "introspective conscience of the West" and rereading of Romans 7 form the core thesis of this classic 1963 essay -->

**N. T. Wright**, New Testament scholar and former Anglican bishop, reads the "I" of chapter 7 primarily as the story of Israel under Torah, replaying Adam's fall at Mount Sinai. The holy law arrived, yet because sin dwelt within humanity, the law could not rescue them—clearing the way for the Spirit's deliverance in chapter 8. *(The Letter to the Romans, New Interpreter's Bible, 2002, on chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading the "I" of Romans 7 as Israel's story reenacting Adam's fall is Wright's distinctive interpretive approach -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, understood 7:14–25 as the condition of humanity before coming to Christ—aware of what is good through the law yet pinned down by sin.
