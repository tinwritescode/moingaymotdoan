# Romans 3 — All Have Sinned

## Story

If a true Jew is one inwardly, what advantage is there in being Jewish, or what benefit is there in circumcision? Paul answers, "Much in every way!" First and foremost, the very words of God were entrusted to the Jewish people. What if some of them proved unfaithful—will their unfaithfulness nullify God's faithfulness? "By no means!" Let God be found true though every human being prove a liar, just as it stands written: that you may be proved right in your words and triumph when judged (3:1–4; Psalms 51:4).

Paul anticipates the next objection. If our unrighteousness highlights God's righteousness all the more clearly, isn't God unfair to bring wrath upon us? Adding that he speaks in human terms, he replies: by no means! Otherwise, how could God ever judge the world? Someone pushes even further: if my falsehood magnifies God's truthfulness to his glory, why am I still condemned as a sinner? Why not do evil so good may come? Paul notes that slanderers actually accuse him of teaching just that. He wastes zero breath debating them: their condemnation is thoroughly deserved (3:5–8).

Now he closes the case opened back in chapter 1. Are we any better off? Not at all! We have already charged that Jews and Greeks alike stand completely under sin's power. To clinch the point, Paul weaves together a chain of Old Testament verses, mostly from the Psalms: "There is no one righteous, not even one." Nobody understands, nobody seeks God; all have turned away and together become worthless. Their throats are open graves, their tongues practice deceit, snake venom sits beneath their lips, their mouths overflow with curses and bitterness, their feet race to shed blood, ruin and misery mark their tracks, they have never known the path of peace, and no fear of God stands before their eyes (3:9–18; Psalms 14:1–3; Psalms 5:9; Psalms 140:3; Psalms 10:7; Isaiah 59:7–8; Psalms 36:1). The indictment sweeps from throat, tongue, lips, and mouth down to feet and eyes: sin touches every part of the human person.

Everything the law says, it speaks to those under the law, so that every mouth may be silenced and the entire world held accountable before God. Nobody will be declared righteous in his sight by works of the law, because through the law comes simply the awareness of sin (3:19–20). At last the courtroom indictment wraps up: Gentiles have no excuse, and Jews have no excuse either.

"But now," Paul writes—and the entire letter turns on a hinge. Apart from the law, a righteousness from God has been revealed, though the Law and the Prophets both bear witness to it. This righteousness of God comes through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe, with no distinction, "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." They are justified freely by his grace through the redemption that came by Christ Jesus (3:21–24). No distinction in sinning; therefore no distinction in being saved.

God presented Jesus as an atoning sacrifice through his blood, received through faith. Paul explains why this was necessary. In his divine forbearance, God had passed over sins committed in earlier generations, raising a hard question: can a judge who leaves sins unpunished still be just? The cross supplies the answer: right there God demonstrates that he is both completely righteous himself and the One who declares righteous anyone who has faith in Jesus (3:25–26). God never sweeps sin under the rug; he steps forward and absorbs its cost himself.

Where, then, does boasting go? It is shut out completely—not by a principle of works, but by the principle of faith, since we conclude that a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law. Or is God the God of Jews alone? Is he not the God of Gentiles too? Yes, of Gentiles as well, because there is only one God, who will justify the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through that exact same faith. Do we then nullify the law through this faith? "Certainly not! On the contrary, we uphold the law" (3:27–31). That final claim is a promise chapter 4 immediately keeps, using the book of the law itself as proof.

## Key Takeaways

**All humanity—Jew and Gentile alike—stands on level ground beneath sin's sway.** Paul wraps up the sweeping indictment started in chapter 1 with an exceptionless verdict: "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (3:23). Nobody standing before God's bench can boast of moral superiority over anyone else (3:9–10).

**The law works like a diagnostic mirror exposing disease, never the cure that heals it.** Paul states plainly that nobody is justified by works of the law, "for through the law comes knowledge of sin" (3:20). The law's role is diagnosing the illness deep in the human heart and quieting every self-justifying excuse (3:19).

**God's righteousness comes completely free through Christ's atoning cross.** The twin words "But now" in 3:21 open the grand turning point of the whole epistle. Far from lowering his standard of justice to wink at evil, God sets forth Jesus as an atoning sacrifice so he remains totally just while freely justifying everyone who trusts him (3:24–26).

**Faith banishes all boasting and welcomes every nation on equal footing.** If salvation came by achievement, people could brag; because salvation arrives as a gift received through faith, all boasting vanishes (3:27–28). Since only one true God exists, he rescues Jews and Gentiles by one single path (3:29–30).

## Deeper Meaning

**Declaring that nobody is justified by works echoes King David's own prayer.** When Paul insists no living person is declared righteous before God by works of the law (3:20), he replays the psalmist's plea: "For in your sight no one living is righteous" (Psalms 143:2). Paul's gospel moves in total harmony with the heart of the Old Testament.

**The term "atoning sacrifice" links the cross directly to the mercy seat on the Day of Atonement.** The Greek word Paul selects in 3:25 is the exact term used in the Greek Old Testament for the "mercy seat" atop the ark of the covenant, where the high priest sprinkled sacrificial blood once a year for Israel's sins (Leviticus 16:15–16). Jesus himself has now become the true meeting place of God's mercy and forgiveness.

**Confessing "there is one God" grounds the full welcome of Gentiles.** Echoing Judaism's foundational confession that God is one (3:30; Deuteronomy 6:4), Paul draws an inescapable conclusion: because God is no tribal deity belonging to one nation alone, he is equally God of the Gentiles and saves them on the exact same basis of faith (3:29–30).

**Christ's atonement fulfills Isaiah's vision of the sin-bearing Servant.** God never shrugs off guilt cheaply; he confronts sin squarely by placing it upon his Son (3:25–26). Centuries earlier, Isaiah foresaw this exact Servant pierced for our transgressions and carrying all our iniquity to bring us peace (Isaiah 53:5–6).

## Notes & Nuances

**The phrase "atoning sacrifice" (propitiation / expiation) in 3:25 carries two rich nuances.** One emphasis highlights satisfying God's righteous wrath against sin (propitiation); the other stresses cleansing and wiping away sin's stain (expiation). Many commentators note that both realities belong together—and the crucial point is that God himself put Christ forward: the cross is never a gentle Son talking down an angry Father, but God himself stepping in out of love to rescue sinners (3:25).

**Scholars debate two ways to translate "faith in Jesus Christ" in 3:22.** The traditional translation takes it as the believer's faith placed in Jesus Christ. Several contemporary scholars translate it "the faithfulness of Jesus Christ," underscoring Christ's own loyal obedience all the way to the cross. Either way, the second half of the very same verse explicitly confirms this righteousness is "for all who believe" (3:22).

**Justification by faith never means despising or discarding God's law.** Paul raises that exact objection right at the chapter's close and rejects it outright: "Certainly not! On the contrary, we uphold the law" (3:31). The law already bore witness to the gospel (3:21) and aimed at genuine righteousness; faith never cancels that aim—it brings it to fulfillment.

**Origen**, third-century Alexandrian biblical scholar, recognized that the Greek word for "atoning sacrifice" in 3:25 directly matches the mercy seat resting on the ark of the covenant in Israel's sanctuary. **John Stott**, twentieth-century British Anglican pastor, emphasizes on 3:25–26 that God himself is the active subject behind atonement. Far from a compassionate Jesus shielding humanity from an unforgiving Father, the cross shows God himself in self-giving love personally bearing the cost of his own justice. *(The Cross of Christ, 1986; The Message of Romans, 1994)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: emphasizing God's self-substitution in Romans 3:25–26 lies at the core of Stott's The Cross of Christ -->
