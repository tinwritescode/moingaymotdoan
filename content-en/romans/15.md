# Romans 15 — Accept One Another

## Story

Paul continues his discussion of the strong and the weak, and now explicitly places himself among the strong. We who are strong ought to bear with the failings of the weak and not to please ourselves. Each of us should please his neighbor for his good, to build him up. For even Christ did not please Himself, as it is written: "The insults of those who insult You have fallen on me." For everything that was written in the past was written for our instruction, so that through endurance and the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope (15:1–4; Psalms 69:9). Paul does not resolve the dispute over food with a rulebook; he resolves it with the cross.

He turns exhortation into prayer: may the God who gives endurance and encouragement grant them the same attitude of mind toward each other that Christ Jesus had, so that with one mind and one voice they may glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. "Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring glory to God" (15:5–7).

Paul then explains why Jews and Gentiles must welcome one another. Christ became a servant of the circumcised on behalf of God's truth, to confirm the promises made to the patriarchs, and so that the Gentiles might glorify God for His mercy. He weaves together four Scripture passages drawn from the Psalms, the Law, and the Prophets: I will praise You among the Gentiles; rejoice, O Gentiles, with His people; praise the Lord, all you Gentiles; and Isaiah declares that the Root of Jesse will spring up, one who will arise to rule over the nations, and in Him the Gentiles will hope (15:8–12; Psalms 18:49; Deuteronomy 32:43; Psalms 117:1; Isaiah 11:10). From the very beginning, God's plan had a shared table in view.

Paul closes the teaching section of the letter with a benediction: "Now may the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace as you believe in Him, so that you may overflow with hope by the power of the Holy Spirit" (15:13).

He then speaks personally about himself. He is convinced that the Roman believers are full of goodness, filled with all knowledge, and competent to instruct one another. He has written boldly on some points to remind them, because of the grace God gave him to be a minister of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles. He describes his ministry in temple language: he serves as a priest of the gospel of God, so that the Gentiles might become an offering acceptable to God, sanctified by the Holy Spirit. Therefore he has reason to glory in Christ Jesus in his service to God. He will not venture to speak of anything except what Christ has accomplished through him in leading the Gentiles to obey God—by word and deed, by the power of signs and wonders, and by the power of the Spirit. From Jerusalem all the way around to Illyricum, he has fully proclaimed the gospel of Christ (15:14–19). Illyricum lay along the Adriatic coast, west of Macedonia. Half an empire had heard the gospel through him.

Paul lived by a clear ambition: to preach where Christ was not yet known, so he would not build on someone else's foundation, as it is written: those who were not told about Him will see, and those who have not heard will understand. That is why he has so often been hindered from coming to them. But now that there is no more place for him to work in these regions, and since he has longed for many years to see them, he plans to travel to Spain and hopes to visit Rome on the way, enjoy their company for a while, and be assisted by them for his journey (15:20–24; Isaiah 52:15). Rome is not his final destination; it is his launchpad for the mission further west.

First, however, he must go to Jerusalem to serve the saints. The churches in Macedonia and Achaia were pleased to make a contribution for the poor among the saints in Jerusalem. They were pleased to do it—and indeed they owe it to them, for if the Gentiles have shared in the Jews' spiritual blessings, they owe it to the Jews to share with them their material blessings. Once he has completed this task and safely delivered this fruit to them, he will pass through Rome on his way to Spain, knowing that he will come to them in the full measure of the blessing of Christ (15:25–29). That gift embodies the very truth the letter teaches: Gentiles and Jews belong to each other.

Finally, he urges them to join in his struggle by praying to God for him: that he may be rescued from the unbelievers in Judea, that his service in Jerusalem may be acceptable to the saints there, and that by God's will he may come to them with joy and be refreshed in their company. He prays that the God of peace will be with them all (15:30–33). The Book of Acts reveals that this prayer was answered in a way no one anticipated: Paul was arrested in Jerusalem and eventually arrived in Rome in chains (Acts 21:30–33; Acts 28:16).

## Key Takeaways

**Christ's self-sacrificing example is the final standard for unity in the church.** Paul concludes the discussion of the strong and the weak with a clear rule: the strong do not live to please themselves, but must "bear with the failings of the weak," following Christ Himself, who did not please Himself and bore insults for others (15:1–3). From that foundation comes his appeal: "Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you" (15:7).

**Christ came both to keep God's promises to Israel and to open the door of mercy so that all nations might praise God together.** Paul shows the two groups in Rome that they belong to a single divine plan: Jesus confirms God's faithfulness to Israel's patriarchs while bringing the Gentiles to join in praising God (15:8–13).

**Paul's missionary vision presses toward lands that have never heard the name of Christ.** Having preached across the eastern half of the empire from Jerusalem to Illyricum, he longs to carry the gospel all the way to Spain and hopes the Roman church will become a stopover and partner for this new frontier (15:19–24).

**The Gentile churches' collection for the poor in Jerusalem is tangible proof of unity.** Before heading to Rome, Paul must personally deliver the offering raised by believers in Macedonia and Achaia to Jerusalem. In his eyes, this is not top-down charity, but a fitting response from those who have received "spiritual blessings" from the Jewish people (15:25–28).

## Deeper Meaning

**The quotation from Psalm 69 makes the cross the pattern for life together in the church.** When Paul writes, "The insults of those who insult You have fallen on me" (15:3; Psalms 69:9), he applies the words of the righteous sufferer in the Psalms to Jesus. If Christ willingly shouldered reproach in our place, then yielding to one another over a meal or a holiday becomes a very small thing.

**Four quotations from the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms form a chorus celebrating the day all nations worship together.** In 15:9–12, Paul deliberately cites all three divisions of the Hebrew Scriptures—the Law of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:43), the Psalms (Psalms 18:49; Psalms 117:1), and the prophet Isaiah (Isaiah 11:10)—to show that Gentile rejoicing alongside Israel was the intended destination of the entire Old Testament.

**The Jerusalem collection connects Romans 15 with the remarkable generosity described in 2 Corinthians.** The contribution from Macedonia and Achaia for the poor saints in Jerusalem (15:26) is the very offering Paul praises so warmly elsewhere, noting that those believers gave voluntarily according to their means and "even beyond their ability" despite their own severe trial and poverty (2 Corinthians 8:1–4).

**Paul's prayer to reach Rome was ultimately answered along an unexpected road.** Paul asked the church to pray that he would be rescued from opponents in Judea and arrive in Rome with joy (15:30–32). He did indeed reach Rome, though as a prisoner in chains sent to stand trial before Caesar (Acts 28:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 15:4 affirms the lasting value of the Old Testament Scriptures for believers.** Although Paul spends much of the letter distinguishing law from grace, he never discards the Old Testament. On the contrary, he affirms that "everything that was written in the past was written for our instruction," nurturing endurance, encouragement, and hope in believers (15:4).

**Did Paul ever actually make it to Spain as he planned?** The New Testament does not tell us what happened after the two years of house arrest in Rome at the close of Acts (Acts 28:30). A very early Christian letter from the late first century notes that Paul preached all the way to "the farthest limits of the west," leading many historians to think he may have been released and traveled to Spain before a later arrest and martyrdom (15:24; 15:28).

**The phrase "the priestly duty of proclaiming the gospel" in 15:16 is metaphorical and does not reinstate the old priesthood.** Paul borrows temple imagery to describe his preaching ministry: the sacrifice he offers to God is not an animal on an altar, but the Gentile believers themselves, made holy by the Holy Spirit—continuing the "living sacrifice" imagery from the opening of chapter 12 (15:16; 12:1).

## What Commentators Say

**Clement of Rome**, a leader of the Roman church near the end of the first century, writes in his letter to the Corinthians that the apostle Paul taught righteousness to the whole world and reached "the farthest limits of the west" before his martyrdom. **John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, notices the delicacy with which Paul frames the collection for Jerusalem in 15:27. By calling it the repayment of a debt owed for spiritual blessings received from the Jews, Paul keeps the givers from pride while preserving the dignity of the recipients. *(Homilies on Romans)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Chrysostom praising Paul's tactful use of "debt" in 15:27; uncertain of the exact homily number -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, regards 15:4 as a decisive answer to every attempt to set aside the Old Testament. He insists that all the ancient Scriptures retain their full power to instruct the church today, sustaining patience and hope under trial. *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 15 verse 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin emphasizes the enduring usefulness of the entire Old Testament in his commentary on Romans 15:4 -->

**Richard Hays**, American New Testament scholar, points out Paul's scriptural artistry in 15:9–12: the four quotations come from all three sections of the Jewish Scriptures—the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings. In doing so, Paul demonstrates that Gentiles worshiping alongside Israel was never a backup plan, but the grand chorus toward which the entire Old Testament was moving. *(Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul, 1989)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the analysis of the four Old Testament citations in Romans 15:9–12 is one of Hays's signature examples in this book -->
