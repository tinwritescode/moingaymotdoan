# Romans 8 — Life in the Spirit

## Story

Chapter 7 ends with a cry of distress; chapter 8 opens with a ringing verdict: "Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." The law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set the believer free from the law of sin and death. What the law was powerless to do, weakened by the flesh, God did. He sent his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh to be a sin offering, and condemned sin right in the flesh. As a result, the righteous requirement of the law is fulfilled in us, who do not walk according to the flesh but according to the Spirit (8:1–4). The Spirit appeared only once in chapter 7; in chapter 8 the Spirit dominates the whole passage, appearing nearly twenty times.

Paul sets two ways of living side by side. Those who live according to the flesh set their minds on what the flesh desires; those who live according to the Spirit set their minds on what the Spirit desires. The mind of the flesh is death, whereas the mind of the Spirit is life and peace. The mindset of the flesh is hostile to God: it does not submit to God's law, nor can it do so. Those controlled by the flesh cannot please God (8:5–8).

Yet you are not in the flesh, Paul writes, if God's Spirit truly dwells in you; anyone who does not have the Spirit of Christ does not belong to him. If Christ is in you, even though the body is subject to death because of sin, the Spirit gives life because of righteousness. And the One who raised Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who lives in you. So we have an obligation—not to the flesh, to live according to it. If you live according to the flesh, you will die; but if by the Spirit you put to death the misdeeds of the body, you will live (8:9–13).

Paul then turns to a family portrait. All who are led by the Spirit of God are sons of God. You did not receive a spirit of slavery that returns you to fear; you received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, "Abba, Father!" The Spirit himself testifies with our spirit that we are God's children. And if we are children, then we are heirs—heirs of God and co-heirs with Christ—provided we share in his sufferings so that we may also share in his glory (8:14–17). "Abba" is the Aramaic word Jesus himself used when praying in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36).

Suffering is real, yet Paul places it on the scales: our present sufferings are not worth comparing with the glory that will be revealed in us. The whole creation waits with eager longing for the children of God to be revealed. Creation was subjected to futility—not by its own choice, but by the will of the one who subjected it—in hope that creation itself will be liberated from its bondage to decay and brought into the glorious freedom of God's children. The entire creation has been groaning in the pains of childbirth right up to the present moment. Not only creation, but we ourselves, who hold the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for our full adoption, the redemption of our bodies (8:18–23; Genesis 3:17–19). The curse on the ground in Eden is not the final word; the earth itself is waiting to be rescued.

In this hope we were saved. Hope that is already seen is no longer hope; we wait for what we do not yet see, so we wait with patience. In the same way, the Spirit helps us in our weakness. We do not know what we ought to pray for, yet the Spirit himself intercedes for us through groans too deep for words. And the One who searches hearts knows the mind of the Spirit, because the Spirit intercedes for the saints according to God's will (8:24–27). Creation groans, we groan, and the Spirit groans right alongside us.

"And we know that God works all things together for the good of those who love Him, who are called according to His purpose." Paul traces a five-link chain: those God foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, so that he might be the firstborn among many brothers; those he predestined he also called; those he called he also justified; and those he justified he also glorified (8:28–30). Glorification still lies ahead, yet Paul writes it in the past tense, as something already settled.

He then fires off a volley of unanswerable questions. "If God is for us, who can be against us?" He who did not spare his own Son, but gave him up for us all—how will he not also, along with him, graciously give us all things? Who will bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies. Who is there to condemn? Christ Jesus died, was raised to life, sits at God's right hand, and intercedes for us. Who can separate us from Christ's love? Trouble, hardship, persecution, famine, nakedness, danger, or sword? As it is written, for your sake we face death all day long; we are considered as sheep to be slaughtered (8:31–36; Psalms 44:22). Paul never promises these afflictions will not strike.

"No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through Him who loved us." Paul declares he is convinced that neither death nor life, neither angels nor rulers, neither the present nor the future, nor any powers, neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord (8:37–39). The chapter opens with no condemnation, and closes with no separation.

## Key Takeaways

**There is no longer any sentence of condemnation for those in Christ, because the Holy Spirit has opened a new life.** The opening line of chapter 8 lifts the suffocating tension of chapter 7: "Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (8:1). What the law could not achieve because of human weakness, God accomplished through his Son, enabling believers to walk under the Spirit's guidance (8:2–4).

**The Spirit does not turn believers into trembling servants, but grants them the intimate standing of God's children.** Believers do not live in dread of dismissal or punishment like slaves; they are adopted into God's family, free to cry "Abba, Father!" and made co-heirs with Christ (8:14–17).

**God's redemption does not abandon the physical world; it embraces both our bodies and the entire creation.** Present suffering is real, and both creation and believers groan while awaiting rescue. Yet the destination is not disembodied souls floating away, but "the redemption of our bodies" and the liberation of all creation from decay (8:18–23).

**No hardship or cosmic force can sever believers from God's love.** In our weakness, the Spirit intercedes for us (8:26–27). From start to finish, God actively sustains his purpose, and the One who did not spare his own Son will never permit anything to tear us away from his love (8:28–39).

## Deeper Meaning

**The cry "Abba, Father!" invites believers into Jesus' own intimacy with the Father.** In Gethsemane on the eve of his death, Jesus prayed with that same tender Aramaic word: "Abba, Father" (Mark 14:36). When the Spirit moves believers to address God this way (8:15), he lets them share in Jesus' own relationship as Son.

**The phrase "did not spare His own Son" recalls Abraham offering Isaac on Mount Moriah.** In Genesis, God commended Abraham because he had not withheld his only son, yet God intervened to stay Abraham's hand (Genesis 22:16). In 8:32, Paul uses that same phrasing to show that God the Father truly did not spare his own Son, handing him over for us.

**Creation's groaning bridges the curse of Eden and the promise of a new heaven and new earth.** The ground was cursed when humanity fell (Genesis 3:17–19), subjecting all creation to futility and decay (8:20–22). Creation's longing here looks straight toward the day God makes everything new in the new heaven and new earth (Revelation 21:1).

**Quoting Psalm 44 shows that suffering for God is no mark of his abandonment.** When Paul cites "For Your sake we face death all day long" (8:36; Psalms 44:22), he reminds readers that God's people have always walked through severe trials. Precisely inside circumstances that look like defeat, believers emerge "more than conquerors" through the One who loves them (8:37).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 28 does not promise that every event in life is pleasant or intrinsically good.** The assurance that God works all things together for good for those who love him never minimizes the pain of illness, bereavement, or injustice. Verse 29 immediately clarifies the supreme "good" God is shaping: conforming believers "to the image of His Son" (8:28–29).

**The words "foreknew" and "predestined" in 8:29–30 mark a long-standing difference among Christian traditions.** The Reformed tradition (following Augustine and Calvin) understands "foreknew" in the Old Testament covenantal sense of choosing and loving beforehand, underscoring God's sovereign initiative in salvation. The Arminian tradition (such as John Wesley) understands "foreknew" as God seeing ahead of time who will respond in faith and predestining them to grow into Christ's likeness. Whichever view one holds, both agree Paul's aim here is not cold speculation, but comforting suffering believers that God will surely finish what he started (8:29–30).

**Reading chapter 8 calls for distinguishing "flesh" from "body."** Paul opposes living "according to the flesh"—the old self oriented away from God (8:5–8)—without ever despising the physical body. On the contrary, he affirms that God will raise believers' mortal bodies to life and redeem them fully (8:11; 8:23).

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, noted that in the five-link chain of 8:29–30—foreknew, predestined, called, justified, glorified—Paul puts even "glorified" in the past tense, though it still awaits the future. In Calvin's reading, Paul writes this way to assure believers that their final salvation is as certain as if it were already accomplished. *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 8 verses 29–30)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: observing the past tense of "glorified" as a pledge of absolute certainty is a classic point in Calvin's commentary on Romans 8:30 -->

**John Wesley**, eighteenth-century English evangelist and founder of Methodism, treated 8:16—the Spirit testifying with our spirit—as the bedrock of inward assurance of God's love. On 8:29, differing from Calvin, he interpreted "foreknew" as God knowing beforehand those who would respond to the gospel in faith. *(Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament, on chapter 8 verses 16 and 29)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the direct witness of the Spirit in 8:16 is a core Wesleyan doctrine, and reading "foreknew" as foreseen faith is explicit in Wesley's Notes -->

**N. T. Wright**, New Testament scholar and former Anglican bishop, highlights how all of Romans 8 echoes the Exodus story: set free from slavery (8:15), led by the Spirit like the ancient pillar of cloud and fire (8:14), and journeying toward a promised inheritance (8:17)—except this time the inheritance is not one narrow strip of land, but the whole renewed creation (8:19–21). *(The Letter to the Romans, New Interpreter's Bible, 2002, on chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Romans 8 through the framework of a new Exodus is one of the defining features of Wright's commentary -->

**Douglas Moo**, American New Testament scholar, remarks that the opening verse (8:1) and the closing verse (8:39) stand like two pillars framing the entire chapter: it begins with "no condemnation" and ends with "no separation" from God's love.
