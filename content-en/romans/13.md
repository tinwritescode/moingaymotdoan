# Romans 13 — Governing Authorities and the Debt of Love

## Story

Paul has just urged believers not to avenge themselves, leaving room for God's wrath. Now he turns to one way God punishes wrongdoing even in this present life: governing authorities. Everyone must submit to the governing authorities, for there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by Him. Consequently, whoever resists authority resists what God has appointed, and those who resist will bring judgment on themselves (13:1–2).

Rulers are not a terror to good conduct, but to bad. If you want to live free of fear, do what is right, and you will be commended. For the governing authority is God's servant for your good. But if you do wrong, be afraid, for he does not carry the sword in vain; he is God's servant, an avenger who carries out wrath on the wrongdoer. Therefore one must submit, not only to avoid punishment, but also for the sake of conscience (13:3–5). Paul wrote these words during the reign of Nero, before the Roman state had begun systematic persecution of the church. He speaks of government as it is meant to function, and of a God who remains sovereign even over rulers who do not know Him.

For the same reason you also pay taxes, because the authorities are God's servants who devote themselves to this very task. Pay to all what is owed to them: taxes to whom taxes are owed, revenue to whom revenue is owed, respect to whom respect is owed, honor to whom honor is owed (13:6–7). These words echo Jesus' own teaching: give to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's (Matthew 22:21).

Paul then pivots from debts that can be paid off to a debt that is never finished. "Owe no one anything, except to love one another," for whoever loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law. The commandments against adultery, murder, stealing, and coveting, along with every other commandment, are summed up in a single saying: "Love your neighbor as yourself." Love does no wrong to a neighbor, so love is the fulfillment of the law (13:8–10; Leviticus 19:18). Back in chapter 3, Paul promised that faith upholds the law; here we see how it does so.

At the close of the chapter, Paul reminds them what hour it is. The time has come to wake up from sleep, because our salvation is nearer now than when we first believed. "The night is nearly over; the day has drawn near." So let us cast off the deeds of darkness and put on the armor of light. Let us behave decently, as in the daytime—not in carousing and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and debauchery, not in dissension and jealousy—"instead, clothe yourselves with the Lord Jesus Christ," and make no provision for the desires of the flesh (13:11–14). More than three centuries later, these very verses moved a young man named Augustine to leave his old life behind when he opened a copy of Paul's letters in a garden in Milan.

## Key Takeaways

**Social order stands under God's sovereignty, so believers live as exemplary, law-abiding citizens.** Immediately after forbidding personal vengeance at the end of chapter 12, Paul explains that governing authorities are appointed to preserve order and restrain evil (13:1–4). Believers therefore obey the law and pay their taxes in full, not merely out of fear of punishment, but for the sake of conscience before God (13:5–7).

**Every financial debt can be paid in full, but the debt of love is owed for life.** After instructing readers to pay all taxes and honor to whom they are due, Paul turns to a different kind of obligation: "Owe no one anything, except to love one another" (13:8). No day ever arrives when a person can say they have loved their neighbor enough.

**Love does not abolish the law; it fulfills the purpose of every commandment.** The commands against adultery, murder, theft, and coveting all converge on a single point: doing no harm to one's neighbor and loving them as oneself (13:9–10).

**Knowing that the Lord's day is drawing near motivates believers to live awake and "clothe yourselves with the Lord Jesus Christ."** Paul rouses his readers with the image of a late night giving way to dawn: it is time to shed dark, indulgent, quarrelsome habits and walk honorably as in broad daylight (13:11–14).

## Deeper Meaning

**The call to submit and pay taxes continues both Jesus' words about Caesar and the Old Testament conviction that God installs kings.** Paul draws on the faith of the Book of Daniel that God Himself removes kings and establishes them (13:1; Daniel 2:21), while directly echoing Jesus' command: "Give to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's" (13:6–7; Matthew 22:21).

**Biblical submission to government always finds its ultimate boundary in obedience to God.** Reading Romans 13:1–7 alongside the rest of the New Testament reveals the clear line the apostles drew when authorities forbade them to preach the gospel: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). The state is God's servant, not God Himself.

**Summing up the law in love of neighbor links Leviticus with the core teaching of Jesus.** The command Paul quotes in 13:9—"Love your neighbor as yourself"—comes from Leviticus (Leviticus 19:18) and is the very second greatest commandment by which Jesus summarized all the Law and the Prophets (Matthew 22:39–40).

**The imagery of waking from sleep and putting on the "armor of light" echoes Paul's earliest letter.** Just as in 1 Thessalonians, Paul reminds believers that they belong to the day rather than the night, and must therefore stay alert and put on the armor of faith and love (13:11–12; 1 Thessalonians 5:5–8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Romans 13:1–7 has repeatedly been misused in history to demand blind obedience to unjust regimes.** People have cited these verses to defend slavery, racial segregation, and unquestioning submission to dictators. A close reading, however, shows that Paul is stating the general principle of a government performing its proper role—commending good and punishing evil (13:3–4)—rather than declaring every state decree blameless. In fact, calling the Roman emperor "God's servant" (13:4; 13:6) quietly strips away imperial pretensions to divinity: every government is merely a subordinate servant answerable to God's judgment, and whenever rulers demand what violates conscience and God's law, believers must obey God first (Acts 5:29).

**The instruction "Owe no one anything" in 13:8 is not an absolute ban on all legitimate borrowing.** Placed immediately after 13:7 on paying taxes and civic obligations, Paul's point is not to delay or default on any obligation owed to others, so that the only standing debt left unpaid is the lifelong debt to love one another (13:7–8).

**Why does Paul say our salvation is nearer now than when we first believed (13:11)?** "Salvation" here does not refer to the past moment of initial conversion, but to the complete redemption awaiting believers when Jesus returns. Though no one knows the exact hour, each passing day brings that dawn closer, leaving no excuse for spiritual drowsiness or careless living (13:11–12).

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, recounts the most famous turning point of his life in connection with 13:13–14. In a Milan garden in AD 386, torn apart by his inability to break free from his old dissolute habits, he heard a child's voice chanting "take up and read," opened a codex of Paul's letters, and read straight through 13:13–14—at that instant all the shadows of doubt dissolved, and he surrendered his life to Christ. *(Confessions, Book 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the "tolle, lege" scene in the Milan garden reading Romans 13:13–14 in Book 8 of Confessions is one of the most famous passages in Christian history -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, makes a perceptive distinction on 13:1: Paul says that "the authorities"—that is, the institution of government itself for maintaining social order—are established by God, which does not mean that every wicked individual ruler enjoys God's approval. In this way Chrysostom upholds civic order without excusing tyranny. *(Homilies on Romans)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's distinction between the office of government and individual rulers on Romans 13:1 is widely cited; exact homily number not recalled -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, teaches on 13:1–7 that believers should honor governing authorities even when rulers are imperfect, because anarchy is usually even more destructive. Yet in his *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, he draws a firm limit: whenever rulers command anything contrary to God, their decree loses all binding force, for obedience to God always stands first. *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 13 verses 1–7; Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: both Calvin's respect for civil order and his limit requiring disobedience when rulers oppose God are very clear in these two works -->

**Ernst Käsemann**, twentieth-century German New Testament scholar and opponent of the Nazi regime, warned sharply against turning 13:1–7 into a dogma of unconditional submission to the state. He stressed that Paul was offering practical counsel to a small church in Rome facing the temptation of tax resistance or spiritual arrogance, not handing the state absolute authority above Christ. *(Commentary on Romans, 1980, on chapter 13 verses 1–7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Käsemann is well known for his historical-political critique against deifying the state in his reading of Romans 13:1–7 -->

**N. T. Wright**, New Testament scholar and former Anglican bishop, highlights the quiet irony in Paul sending 13:1–7 straight to the capital of the Roman Empire. While Roman propaganda exalted Caesar as lord and god, Paul subtly demotes the emperor's divine claims by calling Roman officials mere "servants of God" (13:4; 13:6), accountable to the true King, Jesus. *(The Letter to the Romans, New Interpreter's Bible, 2002, on chapter 13 verses 1–7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading "God's servants" as a subtle subversion of Caesar's divine pretensions is a signature point of Wright's commentary -->
