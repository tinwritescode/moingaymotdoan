# Romans 16 — Personal Greetings

## Story

Paul's longest theological letter ends with a list of names. First comes Phoebe, a sister in the Lord and a deacon of the church in Cenchreae, the port town near Corinth. Paul commends her to them: welcome her in the Lord in a way worthy of the saints, and help her in whatever matter she may need from them, for she has been a benefactor to many, including Paul himself (16:1–2). Most scholars think Phoebe was the courier who carried this letter to Rome.

Paul then sends personal greetings down the line. First are Priscilla and Aquila, his fellow workers in Christ Jesus, who risked their own necks for his life. Not only Paul, but all the churches of the Gentiles owe them gratitude. He also greets the church that meets in their house (16:3–5; Acts 18:1–3). This couple had earlier been expelled from Rome, worked alongside Paul making tents in Corinth, and had now returned to Rome.

The list rolls on. Epenetus, the first convert to Christ in the province of Asia; Mary, who worked very hard for them; Andronicus and Junia, Paul's kinsmen and fellow prisoners, who are outstanding among the apostles and were in Christ before he was. Then Ampliatus, Urbanus, Apelles, those belonging to the household of Aristobulus, Herodion, and those in the Lord from the household of Narcissus. Then Tryphena and Tryphosa, women who work hard in the Lord, and dear Persis, who has worked very hard in the Lord. Then Rufus, chosen in the Lord, and his mother, who has been a mother to Paul as well (16:5–13). Scholars continue to debate whether Junia is a woman counted among the apostles or simply well known to the apostles. And Rufus may well be the son of Simon of Cyrene, the man who carried Jesus' cross (Mark 15:21).

Paul greets two more groups along with the brothers and saints with them, then tells them to greet one another with a holy kiss. All the churches of Christ send them greetings (16:14–16). Scattered through this list are Jewish names, Greek names, Roman names, common slave names, and freeborn names—men and women alike. Most of those singled out for hard work are women. Here is the church the previous fifteen chapters described: a brand-new family where old dividing walls fall away.

Right in the middle of his greetings, Paul inserts a sharp warning. Watch out for those who cause divisions and put obstacles in your way contrary to the teaching you have learned; turn away from them. Such people are not serving our Lord Christ, but their own appetites, and by smooth talk and flattery they deceive the hearts of the naive. Everyone has heard of the Romans' obedience, so Paul rejoices over them; he wants them to be wise about what is good and innocent about what is evil. "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet." Then he pronounces the grace of our Lord Jesus upon them (16:17–20; Genesis 3:15). The very first promise in Scripture declared that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head; Paul says that triumph is about to unfold beneath the church's feet.

Next, those with Paul add their greetings: Timothy, his fellow worker, along with Lucius, Jason, and Sosipater, his kinsmen. Even the scribe taking dictation slips in a line of his own: "I, Tertius, who wrote down this letter, greet you in the Lord." Gaius, whose hospitality Paul and the whole church enjoy, sends his greetings, along with Erastus, the city treasurer, and our brother Quartus (16:21–23). Earlier manuscript traditions sometimes added a short benediction as verse 24, whereas modern critical editions omit it and keep Erastus and Quartus together in verse 23.

The letter concludes with a sweeping doxology. Praise belongs to Him who is able to strengthen them according to Paul's gospel and the proclamation of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery kept hidden for long ages past, but now revealed and made known through the prophetic writings by the command of the eternal God, so that all nations might come to the obedience of faith. To the only wise God be glory forever through Jesus Christ! Amen (16:25–27). In the surviving manuscripts, this doxology does not always appear at the end of chapter 16; some manuscripts place it after chapter 14 or chapter 15. Wherever it stands, it loops cleanly back to the opening lines of the letter: the gospel promised beforehand through the prophets to bring all Gentiles to the obedience of faith (1:2; 1:5).

## Key Takeaways

**Paul's theology has real names attached to it.** Fifteen chapters explored sin, grace, Israel, and the Gentiles. The final chapter shows what those truths look like in flesh and blood: more than thirty names, many accompanied by a brief word of affection. Paul remembers who risked their lives for him, who trusted Christ before him, and who labored tirelessly (16:3–13). For him, the gospel is never merely a chain of ideas; it is a network of friends.

**Everyone has a share in the work.** Paul calls numerous people "fellow workers" or those who "work hard in the Lord," applying those terms to women and men alike (16:3; 16:9; 16:12). Phoebe is commended with a recognized role of service, and Priscilla is named ahead of her husband. The early church did not stand on the shoulders of a few celebrities, but on the steady devotion of many ordinary, hardworking believers.

**Unity must be guarded.** Right inside his farewell greetings, Paul warns against divisive troublemakers. They do not arrive swinging swords, but speaking with "smooth talk and flattery" (16:17–18). The remedy is not endless argument with them, but turning away from them while staying "wise about what is good, and innocent about what is evil" (16:19).

**The God of peace is the God who conquers.** Paul calls Him "the God of peace" in the very breath that he promises He will crush Satan (16:20). God's peace is never weakness. The One who brings harmony into the church is also the One who defeats the divider, and He does it "under your feet"—through ordinary believers standing firm.

**God Himself is the One who keeps believers standing.** The closing doxology does not praise Paul or the strength of the Roman church, but "Him who is able to strengthen you" (16:25). Paul has urged and warned, yet he knows advice alone keeps no one standing. Only God can do that, and therefore all glory belongs to Him (16:27).

## Deeper Meaning

**The ancient serpent crushed beneath disciples' feet.** Revelation identifies Satan as "that ancient serpent" who is hurled down (Revelation 12:9). When the seventy-two disciples returned from their mission, Jesus said He saw Satan "fall like lightning from heaven," and He gave them authority "to tread on snakes and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy" (Luke 10:18–19). Paul gives the Roman church the same assurance: Christ's victory takes shape under the feet of His people (16:20).

**A married couple woven throughout the New Testament.** Priscilla and Aquila appear in Corinth, then Ephesus, then Rome, and finally back in Ephesus in Paul's last letter (Acts 18:2; 1 Corinthians 16:19; 2 Timothy 4:19). Wherever they moved, they opened their home for a church to meet. They were also the ones who invited Apollos in and "explained to him the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:26). A tentmaker and his wife mentored a gifted preacher.

**Those whose appetite is their god.** Paul's warning here resembles his words in Philippians about those whose "god is their belly" (Philippians 3:19). "Appetite" is not just about food; it describes anyone ruled by self-interest and personal craving. False teachers can speak sweetly about Christ while really "serving their own appetites" (16:18).

**Wise as serpents, innocent as doves.** The counsel in 16:19 echoes Jesus' instruction to His disciples: "be as shrewd as snakes and as innocent as doves" (Matthew 10:16). Paul similarly tells the Corinthians, "in regard to evil be infants" (1 Corinthians 14:20). Believers do not need to become experts in evil in order to resist it; they need to become deeply versed in what is good.

**A mystery now unveiled.** The mystery "kept hidden for long ages past" (16:25) is also a central theme of Ephesians and Colossians (Colossians 1:26). There Paul states plainly what that mystery is: "through the gospel the Gentiles are fellow heirs" (Ephesians 3:6). A greeting list of Jewish and Gentile believers meeting together in Roman homes is that very mystery made visible in a single city.

## Notes & Nuances

**In what sense is Phoebe called a "deacon" or "servant"?** The Greek word used here is the ordinary word for a servant, which also became the title for deacons in the church. Some take it simply as praise for a helpful woman. Many others note that the phrase "of the church in Cenchreae" points to a recognized office or role of ministry in that congregation (16:1). First Timothy contains a verse often translated "their wives" in connection with deacons, though the Greek can equally refer to women serving as deacons (1 Timothy 3:11). In 16:2, the word rendered "benefactor" or "helper" commonly denoted a patron of means and social standing who used her resources to support and protect others.

**Junia: whose name is it, and what does "among the apostles" mean?** Two questions arise here. First, is this Junia, a very common Roman woman's name, or an abbreviated man's name? No masculine form has ever been attested in ancient literature, and the early church commentators unanimously read it as a woman's name. Second, the phrase "outstanding among the apostles" can mean either prominent as apostles themselves or well known to the apostles (16:7). On the first reading, "apostle" is likely used in the broader sense of a commissioned missionary messenger—just as Barnabas is called an apostle (Acts 14:14)—rather than one of the Twelve.

**Was chapter 16 originally sent to Rome?** Because Paul had never visited Rome yet greets more than twenty people by name, some scholars proposed that this chapter was originally a separate note to Ephesus, where Priscilla and Aquila had lived and where Epenetus, "the first convert to Christ in Asia," might have resided (16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19). Most scholars today still hold that chapter 16 belongs to Romans. Travel across the empire was remarkably frequent, and after Emperor Claudius's edict expired, many Jews returned to Rome (Acts 18:2). Naming mutual friends in an unfamiliar church was a natural way for Paul to build trust.

**Is Erastus the city official named on the Corinthian inscription?** In 1929, archaeologists in Corinth uncovered a Latin paving inscription recording that a certain Erastus paved the plaza at his own expense in gratitude for his election as aedile. Many identify him with "Erastus, the city treasurer" (16:23). Others are more cautious, noting that the Latin title does not match the Greek term exactly and that Erastus was not a rare name. Either way, alongside Gaius, whom Paul baptized in Corinth (1 Corinthians 1:14), this greeting confirms that Paul wrote Romans from Corinth and that the gospel had reached people of civic standing in the city.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, third-century Alexandrian biblical scholar, wrote the earliest surviving commentary on Romans, preserved mostly in Rufinus's Latin translation. He records that Marcion, who sought to sever the gospel from the Old Testament, excised the closing doxology along with everything after chapter 14—a testimony that helps explain why 16:25–27 appears in different positions across manuscripts. **John Chrysostom**, great fourth-century Greek preacher, reads Junia as a woman and marvels at Paul's praise. He exclaims that to be an apostle is something great, and to be outstanding among the apostles is greater still—how great must this woman's devotion and wisdom have been to be counted worthy of the title of apostle! *(Homilies on Romans, Homily 31)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's praise of Junia in Homily 31 on Romans is widely quoted in discussions of 16:7 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observes that Paul commends Phoebe first of all for her office—an honorable and sacred ministry in the church. He connects her role with the enrolled widows of 1 Timothy, women entrusted by the congregation with caring for the poor and the sick (1 Timothy 5:9). *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 16 verse 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin treating Phoebe as holding a public church office linked to the widows of 1 Timothy 5; exact phrasing uncertain -->

**Philo of Alexandria**, first-century Jewish philosopher, reports that an entire district across the Tiber in Rome was inhabited by Jews, most of whom had been brought to Italy as prisoners of war and later freed by their masters. His account illuminates why the greeting list in Romans 16 contains both Paul's Jewish "kinsmen" and numerous names typical of freed slaves (16:7; 16:11). *(Embassy to Gaius)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Philo describes the Trastevere Jewish quarter and its freedman origins in this treatise; he does not discuss Romans 16, the connection is the editor's; section number omitted as uncertain -->

**Peter Lampe**, German New Testament scholar, examined every name in Romans 16 and found that the majority are Greek names commonly borne by slaves and freedpersons in Rome, with only a few likely freeborn. He also counts at least five distinct household groups (16:5; 16:10; 16:11; 16:14; 16:15), concluding that believers in Rome met in scattered house churches rather than a single central assembly. *(From Paul to Valentinus: Christians at Rome in the First Two Centuries)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the onomastic analysis and identification of house-church clusters in Romans 16 is the best-known portion of Lampe's study -->

**Eldon Epp**, American New Testament textual critic, devoted an entire monograph to 16:7. He demonstrates that early Christian writers almost uniformly read Junia as a woman's name, and that only in the Middle Ages did scribes begin altering it to a masculine name. Printed Greek New Testaments throughout much of the twentieth century kept the masculine form until later editions corrected it. Epp concludes that Junia was a woman and was numbered among the apostles. *(Junia: The First Woman Apostle, 2005)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this is the central thesis of Epp's book; exact years of the Greek edition revisions not recalled -->
