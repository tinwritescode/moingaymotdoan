# Romans 11 — The Olive Tree and the Mystery

## Story

Chapter 10 ended with a stubborn people. Has God therefore rejected his people? "Certainly not!" Paul offers himself as living proof: he is an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, from the tribe of Benjamin. God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew. He recalls how Elijah complained to God about Israel: they have killed your prophets, torn down your altars, I alone am left, and now they seek my life. What was God's reply? I have reserved for myself seven thousand who have not bowed the knee to Baal (11:1–4; 1 Kings 19:10; 1 Kings 19:18). Elijah thought he stood alone; he could not see the seven thousand.

In the same way today, a remnant exists, chosen by grace. And if by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would cease to be grace. What then? What Israel sought it did not obtain; the elect obtained it, while the rest were hardened. As it is written, God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes that cannot see and ears that cannot hear, down to this very day. David likewise says: may their table become a snare and a trap, a stumbling block and a retribution for them; may their eyes be darkened so they cannot see, and their backs be bent forever (11:5–10; Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 29:10; Psalms 69:22–23).

Did they stumble so as to fall beyond recovery? "Certainly not!" Rather, through their trespass salvation has come to the Gentiles, to make Israel jealous. If their trespass means riches for the world, and their failure means riches for the Gentiles, how much greater riches will their full inclusion bring! Paul speaks straight to the Gentile believers: as apostle to the Gentiles he honors his ministry, hoping somehow to stir his own people to jealousy and save some of them. For if their rejection brought reconciliation to the world, what will their acceptance be but life from the dead? (11:11–15). Israel's hardening is not the last chapter; it is a bend in the road.

Paul then draws a picture from the orchard. If the firstfruits of the dough are holy, so is the whole batch; if the root is holy, so are the branches. Some branches of the olive tree were broken off, and you—a wild olive shoot—were grafted in among them to share the nourishing sap of the root. So do not boast over the branches. If you feel tempted to boast, remember: you do not support the root, the root supports you (11:16–18). That root is the patriarchs and the promises made to Abraham. The Gentile church did not plant a brand-new tree; it was grafted into an existing one.

You might object: branches were broken off so I could be grafted in. Fair enough, Paul answers: they were broken off because of unbelief, and you stand only through faith. So do not become arrogant, but fear. If God did not spare the natural branches, he will not spare you either. Behold both the kindness and the severity of God: severity toward those who fell, but kindness toward you, provided you continue in his kindness; otherwise you too will be cut off. And even they, if they do not persist in unbelief, will be grafted back in, for God has the power to graft them in again. If you were cut from a wild olive tree by nature and grafted contrary to nature into a cultivated olive tree, how much more readily will these natural branches be grafted back into their own olive tree (11:19–24). Real gardeners rarely graft a wild branch onto a cultivated tree; Paul knows this and calls it "contrary to nature." Grace always runs contrary to nature.

Not wanting them to be wise in their own eyes, Paul discloses a mystery: a partial hardening has come upon Israel until the full number of the Gentiles has come in, and in this way all Israel will be saved. As it is written: the Deliverer will come from Zion and banish ungodliness from Jacob; this is my covenant with them when I take away their sins (11:25–27; Isaiah 59:20–21; Jeremiah 31:33–34). Interpreters still debate whether "all Israel" refers to the Jewish people in the future, the whole people of God made up of Jews and Gentiles together, or the faithful remnant across every era. Paul calls it a mystery and says no more.

With respect to the gospel they are enemies for your sake, yet with respect to election they remain beloved because of the patriarchs, "for God's gifts and His call are irrevocable." Just as you were once disobedient to God but have now received mercy through their disobedience, so they have now become disobedient in order that, by the mercy shown to you, they too may now receive mercy. For God has bound everyone over to disobedience so that he may have mercy on everyone (11:28–32). The indictment of chapters 1–3 locked everyone up; here Paul reveals why he locked them up.

At this point argument breaks open into praise. "O, the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God!" How unsearchable are his judgments, and how untraceable his paths! Who has known the mind of the Lord, or been his counselor? Who has first given to him that God should repay them? "For from Him and through Him and to Him are all things. To Him be the glory forever! Amen" (11:33–36; Isaiah 40:13; Job 41:11). Eleven chapters of doctrine end not with a tidy conclusion, but with worship.

## Key Takeaways

**God has never cast off Israel; their current hardening is partial and temporary.** Paul cites himself and the seven thousand faithful in Elijah's day to prove that a remnant always remains, preserved by grace (11:1–5). Even the stumbling of the majority is not the final outcome, becoming instead the doorway for the gospel to spread throughout the nations (11:11–15).

**The Gentile church does not replace Israel; it is grafted into the existing olive tree and its root.** Paul warns Gentile believers sharply against looking down on the broken natural branches: "it is not you who support the root, but the root supports you" (11:17–20). They stand purely by faith and grace, and God holds full power to graft the natural branches right back into their own tree (11:23–24).

**God's gifts and calling are never taken back.** Paul grounds hope in God's unwavering character: "for God's gifts and His call are irrevocable" (11:29). Because of the patriarchs and God's covenant fidelity, Israel remains deeply loved (11:28).

**The destination of all redemptive history is mercy and the glory of God.** After three chapters wrestling with anguish and mystery concerning Israel, Paul closes not with a neat formula answering every puzzle, but with a song of adoration before the fathomless wisdom of God, from whom, through whom, and to whom are all things (11:33–36).

## Deeper Meaning

**The seven thousand who refused to bow to Baal in Elijah's time prove God always preserves a remnant.** When Elijah despaired, thinking he was the last faithful soul left, God revealed he had kept seven thousand for himself (11:2–5; 1 Kings 19:18). The story of God's people never snaps completely, because their survival rests on his gracious choosing.

**The promise that "the Deliverer will come from Zion" carries forward Isaiah's everlasting covenant.** Speaking of the day God removes Jacob's sins (11:26–27), Paul draws on Isaiah's prophecy of the Redeemer coming to Zion to establish an enduring covenant with Israel's descendants (Isaiah 59:20–21). Paul's hope for his people is rooted deep in Old Testament promise.

**The closing doxology links directly back to Isaiah and Job before a wisdom beyond human grasp.** The rhetorical questions—"Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been His counselor?" and "Who has first given to God?" (11:34–35)—echo God's words to exiles in Isaiah and to Job out of the whirlwind (Isaiah 40:13; Job 41:11). The ultimate response to the mystery of God's ways is humility and worship.

**The wild olive shoot grafted into the root mirrors the single household pictured in Ephesians.** In Romans 11 Gentiles do not grow into a separate tree, but are grafted into the covenant root (11:17–18); similarly, in Ephesians Paul writes that Gentiles are no longer strangers, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's single household (Ephesians 2:19).

## Notes & Nuances

**"And so all Israel will be saved" in 11:26 has three major interpretations.** The most widely held view today (supported by Moo and Cranfield) anticipates a large-scale turning of ethnic Israel to the Messiah near history's climax, once the full number of Gentiles enters. A second view takes "all Israel" to mean the complete sum of believing Jews (the remnant) across all generations. A third view (held by Calvin and N. T. Wright) reads it as the entire covenant family composed of both believing Jews and believing Gentiles. On any reading, Paul makes clear that salvation comes through the Deliverer from Zion and through faith, not via a separate path apart from Christ (11:23; 11:26).

**Romans 11 flatly rejects arrogance or the notion that the Gentile church has simply "replaced" Israel.** Paul writes sternly to extinguish complacency among Gentile believers in Rome: "do not be arrogant, but be afraid" (11:20). The tragic history of antisemitism within Christian societies ran squarely against Paul's unmistakable warning here (11:17–22; 11:28–29).

**Verse 32's statement that God wants "to have mercy on all" fits the context of two people groups.** Having just compared Gentiles and Jews as two corporate groups (11:30–31), Paul uses "all" to drive home that neither group can boast of merit before God: both have experienced disobedience, and both can stand before him solely by mercy (11:30–32).
