# Romans 10 — Everyone Who Calls on the Name of the Lord Will Be Saved

## Story

Paul again opens with his own heart. His deepest longing and his prayer to God for the Israelites is that they may be saved. He testifies that they have real zeal for God, yet their zeal lacks true knowledge. Because they did not recognize the righteousness that comes from God and tried to establish their own, they did not submit to God's righteousness. "For Christ is the end of the law, to bring righteousness to everyone who believes" (10:1–4). The word "end" can mean termination or destination; many readers hear both senses at once. Paul himself once burned with that very zeal (Philippians 3:6).

Paul places two voices side by side. Moses writes about righteousness based on the law: the person who practices these things will live by them. Righteousness based on faith speaks differently: do not say in your heart, "Who will ascend into heaven?"—that is, to bring Christ down—or "Who will descend into the abyss?"—that is, to bring Christ up from the dead. What does it say instead? The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart—that is, the word of faith we proclaim (10:5–8; Leviticus 18:5; Deuteronomy 30:12–14). Nobody needs to climb into heaven or plunge into the depths; Christ has already come down and already risen.

What is that word? If you confess with your mouth, "Jesus is Lord," and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. With the heart one believes and is justified, and with the mouth one confesses and is saved. As Scripture says, "Anyone who believes in Him will never be put to shame." There is no distinction between Jew and Greek, because the same Lord is Lord of all and richly blesses everyone who calls on him. "For everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (10:9–13; Isaiah 28:16; Joel 2:32). Joel's verse spoke of the LORD (YHWH); Paul applies it directly to Jesus without pausing to explain.

Paul then traces the chain backward step by step. How can people call on one they have not believed in? How can they believe in one of whom they have not heard? How can they hear without someone preaching? And how can anyone preach unless they are sent? As it is written: "How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!" (10:14–15; Isaiah 52:7). A single chain starts with the messenger being sent and ends with a person being saved.

Yet not everyone has accepted the good news, for Isaiah asked, "Lord, who has believed our message?" Consequently, faith comes from hearing, and hearing comes through the word of Christ (10:16–17; Isaiah 53:1). Isaiah's question opens the very chapter about the Suffering Servant.

Did Israel fail to hear? Of course they heard, for their voice has gone out into all the earth and their words to the ends of the world. Did Israel fail to understand? Moses already foretold it: I will make you jealous by those who are not a nation; I will provoke you to anger by a foolish nation. Isaiah speaks even more boldly: I was found by those who did not seek me; I revealed myself to those who did not ask for me. Yet concerning Israel he says: all day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and obstinate people (10:18–21; Psalms 19:4; Deuteronomy 32:21; Isaiah 65:1–2). The chapter closes with God standing with outstretched hands all day long. The door is not shut; his own people simply have not stepped inside.

## Key Takeaways

**Religious passion can still miss the mark when it overlooks the righteousness God provides.** Paul never belittles his fellow Jews' earnestness; he once shared that exact fervor (Philippians 3:6). Yet by trying to construct a righteousness of their own instead of submitting to the righteousness God gives in Christ, they missed the very aim of the law (10:1–4).

**Salvation demands no impossible feats, because Christ has finished the work and brought it close.** People do not have to scale heaven or dive into the abyss to fetch salvation. The word of faith sits right in their mouth and heart: trust in the heart that God raised Jesus from the dead, and confess with the mouth that he is Lord (10:6–10).

**The gospel throws open an equal door to everyone who calls on the Lord.** No barrier separates Jew from Gentile before God's grace, because both share one Lord who "gives richly to all who call on Him" (10:12–13).

**Faith comes by hearing, making sending and proclamation indispensable links.** Paul steps backward from calling out to believing, from believing to hearing, from hearing to the preacher and the sending (10:14–17). God chooses human voices to carry the message of Christ into the world.

## Deeper Meaning

**"Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" places Jesus alongside the LORD of the Old Testament.** In Joel, that promise explicitly concerns calling on the name of YHWH (Joel 2:32). Paul's seamless application of the verse to Jesus (10:13) stands among the earliest and clearest affirmations of Christ's divinity in the New Testament.

**Paul rereads Moses' farewell sermon as pointing toward Christ.** When Paul says no one must ascend to heaven or descend into the deep to find the message (10:6–8), he draws straight from Moses' encouragement to Israel that God's word was already near, in their mouths and hearts (Deuteronomy 30:12–14). For Paul, that near word has now taken personal form in Jesus.

**The messenger's feet continue Isaiah's announcement of God's royal return.** Praising the feet of those who bring good news in 10:15 echoes Isaiah's song celebrating the day God returns to rescue Zion from exile (Isaiah 52:7). The gospel Paul preaches is the announcement that this saving reign has dawned.

**God's outstretched hands show his unwavering patience across history.** The closing verse (10:21) quotes God's lament in Isaiah over a rebellious people toward whom he still extends open arms (Isaiah 65:2). The door of mercy toward Israel has never been slammed shut.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Christ is the end of the law" in 10:4 carries two widely discussed senses.** The Greek noun behind "end" can signify both termination and goal or fulfillment. Many scholars stress that Christ is the destination to which the whole law pointed; others emphasize that he ends the era of living under the Mosaic law as the path to righteousness. The two ideas belong closely together: a race finishes at the exact instant the runner crosses the finish line (10:4).

**Chapter 10 stands right after chapter 9 to preserve the balance between divine sovereignty and human responsibility.** If chapter 9 leaves readers wondering whether human beings are purely passive before election, chapter 10 shows Paul praying earnestly for Israel's salvation (10:1), inviting everyone to call upon the Lord (10:13), and picturing God holding out his hands to those who refuse to listen (10:21).

**Verses 10:9–10 do not split "believing in the heart" and "confessing with the mouth" into two separate stages.** Paul uses familiar biblical parallelism to depict a single wholehearted response: genuine inner faith naturally surfaces in public confession that Jesus is Lord—a bold declaration inside a Roman Empire that reserved the title "Lord" for Caesar (10:9–10).

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, read "the end of the law" in 10:4 primarily as its completion or ultimate aim. In his view, Christ does not abolish the law but fulfills everything it aimed at, since every ceremony and command of old led sinners to him. *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 10 verse 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin is well known for interpreting telos in Romans 10:4 as the fulfillment or completion of the law -->

He argued that throughout Romans Paul never claims Christ discards the law, but rather affirms that Christ is the sole objective toward which the law always pointed. *(The Epistle to the Romans, ICC, 1979, on chapter 10 verse 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Cranfield is the premier twentieth-century advocate for reading telos as "goal" in Romans 10:4 -->

**Douglas Moo**, American New Testament scholar, contends that the Greek term in 10:4 carries both nuances—"termination" and "goal"—with termination clearly prominent. With Christ's coming, the old covenant era under the Mosaic law reaches its stopping point precisely because its purpose has been fulfilled. *(The Epistle to the Romans, NICNT, 1996, on chapter 10 verse 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Moo carefully weighs both meanings of telos in Romans 10:4 and argues for a combined sense highlighting the end of the old era -->
