# Romans 14 — The Strong and the Weak

## Story

The church in Rome included both Jewish and Gentile believers, and they disagreed over very everyday matters: what to eat and which days to observe. Paul begins plainly: accept the one whose faith is weak, without quarreling over disputable opinions. One person's faith allows them to eat anything, while the one who is weak eats only vegetables. The one who eats everything must not look down on the one who does not, and the one who abstains must not pass judgment on the one who eats, for God has accepted him. Who are you to judge someone else's servant? To his own master he stands or falls—and he will stand, for the Lord is able to make him stand (14:1–4). Each side has its own temptation: the strong are tempted to despise, and the weak are tempted to condemn.

The same holds true for sacred days. One person regards one day as more sacred than another; another regards every day alike. Each should be fully convinced in his own mind. Whoever observes the day does so to the Lord; whoever eats does so to the Lord, since he gives thanks to God; and whoever abstains does so to the Lord and gives thanks to God as well. None of us lives to himself alone, and none of us dies to himself alone. If we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord; so whether we live or die, we belong to the Lord. For this very reason Christ died and returned to life, that He might be the Lord of both the dead and the living (14:5–9).

Why, then, do you judge your brother? Or why do you look down on your brother? For we will all stand before God's judgment seat. As it is written: "As surely as I live, says the Lord, every knee will bow before Me; every tongue will confess to God." So then, each of us will give an account of himself to God (14:10–12; Isaiah 45:23). Anyone standing before that judgment seat will have no time to give an account for someone else.

Instead of passing judgment on one another, make up your mind not to put a stumbling block or obstacle in your brother's way. Paul makes clear where he himself stands: he knows and is persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean in itself, yet if anyone regards something as unclean, then for him it is unclean. If your brother is distressed by what you eat, you are no longer walking in love. Do not by your eating destroy the one for whom Christ died. Do not let what you consider good be spoken of as evil (14:13–16; Mark 7:19). Paul is one of the strong, yet he refuses to wield his freedom as a weapon.

"For the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking, but of righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit." Whoever serves Christ in this way is pleasing to God and approved by men. Let us therefore pursue what leads to peace and to mutual edification. Do not destroy the work of God for the sake of food. All food is indeed clean, but it is wrong for a man to eat anything that causes someone else to stumble. It is better not to eat meat or drink wine or do anything else that will cause your brother to fall (14:17–21).

Paul closes the chapter by speaking to both groups. Whatever conviction you hold about these things, keep it between yourself and God. Blessed is the one who does not condemn himself by what he approves. But whoever has doubts is condemned if he eats, because his eating is not from faith; "and everything that is not from faith is sin" (14:22–23). That closing line is not talking about every mundane action in life, but about acting against one's own conscience. The strong must never pressure the weak into doing what their conscience does not yet permit.

## Key Takeaways

**On matters outside the core of the faith, the church must welcome one another instead of dividing.** Paul addresses everyday disagreements over food and special days among the believers in Rome. He names the distinctive temptation of each camp—those who feel free are prone to contempt, while those who abstain are prone to judgment—and commands both to stop, "for God has accepted him" (14:1–6).

**Every believer belongs to the Lord and will give an account of himself before God's judgment seat.** No one lives or dies for himself alone, because Christ is Lord of all (14:7–9). Because each person will answer to God for his own life, no one has the right to climb onto the judge's bench and condemn the Lord's servant (14:4; 14:10–12).

**Love ranks higher than personal freedom.** Paul himself stands with those who grasp their freedom in Christ—"nothing is unclean in itself" (14:14)—yet he places the heavier burden on the strong: willingly restraining what they have every right to do so they do not wound or ruin a brother for whom Christ died (14:13–15; 14:20–21).

**The kingdom of God consists of righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit, not food and drink.** Verse 14:17 lifts the church out of petty disputes to refocus on what genuinely matters in life with God and with one another (14:17–19).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul's assertion that "nothing is unclean in itself" directly continues Jesus' own teaching.** When Paul writes that he is convinced in the Lord Jesus that all food is clean (14:14; 14:20), he echoes the Gospel of Mark's remark that in His teaching Jesus "declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19).

**The prophecy that every knee will bow before God reminds believers who the ultimate Judge is.** Paul cites Isaiah (14:11; Isaiah 45:23) to place every minor church dispute under the light of the final judgment—the same reminder he wrote to the Corinthians when he told them that we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:10).

**The principle of laying down freedom for a weaker brother parallels Paul's counsel on food offered to idols in Corinth.** Both in Romans 14 and in 1 Corinthians 8, Paul applies the same measure: right knowledge must never trample a brother's conscience, and if food causes a brother to stumble, it is better never to eat meat at all (14:15; 14:21; 1 Corinthians 8:9; 1 Corinthians 8:13).

**Belonging to the Lord in life and death puts the call to be a "living sacrifice" into daily practice.** In chapter 12 Paul urged believers to offer their bodies wholly to God (12:1); here he shows what that looks like on the ground—from meals and calendar days to life and death itself, everything falls under the lordship of Christ (14:6–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who exactly are the "weak" and the "strong" in Romans 14?** Most scholars understand the "weak" to be mainly Jewish believers (along with some God-fearing Gentiles) in Rome who still observed Jewish dietary laws—choosing to eat only vegetables when kosher meat was hard to verify—and continued to keep the Sabbath or Jewish festival days. The "strong" were largely Gentile believers (along with Paul himself) convinced of their freedom from those food laws. Calling someone "weak" here is not a moral insult; it simply describes a conscience not yet firm enough to embrace this new freedom in Christ (14:1–6).

**The call for mutual forbearance in chapter 14 applies to secondary matters, not clear sin or the foundation of the gospel.** Paul specifies that these are "disputable matters"—areas of differing conviction where both sides can act "to the Lord" and give thanks to God (14:1; 14:6). When the core of the gospel itself was threatened—as in Galatians, where opponents demanded circumcision for salvation—Paul refused to yield an inch.

**Causing a brother to "stumble" (14:13; 14:21) means far more than simply annoying or displeasing him.** Verses 14:22–23 show Paul's precise meaning: causing someone to stumble means pressuring or drawing a believer with a sensitive conscience into doing something they still inwardly suspect is sinful, thereby damaging their walk of faith (14:20–23).

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, admires Paul's pastoral tact in 14:1–15: Paul neither ridicules those who cling to familiar dietary scruples nor allows them to impose those scruples on others, reminding both sides that the fellow believer across from them is "the one for whom Christ died" (14:15). *(Homilies on Romans)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Chrysostom emphasizing Paul's balanced pastoral tone and the weight of 14:15; uncertain of the exact homily number -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, builds his doctrine of Christian liberty in morally indifferent matters directly on Romans 14. He teaches that freedom before God must always walk hand in hand with love toward one's neighbor, gladly limiting itself to protect a weaker conscience. *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 14; Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin cites Romans 14 extensively in his chapter on Christian freedom in Book 3 of the Institutes and in his Romans commentary -->

**James D. G. Dunn**, British New Testament scholar, argues that Romans 14–15 is no generic ethical appendix, but reflects the exact historical tension that prompted Paul to write Romans: friction between Torah-observant Jewish believers ("the weak") and Gentile believers ("the strong") after Jewish Christians returned to Rome. *(Romans 9–16, Word Biblical Commentary, 1988, on chapter 14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Romans 14–15 as the concrete historical setting of Jewish-Gentile tension in Rome is central to Dunn's commentary -->

**Mark Nanos**, American Jewish scholar, offers a notable alternative reading of the "weak" in Romans 14–15: he proposes that this group may include non-Christian Jews in the synagogues of Rome whom Paul wanted Gentile believers to respect and not offend, though the majority of scholars still view them as Jewish believers within the church. *(The Mystery of Romans, 1996)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the hypothesis that the "weak" are non-Christian Jews is Nanos's distinctive proposal in The Mystery of Romans; exact chapter number not recalled -->

**John Stott**, twentieth-century English Anglican pastor, observes that 14:17 provides a healthy benchmark for every church controversy: whenever believers quarrel bitterly over secondary practices, they have forgotten that the kingdom of God is not about eating and drinking, but about righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. *(The Message of Romans, 1994, on chapter 14 verse 17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Stott highlights Romans 14:17 as the standard separating essentials from non-essentials in his Romans exposition -->
