# Romans 6 — Dead to Sin, Alive to God

## Story

Paul has just written that where sin increased, grace increased all the more. He knows immediately what question will follow: "Shall we continue in sin so that grace may increase?" His answer is emphatic: "Certainly not! How can we who died to sin live in it any longer?" (6:1–2). The very objection proves Paul truly preached free grace. Had he taught salvation by human effort, nobody would have thought to ask that question.

To explain, he reminds them of baptism. All who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death. They were buried with him so that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, they too might walk in newness of life. If they have been united with him in a death like his, they will certainly also be united with him in his resurrection (6:3–5). In Paul's eyes, baptism is more than a washing ritual; it is a funeral and a rising to life.

Our old self was crucified with him so that the body of sin might be rendered powerless and we would no longer be enslaved to sin, because anyone who has died has been freed from sin. If we died with Christ, we believe that we will also live with him. Christ, having been raised from the dead, cannot die again; death no longer has mastery over him. The death he died, he died to sin once for all, and the life he lives, he lives to God. In the same way, Paul writes, count yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus (6:6–11). This is the very first command in Romans. It does not tell readers to manufacture a new status; it tells them to believe what has already happened.

Only from that foundation does action follow. Do not let sin reign in your mortal body so that you obey its desires. Do not offer the parts of your body to sin as instruments of wickedness; instead, offer yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life, and offer the parts of your body to him as instruments of righteousness. Sin will not be your master, because you are not under law, but under grace (6:12–14).

That last remark triggers the same objection in a new form: if we are not under law, may we go on sinning? "Certainly not!" Paul turns to the picture of slavery. Whoever you present yourselves to as obedient servants, you are slaves to the one you obey—either of sin, leading to death, or of obedience, leading to righteousness. There is no third place to stand. Thanks be to God that those who were once slaves to sin have now wholeheartedly obeyed the pattern of teaching to which they were entrusted. Freed from sin, they have become slaves to righteousness (6:15–18).

Paul apologizes for the blunt metaphor: he is speaking in human terms because of their weakness. Just as they once offered the parts of their body as slaves to impurity and escalating lawlessness, they should now offer them as slaves to righteousness leading to sanctification. When they were slaves to sin, they were free from the control of righteousness. What fruit did that freedom produce? Things they are now ashamed of, whose end is death. Now, freed from sin and enslaved to God, they reap the fruit of sanctification, and the end is eternal life (6:19–22).

Paul closes the chapter with a sentence familiar to generations: "For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (6:23). Sin pays wages, and pays them in full. God does not pay wages; he gives a gift.

## Key Takeaways

**Overflowing grace is never a permit to stay in sin.** Twice Paul shuts down the casual assumption that grace lets believers keep sinning (6:1–2; 6:15). Grace does not merely cancel the penalty of sin; it transforms the believer's identity so completely that living the old way no longer fits who they are.

**New life starts with grasping what is already true in Christ, and only then moves to obedience.** Before issuing a single imperative, Paul states accomplished facts: the old self was crucified, buried, and raised with Christ (6:3–10). The first command in the entire letter (6:11) is to consider ourselves dead to sin and alive to God—and from that conviction comes refusing to let sin rule (6:12–13).

**There is no neutral territory between two masters.** Paul uses the image of slavery to shatter the illusion of total human autonomy (6:16–18). Every person either serves sin and receives death, or serves God and receives holiness and life (6:20–22).

**Eternal life is not earned pay; it is a free gift.** The closing verse sets two worlds side by side: sin settles its payroll cleanly with death, whereas God does not issue wages at all, bestowing eternal life freely in Christ Jesus (6:23).

## Deeper Meaning

**Being crucified and raised with Christ is a thread running across Paul's letters.** The image of baptism burying the old self so that believers step into new life (6:4–6) reappears closely in Colossians (Colossians 2:12), and captures the personal reality Paul states in Galatians: "I have been crucified with Christ" (Galatians 2:20).

**Jesus himself taught that practicing sin makes a person a slave.** When Paul depicts sin as a master commanding the human body (6:16–17), he unfolds the very truth Jesus spoke in the Gospel of John: "everyone who sins is a slave to sin" (John 8:34).

**The aim of the cross is to bring believers from death to sin into life for righteousness.** What Paul teaches in 6:10–11 aligns seamlessly with what Peter writes to the scattered churches: Christ bore our sins on the tree "so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness" (1 Peter 2:24).

**Liberation from sin's bondage carries echoes of the ancient Exodus.** In the Old Testament, God redeemed Israel out of slavery in Egypt not so they could live without a Lord, but so they would belong to him and worship him (Exodus 20:2). In Romans 6, believers are likewise freed from sin to become servants of God (6:18; 6:22).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Paul say believers "died to sin" (6:2) and yet still warn them "do not let sin reign" (6:12)?** Dying to sin does not mean temptation vanishes or believers instantly reach sinless perfection. Paul means the old master's ownership and right to command have been severed: sin may still knock at the door or try to pull believers back, yet it no longer holds authority to force their obedience (6:6–14).

**Does baptism in 6:3–4 automatically save the person who undergoes the rite?** In the early church, coming to faith and being baptized went hand in hand as two sides of entering union with Christ, so Paul could appeal to baptism as the unmistakable marker of that union. Christian traditions today describe baptism's role differently—some as a means conveying grace, others as an outward sign of inward faith—yet all agree that Paul is summoning baptized believers to live in step with their new life (6:3–4).

**Calling believers "slaves to righteousness" is not an endorsement of slavery.** Paul himself qualifies the metaphor in 6:19—"I am speaking in human terms"—knowing how imperfect it is for describing life with God. He borrows a stark reality of the Roman world to drive home one point: human beings are never masterless, and true freedom is found only in belonging to a good Master (6:18–19).

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, painted a vivid picture of baptism in 6:4: as believers go down beneath the water, the water acts like a tomb burying the old self; as they rise out of it, a new person emerges ready for a new life. *(Homilies on Romans)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's imagery of baptismal water as a tomb burying the old self in Romans 6:4 is one of the best-known passages in his Romans homilies; uncertain of the exact homily number -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, insisted on 6:1–6 that Christ cannot be torn in two. Whoever receives Christ for justification is simultaneously united with him for sanctification, so genuine grace can never excuse a life settled in sin. *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 6 verses 1–6; Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the principle of double grace—that justification and sanctification cannot be separated because both flow from union with Christ—is central to Calvin's exposition of Romans 6 -->

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, twentieth-century German pastor and theologian, echoed the warning of Romans 6 to expose what he called "cheap grace"—preaching forgiveness without repentance or discipleship. Costly grace, by contrast, is real grace because it calls people to die with Christ in order to live anew. *(The Cost of Discipleship, 1937)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the contrast between cheap grace and costly grace tied to dying with Christ forms the famous opening chapter of this book -->

**Martyn Lloyd-Jones**, twentieth-century Welsh preacher, examined the verb "count yourselves" in 6:11 and pointed out that it is neither self-hypnosis nor pretending something unreal. Rather, it is an act of faith reckoning accurately with a fact already finished at the cross, and living accordingly as someone who has genuinely changed masters. *(Romans: An Exposition of Chapter 6, 1972)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Lloyd-Jones's exposition of the verb logizomai in Romans 6:11 is a well-known sermon in his volume on Romans 6 -->

**Ernst Käsemann**, twentieth-century German New Testament scholar, observed that in Romans 6 sin is not merely a handful of isolated bad deeds, but a ruling power holding dominion over the world. Christian freedom therefore is never doing whatever one pleases, but a transfer of sovereignty—stepping out from sin's dominion into the lordship of Christ. *(Commentary on Romans, 1980, on chapter 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading sin and grace as competing cosmic powers claiming lordship is a signature hallmark of Käsemann's commentary on Romans -->
