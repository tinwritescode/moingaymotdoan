# Romans 4 — Abraham Believed God

## Story

Paul has just stated that faith upholds the law. Now he proves it using the figure every Jew honors most: Abraham, Israel's physical forefather. If Abraham had been justified by works, he would have had grounds to boast—yet not before God. What does Scripture actually say? "Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (4:1–3; Genesis 15:6). The crucial word is "credited": righteousness was entered onto Abraham's ledger as a gift, never as wages earned.

Paul illustrates that word through workplace pay. To a worker, wages are never treated as a favor, but as a debt owed. Yet to the one who does not work, but trusts him who justifies the ungodly, their faith is credited as righteousness. King David speaks the exact same language about the blessing of the person to whom God credits righteousness apart from works: "Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, whose sins are covered! Blessed is the person whose sin the Lord will never count against them" (4:4–8; Psalms 32:1–2). Abraham has righteousness credited to him; David has sin not counted against him. Two sides of one single grace.

Does that blessing belong solely to the circumcised? Paul asks a simple timeline question: when was Abraham's faith credited as righteousness—before or after he was circumcised? Before! He was declared righteous back in Genesis 15, whereas circumcision did not arrive until Genesis 17, many years later. Circumcision was simply a sign and seal of the righteousness he already possessed through faith while still uncircumcised. Consequently, Abraham stands as father of all who believe without being circumcised—and also father of the circumcised who follow in the footsteps of the faith Abraham exercised before he was circumcised (4:9–12). Jews proudly call Abraham their father; Paul shows he was father of believing Gentiles first.

The promise works the exact same way. The promise to Abraham and his offspring that they would inherit the world came not through the law, but through the righteousness of faith. If heirs are defined strictly by the law, then faith becomes hollow and the promise falls apart, because the law brings wrath—where there is no law, there is no transgression. Therefore the promise depends on faith, so that it rests on grace and stands guaranteed to all Abraham's offspring—not only those under the law, but also everyone sharing the faith of Abraham, the father of us all (4:13–16). A promise hanging on human track records is never secure; a promise resting on grace stands as solid as the God who gave it.

Just as it stands written, God appointed him father of many nations. He trusted the God who gives life to the dead and calls into existence things that do not yet exist. Against every human reason for hope, Abraham believed in hope, becoming the father of many nations just as promised: "So shall your offspring be" (4:17–18; Genesis 17:5; Genesis 15:5).

That faith never shut its eyes to reality. Abraham looked squarely at his own body—already as good as dead at nearly a hundred years old—and at the lifelessness of Sarah's womb. Yet his faith did not weaken. He did not waver through unbelief regarding God's promise, but grew stronger in faith and gave glory to God, fully convinced that God held the power to deliver on what he had promised. Therefore it was credited to him as righteousness (4:19–22). Paul skips mentioning Ishmael or Sarah's laughter; he traces Abraham's faith as a lifelong road that, despite stumbles along the way, kept walking to the finish.

Then Paul turns directly toward his readers. The words "it was credited to him" were not penned for Abraham's sake alone, but for us as well—we whose faith will also be credited as righteousness as we believe in the One who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead. Abraham trusted God to bring life out of an aged, barren body; we trust God who brought Jesus out of a sealed tomb. "He was delivered over to death for our trespasses and was raised to life for our justification" (4:23–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Salvation is a free gift, never a paycheck earned by performance.** Paul reaches for an everyday workplace contrast: when a worker collects wages, nobody calls it generosity—the employer simply pays a debt owed. Before God, nobody builds up credit putting God in their debt; righteous standing belongs to empty-handed people who trust "him who justifies the ungodly" (4:4–5).

**Abraham is the shared spiritual father of Jews and Gentiles alike who trust God.** Paul zeroes in on a simple timeline fact in Genesis: Abraham was counted righteous many years before ever receiving circumcision (4:9–11). Thus he stands as father to all people of faith, circumcised or uncircumcised alike (4:11–12; 4:16).

**Authentic faith never pretends hard facts do not exist.** Abraham looked straight at his near-century-old body and Sarah's barren womb without fooling himself, yet stayed fully persuaded that God possessed the power to keep his word (4:19–21).

**Jesus' resurrection guarantees that our debt of sin is paid in full.** Paul closes chapter 4 by linking Abraham's trust in the God who brings life to the dead with Christian trust today: Jesus "was delivered over for our trespasses and raised for our justification" (4:24–25).

## Deeper Meaning

**Both towering pillars of the Old Testament—Abraham and King David—testify to justification by faith.** Paul pairs Genesis' verdict on patriarch Abraham (4:3; Genesis 15:6) with King David's joyous psalm celebrating those whose sins are forgiven and never counted against them (4:6–8; Psalms 32:1–2). Patriarchal promise and royal Israel rest on the exact same grace.

**The promise making Abraham father of many nations blossoms inside the church.** In Genesis, God renamed Abram to Abraham, pledging he would father a multitude of nations (Genesis 17:5). Paul shows that promise always reached beyond biology, finding fulfillment as peoples from every nation enter God's family through faith (4:16–18; Galatians 3:6–9).

**Life springing from Sarah's barren womb foreshadows Christ rising from the grave.** Paul names Abraham's God as "the God who gives life to the dead" (4:17). From the very birth of Israel, God delighted in drawing fresh life out of dead places—culminating in raising Jesus from the tomb (4:24).

**Paul and James both appeal to Abraham from two complementary angles.** Both apostles quote Genesis 15:6, looking at two moments along Abraham's life: Paul focuses on Abraham trusting God's promise to be welcomed by God (4:3), while James points years later to Abraham offering Isaac as proof that living faith expresses itself through obedience (James 2:21–24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Do Paul and James contradict each other regarding Abraham?** Comparing 4:2–5 with James 2:24 can look contradictory at first glance. Yet the two writers answer two completely different distortions: Paul confronts the illusion that humans can earn God's acceptance through moral achievement, whereas James confronts lifeless talk that claims faith without producing real action. Put concisely: we are saved by faith alone, yet genuine saving faith is never alone (4:5; James 2:22–24).

**Why does Paul say Abraham did not waver in unbelief (4:20) when Genesis records him laughing at the promise?** Paul summarizes the overarching trajectory of Abraham's whole life rather than erasing isolated human moments of doubt. Despite momentary stumbles, Abraham kept clinging to God's promise and refused to give up on God's power (4:19–21).

**What does "raised for our justification" in 4:25 mean?** Paul is not slicing Christ's cross and resurrection into unrelated compartments. Christ's death settled sin's penalty in full, while his resurrection stands as God's public declaration that the sacrifice succeeded completely—confirming that everyone united to him stands fully acquitted (4:25).

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, pauses in astonishment at the phrase "justifies the ungodly" in 4:5. In any human courtroom, a judge who acquits a guilty criminal acts corruptly; only God's extraordinary grace accomplishes that apparent paradox while remaining completely righteous. *(Homilies on Romans)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Chrysostom marveling at the phrase "justifies the ungodly" in 4:5 in these homilies, though exact homily number is unverified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pays special attention to 4:11 calling circumcision a "seal" of righteousness already possessed by faith. From this verse he develops his view of sacraments like baptism: they do not manufacture grace mechanically on their own, but function as visible seals confirming God's promise to believers. *(Commentary on Romans, on chapter 4, verse 11; Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin uses Romans 4:11 as a foundational text for his doctrine of sacraments as signs and seals in both works -->

**Second Temple Jewish tradition**, seen in *1 Maccabees* and the book of *Jubilees*, frequently hailed Abraham as counted righteous because of tested loyalty through trials—above all offering Isaac. Setting Romans 4 alongside this backdrop clarifies why Paul carefully pinpoints Genesis 15, years prior to both circumcision and the binding of Isaac. *(1 Maccabees, chapter 2, verse 52; Jubilees, chapter 17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: 1 Maccabees 2:52 directly links Abraham's faithfulness under testing with being reckoned righteous, forming the classic Jewish backdrop to Romans 4 -->

**N. T. Wright**, New Testament scholar and former Anglican bishop, maintains that chapter 4 is far more than a handy sermon illustration of individual salvation by faith. Rather, Paul answers the covenant question closing chapter 3: how God keeps his oath to grant Abraham a single worldwide family of Jews and Gentiles inheriting the earth together (4:13). *(The Letter to the Romans, New Interpreter's Bible, 2002, on chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Romans 4 as the story of Abraham's single global covenant family is a signature argument of Wright -->

**Douglas Moo**, American New Testament scholar, emphasizes that the contrast between wages and grace in 4:4–5 strikes straight to the heart of the gospel. In Moo's view, Paul rejects not merely ethnic identity markers, but every mindset that treats obedience as earned credit obligating God. *(The Epistle to the Romans, NICNT, 1996, on chapter 4, verses 4–5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Moo appeals specifically to Romans 4:4–5 to counter narrowing "works" exclusively to ethnic boundary markers -->
