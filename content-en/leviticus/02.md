# Leviticus 2 — The Grain Offering

## Story

Following the animal burnt offering comes the *grain offering*—an offering of fine wheat flour, olive oil, and frankincense, with no blood shed. It is a gift brought from the harvest and daily labor of the farmer.

A worshiper may bring uncooked fine flour with oil poured over it and frankincense placed on top, or baked goods prepared from that flour: cakes baked in an oven, cooked on a griddle, or prepared in a pan (2:1; 2:4–7). Whatever the form, the priest takes only a handful of the flour and oil along with all the frankincense and burns it on the altar as a *memorial portion* to the LORD. The entire remainder belongs to Aaron and his sons for food; Scripture calls it "a most holy part of the offerings made by fire to the LORD" (2:2–3; 2:8–10).

The law lays down two strict rules regarding ingredients. First, neither leaven nor honey may ever be burned in any offering made by fire on the altar; people may still bring leaven and honey as an offering of firstfruits for the priests to enjoy, just never upon the altar fire (2:11–12). Second, every grain offering must be seasoned with salt without fail, because salt is the sign of "the salt of the covenant of your God" (2:13).

The chapter closes with a grain offering of firstfruits: fresh heads of grain roasted in the fire and crushed, dressed with olive oil and frankincense. Once again, the priest burns a portion as a memorial to the LORD (2:14–16).

## Key Takeaways

**Daily work itself becomes an offering to God.** The burnt offering represents life, while the grain offering consists of wheat, flour, and oil—things produced by sweat in the fields. Presenting a share of one's food to the LORD acknowledges that the land, the harvest, and the strength to labor all come from his hand.

**Worship sustains those who serve God.** The priests receive no tribal land of their own to farm like the other tribes. When the people bring their grain offerings, a handful burns on the altar as God's portion, and the rest is given by God to Aaron and his sons (2:3; 2:10). The LORD provides for his servants through the generosity of his people.

**Salt speaks of an enduring covenant.** In the ancient world, salt preserved food from spoiling and symbolized lasting fidelity. Sharing a meal with salt across the ancient Near East sealed a durable bond of loyalty. Seasoning every offering with salt reminded Israel that their covenant with God was no passing arrangement (2:13).

**No leaven and no honey on the altar.** Both leaven and honey ferment easily and alter whatever they touch. Barring them from the altar fire reminded worshipers to bring God what is pure and uncorrupted, keeping anything prone to decay out of the holy place (2:11).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus is the spotless Bread of Life.** The grain offering was made from the finest flour, free of leaven, and then crushed, roasted, or baked before being offered to God. Jesus declares himself to be the living bread that came down from heaven (John 6:35; 6:51). His sinless, perfected human life is the purest grain offering ever presented to God.

**Leaven becomes a picture of corruption spreading swiftly.** Later in the New Testament, Paul draws on the image of unleavened bread to urge the church to sweep out malice and wickedness and keep the festival with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth (1 Corinthians 5:7–8). Jesus likewise compares hypocrisy to yeast working its way through a batch of dough (Matthew 16:6; Luke 12:1).

**The call to keep salt in the life of discipleship.** When Jesus says that everyone will be salted with fire and urges his disciples to have salt among themselves and be at peace with one another (Mark 9:49–50), he directly echoes the command to salt every offering here (2:13). A life faithful to God's covenant carries a preserving, purifying savor in the world (Matthew 5:13).

## Notes & Nuances

**The grain offering is not a vegetarian dietary rule.** Some traditional renderings call it a "meatless offering" (and older English versions say "meat offering," using an archaic word for food), which can lead modern readers to confuse it with religious vegetarianism. In reality, the original Hebrew term simply means *gift*, *tribute*, or *grain offering*, setting an offering of wheat flour apart from animal sacrifices.

**Why are leaven and honey barred from the altar yet allowed as firstfruits?** Verses 11 and 12 show that leaven and honey are not inherently evil—Israelites could still bring them for the priests to eat (2:11–12; 7:13; 23:17). They were excluded only from the altar fire, likely because fermentation was associated with decay, or because neighboring pagan cults commonly burned honey in their rituals.

**What is frankincense?** Frankincense is a prized aromatic resin imported from southern Arabia that releases a sweet fragrance when burned. It was never eaten; the priest burned all of it on the altar so its fragrance rose before the LORD (2:2; 2:16).

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, notes that the grain offering follows immediately after the burnt offering to teach a proper order in godly living: first we must give ourselves to God, and only then our possessions and produce. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explains why the cakes offered to God were simple yet pure: God does not demand luxury, but genuine gratitude toward the One who supplies daily bread. **Andrew Bonar**, nineteenth-century Scottish minister, sees in the fine, even flour a picture of Christ's perfectly balanced humanity, untouched by any uneven temperament. The oil poured over the flour pictures the Holy Spirit anointing him, while the oven and griddle recall the fiery sufferings he endured during his life. *(A Commentary on the Book of Leviticus, 1846, on chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Bonar is well known for detailed Christological typology throughout his Leviticus commentary; exact phrasing not verified -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, points out that the Hebrew word for grain offering originally denoted tribute brought by a vassal to a king as a token of loyalty. When Israel offered the grain offering, they were acknowledging the LORD as their true King, the rightful owner of the very soil they tilled. *(The Book of Leviticus, NICOT, 1979, on chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the tribute meaning of minhah is a standard scholarly observation noted by Wenham on chapter 2; exact page not verified -->
