# Leviticus 8 — The Ordination of the Priests

## Story

After seven chapters of sacrificial laws, the book turns to historical narrative: the day Aaron and his four sons are formally consecrated as priests, carrying out the instructions the LORD had given Moses earlier (Exodus 29:1–37). Before the entire congregation gathered at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, Moses declares that everything about to take place is by the LORD's command (8:1–5).

The ceremony begins with washing and vesting. Moses brings Aaron and his sons forward and washes them with water (8:6). He dresses Aaron in the full high priestly regalia: tunic, sash, robe, ephod, the breastpiece containing the *Urim* and *Thummim* for discerning God's will, and the turban bearing the golden plate of holiness (8:7–9). Next, Moses takes the anointing oil, sprinkles the altar seven times, anoints the Tabernacle and all its furnishings, and pours the anointing oil upon Aaron's head to consecrate him. Finally, he clothes Aaron's sons in tunics, fastens their sashes, and ties caps on their heads (8:10–13).

Three sacrifices follow in sequence, with Moses temporarily serving as the officiating priest. First is the bull for the sin offering: Aaron and his sons lay their hands on its head, Moses slaughters it, applies its blood to the horns of the altar to purify the altar, burns its fat on the altar, and burns its flesh, hide, and offal outside the camp (8:14–17). Second is the ram for the burnt offering, burned entirely on the altar as a sign of complete devotion (8:18–21).

Third is the special ram of *ordination*. After slaughtering it, Moses puts some of its blood on three spots on Aaron and his sons: the lobe of the right ear, the thumb of the right hand, and the big toe of the right foot (8:22–24). He places the fat, the right thigh, and three kinds of unleavened bread into their hands to present as a wave offering before the LORD, then burns them on the altar; Moses himself receives the ram's breast as his share. Finally, Moses mixes anointing oil with blood from the altar and sprinkles it over Aaron, his sons, and their garments (8:25–30).

The ceremony closes with a sacred meal: Aaron and his sons boil the ordination ram at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting and eat it with the unleavened bread, burning whatever remains. Moses instructs them to stay day and night at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting for seven full days without leaving until their ordination is complete, keeping the LORD's charge "so that you will not die." And they did everything just as the LORD commanded (8:31–36).

## Key Takeaways

**The priesthood is God's call, never self-appointed.** Moses does not elevate his brother on his own whim. Throughout chapter 8, the refrain "as the LORD had commanded Moses" sounds seven times (8:4; 8:9; 8:13; 8:17; 8:21; 8:29; 8:36). Authority to represent the people before God is valid only when it follows his express will.

**Those who make atonement for others must first be atoned for themselves.** Before Aaron can ever offer a sacrifice for anyone else, he himself must be washed with water and present a bull for his own sin (8:6; 8:14). Priests are not flawless saints; they are sinners cleansed and set apart by grace.

**Whole-body consecration from listening ear to working hand and walking foot.** Putting blood on the right earlobe, right thumb, and right big toe (8:23–24) paints a vivid picture: a servant of God needs ears tuned to hear his word, hands devoted to doing his work, and feet walking steadily in his ways.

**Serving God begins with remaining quietly in his presence.** Seven days and nights without leaving the entrance of the Tent of Meeting (8:33–35) set aside a season of stillness and complete obedience before the busy demands of ministry begin. Service for God flows out of abiding with God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus is a High Priest far greater than Aaron.** Hebrews points straight to Aaron's ordination to affirm that no one takes this honor upon himself, but must be called by God—and so Christ too was appointed by God as a priest forever (Hebrews 5:4–6). Yet unlike Aaron, who had to offer a bull for his own sins (8:14), Jesus is wholly sinless and therefore offered himself once for all for the sins of the people (Hebrews 7:26–28; 9:11–14).

**The oil poured on Aaron's head becomes a symbol of unity and the Holy Spirit.** Psalm 133 recalls the very moment precious oil poured over Aaron's head ran down his beard and onto the collar of his robes (8:12) to celebrate how good and pleasant it is when brothers dwell in unity (Psalms 133:1–2). The title *Christ* (or *Messiah*) itself means *the Anointed One*—the One anointed by the Holy Spirit without measure (Luke 4:18; Acts 10:38).

**The whole church is now called to be a holy priesthood.** Under the Old Covenant, only Aaron's descendants could be washed, vested, and stationed in the Tabernacle. Under the New Covenant, the apostle Peter calls every believer in Jesus a "holy priesthood" and a "royal priesthood," washed clean by Christ's blood to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God (1 Peter 2:5; 2:9; Revelation 1:5–6).

## Notes & Nuances

**What were the *Urim* and *Thummim* (8:8)?** Scripture never describes the physical shape of these two objects; we know only that they were kept inside the high priest's breastpiece to inquire of the LORD's will in major national decisions (Numbers 27:21; 1 Samuel 28:6). Many scholars suggest they were two small stones or lots drawn to yield a *yes* or *no* answer.

**Why is ordination literally called "filling the hand"?** In Hebrew, the word translated *ordination* (8:22; 8:33) literally means *filling the hand*. When Moses loaded Aaron's and his sons' hands with the choice meat and unleavened cakes (8:27), the gesture signified entrusting the full authority and responsibility of priestly office into their hands.

**Why the stark warning "so that you will not die" (8:35)?** That sober reminder underscores the life-and-death seriousness of ministry inside God's sanctuary. It quietly foreshadows the heartbreaking judgment that is about to strike two of Aaron's sons in chapter 10.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, reflects on the rich symbolism of applying blood to Aaron's and his sons' ear, hand, and foot (8:23–24). In his view, every servant of God needs atoning blood to sanctify all three areas: ears attentive to God's word, hands diligent in good works, and feet walking uprightly in his paths. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Leviticus 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: symbolic interpretation of the ear, thumb, and big toe is a familiar feature in Matthew Henry's commentary on chapter 8; exact wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, notices that the entire assembly is summoned to witness the ordination at the Tent of Meeting (8:3–4). He argues that God made the ceremony public so all Israel would know for certain that Aaron had not seized the priesthood for himself, but held it by divine appointment, inspiring proper respect for their spiritual leaders. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on Leviticus 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing the public nature of the ordination to validate divine authority is consistent with Calvin; exact location not verified -->

**Andrew Bonar**, nineteenth-century Scottish minister, draws attention to a subtle contrast between verses 12 and 30: anointing oil is poured abundantly over Aaron's head alone before any ordination blood is shed, whereas his sons are only sprinkled afterward with oil mixed with blood (8:12; 8:30). To Bonar, this foreshadows Christ the sinless Head anointed in fullness, while the church as his members receive the Spirit only through his atoning blood. *(A Commentary on the Book of Leviticus, 1846, on chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Bonar frequently contrasts Aaron's anointing prior to blood with Christ and the church; exact phrasing not verified -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, points out that the seven days of ordination in chapter 8 intentionally echo the seven days of creation in Genesis 1, matched by the sevenfold repetition of "as the LORD had commanded Moses."
