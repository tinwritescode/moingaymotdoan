# Leviticus 15 — Laws Concerning Bodily Discharges

## Story

Chapter 15 brings the cycle of everyday clean and unclean laws to a close, addressing bodily discharges in both men and women. The LORD instructs Moses and Aaron to announce to Israel four cases arranged in crisp symmetry: two for men and two for women (15:1–2).

The first case is a man with an abnormal bodily discharge caused by illness. Throughout his condition he is unclean, whether the discharge flows or is blocked. Any bed he lies on, any chair or saddle he sits on, and anything placed beneath him becomes unclean. Whoever touches him, touches those objects, or is spat upon by him must wash his clothes, bathe in water, and remain unclean until evening. Any clay pot he touches must be broken, while wooden vessels must be rinsed with water. Once healed, he counts off seven days, washes his clothes, and bathes in fresh running water; on the eighth day he brings two turtledoves or two young pigeons to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting for the priest to offer one as a sin offering and one as a burnt offering (15:2–15).

The second case is milder: a man who has an emission of semen, or a husband and wife after marital relations. Both simply bathe in water, wash whatever was touched, and remain unclean until evening, requiring no sacrifice (15:16–18).

The two female cases run in exact parallel. When a woman has her regular monthly period, she is ritually unclean for seven days. Her bed or seat becomes unclean, and anyone touching them washes clothes, bathes, and stays unclean until evening. If her husband lies with her during this time, he likewise becomes unclean for seven days. Once the seven days pass, she naturally returns to cleanness without bringing any sacrifice (15:19–24). If, however, she suffers prolonged bleeding outside her normal cycle or continuing past it, she remains unclean throughout that condition just like the man with an abnormal discharge. Once the bleeding stops, she counts off seven days, and on the eighth day brings two turtledoves or two young pigeons to the Tent of Meeting for the priest to present as a sin offering and a burnt offering (15:25–30).

At the close of the chapter, the LORD spells out the reason behind all these detailed measures: Israel must keep themselves separate from their uncleanness so they will not die in their uncleanness by defiling the Tabernacle where he "dwells among them" (15:31–33).

## Key Takeaways

**Ritual uncleanness is not moral sin.** Marital intimacy and a woman's menstrual cycle are natural functions created by God himself—never shameful or wicked. These statutes do not brand the human body as filthy; they mark a boundary between states tied to lost vitality and the absolute holiness of God's dwelling place (15:18–19; 15:31).

**Life belongs to God, and every loss of life-giving fluids recalls the boundary between life and death.** Scholars note that both semen and blood are directly bound up with reproduction and human life. When fluids linked to the source of life flow out of the body—especially persistently through illness—they echo decay and mortality. Because the Tabernacle is the home of the Living God, traces of ebbing life are not brought straight into the sanctuary (15:31).

**These rules fostered order and reverence in a community camped around the Tabernacle.** Among hundreds of thousands living side by side in desert tents, bathing, laundering, and brief isolation both curbed communicable diseases and provided a daily reminder that the Holy One dwelt right at the center of camp (15:31).

**The way back into worship always stays open at the lowest cost.** Even for protracted chronic conditions, the restoration offering required only two turtledoves or two young pigeons—the least expensive offering allowed for the poor anywhere in Leviticus (15:14; 15:29). Neither sickness nor poverty shut anyone out of worship forever.

## Deeper Meaning

**The woman with the issue of blood touches Jesus' garment.** Twelve years of chronic bleeding placed the woman in the Gospels squarely inside the situation of Leviticus 15:25–27: not only exhausted by illness, she lived in unending ritual uncleanness, passing defilement to anyone or anything she touched. Even so, she pressed through the crowd to touch the fringe of Jesus' cloak (Mark 5:25–29).

**Jesus' holiness reverses the direction of contagion.** Under Leviticus 15, whenever the unclean touches the clean, uncleanness spreads to the clean. Yet when the bleeding woman touches Jesus, he does not become unclean; instead, healing power surges out from him, curing her instantly as he tenderly calls her *Daughter* (Mark 5:30–34; Luke 8:43–48).

**From outward washings to inner cleansing of the heart.** Hebrews looks back on all the ceremonial washings and bodily regulations of the old covenant as temporary figures awaiting the time of reformation. The blood of Christ does not merely wash the outer body—it purifies the conscience so believers may directly serve the living God (Hebrews 9:10; 9:13–14; 10:22).

## Notes & Nuances

**What kind of male "discharge" is meant here?** Some older translations render the Hebrew phrase with narrow or archaic terms that sound like a single ailment, whereas the original Hebrew expression is broader, encompassing any abnormal discharge from the male reproductive organ caused by infection or disease, distinct from a normal seminal emission (15:16).

**Do not mistake this purity law for prejudice against women.** At first glance, modern readers may feel uneasy seeing menstruation labeled unclean for seven days. Yet all of chapter 15 is built on strict gender symmetry: both men and women have routine natural states and abnormal chronic conditions requiring the exact same sacrifices. Marital intercourse likewise makes husband and wife equally unclean until evening (15:18). The rules governed access to the sanctuary and never diminished a woman's dignity.

**Why must someone with a physical illness bring a *sin offering*?** Throughout Leviticus, that sacrifice does not apply solely to willful moral offenses; it also functions broadly as a *purification offering*—cleansing traces of ritual defilement from the altar and sanctuary so the healed person can formally resume communal worship (15:15; 15:30).

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Puritan pastor, observes that these detailed rules on bodily discharges not only promoted cleanliness and modesty in Israel, but also taught a spiritual lesson about the contagious nature of sin. Just as bodily impurity could pass to beds, chairs, and anyone who touched them, unconfessed sin silently taints shared life unless brought to God for cleansing. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Leviticus 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: drawing moral typology and stressing sin's contagious character is typical of Matthew Henry on the Levitical purity laws; exact text not checked -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, interprets the clean–unclean system of Leviticus 11–15 through the polarity of life and death.
