# Leviticus 14 — Cleansing Skin Diseases and Infected Houses

## Story

Chapter 13 ended with the afflicted person dwelling alone outside the camp; chapter 14 opens onto the happiest day of his life: the day he is healed and welcomed back into the community and the Tabernacle (14:1–2). Restoration unfolds in two clear stages.

The first stage takes place *outside the camp*, readmitting the healed person to the human community. The priest himself goes outside the camp to examine him. Once he confirms the disease is healed, the priest calls for two live clean birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop. One bird is slaughtered in a clay pot over fresh running water; the priest dips the live bird together with the cedar wood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop into the blood mixed with water, sprinkles the healed person seven times, declares him clean, and releases the live bird into the open field (14:3–7). The person washes his clothes, shaves off all his hair, and bathes in water; he may then enter the camp, though staying outside his own tent for seven days. On the seventh day he shaves off all his hair, washes his clothes, and bathes once more (14:8–9).

The second stage occurs on *the eighth day at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting*, restoring him fully to worship before God. The healed person brings three lambs (for a guilt offering, a sin offering, and a burnt offering), fine flour mixed with oil for a grain offering, and a log of olive oil (14:10–11). The priest slaughters the guilt-offering lamb and applies some of its blood to the healed person's right earlobe, right thumb, and right big toe; he sprinkles oil seven times before the LORD, places oil directly over those exact three blood-marked spots, and pours the remaining oil on the person's head, then offers the remaining sacrifices to make atonement (14:12–20). As always, God makes generous provision for the poor: anyone unable to afford three lambs brings just one male lamb plus two turtledoves or two young pigeons, receiving the exact same blood and oil anointing as the wealthy (14:21–32).

The second half of the chapter deals with *mildew or mold inside a house* once Israel settles in the land of Canaan. When a homeowner reports greenish or reddish streaks sunken into the walls, before stepping inside to inspect, the priest orders the house emptied of all furnishings so none of the family's belongings will be declared unclean along with the house (14:33–36). If the mold spreads after shutting up the house for seven days, the infected stones are removed outside the city and the interior walls are scraped and replastered. Only if the mold breaks out aggressively again must the house be torn down to safeguard the community's health. If repairs stop the outbreak, the priest performs the very same rite with two birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop to pronounce the home clean (14:37–57).

## Key Takeaways

**The priest must go outside the camp to find the outcast.** The afflicted person was forbidden to enter the camp, so verse 3 states explicitly: "the priest shall go outside the camp to examine him" (14:3). Restoration begins when God's representative leaves his comfortable circle to meet the isolated right where they are.

**The former outcast is welcomed back with a ceremony matching priestly ordination.** Astonishingly, placing blood and oil on the right earlobe, right thumb, and right big toe and pouring oil on the healed person's head (14:14–18) mirrors the ordination of Aaron and his sons in chapter 8 (8:23–24; 8:12). God does not merely let the former outcast sit quietly in the back corner of the camp; he restores their full dignity as one consecrated to serve him.

**The slain bird and the freed bird tell the whole story of redemption.** One bird pours out its blood over fresh water; the other is dipped in that blood and set free to soar across the open sky (14:5–7). One life dies so another can be liberated into fullness of life.

**God's law cares deeply about ordinary households' livelihoods.** Allowing the poor to bring two birds instead of two extra lambs (14:21–22) and ordering every piece of furniture carried out before declaring a house unclean (14:36) reveal the practical, compassionate heart of God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus honors chapter 14 completely when he heals people with leprosy.** After reaching out to touch and cleanse a leper, Jesus directs him to go show himself to the priest and present the offering Moses commanded as a testimony to all that he has truly been healed (Matthew 8:4; Mark 1:44; Luke 17:14). Jesus does not merely clear up skin; he restores broken lives to family and community.

**Hyssop and cleansing water point toward forgiveness in Christ.** When King David repents of his grave sin, he prays in the exact imagery of Leviticus 14: cleanse me with hyssop, and I will be clean; wash me, and I will be whiter than snow (Psalms 51:7). In the New Testament, Hebrews explains that ancient sprinklings of blood and water were shadows pointing ahead; now through the blood of Jesus, our hearts are sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water so we may draw near boldly into the Most Holy Place (Hebrews 9:13–14; 10:19–22).

**Christ's death and resurrection shine through the two-bird ceremony.** Generations of Bible readers have seen in the bird slain over fresh water and the living bird released into open air (14:5–7) a luminous emblem of Jesus, who was delivered over to death for our trespasses and raised to life for our justification (Romans 4:25).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why use cedar wood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop (14:4)?** Cedar wood was prized for its fragrance and resistance to decay; scarlet recalled rich blood and vitality; hyssop (a small bushy herb) worked like a brush for sprinkling blood and water, just as on Passover night (Exodus 12:22). Together these three items formed a vivid bouquet of fresh life countering the decay of skin disease.

**What does "fresh water" mean (14:5)?** In the original Hebrew, the phrase literally means *living water*—water drawn from a flowing spring or running stream rather than stagnant water. Once again, a vivid symbol of abundant life stands directly opposite the marks of death.

**What does verse 34 mean by "when I put a mark of mildew in a house" (14:34)?** In Old Testament usage, every natural event falls under God's sovereign governance. Interestingly, some ancient Jewish commentators even read this positively—suggesting that tearing down moldy Canaanite walls occasionally revealed hidden treasure inside—yet at its plainest level, the statute protected families in their new land from toxic structural mold harmful to their health.

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, likens the two birds at the start of chapter 14 to the two sides of Christ's redemptive work: the slain bird depicts his death on the cross, while the living bird dipped in blood and soaring skyward pictures his resurrection and ascension. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, praises the humane wisdom of verse 36 where the priest tells the homeowner to clear out all belongings before inspecting for mold (14:36). God takes no pleasure in needlessly wasting his people's property, Calvin remarks; strict as his law is against impurity, it remains full of tenderness toward people. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on Leviticus 14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting God's humane concern to spare household property in 14:36 matches Calvin's interpretive spirit; exact section not verified -->
