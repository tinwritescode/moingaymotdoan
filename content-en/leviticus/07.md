# Leviticus 7 — Priestly Portions and the Law of the Peace Offering

## Story

Chapter 7 wraps up the opening series of laws on the five sacrifices. First comes the priestly guidance for the guilt offering: slaughter the animal where the burnt offering is slaughtered, splash its blood around the altar, burn all its fat and fatty organs, and let the male priests eat its most-holy meat in a holy place (7:1–7). The law also assigns clear compensation for the priests' service: the priest who offers a burnt offering keeps its hide; baked or fried grain offerings belong to the officiating priest, while uncooked grain offerings mixed with oil or dry are shared equally among all of Aaron's sons (7:8–10).

The heart of the chapter sets out detailed rules for the *peace offering*—the one joyful banquet where ordinary worshipers share in eating the sacrificial meat (7:11–21). Three occasions are distinguished. When offered for *thanksgiving*, the worshiper brings unleavened cakes as well as leavened bread, sets aside one loaf for the priest, and must eat all the meat on the very same day, leaving none until morning (7:12–15). When offered to fulfill a *vow* or as a *freewill offering*, the meat may be eaten over two days, but anything left on the third day must be burned; anyone who eats it on the third day invalidates the whole offering and bears guilt (7:16–18). Nor may the meat touch anything unclean, and only someone who is ceremonially clean may eat it; anyone who joins that sacred meal while unclean "must be cut off from his people" (7:19–21).

Next, the LORD reiterates the ban on eating the fat of cattle, sheep, or goats—fat from an animal that died naturally or was torn by wild beasts may be put to other practical uses such as lamp fuel or ointment, yet never eaten—along with the ban on eating any blood of bird or animal, under penalty of being cut off from one's people (7:22–27).

Finally, the law defines the priests' special portion of the peace offering: the worshiper brings the fat and the breast with his own hands to be presented as a wave offering before the LORD. The fat is burned on the altar, the breast goes jointly to Aaron and his sons, and the right thigh belongs specifically to the priest who splashed the blood and offered the fat. The chapter ends with a formal summary of all the sacrificial laws the LORD commanded Moses on Mount Sinai (7:28–38).

## Key Takeaways

**Thanksgiving joy is meant to be shared, not hoarded.** A single small family can scarcely consume an entire bull or sheep in one day. Requiring that a thanksgiving peace offering be eaten before morning (7:15) naturally moved the worshiper to invite neighbors, the poor, and Levites to gather around the table and rejoice together.

**Those who serve the altar live from the altar.** Assigning the hides, baked grain offerings, breasts, and right thighs (7:8–10; 7:31–34) created a clear, transparent livelihood. No priest had to beg or skim from the offerings; the system rewarded active ministry while also sharing uncooked grain equally so no member of the priestly family went hungry.

**Feasting with God requires purity.** The peace offering is a glad banquet, yet never a casual free-for-all. Meat kept into the third day becomes detestable, and an unclean person who presumes to eat it is cut off from the community (7:18–21). Nearness to God always walks hand in hand with reverence.

**Bring your offering yourself, not merely through an intermediary.** Verses 29 and 30 emphasize that the worshiper "must bring his own offering to the LORD; with his own hands he is to bring the offerings made by fire" (7:29–30). Even with priests assisting, true worship requires stepping personally before God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul draws directly on this chapter for supporting gospel workers.** When defending the right of those who proclaim the gospel to earn their living from the gospel, Paul points straight back to Leviticus 7: those who work in the temple get their food from the temple, and those who serve at the altar share in what is offered on the altar (1 Corinthians 9:13–14).

**The call to purity at the sacrificial meal echoes in the Lord's Supper.** The rule that only clean worshipers may eat the peace offering (7:19–21) sheds light on Paul's sober warning to the Corinthian church: let each person examine himself before eating the bread and drinking the cup, rather than treating the Lord's Table lightly (1 Corinthians 11:27–29).

**A new sacrifice of thanksgiving in Christ.** Hebrews explains that the old animal sacrifices gave way to Jesus' once-for-all offering of himself (Hebrews 9:11–14; 10:10–14). Today the thanksgiving offering that pleases God is no longer a bull or basket of loaves, but a continual sacrifice of praise and generous sharing with those in need (Hebrews 13:15–16).

## Notes & Nuances

**What are the breast, the wave offering, and the contribution (7:30–31; 7:34)?** Some older translations use archaic anatomical terms for the breast of the animal set aside for Aaron and his sons. The *wave offering* and the *contribution* (sometimes rendered *heave offering*) involved ceremonial motions of lifting or presenting the portions toward the altar and drawing them back, symbolizing that they were first given to God and then received back from his hand to sustain the priests.

**Why does the thanksgiving offering include leavened bread when chapter 2 forbids leaven?** Chapter 2 only forbids *burning* leavened bread on the altar fire (2:11–12). Here, the leavened loaves are never placed on the altar (7:13); they are simply enjoyed by the priest and the worshiper during the celebratory meal.

**What penalty is meant by "cut off from his people" (7:20–21; 7:25; 7:27)?** The phrase means being severed from Israel's covenant community. Commentators generally understand it in two complementary ways: formal banishment from the assembly, or direct divine judgment such as premature death or dying without descendants.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, sees practical kindness in the command to finish the meat of a thanksgiving offering on the very same day (7:15). Because one household could hardly eat an entire animal in a day, the rule naturally fostered hospitality and generosity toward the poor, turning private gratitude into shared community joy. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Leviticus 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting that the one-day eating limit promoted hospitality and care for the poor is characteristic of Matthew Henry; exact wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, contends that banning sacrificial meat on the third day (7:17–18) was not merely to avoid spoilage in a hot climate, but to keep people from treating sacred food like ordinary market meat saved for thrift. Delay or carelessness with holy things dulled the solemn joy of the covenant meal. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on Leviticus 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: warning against turning sacred gifts into everyday fare and preserving reverence is characteristic of Calvin; exact location not verified -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, explains why thanksgiving offerings had to be eaten in one day while vow and freewill offerings allowed two: thanksgiving offerings carried a higher degree of sanctity and thus demanded stricter handling. He also observes that dividing uncooked flour equally among all of Aaron's sons (7:10) ensured that elderly or infirm priests unable to officiate at the altar still received their livelihood. *(The Book of Leviticus, NICOT, 1979, on chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Wenham discusses graded holiness among peace offerings and the social provision of dividing uncooked grain; exact page not verified -->

**Jacob Milgrom**, Jewish scholar specializing in Leviticus, highlights verse 24, which permits using the fat of an animal that died naturally for everyday purposes (such as lamp fuel or leatherwork) while strictly forbidding eating it (7:24). In his view, this proves fat is not intrinsically unclean or magical; it is forbidden as food simply because God ordained it so to train Israel in restraint and respect for life. *(Leviticus 1–16, Anchor Bible, 1991, on chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: analyzing 7:24 on the non-impure nature of animal fat and the pedagogical role of dietary laws is a well-known point of Milgrom; exact page not verified -->
