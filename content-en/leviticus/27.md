# Leviticus 27 — Vows, Dedicated Gifts, and Tithes

## Story

Chapter 27 closes Leviticus with a remarkably practical appendix: how to value, redeem, or fulfill voluntary vows made to the LORD—covering persons, animals, houses, and fields—alongside the mandatory tithe.

First comes the case of someone vowing to dedicate himself or a family member to the LORD (for instance, in gratitude after deliverance from danger). Rather than serving permanently at the Tabernacle, the person pays a fixed redemption valuation scaled by age and working strength: fifty shekels of silver for a man aged twenty to sixty and thirty shekels for a woman; twenty shekels for a young man aged five to twenty and ten for a young woman; five and three shekels for young children from one month to five years; fifteen and ten shekels for those over sixty. Yet if the person making the vow is too poor to pay the set rate, the priest evaluates their actual circumstances and fixes an affordable amount (27:1–8).

Next come vows of livestock, houses, and land. Once a clean sacrificial animal is vowed, the owner may never swap a good animal for a bad one or vice versa; anyone who tries forfeits both animals to the LORD. For an unclean animal (like a donkey or camel) or a house, the priest sets its valuation, and if the original owner wishes to buy it back, he pays the assessed price plus an extra fifth (27:9–15). For ancestral farmland, value is calculated by the seed required to sow it and the years remaining until the Year of Jubilee; the owner may redeem it by adding one fifth, but if left unredeemed and sold to someone else, it passes permanently to the sanctuary at Jubilee. Bought land (not ancestral property) contributes only its crop value until Jubilee, when it returns to its original family clan (27:16–25).

The closing section distinguishes three categories outside voluntary vows. First is the firstborn of clean livestock, which already belongs to the LORD by law (while unclean firstborn animals may be redeemed with a fifth added, or sold) (27:26–27). Second is anything or anyone irrevocably set apart to the LORD—*devoted things*—which can never be sold or redeemed (27:28–29). Third is the tithe of grain, fruit, and herds (every tenth animal passing under the shepherd's rod, chosen impartially without sorting good from bad or substituting): all tithes are holy to the LORD, and redeeming agricultural produce with money requires adding a fifth. The book closes with a formal summary: these are the commands the LORD gave Moses on Mount Sinai (27:30–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Promises made to God must be honored seriously, without rashness or deceit.** Scripture compels no one to make a vow, yet once a person pledges a gift to God, he may neither swap in inferior goods nor go back on his word (27:9–10; 27:33). Requiring an extra twenty percent fee to redeem a vowed item discouraged impulsive pledges (27:13; 27:15; 27:19).

**God's fairness always makes room for the poor.** Immediately after laying down the standard valuation schedule, verse 8 builds in a compassionate safeguard: the priest adjusts the fee to fit what a poor person can actually pay (27:8). A willing heart weighs more than silver in the sanctuary treasury.

**You cannot claim credit for voluntarily gifting what already belongs to God.** Firstborn livestock and the tenth of every harvest already belong to the LORD by covenant law (27:26; 27:30). True voluntary generosity starts only after honoring basic faithful stewardship.

**Worship requires practical, transparent financial support.** Ending a book filled with sacrifices and moral holiness on an earthy chapter about sanctuary finances reminds us that faith does not float in the clouds: the Tabernacle, priests, and worshiping community needed clear, fair, accountable funding to endure.

## Deeper Meaning

**Guard against impulsive vows and religious loopholes.** The respect for spoken promises in Leviticus 27 echoes in Ecclesiastes: it is better not to vow than to make a vow and fail to pay it (Ecclesiastes 5:4–5). In the New Testament, Jesus calls disciples to such transparent honesty that ornate oaths become needless—"Yes, yes" or "No, no" (Matthew 5:37). He also exposes those who used a vow dedicating assets to the temple (*Corban*) as a pious excuse to neglect caring for aging parents (Mark 7:10–13).

**From mandatory tithes to cheerful generosity in the New Testament.** The tithing law of verses 30 through 32 trained Israel to put God first in their income and support the Levites serving the sanctuary (27:30–32). Jesus affirms tithing as fitting—provided people never neglect justice, mercy, and faithfulness (Matthew 23:23). In the epistles, Paul elevates giving into joyful, voluntary generosity: each should give what he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly, for God loves a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:7).

**Everything belongs to God, and Christ has paid our full redemption price.** Across Leviticus 27 rings the recurring note of *redemption* paid in sanctuary shekels. The entire book of Leviticus—from the offerings of chapter 1, the Day of Atonement in chapter 16, and Jubilee in chapter 25 to the redemption price of chapter 27—converges in Christ: he redeemed us not with perishable silver or gold, but with his own precious life, making our whole existence a holy offering belonging to God (1 Peter 1:18–19; Hebrews 10:10).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why do the valuation rates for men and women differ in verses 3 through 7 (27:3–7)?** Seeing fifty shekels for men and thirty shekels for women aged twenty to sixty can easily make modern readers assume Scripture values men above women. Yet look at the entire table: men over sixty drop to fifteen shekels (half the rate of adult women at thirty shekels), and children under five are set at five and three shekels. This scale never measures *human worth* before God—since male and female bear God's image equally (Genesis 1:27)—but mirrors average physical labor wages in an ancient agrarian economy, representing the replacement cost of manual labor at the Tabernacle.

**Does verse 29 ("no person devoted to destruction may be ransomed; they must be put to death") permit human sacrifice (27:29)?** Absolutely not. Leviticus itself fiercely outlaws child and human sacrifice under penalty of death in chapters 18 and 20 (18:21; 20:2–5). The Hebrew term *cherem* ("devoted to destruction") here does not refer to a parent's voluntary vow as in verse 2, but to grave criminals or enemies placed under judicial or divine sentence of destruction under ancient criminal and wartime law (such as Achan in Joshua 7): once condemned under that sentence, no bribe or ransom money could buy them off.

**Leviticus 27 links directly to Hannah's vow dedicating Samuel.** Later, when barren Hannah prayed for a son and vowed to give him to the LORD for all the days of his life (1 Samuel 1:11), she chose not to pay the monetary redemption of Leviticus 27:2–8, literally bringing young Samuel to serve at the sanctuary lifelong.

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Puritan pastor, finds it deeply fitting that Leviticus closes with a chapter on voluntary vows: after God defines every required duty, he still welcomes loving hearts eager to offer more freely. **Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, offers a convincing explanation of the valuation scale in verses 2 through 7: the sharp variation across age groups confirms it represents ancient physical labor rates (matching ancient Near Eastern wage lists), never spiritual worth or human dignity (27:2–7).
