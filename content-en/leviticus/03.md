# Leviticus 3 — The Peace Offering

## Story

The third kind of sacrifice is the *peace offering*—often also called the *fellowship offering* or *well-being offering*. It is the most joyful of the five offerings, because after God's portion is burned on the altar, the worshiper, together with family and friends, sits down to eat the meat right in the LORD's presence (detailed further in chapter 7).

Unlike the burnt offering, which requires a male animal, the peace offering may be either male or female, so long as it is "without blemish." Worshipers may choose from the herd or from the flock—cattle, sheep, or goats—according to their means; birds are not used for this offering because they are too small to provide a shared meal (3:1; 3:6; 3:12).

The opening steps mirror the burnt offering: the worshiper leads the animal to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, lays a hand on its head, and slaughters it, while the priests splash the blood on all sides of the altar (3:2; 3:8; 3:13). What sets this offering apart is the specific portion burned on the altar. The priest removes all the fat covering the entrails, the two kidneys with the fat on the loins, and the lobe of the liver—plus the thick fat tail in the case of a sheep—and burns them on the altar on top of the burnt offering (3:3–5; 3:9–11; 3:14–16).

The chapter closes with a permanent statute binding on every generation wherever they live: "All the fat belongs to the LORD," and the people must never eat either fat or blood (3:16–17).

## Key Takeaways

**The ultimate goal of worship is peace and table fellowship with God.** The burnt offering speaks of total surrender, the grain offering of gratitude, and the peace offering portrays people who have been reconciled to God now sitting down to share a joyful meal in his presence.

**Give God the choicest part.** In the ancient world, fat was the richest, most prized portion of an animal, symbolizing vitality and abundance. Burning all the fat on the altar with the declaration "All the fat belongs to the LORD" (3:16) warns worshipers never to bring God leftovers, but always the very best.

**Life belongs to God.** The ban on eating blood stands right beside the ban on eating fat (3:17). Later, in chapter 17, Scripture states the reason plainly: the life of a creature is in the blood, and God has given it on the altar to make atonement for souls (17:11). Human beings are permitted to eat meat, but never to treat the source of life lightly.

**Peace rests on the foundation of the burnt offering.** Verse 5 explicitly notes that the priest burns the fat of the peace offering *on top of* the burnt offering already burning on the altar (3:5). That order carries profound truth: human beings can enjoy peace and fellowship with God only upon the foundation of atonement and full surrender.

## Deeper Meaning

**Christ himself is our peace.** When Israel brought a peace offering, they celebrated a restored relationship with God. In the New Testament, Paul declares that Christ made peace through the blood of his cross, reconciling all things to God (Colossians 1:20), and that he himself is our peace, tearing down the dividing wall of hostility between Jew and Gentile (Ephesians 2:14–16).

**The Lord's Supper carries forward the shared meal before God.** The peace offering was a covenant banquet: the LORD received his share on the altar, the priest received his allotted portion, and the worshiper ate the rest with family in gladness. When Jesus institutes the Lord's Supper and invites his disciples to share the bread and the cup of the new covenant (Matthew 26:26–28; 1 Corinthians 10:16–18), he opens a new meal of fellowship that looks ahead to the marriage supper of the Lamb in God's kingdom.

**Old dietary barriers are fulfilled in Christ, yet reverence for life endures.** The prohibitions against eating fat and blood belonged to the sacrificial system of the Tabernacle. Hebrews shows that once Christ offered himself as the one complete sacrifice, the old ceremonial order gave way (Hebrews 9:9–14; 10:1–10). Even so, in the early church the apostles still asked Gentile believers to abstain from blood in order to maintain harmony with their Jewish brothers and sisters (Acts 15:20; 15:29).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does "peace offering" mean?** Some classic translations render it as an offering of thanksgiving or repaid grace. In the original Hebrew, its name is closely linked to the word *peace* (*shalom*), which denotes far more than the absence of conflict: it describes wholeness, harmony, and flourishing with God and with one's neighbors.

**Which fat is forbidden?** The command in verse 17 does not forbid marbling or streaks of fat within lean meat (3:17). Verses 3–4 and 9–10 specify the thick, separable layers of fat surrounding the internal organs, kidneys, and sheep's tail—the exact portions dedicated to the altar (3:3–4; 3:9–10). Ancient Jewish interpreters likewise understood the prohibition to apply specifically to the fat of the three sacrificial species: cattle, sheep, and goats.

**Why does Scripture call the portion burned on the altar "food"?** The Hebrew term literally means *bread* or *food* (3:11; 3:16)—rendered in some older versions with archaic words for provisions. It is the vivid metaphor of a covenant meal: the LORD and his people are pictured sharing a common table in peace.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, maintains that the ban on eating fat and blood was not a health diet, but a daily discipline in reverence. As Israelites continually held back their hands from the portions set apart for the altar, they were reminded day by day that they lived under God's covenant. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on the peace offering)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading dietary commands as training in obedience and reverence is characteristic of Calvin; exact location not verified -->
