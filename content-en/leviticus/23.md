# Leviticus 23 — The Appointed Feasts of the LORD

## Story

Whereas earlier chapters consecrated space (the Tabernacle) and people (Israel and its priests), chapter 23 consecrates *time*. The LORD reveals to Moses the annual calendar of festivals, calling them "the appointed feasts of the LORD"—*sacred assemblies* when the entire nation pauses daily work to worship him together (23:1–2).

The bedrock of the entire liturgical calendar is the weekly Sabbath: six days for work, and the seventh day a Sabbath of complete rest in every home (23:3). Upon that weekly rhythm, God sets seven annual feasts grouped into two seasons: spring and autumn.

Spring opens in the first month with three closely linked celebrations. At twilight on the fourteenth day comes Passover, remembering the night God rescued Israel from Egypt. The very next day, the fifteenth, launches the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread, with sacred assemblies and rest from work on its first and seventh days (23:4–8). During that week, on the day after the Sabbath, the people bring a sheaf of the firstfruits of the harvest for the priest to wave before the LORD alongside burnt, grain, and drink offerings; until that day, no one may eat bread or grain from the new crop (23:9–14).

Counting seven full weeks—fifty days—from the day of the firstfruits sheaf brings Israel to the fourth festival (later called Pentecost or the Feast of Weeks). They present two leavened loaves baked from the new wheat harvest together with burnt, sin, and peace offerings, halting work for a sacred assembly. Right in the middle of this harvest celebration, God inserts a touching reminder: do not reap to the very edges of your fields or pick up fallen gleanings; leave them for the poor and the foreigner (23:15–22).

Autumn arrives in the seventh month with three solemn and joyous festivals crowning the agricultural year. The first day of the seventh month is the Feast of Trumpets, a day of rest and sacred assembly heralded by trumpet blasts (23:23–25). The tenth day is the Day of Atonement, a day of absolute rest when everyone fasts and humbles themselves from the evening of the ninth to the evening of the tenth to be cleansed before the LORD (23:26–32). Finally, beginning on the fifteenth day of the seventh month come the seven days of the Feast of Tabernacles (plus a solemn eighth day), celebrating the completion of the harvest. Gathering choice fruit, palm branches, leafy boughs, and willows, the people "rejoice before the LORD your God for seven days," and native Israelites live in temporary shelters so future generations will never forget how God housed and cared for their ancestors in booths after bringing them out of Egypt (23:33–44).

## Key Takeaways

**Time belongs to God, and human beings need regular pauses to remember who they are.** The weekly Sabbath and seven yearly festivals pulled Israel out of the endless grind of production. Those days of rest reminded them they were no longer slaves churning out bricks nonstop in Egypt, but a free people belonging to God (23:3; 23:43).

**Every festival weaves together gratitude for creation and memory of redemption.** Each feast aligns with the farming calendar (early barley, wheat harvest, final grape and fruit harvest) while simultaneously anchoring Israel in God's historical rescue from Egypt and wilderness care (23:5–6; 23:10; 23:42–43). The God who feeds the earth is the very same God who redeems history.

**Joyful worship cannot be divorced from sharing with the poor.** Re-inserting the gleaning law for the poor and foreigner straight into the Feast of Weeks instructions (23:22) sends an unmistakable message: thanksgiving for an abundant harvest must overflow into openhanded care for those who own no land.

**A life of faith embraces both solemn repentance and wholehearted celebration.** Just five days apart in the seventh month, Israel moves from fasting and self-humbling on the Day of Atonement into seven days of singing, dwelling in booths, and rejoicing before God at Tabernacles (23:27; 23:40). True godliness is never perpetual gloom; forgiven sin opens the door to deep joy.

## Deeper Meaning

**The four spring festivals find step-by-step fulfillment in Christ's death, resurrection, and Pentecost.** Looking back at Leviticus 23, the New Testament discovers an astonishing pattern. Jesus dies at Passover, becoming our Passover Lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7). Unleavened Bread becomes a call to live a new life purified of malice and wickedness (1 Corinthians 5:8). Jesus rises on *the day after the Sabbath*—the exact day of the firstfruits sheaf—leading Paul to proclaim him "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). And exactly fifty days later, on the Day of Pentecost, the Holy Spirit descends to launch the church (Acts 2:1–4).

**Jesus stands at the Feast of Tabernacles promising living water.** In John's Gospel, Jesus travels to Jerusalem for this very Feast of Tabernacles. On the last and greatest day of the festival, he stands up and cries out that anyone who thirsts should come to him and drink, so streams of living water will flow from within them (John 7:2; 7:37–38).

**The autumn festivals look forward to the final consummation.** Colossians declares that festivals and Sabbaths are "a shadow of the things that were to come; the reality, however, is found in Christ" (Colossians 2:16–17). Many interpreters see in the trumpet blast (Feast of Trumpets), final cleansing (Day of Atonement), and God pitching his tent among humanity (Feast of Tabernacles) a portrait pointing toward Christ's return and the new heaven and new earth, where God's dwelling stands with his people forever (1 Thessalonians 4:16; Revelation 21:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**"Appointed feasts" (23:2) literally means *set appointments*.** The underlying Hebrew word does not simply denote banquets; it means *scheduled meeting times*—as though God has penciled regular dates into the calendar all year long so his people pause together to meet with him.

**Why are the Pentecost loaves baked with leaven (23:17) when Unleavened Bread forbids it?** Leviticus 2:11 forbids burning leavened bread on the altar fire, yet the two Pentecost loaves were never burned on the altar; they were presented as a *wave offering* and given to the priests to eat (23:20). They represented the finest everyday loaves baked from the new wheat harvest in thanksgiving to God.

**These festivals still shape the Jewish year today.** Passover (*Pesach*), Pentecost (*Shavuot*), the Feast of Trumpets (now known as *Rosh Hashanah*, the Jewish New Year), the Day of Atonement (*Yom Kippur*), and Tabernacles (*Sukkot*) continue to be observed worldwide every year along the very first-month and seventh-month calendar set out in Leviticus 23.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Puritan pastor, compares the festival calendar of Leviticus 23 to a map of grace training God's people to use time in grateful memory of their Creator and Redeemer. **Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, traces the number seven woven throughout the structure of chapter 23: the seventh day is Sabbath, seven weeks lead to Pentecost, the seventh month gathers three major feasts, and both Unleavened Bread and Tabernacles span seven days.
