# Leviticus 4 — The Sin Offering

## Story

The first three chapters describe voluntary offerings; chapter 4 introduces the *sin offering*—a mandatory sacrifice whenever someone "sins unintentionally against any of the LORD's commandments" (4:1–2). Unlike high-handed, defiant rebellion against God, these are sins committed through carelessness, ignorance, or human weakness that must be purified and forgiven once they come to light.

The law divides these cases into four tiers according to the offender's influence in the community, moving from highest to lowest. First is the anointed priest—the high priest—whose sin brings guilt upon the entire people. Second is the whole congregation of Israel sinning unintentionally (4:3; 4:13). In both of these grave cases, the offering must be a young bull without blemish. The priest himself, or the elders of the congregation, lay their hands on the bull's head and slaughter it. The priest brings some of the blood inside the Tent of Meeting, dips his finger to sprinkle it seven times before the veil of the sanctuary, applies blood to the horns of the altar of incense, and pours out the rest at the base of the bronze altar outside. The bull's fat is burned on the altar just as in the peace offering, while the entire remaining carcass—hide, flesh, head, legs, and entrails—must be carried outside the camp to the clean place where the ashes are poured out and burned completely (4:4–12; 4:14–21).

The next two cases are less severe: when a leader sins, he brings a male goat; when an ordinary member of the community sins, he or she brings a female goat or a female lamb (4:22–23; 4:27–28; 4:32). The procedure is similar—laying on of hands, slaughtering the animal, burning the fat on the altar—except the blood is not taken inside the Tent of Meeting. Instead, it is placed on the horns of the altar of burnt offering in the courtyard, with the remainder poured at its base (4:24–26; 4:29–35). After each rite, the chapter repeats the same comforting assurance: the priest shall make atonement for that person, "and he will be forgiven" (4:26; 4:31; 4:35).

## Key Takeaways

**Greater office carries heavier responsibility.** When an ordinary citizen sins, a female goat or lamb suffices. When a leader sins, a male goat is required. Yet when the high priest sins, the sacrifice is as weighty as one for the entire nation: a young bull, with blood carried deep inside the Tent of Meeting (4:3; 4:13–14). A leader's failure does not harm only himself; it defiles the whole community.

**Unintentional sin is still real sin.** Many assume that if an offense was accidental or committed in ignorance, it scarcely counts. Chapter 4 teaches the opposite: even unwitting sin fractures fellowship with God and pollutes his sanctuary, and therefore must be confronted and cleansed by atoning blood (4:2; 4:13–14).

**Blood cleanses the sanctuary.** What stands out most in the sin offering is the ritual use of blood: sprinkled seven times before the veil, smeared on the horns of the incense altar or the burnt-offering altar (4:6–7; 4:25). Human sin acts like a stain clinging to the very place where God dwells among his people; without cleansing, the Holy One cannot remain in their midst.

**God holds open the doorway of forgiveness.** The refrain "and he will be forgiven" rings throughout the chapter (4:20; 4:26; 4:31; 4:35). The law of Leviticus is exacting, never cold: the moment sin comes to light and the offender draws near in God's appointed way, forgiveness is assured.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus dies outside the city like the sin-offering bull.** The bull offered for the sin of the whole congregation was carried outside the camp and burned (4:11–12; 4:21). The Letter to the Hebrews cites this very detail to explain why Jesus was crucified outside the gate of Jerusalem: he suffered outside the camp to sanctify the people through his own blood (Hebrews 13:11–12).

**Christ's blood enters the true Most Holy Place in heaven.** Under the old law, priests had to carry the blood of bulls and goats into the Tent of Meeting again and again for every unwitting offense. Hebrews reveals that Christ entered the heavenly sanctuary once for all, not by the blood of animals but by his own blood, securing eternal redemption and cleansing the human conscience (Hebrews 9:11–14; 10:1–4).

**Even deliberate sin is not beyond the grace of the cross.** Old Testament law distinguished between unintentional sins—covered by the sin offering here—and defiant, high-handed rebellion (Numbers 15:30–31). Under the New Covenant, however, the blood of Jesus cleanses from every sin those who honestly confess and repent (1 John 1:7–9), and as our sinless High Priest, Christ never needs to offer a sacrifice for his own sins as Aaron did (Hebrews 7:27).

## Notes & Nuances

**The sin offering is also called the *purification offering*.** Many scholars today prefer the rendering *purification offering*, since the blood is not sprinkled on the sinner but applied to the veil and the horns of the altars (4:6–7; 4:25). It both secures forgiveness for the worshiper and scrubs away the defilement that sin leaves upon the Tabernacle.

**Which veil is meant in verse 6 (4:6)?** It is the inner curtain separating the Holy Place (where the altar of incense stood) from the Most Holy Place (where the Ark of the Covenant rested). Standing on the outer side of that curtain, the priest sprinkled blood seven times toward it. The number seven in Scripture regularly signifies completeness.

**What happens to the meat of the leader's or commoner's goat or lamb?** Chapter 4 states only that the fat is burned on the altar, without saying where the meat goes. Later, in chapter 6 verse 26, the law clarifies that whenever a sin offering's blood is not brought inside the Tent of Meeting, the priest eats its flesh in the holy place (6:26).

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, remarks that requiring the exact same sacrifice for the high priest as for the entire nation stands as a sober warning to all church leaders. The sin of a spiritual teacher easily causes many others to stumble and can never be taken lightly. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Leviticus 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing the heavy responsibility of the high priest compared to ordinary people is characteristic of Matthew Henry on chapter 4; exact wording not verified -->

**Jacob Milgrom**, Jewish scholar specializing in Leviticus, is famous for interpreting this sacrifice as a *purification offering* for the sanctuary itself. He compares Israel's sins to airborne pollution clinging from afar to the Tabernacle; unless purged with blood, God's glory would be forced to depart from his dwelling. The graver the sin and the higher the offender's rank, the deeper the stain penetrates into the sanctuary, demanding that blood be carried further inside. *(Leviticus 1–16, Anchor Bible, 1991, on chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the model of graduated sanctuary defilement penetrating deeper according to offense and rank is Milgrom's classic thesis on Leviticus 4 -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, adds that even while highlighting sanctuary purification, the sin offering remains tightly bound to personal forgiveness—as proven by the repeated refrain "he will be forgiven" throughout the chapter.
