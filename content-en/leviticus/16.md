# Leviticus 16 — The Day of Atonement

## Story

Chapter 16 opens with a sobering reminder: immediately after Aaron's two sons Nadab and Abihu died for approaching God improperly, the LORD tells Moses to warn Aaron. Not even the high priest may stroll at will into the Most Holy Place behind the veil, in front of the mercy seat atop the Ark of the Testimony, for the LORD appears there in the cloud. Anyone who enters uninvited will die (16:1–2).

Once a year—and only once—Aaron may enter that inner room, following an exacting sequence. First he bathes his body in water, then sets aside his resplendent everyday vestments to put on simple white linen garments: linen tunic, undergarments, sash, and turban. He prepares a young bull as a sin offering and a ram as a burnt offering for himself and his household, and receives from the assembly two male goats as a sin offering and a ram as a burnt offering for the nation (16:3–5).

At the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, Aaron casts lots over the two goats: "one lot for the LORD and the other for Azazel." The goat chosen for the LORD will be slain as a sin offering; the goat chosen for Azazel is kept alive to carry Israel's sins away into the wilderness (16:6–10).

As the ceremony begins, Aaron first slaughters the bull to make atonement for himself and his household. Taking a censer full of glowing coals from the altar along with two handfuls of finely ground fragrant incense, he passes behind the veil. He places the incense on the fire so a thick cloud of smoke conceals the mercy seat, sparing his life. Then he sprinkles some of the bull's blood onto the mercy seat and seven times in front of it (16:11–14).

Next, he slaughters the LORD's goat, carries its blood behind the veil, and sprinkles it just as he did with the bull's blood. In this way he makes atonement for the Holy Place and the Tent of Meeting—defiled by standing amid a sinful, unclean people. Throughout this entire rite, no one else may be inside the Tent of Meeting. Stepping back out, he applies blood from both sacrifices to the four horns of the bronze altar and sprinkles the altar seven times to purify it (16:15–19).

With the sanctuary cleansed, Aaron summons the live goat. Laying both hands firmly on its head, he confesses over it all the iniquities, rebellions, and sins of Israel, symbolically placing every offense onto the animal's head. A designated man immediately leads the goat deep into a solitary wilderness, so that it "shall bear on itself all their iniquities into a solitary land" (16:20–22).

Aaron re-enters the Tent of Meeting, takes off his white linen garments and leaves them there, bathes, puts back on his regular vestments, and comes out to offer the burnt offerings for himself and the people, burning the fat of the sin offerings on the altar. Both the man who led the scapegoat into the wilderness and the man who burned the carcasses of the bull and goat outside the camp must wash their clothes and bathe before re-entering the camp (16:23–28).

God establishes this as a lasting statute on the tenth day of the seventh month every year. Both native Israelites and resident foreigners must cease all work and *humble themselves* through fasting and contrite repentance. It is a solemn Sabbath of complete rest so that the sanctuary, the priests, and the whole congregation may be cleansed from all their sins before the LORD (16:29–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Sin blocks the road to a holy God, yet God himself opens a way.** The inner veil and the lethal warning at the start of the chapter prove that humanity cannot barge casually into God's presence. Still, God does not bolt the door forever; he himself institutes the Day of Atonement so his people can keep living before him (16:2; 16:30).

**Even the highest spiritual representative needs forgiveness first.** Aaron does not enter the Most Holy Place in glittering royal robes, but in plain white servant's linen, and his very first act is sacrificing for his own sins and those of his family (16:4; 16:11). No human priest is sinless.

**Sin must both be cleansed by blood and carried far away.** The two goats portray two sides of one complete forgiveness. The slain goat's blood purifies the sanctuary and satisfies justice before God; the goat sent into the wilderness lets the people see with their own eyes that their sins have been removed from the community, never to linger among them again (16:15; 16:21–22).

**Forgiveness calls for genuine repentance.** Although the high priest enters the Tent alone, the people may not watch passively. Everyone must lay down daily business and humble themselves in fasting before God, owning their deep need for his mercy (16:29–31).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus is a High Priest far superior to Aaron.** Hebrews reads all of Leviticus 16 as a living blueprint of Christ's saving work. Aaron had to repeat this rite year after year, sacrifice first for his own sins, and enter an earthly tent built by human hands. By contrast, Christ is sinless, offered himself once for all, and entered the true heavenly sanctuary to secure eternal redemption for us (Hebrews 9:7–12; 9:24–26; 10:1–12).

**The veil is torn and a living way stands open.** In Leviticus 16, only one man could cross the veil once a year, shielded by dense incense smoke lest he die. When Jesus breathes his last on the cross, the temple curtain tears in two from top to bottom (Matthew 27:51). Through his blood, every believer can now step boldly into the Most Holy Place by a new and living way (Hebrews 10:19–20).

**The sacrifice burned outside the camp.** The detail that the carcasses of the sin-offering bull and goat were carried outside the camp to be burned (16:27) is tied directly by Hebrews to Jesus suffering outside the gate of Jerusalem. He bore our reproach outside the city to make his people holy (Hebrews 13:11–13).

**The goat bearing sin far away.** The live goat driven into the remote wilderness carrying Israel's iniquities foreshadows the Lamb who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29) and the psalmist's promise that as far as the east is from the west, so far has God removed our transgressions from us (Psalms 103:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does the word *Azazel* mean (16:8)?** It is one of the most debated words in the Old Testament, with three main explanations. One translates it as *the goat sent away* (giving rise to the English word *scapegoat*). A second views it as the name of a rugged desert cliff or wilderness region. A third sees Azazel as a desert demon or evil spirit standing opposite the LORD—not receiving a sacrifice from Israel, but having sin banished back to its own dark realm. Whichever view one takes, the ritual's core message is unmistakable: sin is completely expelled from the camp of God's people.

**Why do the sanctuary and altar need *atonement*?** Lifeless objects do not sin, yet in Leviticus the people's sins and impurities accumulate throughout the year, staining the house where God dwells among them. Making atonement for the sanctuary here means purging the worship space so the holy God can continue residing with his people (16:16–19).

**This remains the holiest day on the Jewish calendar.** The tenth day of the seventh month is *Yom Kippur* (the Day of Atonement). Since the Jerusalem temple was destroyed in AD 70 and sacrifices ceased, Jewish communities observe the day with a full-day fast, prayer, and confession in the synagogue.

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, is deeply moved by the two goats of Leviticus 16 because together they paint the full cross of Christ. No single animal could display the whole mystery of atonement: the slain goat shows the blood price paid to satisfy God's justice, while the live goat banished into the wilderness shows that the believer's sins have vanished forever from God's sight, never to return and accuse them. *(The Scapegoat, Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's famous sermon The Scapegoat on Leviticus 16 centers squarely on the complementary work of the two goats foreshadowing the cross -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, draws attention to Aaron laying aside his splendid robes for plain white linen before entering the Most Holy Place. This rite, he notes, warned the high priest against pride before God, calling him to enter humbly as a sinner needing cleansing. **Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, regards Leviticus 16 as the climax and structural center of the entire book of Leviticus—indeed of the whole Pentateuch. Whereas chapters 1 through 15 address individual sins and impurities piecemeal, chapter 16 resolves once for the entire community how a sinful people can go on dwelling safely alongside a holy God. *(The Book of Leviticus, NICOT, 1979)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: identifying chapter 16 as the climax and structural centerpiece of Leviticus is a widely cited hallmark of Wenham's commentary -->

**Jacob Milgrom**, Jewish scholar specializing in Leviticus, reads the blood-sprinkling ceremony of chapter 16 as an annual spiritual spring-cleaning of the Tabernacle.
