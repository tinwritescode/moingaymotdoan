# Leviticus 20 — Penalties for Grave Sins and the Call to Holiness

## Story

Whereas chapter 18 listed forbidden practices in worship and family life, chapter 20 revisits many of those same offenses to establish explicit penalties and define the community's shared responsibility to preserve holiness.

The first group of offenses concerns pagan worship and family authority. Any Israelite or resident foreigner who gives a child in sacrifice to Molech must be stoned to death by the people of the land. If the community turns a blind eye, the LORD himself will set his face against that person and his clan and cut them off from the people. Anyone who turns to mediums and spiritists will likewise be cut off by God; and the closing verse stipulates that any man or woman practicing as a medium or spiritist must be stoned (20:1–6; 20:27). Right after the call to consecrate themselves and be holy, the law prescribes the death penalty for anyone who curses father or mother (20:7–9).

The second group covers severe violations of marriage and sexuality, ordered in descending severity of penalty. The gravest carry the death penalty: adultery with another man's wife, relations with a stepmother or daughter-in-law, a man lying with a male as with a woman, marrying both a woman and her mother (punished by burning), and bestiality (20:10–16). Lesser yet severe offenses incur being cut off from the people: incest between brother and sister, or sexual relations during a woman's monthly period (20:17–18). Finally come cases where offenders *bear their guilt* or *die childless*—left to direct divine judgment—for relations with an aunt or sister-in-law (20:19–21).

God ends the chapter with an earnest appeal. Israel must keep his decrees so the land will not vomit them out like the previous nations. The LORD himself has gifted them "a land flowing with milk and honey" and set them apart from the nations; therefore, from major moral boundaries right down to distinguishing clean from unclean food every day, they must live distinctly: "You are to be holy to Me because I, the LORD, am holy, and I have set you apart from the nations to be My own" (20:22–26).

## Key Takeaways

**God himself is the One who makes his people holy.** Amid stark judicial penalties, verse 8 lays down a crucial foundation of grace: God does not merely command *be holy*, he identifies himself as "the LORD, who sanctifies you" (20:8). Holiness is a grateful response to the God who first chose and separated his people for himself (20:24; 20:26).

**A community may never look away from evil.** A striking feature at the opening of the chapter is God's warning aimed at those who *close their eyes* when someone sacrifices children to Molech (20:4–5). In the face of brutal evil that crushes human life and profanes God's name, numb silence is not tolerance—it is complicity.

**Not all sins are equal, and penalties are carefully graded.** Although every offense listed is condemned, chapter 20 arranges three distinct tiers of accountability: capital punishment carried out by the community for crimes that strike at the root of life and marriage; exclusion from the covenant community; and cases left directly to God's own judgment (*dying childless*, *bearing their guilt*) (20:10–21).

**False worship and moral collapse always walk together.** Grouping child sacrifice and occult mediums right beside incest and adultery shows that in Scripture's eyes, how human beings worship God directly shapes how they treat parents, spouses, and children (20:2–10).

## Deeper Meaning

**Ancient Israel's civil death penalty gives way to redemptive church discipline in the New Testament.** Old Testament Israel was a theocratic nation exercising civil criminal jurisdiction. The New Testament church does not bear the sword and executes no one. Confronting a case of incest matching Leviticus 20:11 in Corinth, Paul never calls for stoning; instead he instructs the church to remove the offender from fellowship—with the ultimate hope that he will repent and be saved (1 Corinthians 5:1–5; 5:13; cf. 2 Corinthians 2:6–8).

**Jesus shields the woman caught in adultery from hypocritical fury.** When scribes and Pharisees drag a woman caught in adultery before Jesus and cite Moses' law demanding stoning (20:10; cf. Deuteronomy 22:22), Jesus neither excuses sin nor condones their sham justice—they brought only the woman while ignoring the man, though Leviticus 20:10 explicitly required both to stand trial. He rescues her life with grace, then charges her: go, and sin no more (John 8:3–11).

**Complete holiness through Christ.** The charge *be holy* and the title *the LORD who sanctifies you* (20:8; 20:26) come to full flower in the New Testament: believers have been made holy through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all, and are urged to pursue holiness, without which no one will see the Lord (Hebrews 10:10; 12:14).

## Notes & Nuances

**How should modern readers approach the severe death penalties in Leviticus 20?** Modern readers understandably feel jarred seeing capital punishment listed for cursing parents, adultery, or same-sex acts (20:9–13). Commentators typically offer three essential points of context. First, these were civil statutes for an ancient Bronze Age theocratic state, never a criminal code for modern governments or Christian churches. Second, scholars note that in actual Jewish legal practice (preserved in the Talmud), evidentiary standards for execution were so stringent that capital sentences were rarely carried out, meaning the maximum penalties primarily functioned as solemn deterrents marking how gravely sin offends God. Third, regarding verse 13 specifically, the same debate between traditional and historical-contextual readings seen at Leviticus 18:22 applies here (20:13); nevertheless, virtually all Christian traditions today firmly reject applying Old Testament capital penalties.

**What does "their blood is upon them" mean?** It is an ancient legal formula (20:9; 20:11–13; 20:16; 20:27) declaring that offenders bring death upon themselves by knowingly violating an explicit statute, clearing the community that executes the sentence from bloodguilt.

**How is dying *childless* understood (20:20–21)?** Two main explanations exist: either God directly strikes the couple with barrenness so no heirs carry on their name, or any children born from the unlawful union are legally denied recognition as legitimate heirs of the offender's clan.
