# Leviticus 6 — Restitution to Neighbors and Laws for the Priests

## Story

Chapter 6 continues directly from the guilt offering at the close of chapter 5, turning from offenses against sacred property to dishonesty toward fellow Israelites. Anyone who deceives a neighbor about something entrusted to his care, steals, extorts, lies about lost property he found, or swears falsely to defraud someone is plainly charged with "unfaithfulness against the LORD" (6:1–3). The offender must return what was taken in full, add a fifth of its value directly to the rightful owner on the very day he presents his offering, and then bring a ram without blemish to the priest to make atonement and receive forgiveness (6:4–7).

Beginning at verse 8, the book shifts perspective: whereas the first five chapters instructed *the people* what to bring, the rest of chapter 6 and all of chapter 7 give specialized instructions to *Aaron and his sons* on how to handle each sacrifice (6:8).

First comes the law of the burnt offering: the sacrifice stays burning on the altar all night until morning. At dawn, the priest puts on his linen garments, removes the ashes and places them beside the altar, then changes into ordinary clothes to carry the ashes to a clean place outside the camp. One command is repeated three times: "The fire on the altar shall be kept burning; it must not go out" (6:8–13).

Next is the law of the grain offering: after burning a handful of flour, oil, and frankincense as a memorial, the unleavened remainder belongs to the male priests and must be eaten inside the courtyard of the Tent of Meeting (6:14–18). By contrast, the daily grain offering brought by the high priest himself—half in the morning and half in the evening—starting from the day of his anointing must be completely burned, with none of it eaten (6:19–23).

The chapter closes with the law of the sin offering: its flesh is "most holy" and must be eaten by the male priests in the courtyard of the Tent of Meeting. If any of its blood spatters on a garment, that garment must be washed in a holy place; an earthen vessel used to boil the meat must be broken, while a bronze vessel must be scoured and rinsed thoroughly with water. However, if the sin offering's blood was carried inside the Tent of Meeting (as for the anointed priest or the whole assembly in chapter 4), no one may eat it—it must be burned completely with fire (6:24–30).

## Key Takeaways

**Deceiving a neighbor is sinning against God.** The opening of chapter 6 speaks bluntly: theft, extortion, or lying about a found item is not merely a private civil dispute—it is "unfaithfulness against the LORD" (6:2). No one can cheat a neighbor and pretend to be on good terms with God.

**Make it right with your neighbor first, then bring your offering.** The wrongdoer had to restore the full amount plus twenty percent to the victim on the very day of the sacrifice (6:5). Religious ritual must never serve as a screen to hide uncorrected injustice.

**The fire on the altar must never go out.** The charge to keep the altar fire continually burning is sounded three times in just five short verses (6:9; 6:12–13). Morning by morning, the priest had to clear the ashes and lay fresh wood. Worship of God and the way of atonement stood continually open, day and night, without pause.

**Holiness is serious business, never to be handled carelessly.** Changing garments to carry ashes outside the camp, washing blood-spattered cloth in a holy place, and breaking clay pots used for most-holy meat (6:10–11; 6:27–28) all trained the priests to maintain reverence even in routine daily chores.

## Deeper Meaning

**Zacchaeus brings the restitution law of chapter 6 to life.** Upon meeting Jesus, the wealthy tax collector Zacchaeus pledges to pay back fourfold anyone he has defrauded—far exceeding the twenty percent penalty of Leviticus (Luke 19:8). Jesus likewise teaches clearly in the Sermon on the Mount: if you are offering your gift at the altar and remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there, go first to be reconciled, and then come back to offer your gift (Matthew 5:23–24).

**The unquenched fire points to unceasing intercession.** The burnt offering smoldering through the night and the high priest's morning-and-evening grain offering (6:9; 6:20) reflect humanity's continual need for intercession. Hebrews assures us that Jesus is the High Priest who always lives to intercede for those who draw near to God through him (Hebrews 7:25; 9:24).

**Priests cannot eat their own sin offerings.** The rules barring the priest from eating his own grain offering (6:23) and forbidding anyone to eat a sin offering whose blood entered the sanctuary (6:30) remind us that human priests were themselves sinners unable to save themselves. Only the sinless Christ could offer one complete sacrifice that takes away the sin of humanity (Hebrews 7:26–28; 10:11–14).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does chapter 6 start at verse 8 in the Hebrew Bible?** In the Hebrew Scriptures, the first seven verses here (6:1–7) form the end of chapter 5—verses 20 through 26—because they continue the topic of the guilt offering. English Bibles follow the chapter divisions of the ancient Latin Vulgate, shifting them to the start of chapter 6. If a commentary cites verses 20–26 of chapter 5, it is referring to these very verses (6:1–7).

**What does "whatever touches it shall become holy" mean (6:18; 6:27)?** Here *holy* does not mean becoming morally virtuous by contact. In ancient ritual terms, touching something *most holy* brought an object or unauthorized person into the restricted sacred sphere so it could no longer return to ordinary use—which is why porous clay pots absorbing sacred broth had to be broken, while nonporous bronze pots only needed thorough scouring and rinsing (6:28).

**Why change clothes just to carry out ashes?** The priest wore sacred linen vestments while serving at the altar, then changed into everyday clothes to carry the ashes outside the camp (6:10–11). This kept a crisp boundary between the consecrated zone of the Tabernacle and ordinary life outside, while also reminding the priests that even clearing away ashes was part of sacred service.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, lingers over the command to keep the fire burning constantly on the altar. He applies the picture to prayer and love for God: the sacred flame in a believer's heart will not keep burning on its own unless we clear away daily ashes and lay on fresh fuel through Scripture and prayer. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Leviticus 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearing ashes and adding fuel to maintain spiritual devotion is a well-known application by Matthew Henry on chapter 6; exact wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, points out that classifying fraud against a neighbor as *unfaithfulness against the LORD* (6:2) shatters the habit of separating social ethics from piety. He also views the priests' eating the meat of the sin offering as a sign that they bore the burden of interceding for the people before God. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on Leviticus 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: uniting justice toward neighbors with fear of God is a consistent point in Calvin; exact location not verified -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, renowned nineteenth-century London preacher, preached on verse 13—"The fire shall be kept burning on the altar continually; it must not go out"—to stir the church to maintain unwavering zeal in worship and service (6:13). He noted that God himself lit that fire at the beginning (9:24), yet he entrusted the responsibility of keeping it alive to his servants. *(Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon preached a well-known sermon on the continual altar fire in Leviticus 6:13; exact sermon number not recalled -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, explains that clay pots used to boil sin-offering meat had to be smashed because unglazed earthenware is porous and absorbs sacred broth beyond complete washing, whereas solid bronze can simply be scoured and rinsed (6:28).
