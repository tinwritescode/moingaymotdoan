# Leviticus 11 — Clean and Unclean Animals

## Story

With chapters 11 through 15, Leviticus turns to the laws of *clean* and *unclean*, equipping the priests for the task just assigned to them in chapter 10 verse 10: distinguishing clean from unclean (10:10). The section begins right at Israel's daily table. The LORD speaks to both Moses and Aaron, classifying creatures across four main habitats (11:1–2).

First are *land animals*: Israel may eat only creatures that both have a completely divided hoof and chew the cud (such as cattle, sheep, goats, and deer). Animals having only one of these two traits—such as the camel, rock badger, and hare (which chew the cud without divided hooves) or the pig (which has divided hooves without chewing the cud)—along with animals that walk on paws are unclean; Israel may neither eat their meat nor touch their carcasses (11:3–8; 11:26–28).

Second are *water creatures*: Israel may eat only fish possessing both fins and scales; aquatic creatures lacking fins and scales (such as shrimp, crabs, shellfish, eels, and catfish) are off-limits (11:9–12). Third are *birds*: rather than giving a general trait, the law lists roughly twenty prohibited species, mostly birds of prey, scavengers, or nocturnal creatures (such as eagles, ravens, hawks, owls, and bats) (11:13–19). Fourth are *insects and creeping things*: winged insects walking on all fours are forbidden, except four families with jointed hind legs for hopping such as locusts and grasshoppers; meanwhile, all small swarming ground creatures (such as mice, lizards, geckos, and skinks) are unclean (11:20–23; 11:29–30; 11:41–43).

The second half of the chapter addresses defilement from *animal carcasses*. Anyone touching the carcass of an unclean animal—or of a clean animal that died naturally—is unclean until evening; carrying its carcass or eating its meat also requires washing one's clothes (11:24–25; 11:39–40). If the carcass of a small creeping creature falls on wood, cloth, or leather, the item must be put in water and stays unclean until evening; if it falls into an earthen pot or clay oven, the object must be broken. Yet the law stays practical: a spring, a cistern holding water, and dry planting seed remain clean, unless water has already been poured on the seed when a carcass falls upon it (11:31–38).

At the close of the chapter, the LORD states plainly the deeper goal behind the entire dietary code—not culinary taste, but the identity of a redeemed people: "For I am the LORD, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt so that I would be your God; therefore be holy, because I am holy" (11:44–47).

## Key Takeaways

**Living distinctly starts right at the dinner table.** Three times a day, the food laws reminded Israelites that they were a people rescued from Egypt and set apart for God (11:45). Holiness was not confined to festival days at the Tabernacle; it reached straight into the kitchen and dining table.

**God calls his people to reflect his own character.** The keynote of this chapter—and of the whole book of Leviticus—rings out twice in succession: "be holy, because I am holy" (11:44–45). Children of the Holy One are called to live with order, boundaries, and undivided allegiance to their Father.

**Defilement from carcasses concerns death, not moral guilt.** Accidentally touching a dead animal or sweeping out a dead lizard was no wicked sin; it was contact with the realm of death, the stark opposite of the living God. Washing clothes, rinsing items, and waiting until evening kept clear the boundary between life and death while promoting basic hygiene in the camp (11:31–40).

**Strict laws remain grounded in common sense.** A dead mouse falling into a clay jar meant breaking the jar, yet falling into a flowing spring or a heap of dry seed grain did not ruin the village's water supply or next season's harvest (11:33; 11:36–37). God did not impose rigid rules that paralyzed daily livelihoods.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus unlocks the true heart of purity in Mark 7.** Over the centuries, many Jews focused so tightly on external food rules that they neglected the inner heart. In the Gospel of Mark, Jesus declares plainly that nothing outside a person can defile them by going into them; rather, the evil coming out of the human heart is what defiles—and by saying this, he declared all foods clean (Mark 7:18–23).

**Peter's vision of the sheet in Acts 10 tears down ethnic walls.** Just as the gospel is about to enter the home of Cornelius, a Roman centurion, the apostle Peter sees a great sheet lowered from heaven filled with four-footed animals, reptiles, and birds—the very creatures barred in Leviticus 11—and hears a voice telling him to kill and eat. Peter protests that he has never eaten anything impure or unclean, yet the Lord replies: do not call anything impure that God has made clean (Acts 10:9–16). Moments later Peter grasps the deeper meaning beyond food: the barrier separating Jews from Gentiles has now been lifted in Christ (Acts 10:28; 10:34–35).

**The call to holiness never expires in the New Testament.** Abstaining from pork or scaleless fish fulfilled its purpose of marking Israel out under the Old Covenant (Colossians 2:16–17; 1 Timothy 4:3–5). Yet the heart of Leviticus 11:44–45 remains timeless: the apostle Peter quotes those exact words to urge today's church to be holy in all they do (1 Peter 1:15–16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Were these animals banned solely for health reasons?** Many try to explain the chapter purely through modern medicine (pork can carry parasites, scavengers carry disease). Practical hygiene played a helpful side role, yet chapter 11 itself never cites health; Scripture states its purpose as *holiness* and *distinction* (11:44–47). Moreover, if health were the sole reason, Jesus and the apostles would hardly have lifted these food bans in the New Testament.

**Why does Leviticus say the hare "chews the cud" (11:6) and include the bat among birds (11:19)?** Scripture uses the ordinary observational language of the ancient world, not modern biological taxonomy. Ancient people grouped all winged flying creatures together as *birds* (hence including bats), and described the hare's constant chewing motion (actually re-chewing partially digested pellets) as *chewing the cud*.

**Locusts and grasshoppers were permitted food (11:22).** This detail explains how John the Baptist could live in the wilderness eating locusts and wild honey (Matthew 3:4) while remaining completely faithful to the Law of Moses.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, suggests three reasons behind the dietary laws of chapter 11: to train Israel in obeying God even in everyday matters, to keep them separated from the dining habits and idolatry of neighboring nations, and to serve as object lessons warning believers away from corrupting habits in moral life. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Leviticus 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the threefold purpose of training obedience, maintaining separation, and teaching moral lessons is typical of Matthew Henry on chapter 11; exact wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, insists that every creature God made is good in itself (Genesis 1:31). God temporarily barred certain animals in Leviticus 11 to school Israel like young children in self-restraint and covenant identity until Christ arrived to bring the church into mature freedom. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on Leviticus 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin regularly treats the food laws as temporary pedagogy for Israel prior to Christ's coming; exact location not verified -->

**Mary Douglas**, influential twentieth-century British anthropologist, proposed a landmark interpretation of Leviticus 11. In her view, *holiness* is tied to *wholeness and proper order* within creation: standard land animals have split hooves and chew the cud, standard water creatures have fins and scales; animals that appear anomalous or fail to match the norm of their realm were classed as unclean, reminding Israel to keep wholeness without mixture. *(Purity and Danger, 1966, chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Mary Douglas's famous thesis on taxonomic order in chapter 3 of Purity and Danger is cited in nearly all modern commentaries on Leviticus 11 -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, adds a historical layer rooted in Israel's calling: the animal classifications of chapter 11 mirror humanity itself—Israel was chosen from among the nations just as clean animals were singled out from among all creatures. Thus, when Peter saw the vision of unclean animals in Acts 10, he immediately recognized that God was speaking about welcoming Gentiles into the church. *(The Book of Leviticus, NICOT, 1979, on chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Wenham combines Mary Douglas's analysis with Israel's election among the nations and connects it to Acts 10; exact page not verified -->
