# Leviticus 25 — The Sabbath Year and the Year of Jubilee

## Story

Chapter 25 expands the weekly rhythm of Sabbath rest to the scale of an entire nation and economy through two of the boldest social institutions in the ancient world: the Sabbath year every seventh year, and the Year of Jubilee every fiftieth year.

First, once Israel settles in the promised land, they may farm their fields for six years, but the seventh year must be a complete Sabbath of rest to the LORD. No sowing, no pruning vineyards, and no commercial harvesting are allowed; whatever the land yields on its own becomes shared food for the landowner, servants, hired workers, resident foreigners, livestock, and even wild animals (25:1–7).

Grander still is the Year of Jubilee. After seven cycles of seven years—forty-nine years—on the Day of Atonement (the tenth day of the seventh month), the ram's horn sounds across the entire land to inaugurate the fiftieth year: "proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants." During Jubilee, the land again lies fallow like a Sabbath year, every piece of ancestral land sold out of poverty returns to its original clan, and every Israelite who had to sell himself into bonded service to pay debts goes free back to his own family (25:8–13).

Because of Jubilee, buying and selling farmland is actually just leasing the remaining harvests until the next Jubilee; prices must be calculated fairly by the number of crops left, without exploiting one another. Anticipating the natural anxiety over what to eat when fields sit unplanted, God gives an explicit promise: in the sixth year he will command such a blessing that the land will yield enough for three years, carrying them safely into the ninth year (25:14–22).

The second half of the chapter sets up a three-tier safety net for impoverished brothers and sisters, anchored in a declaration that overturns every ordinary notion of ownership: "The land must not be sold permanently, because it is Mine, and you are but foreigners and residents with Me" (25:23). If a poor Israelite must sell ancestral land, a close relative has the right to redeem it, or the seller may redeem it himself once he prospers; if not redeemed, it automatically returns to him at Jubilee (houses inside walled cities carry only a one-year redemption window, whereas village homes and Levitical property always keep their right of return) (25:24–34). When a neighbor falls into poverty, Israelites must never charge interest on loans, but support them so they can keep living in the community (25:35–38). Finally, if someone grows so destitute that he sells himself to a fellow Israelite or a wealthy foreigner, he must be treated like a hired worker rather than a brutalized slave, always retaining the right of redemption, and at Jubilee at the latest he and his children walk free—because "the Israelites are My servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt" (25:39–55).

## Key Takeaways

**Human beings are not absolute owners of the earth, but stewards under God.** Verse 23 cuts at the root of unchecked private entitlement: the land belongs to God, and Israel resides upon it as tenants and guests (25:23). Therefore no one may permanently buy up the earth and trap fellow citizens in generational poverty (25:23).

**Economics must serve human dignity and prevent inherited poverty.** In the ancient world, a single drought or high-interest debt could strip a family of land and lock its descendants into permanent slavery. Jubilee functioned like a nationwide *reset button* every fifty years, halting the concentration of land in a wealthy elite's hands and giving the next generation a fair fresh start (25:10; 25:25–28).

**Keeping the rest years required radical trust in God's provision.** Leaving all fields unplanted for a whole year—even two years back-to-back when the forty-ninth Sabbath year met the fiftieth Jubilee—was a towering test of faith. It taught Israel that their ultimate security rested not in exhausting the soil or themselves, but in the blessing of the One who grants every harvest (25:20–22).

**Economic liberation grows directly out of forgiveness of sins.** It is no coincidence that the Jubilee trumpet blasts right on the Day of Atonement (25:9). The very day the nation has its debt of sin erased by God, they are summoned immediately to cancel debts, return land, and set their brothers and sisters free.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus proclaims the great Jubilee in the synagogue at Nazareth.** Launching his public ministry, Jesus unrolls the prophetic scroll drawing directly on the Jubilee imagery of Leviticus 25 (Isaiah 61:1–2), reading his mission to preach good news to the poor, proclaim freedom for the captives, set the oppressed free, and "proclaim the year of the LORD's favor"—and then announces before everyone that this Scripture has been fulfilled that very day in him (Luke 4:18–21).

**The *kinsman-redeemer* foreshadows our Redeemer.** The law enabling a close relative to buy back land and ransom a poor kinsman (25:25; 25:48–49) comes vividly to life when Boaz redeems Naomi's estate and marries Ruth (Ruth 4:1–10). In the New Testament, this portrait reaches its summit in Jesus: he shares our very flesh and blood to pay the ransom rescuing us from slavery to sin and death (Hebrews 2:14–15; Ephesians 1:7).

**Echoes of Jubilee in the early church.** Filled with the Holy Spirit, the first believers in Jerusalem lived out the very spirit of Leviticus 25: sharing possessions and supporting those in need so there was not a needy person among them, while praying daily *forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors* (Acts 4:32–35; Matthew 6:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**How should we understand verses 44 through 46 permitting foreign bondservants (25:44–46)?** Modern readers are rightly troubled to see the law outlaw permanent enslavement of fellow Israelites while permitting the purchase of slaves from surrounding nations. Most commentators set this within its historical horizon: in the Bronze Age, slave labor was the universal economic structure of every civilization. Mosaic law did not abolish the institution overnight, yet immediately began curbing and humanizing it by outlawing kidnapping and human trafficking, sheltering runaway slaves, and mandating Sabbath rest for servants (Exodus 21:16; Deuteronomy 23:15–16). In the New Testament, the gospel seed of equality—*neither slave nor free* in Christ (Galatians 3:28; Philemon 1:16)—ultimately dismantled slavery from within.

**Did Israel ever actually observe the Sabbath years and Jubilee?** Scripture records not a single complete national observance of the Year of Jubilee during the monarchy. On the contrary, the end of 2 Chronicles and the prophet Jeremiah report that because Israel greedily withheld the land's Sabbath years, the seventy years of Babylonian exile finally gave the desolated land its long-overdue Sabbath rest (2 Chronicles 36:21; Jeremiah 34:8–17).

**Verse 10 echoed into modern history (25:10).** The words "proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof" from Leviticus 25:10 became so famous that they were inscribed on the Liberty Bell in Philadelphia, inspiring abolitionist and civil rights movements for generations.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Puritan pastor, views the Year of Jubilee as the Old Testament's finest portrait of the gospel. Just as the trumpet on the Day of Atonement announced canceled debts and liberty for impoverished bondservants, the trumpet of the gospel proclaims release from bondage to sin and restoration of our heavenly inheritance for all who trust in Christ. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Leviticus 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: comparing the Jubilee trumpet with the gospel proclamation is a hallmark of Matthew Henry's commentary on Leviticus 25; exact wording not checked -->

**Christopher J. H. Wright**, British Old Testament ethicist, considers Leviticus 25 one of the most profound models of economic justice in Scripture. In his analysis, Jubilee weaves together three inseparable dimensions—theological (the land belongs to God), economic (preventing land monopoly and predatory lending), and social (preserving the integrity of every family)—offering a lasting lens to critique unjust economic systems today. *(Old Testament Ethics for the People of God, 2004)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing the theological, economic, and familial triad of Leviticus 25 is one of Christopher Wright's best-known contributions in this work -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, highlights the tight link between Jubilee and the promise of verses 20 through 22: this entire humane economic order could function only if Israel genuinely trusted God as their provider (25:20–22). He also observes that although ancient Israel failed to live out Jubilee fully, the ideal was never discarded—it was revived and fulfilled by Jesus himself in Luke 4. *(The Book of Leviticus, NICOT, 1979)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Wenham stresses the faith requirement in 25:20–22 and Christ's fulfillment in Luke 4; exact page not checked -->
