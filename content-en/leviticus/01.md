# Leviticus 1 — The Burnt Offering

## Story

At the close of Exodus, the glory of the LORD fills the Tabernacle so completely that even Moses cannot enter (Exodus 40:34–35). As Leviticus opens, the LORD calls to Moses from inside the Tent of Meeting and teaches the people how to draw near to him through offerings (1:1–2). First among the five kinds of offerings is the *burnt offering*—the only sacrifice in which the entire animal is burned completely on the altar, with no portion eaten by anyone.

The law sets three tiers for the burnt offering according to what the worshiper can afford: a bull from the herd for the wealthy, a male sheep or goat from the flock for those of moderate means, and a turtledove or young pigeon for the poor (1:3; 1:10; 1:14). Large or small, every animal from the herd or flock must be "a male without blemish," free from defect or disease.

The procedure for bulls, sheep, and goats follows much the same pattern. The worshiper personally leads the animal to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, lays his hand on the head of the burnt offering to show that the animal represents and stands in for him, and slaughters it himself before the LORD (1:3–5; 1:10–11). Then Aaron's sons—the priests—collect the blood and sprinkle it on all sides of the altar. The worshiper skins the animal, cuts it into pieces, and washes the entrails and legs; the priests arrange the wood, lay all the pieces upon the altar, and burn the whole offering as "a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (1:6–9; 1:12–13).

For a poor person bringing a bird, the ritual is simpler and handled entirely by the priest. He wrings off its head, drains its blood against the side of the altar, removes the crop and feathers to the ash heap, tears the bird open by its wings without severing it completely, and burns all of it on the altar. Scripture describes this humble gift with the exact same words used for an expensive bull: an offering by fire, "a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (1:14–17).

## Key Takeaways

**God calls before the people give.** Leviticus does not begin with human beings trying to win over a reluctant deity; it begins with the LORD calling to Moses from the Tent of Meeting (1:1). God himself opens the way so that sinful people can draw near to him without being destroyed.

**Total surrender, holding nothing back.** Unlike later offerings that include portions for the priests or the worshiper, the burnt offering is consumed entirely on the altar. It expresses wholehearted devotion: everything the worshiper has belongs to God.

**A life in place of a life.** Pressing a hand firmly on the animal's head (1:4) shows that this is no casual performance. The worshiper acknowledges personal guilt and desert of death before the Holy One, needing an innocent life to take his place and make atonement for him.

**No one is shut out because of poverty.** From a costly bull to a tiny turtledove, the LORD does not demand the same price from everyone (1:3; 1:10; 1:14). What pleases God is not the monetary value of the gift, but a willing heart giving wholly according to its means.

## Deeper Meaning

**The burnt offering foreshadows Jesus giving himself completely.** The Letter to the Hebrews explains that the blood of bulls and goats can never take away sins on its own, being only a shadow of the good things to come; when Christ came into the world, he offered his own body once for all (Hebrews 10:1–10). Paul likewise speaks of Christ's death in the very language of this chapter: he gave himself up for us as a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God (Ephesians 5:2).

**Even the poor's offering enters the story of Jesus' birth.** When Mary and Joseph bring the infant Jesus to the temple for purification, they offer only a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons—the exact provision for the poor in Leviticus (Luke 2:24; Leviticus 12:8). The One who would become the spotless Lamb of God entered the world in a family unable to afford a single lamb.

**A life of devotion replaces the ancient altar.** Under the New Covenant, animal sacrifices on a bronze altar have ceased, yet the spirit of the burnt offering remains intact. Paul urges believers to offer their bodies as living sacrifices, holy and pleasing to God—this is each person's spiritual service of worship (Romans 12:1).

## Notes & Nuances

**God does not hunger or literally smell roasting meat.** The phrase "a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (1:9; 1:13; 1:17) borrows human language to express God's gracious acceptance. Neighboring religions around Israel believed their gods needed to be fed through sacrificial smoke, a notion Scripture repeatedly rejects (Psalms 50:12–13).

**The worshiper personally slaughters the animal.** Many modern readers assume the priest does everything, yet verse 5 shows the worshiper laying his own hand on the animal's head and slaughtering it before the LORD (1:5). This made the worshiper feel firsthand the steep cost of sin: an innocent creature had to shed its blood right before his eyes.

**Why so many details about blood and entrails?** To modern ears, instructions about skinning carcasses and washing entrails can sound stark and heavy. Yet for Israelites fresh out of Egypt, these clear, meticulous directions taught that worshiping God cannot be improvised on a whim, and that every clean, careful detail honors the Holy One.

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, highlights God's gracious allowance for the poor to bring birds instead of bulls or sheep. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, insists that laying a hand on the animal's head holds no automatic magic to erase sin. In his view, the rite taught the worshiper two truths at once: to confess that he deserved death for his sin, and to place his faith in the Mediator whom God would send to die in his stead. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on the burnt offering)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading the laying on of hands as both confession of guilt and pointing to Christ is characteristic of Calvin's Harmony of the Law; exact section not verified -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, notes that the burnt offering is the most basic and ancient of the sacrifices, standing first in the list because it encompasses two great meanings: atonement for sin and complete dedication.
