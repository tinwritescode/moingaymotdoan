# Leviticus 5 — Confession and the Guilt Offering

## Story

Chapter 5 unfolds in two connected halves. The first half (5:1–13) lists several concrete cases requiring a *sin offering*—continuing chapter 4—and then opens an accessible path for the poor. Four offenses are named: hearing a public call to testify in court yet keeping silent about what one saw or knows; accidentally touching an unclean carcass; touching human uncleanness and forgetting until later realizing it; and blurting out a rash oath before realizing one's guilt (5:1–4).

The moment someone realizes they are guilty, the very first step is not rushing off to buy an animal; rather, "he must confess the sin he has committed" (5:5). Then he brings an offering matched to his means: whoever can afford it brings a female lamb or female goat; whoever is too poor brings two turtledoves or two young pigeons—one for a sin offering and one for a burnt offering; and whoever is so poor he cannot afford even two birds simply brings a tenth of an ephah of fine flour, without oil or frankincense so it stays distinct from the joyful grain offering (5:6–13). Whether lamb, birds, or a small measure of flour, the outcome is identical: "he will be forgiven" (5:10; 5:13).

The second half (5:14–19) introduces the fifth and final sacrifice: the *guilt offering* (also called the *reparation offering*). This applies when someone unintentionally violates any of the LORD's holy things—such as withholding tithes or mistakenly eating food dedicated to the priests—or breaks a command and bears guilt without yet realizing every detail (5:15; 5:17). The offender must bring a ram without blemish valued according to the sanctuary shekel, while also repaying the full value of the loss plus an extra fifth (twenty percent) to the priest. Then the priest makes atonement with the ram, and the person is forgiven (5:15–19).

## Key Takeaways

**Forgiveness begins by naming one's sin plainly.** Verse 5 places confession ahead of every sacrificial step (5:5). There is no hiding behind a ritual to purchase peace of mind; honest admission of wrongdoing is the doorway to pardon.

**Silence and reckless promises are sins too.** The four opening examples show that God's law reaches far beyond obvious crimes. Knowing the truth yet staying silent when justice calls, or speaking hasty, careless oaths (5:1; 5:4), injures the community and incurs real guilt before God.

**Even the poorest person has a way to forgiveness.** For the sin offering here, God lowers the required gift all the way down to a modest measure of flour for those in deepest poverty (5:11–13). Forgiveness is never a privilege reserved for the rich.

**True repentance always includes restitution.** With the guilt offering, an apology and a ram are not enough: the offender must restore the full amount plus twenty percent (5:16). Sin is not merely an uneasy feeling inside; it inflicts tangible loss that must be fairly repaid.

## Deeper Meaning

**Christ becomes the guilt offering for humanity.** In the song of the Suffering Servant, the prophet Isaiah declares that the LORD makes his life "a guilt offering"—using the exact Hebrew word for the sacrifice introduced in this chapter (Isaiah 53:10). Every debt humanity could never repay to God, Jesus stepped forward to pay in full once for all on the cross (Hebrews 9:14–15; 10:10–14).

**Plain confession remains the path in the New Testament.** The call to "confess the sin he has committed" (5:5) echoes clearly in First John: if we confess our sins, God is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness (1 John 1:9).

**Do not make rash oaths.** The provision regarding hasty vows (5:4) illuminates Jesus' teaching in the Sermon on the Mount: do not swear at all, but simply let your "Yes" be "Yes" and your "No" be "No" (Matthew 5:33–37; James 5:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why are oil and frankincense left off the flour sin offering?** In the grain offering of chapter 2, oil and frankincense expressed gladness and fragrant thanksgiving. Here, the flour serves as a sin offering—a solemn, sorrowful matter—so oil and frankincense are omitted to fit the tone of penitence (5:11).

**The flour offering has no blood—does that contradict "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness"?** Hebrews 9:22 actually states that under the law *almost* everything is cleansed with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness. That careful word *almost* shows the author of Hebrews knew full well about merciful exceptions such as the poor person's flour offering (5:11)—and even that handful of flour was burned on the very altar already soaked with the blood of daily sacrifices.

**What does valuation by the "sanctuary shekel" mean (5:15)?** A *shekel* was an ancient unit of silver weight (roughly eleven grams). Specifying the official sanctuary standard ensured fair, uniform assessment of the ram and the restitution payment, guarding against dishonest weights.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, pauses over verse 1 concerning the witness who knows the truth yet keeps quiet when called to testify (5:1). He observes that concealing another's crime when justice demands a voice makes one complicit in the offense. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, emphasizes the requirement to repay the loss plus an added fifth in the guilt offering. In his view, God teaches his people that devotion to him can never be separated from fairness toward others' property and rights; whoever truly repents must be willing to suffer a small penalty to make things right. *(Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses, on the guilt offering)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: linking genuine repentance with fair restitution is a consistent emphasis of Calvin on the reparation laws; exact location not verified -->

**Gordon Wenham**, British Old Testament scholar, clarifies the distinction between the *sin offering* and the *guilt offering*: the sin offering centers on purging impurity, whereas the guilt offering focuses on repaying a specific debt or material injury done to God's holy property or to a neighbor. *(The Book of Leviticus, NICOT, 1979, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: distinguishing purification from debt reparation is Wenham's standard summary for chapters 4–5; exact page not verified -->
