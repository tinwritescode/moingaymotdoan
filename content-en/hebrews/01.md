# Hebrews 1 — The Son Greater Than the Angels

## Story

The letter launches immediately with a single sweeping, majestic sentence—skipping the opening greeting typical of Paul's letters. In the past, God spoke to the ancestors through the prophets "at many times and in various ways," piece by piece through dreams, visions, and sermons. Now, "in these last days," He has spoken directly through His own Son. God has appointed the Son "heir of all things," and through Him He also created the universe (1:1–2).

The writer paints this Son in the most dazzling strokes imaginable. The Son is "the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of His nature"—the crisp imprint of the original seal, so seeing the Son means seeing God Himself. He sustains the entire universe from collapsing "by His powerful word." After accomplishing purification for sins, He "sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high"—the posture of One whose work stands finished and who now reigns. Having inherited a name far superior to the angels, He has become just as much superior to them (1:3–4).

To prove this supremacy, the writer strings together seven Old Testament passages. First, God never once said to any angel, "You are My Son; today I have become Your Father," nor "I will be His Father, and He will be My Son." On the contrary, when bringing His Firstborn into the world, God commands all His angels to worship the Son (1:5–6; Psalms 2:7; 2 Samuel 7:14; Psalms 97:7).

Angels are merely servants. Scripture portrays angels like winds and flames of fire—changeable creatures dispatched on errands. Speaking of the Son, however, God addresses Him directly as "O God": His throne endures forever and ever, His royal scepter is a scepter of justice; because He loves righteousness and hates wickedness, God has anointed Him with the oil of joy above His companions (1:7–9; Psalms 104:4; 45:6–7).

Going further still, the writer quotes a psalm praising the Creator and applies it straight to the Son: in the beginning, You, Lord, laid the foundations of the earth, and the heavens are the work of Your hands. Sky and earth will eventually grow worn like a garment, rolled up like a robe and changed—yet You remain the same, and Your years will never end (1:10–12; Psalms 102:25–27).

The seventh and final quotation is an invitation to the royal throne: God never told any angel, "Sit at My right hand until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet." Unlike the enthroned Son, all angels are simply "ministering spirits sent to serve those who will inherit salvation" (1:13–14; Psalms 110:1).

## Key Takeaways

**The Son is God's final and fullest word.** The earlier prophets were priceless yet partial pieces of a puzzle, each shedding part of the light. With the Son's arrival, the whole picture stands complete. The Son does not merely relay a message from God—His very Person and life *are* that word (1:1–2).

**Jesus creates, sustains, and purifies the world.** Verse 1:3 packs all three supreme works into a single breath: He made all things, holds everything together moment by moment, and resolves humanity's deepest helplessness—sin. Work finished, He "sat down at the right hand of the Majesty": temple priests of old stood all day because sacrifices never ended, whereas Jesus sat down because purification for sin was completed once for all (1:3).

**Angels deserve honor, yet they remain servants—not the Son.** First-century Jews held angels in deep reverence, honoring them as heavenly messengers who mediated the law to Moses and stood around God's throne. The writer never belittles angels; he simply sets them in their proper rank—wind, fire, ministering spirits—while Jesus stands as Son, Creator, and eternal King (1:7–14).

**God even sends angels to look after believers.** The closing verse (1:14) leaves a warm reassurance: those mighty heavenly beings do not exist to be worshiped, but are dispatched to assist everyone who will inherit salvation. Believers rest safely in the hands of the One who outranks every angel (1:14).

## Deeper Meaning

**Roots reaching back to the opening verse of Scripture.** Declaring that God created the universe through the Son and that the Son sustains all things by His powerful word (1:2–3) hooks directly into Genesis's opening scene, where God spoke and creation leaped into being (Genesis 1:1–3). John calls this One the Word (John 1:1–3), and Paul calls Him the image of the invisible God in whom all things hold together (Colossians 1:15–17).

**The promise to David's line fulfilled beyond every dream.** In the Old Testament, "I will be His Father, and He will be My Son" was first spoken to King David regarding his son Solomon and an enduring dynasty (2 Samuel 7:14), and Israel's coronation psalm hailed the king as God's Son (Psalms 2:7). Yet earthly monarchs died and their thrones crumbled; only in Jesus does that royal promise reach its true eternal scale (1:5; 1:8).

**Psalms 110:1 forms the backbone of the whole letter to the Hebrews.** "Sit at My right hand" (1:13; Psalms 110:1) is the Old Testament verse cited most frequently across the New Testament. Jesus Himself used it to challenge the Pharisees on the Messiah's identity (Matthew 22:41–45), and Peter preached it on Pentecost (Acts 2:34–35). Later in Hebrews, this same psalm opens straight into the oath of Christ's priesthood in the order of Melchizedek (Psalms 110:4).

**Every angel worshiping the Lamb.** The command "Let all God's angels worship Him" (1:6) rings back in Revelation's heavenly vision, where myriads of angels encircling the throne raise their voices to honor the Lamb who was slain (Revelation 5:11–12).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who wrote the letter to the Hebrews?** The document itself never names its author. Early Eastern church tradition attributed it to Paul, which is why older Bibles titled it Paul's Epistle to the Hebrews. Yet its polished Greek style differs markedly from Paul's letters, and in 2:3 the author places himself among second-generation believers who received the gospel from eyewitnesses—whereas Paul insisted he received his gospel directly from Christ (Galatians 1:11–12). Various candidates have been proposed—Barnabas, Apollos, Luke, Priscilla and Aquila—yet as the ancient scholar Origen famously remarked, only God knows for sure who wrote it.

**"Today I have become Your Father" (1:5) never implies the Son was a created being.** Drawn from Israel's royal coronation psalm (Psalms 2:7), "today" in the New Testament does not mark a moment when the Son came into existence, but His enthronement in glory upon His resurrection and ascension—just as Paul explains in Acts 13:33. Immediately afterward, 1:10–12 confirms the Son is the eternal Creator of heaven and earth.

**Verse 1:6 quotes the ancient Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint).** In many English Old Testaments following the standard Hebrew Masoretic Text, readers will not spot the exact clause "Let all God's angels worship Him" in Deuteronomy 32:43 because the standard Hebrew is shorter; that line appears in the Greek Septuagint (as well as a Dead Sea Scrolls fragment) of Deuteronomy 32:43 and closely echoes Psalms 97:7. Throughout Hebrews, the author quotes Old Testament Scripture from the Septuagint, the Bible widely read by Greek-speaking Jews in the first century.

**The word translated "universe" in 1:2 literally means "the ages."** The Greek term *aionas* spans not just physical space, but time and history as well. The Son not only crafted mountains and stars; He also architects the whole sweep of history from start to finish.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, the towering early third-century scholar of Alexandria, remarked that the theology of Hebrews equals Paul's letters in depth, yet its refined Greek diction stands clearly apart from Paul's style. He closed with the famous verdict still repeated today: who actually wrote the epistle, God alone truly knows. *(Quoted in Eusebius, Church History, Book 6, chapter 25)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Origen's famous remark is preserved by Eusebius in Historia Ecclesiastica 6.25.11–14 -->

**Athanasius**, the fourth-century bishop of Alexandria, championed the Son as "the radiance of God's glory" (1:3) to refute Arius's teaching that the Son was merely a creature. You cannot have a sun without the light shining out from it, Athanasius reasoned; in the same way, God the Father never existed without the Son, because the Son is the eternal radiance streaming from the Father's own nature. *(Orations against the Arians, Discourses 1 and 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the sun-and-radiance analogy built on Hebrews chapter 1 verse 3 is a core argument of Athanasius in Orations against the Arians -->

**John Calvin** highlights the contrast between "in the past" and "in these last days" (1:1–2). God does not contradict Himself by speaking first through prophets and later through the Son; rather, He teaches humanity like a patient schoolmaster leading pupils from basic lessons to maturity. Now that the Son has come and spoken fully, we need await no newer revelation beyond Christ. *(Commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews, on chapter 1 verses 1–2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this point stands at the heart of Calvin's exposition of Hebrews chapter 1 verses 1–2 -->
