# Hebrews 5 — A Priest in the Order of Melchizedek and a Rebuke for Slow Growth

## Story

Opening his deep exploration of Christ's priesthood, the author lays down two basic qualifications for every ancient high priest. First, he must be chosen from among human beings to represent humanity before God, presenting gifts and sacrifices for sins. Because he himself is "subject to weakness," he can deal gently with the ignorant and misguided—and for that same weakness, he must offer sacrifices for his own sins just as much as for the people's. Second, no one takes this high honor on his own initiative; a priest must be called directly by God, just as Aaron was (5:1–4; Exodus 28:1).

Christ fulfills both qualifications—and rises infinitely above them. First, He did not appoint Himself to the glory of becoming high priest; rather, He was appointed by the One who spoke two decisive declarations in Scripture: "You are My Son; today I have become Your Father," and "You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" (5:5–6; Psalms 2:7; 110:4).

Second, He genuinely shared human frailty and anguish. During His days in the flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and petitions with "loud cries and tears" to the One able to save Him from death, and because of His reverent submission He was heard. Although He was a Son, He "learned obedience from what He suffered." Having walked that road to completion, He became "the source of eternal salvation" for all who obey Him and was formally designated by God as high priest in the order of Melchizedek (5:7–10).

Right where readers expect an immediate explanation of Melchizedek, the writer suddenly sets down his pen and sighs in frustration over his audience. He has much to say about this, yet it is hard to explain—not because the topic is impossible, but because they have become "dull of hearing." Judging by how long they have believed, they ought to be teachers by now; instead, they need someone to reteach them the barest ABCs of God's word. They have slipped back to needing "milk, not solid food." Anyone living solely on milk remains unskilled in the word of righteousness, still an infant; "solid food" belongs to the mature, whose faculties have been trained through constant practice to distinguish good from evil (5:11–14).

## Key Takeaways

**A true High Priest must be appointed by God and know human pain firsthand.** Aaron never nominated himself, nor did Christ exalt Himself; His office rests on God's direct appointment. Yet what sets Jesus's priesthood apart is that He never needed to offer sacrifice for personal sin, even while entering completely into human tears and struggle (5:1–8).

**Jesus learned obedience through real agony.** Saying that "although He was a Son, He learned obedience from what He suffered" (5:8) shatters any picture of an insulated Savior untouched by pain. Sonship did not exempt Him from suffering; instead, He chose obedience all the way to the cross to open eternal salvation (5:7–9).

**Years spent as a believer do not automatically produce maturity.** The writer puts his finger on a familiar spiritual ache: plenty of longtime Christians should be steady enough to guide others, yet through lazy reflection and neglected practice they keep circling the elementary basics. Worse still, having once grasped truth, they have "become dull of hearing"—sliding backward rather than merely standing still (5:11–12).

**Spiritual maturity grows by practicing moral discernment every day.** The verb "trained" in 5:14 comes straight from athletic training in the gymnasium. Developing an appetite for "solid food" in Scripture requires more than listening to endless sermons; it takes exercising conscience and judgment through daily choices to tell right from wrong (5:13–14).

## Deeper Meaning

**Cries and tears in the Garden of Gethsemane.** Picturing Jesus offering prayers with "loud cries and tears" to the One able to rescue Him from death (5:7) evokes His agonizing night in Gethsemane before His arrest (Matthew 26:36–44; Luke 22:41–44). He was not spared from passing through death, yet He was "heard" in a far deeper sense: strengthened to obey the Father's will to the end and delivered *out of* death in glorious resurrection.

**Two psalms joined: King and Priest.** In 5:5–6, the writer sets Psalms 2:7 (the royal Davidic King) straight beside Psalms 110:4 (a Priest in the order of Melchizedek). Under the Old Testament, kings came from Judah and priests from Levi—strictly separated offices, with judgment falling on any monarch who presumed to burn incense (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). Only mysterious Melchizedek in Genesis 14:18–20 combined King of Salem and Priest of God Most High—a fitting type pointing straight to Christ, our sole King-Priest.

**Aaron's calling in Exodus.** Naming Aaron in 5:4 recalls the moment God commanded Moses to set Aaron and his sons apart to serve as priests in the tabernacle (Exodus 28:1). The writer lays that marker here so chapter 7 can set Aaron's temporary priesthood directly beside the eternal order of Melchizedek.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Learned obedience" (5:8) never implies Jesus was once disobedient.** Jesus never sinned or resisted His Father (4:15). "Learning" here resembles knowing courage in theory and then stepping onto a real battlefield to taste what courage costs in flesh and blood. As the eternal Son in heaven, His will always aligned with the Father; becoming human meant experiencing firsthand what obedience costs amid pain, betrayal, and death.

**"Reverent submission" in 5:7 translates a Greek word meaning godly awe and devotion.** The verse does not claim Jesus was heard merely for praying loudly, but because of His deeply reverent submission to the Father's will.

**Who is Melchizedek, and why is he "hard to explain"?** This enigmatic figure flashes across only three verses in Genesis 14:18–20 and reappears in one solitary line in Psalms 110:4 before Hebrews devotes all of chapter 7 to unpacking him. The author puts Melchizedek on pause at 5:11 to deliver chapter 6's solemn warning, then returns to explain him thoroughly at the opening of chapter 7.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin** writes on 5:7–8 that Jesus truly felt the full dread of death and wrath against sin, which is why He wept and pleaded; yet that terror never drove Him to rebellion—it drove Him closer to God. For Calvin, Jesus's prayer was answered not by removing the cross, but by triumphing over death in His resurrection. *(Commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews, on chapter 5 verses 7–8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's classic exposition of Christ's real dread and being heard through resurrection in Hebrews chapter 5 verse 7 is well known -->

**John Owen** observes that charging readers with having "become dull of hearing" (5:11) indicts spiritual sloth rather than low native intelligence. In the life of faith, Owen argues, nobody stays stationary: anyone who stops pressing toward "solid food" inevitably slips backward into spiritual infancy. *(An Exposition of the Epistle to the Hebrews, on chapter 5 verses 11–14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Owen's emphasis on spiritual regression whenever growth stops is central to his exposition of Hebrews chapter 5 verses 11–14 -->

**Thomas Aquinas**, the towering medieval theologian, clarifies "learned obedience" (5:8) by distinguishing intellectual knowledge from experiential knowledge. In His divine nature and intellect, the Son always knew obedience; yet by suffering in the flesh, He entered experiential knowledge (*scientia experimentalis*)—tasting firsthand how costly obedience is under torment. *(Commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews, chapter 5, lecture 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Aquinas's distinction between speculative and experiential knowledge (scientia experimentalis) on Hebrews chapter 5 verse 8 is famous in his Super Epistolam ad Hebraeos -->

**George Guthrie**, the American New Testament scholar, sees 5:11–14 as an alarm bell waking hearers up right before the letter's most demanding theological core in chapters 7–10.
