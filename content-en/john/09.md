# John 9 — The Man Born Blind

## Story

Walking along, Jesus saw a man who had been blind from birth. His disciples asked, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" Jesus replied: neither this man nor his parents sinned, but this happened so that the works of God might be displayed in him. While it is still day, we must carry out the works of Him who sent Me; night is coming, when no one can work. "While I am in the world, I am the light of the world." (9:1–5). The disciples ask who is to blame; Jesus asks only what God is about to do here.

Having said this, He spat on the ground, made mud with His saliva, spread the mud over the blind man's eyes, and told him, "Go, wash in the Pool of Siloam"—which means "Sent." The man went, washed, and came back seeing (9:6–7).

Neighbors and those who had seen him begging asked one another: isn't this the man who used to sit and beg? Some said, "It is him." Others said, "No, he only looks like him." He himself kept insisting, "I am the man." They asked how his eyes had been opened. He recounted: the man called Jesus made mud, spread it on my eyes, and told me to go to Siloam and wash; I went and washed, and received sight. They asked where that man was; he answered, "I do not know." (9:8–12).

They brought the man who had been blind to the Pharisees. Now the day Jesus made mud and opened his eyes happened to be a Sabbath. The Pharisees likewise questioned him on how he gained sight. He told them again. Some Pharisees declared, "This man is not from God, because he does not keep the Sabbath." Others countered, "How can a sinner perform such signs?" Division broke out among them. Turning back to the formerly blind man, they asked: what do you say about him, since he opened your eyes? He answered, "He is a prophet." (9:13–17).

The Jewish authorities refused to believe he had really been blind and regained sight until they summoned his parents and asked: is this your son, whom you say was born blind? How is it he can see now? His parents replied: we know this is our son and that he was born blind, yet how he sees now or who opened his eyes we do not know; ask him—he is of age and can speak for himself. They spoke guardedly out of fear, because the Jewish leaders had already agreed that anyone confessing Jesus to be the Christ would be put out of the synagogue (9:18–23).

So they summoned the man a second time and urged him: give glory to God; we know this man is a sinner. He replied, "Whether he is a sinner I do not know; one thing I do know—that though I was blind, now I see." They pressed him again: what did he do to you? How did he open your eyes? He answered: I already told you and you would not listen; why do you want to hear it again? Do you want to become his disciples too? (9:24–27).

They hurled insults at him: you are his disciple, whereas we are disciples of Moses! We know God spoke to Moses, yet as for this fellow, we don't even know where he comes from. The man answered: now that is remarkable—you don't know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes! Everyone knows God does not listen to sinners, but listens to whoever reveres Him and does His will. Never since the world began has anyone heard of opening the eyes of a man born blind. If this man were not from God, he could do nothing. They snapped back: you were steeped in sin at birth, and you dare lecture us? And they threw him out (9:28–34). An unschooled beggar stands firm before learned scholars on the strength of one unshakable fact.

Hearing that they had cast him out, Jesus sought him out and asked, "Do you believe in the Son of Man?" The man answered, "Who is he, sir, that I may believe in him?" Jesus told him, "You have seen Him, and it is He who is speaking with you." He declared, "Lord, I believe," and bowed down in worship before Him (9:35–38). Expelled from the synagogue, he is found by Jesus Himself—and his newly opened eyes behold his healer for the very first time.

Jesus declared: for judgment I came into this world, so that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind. Some Pharisees standing nearby asked, "Are we blind too?" Jesus answered: if you were blind, you would have no guilt; yet now that you say, "We see," your guilt remains (9:39–41). The story opens with a blind man gaining sight and closes with sighted men refusing to see.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus overturns the habit of blaming every affliction on personal sin.** The disciples view a man born blind as a moral puzzle demanding someone to blame. Jesus dismisses that outlook and sees him instead as a canvas where God's compassionate work can shine forth (9:1–5).

**Sight dawns progressively from eyes to heart.** Freshly healed, the man calls Jesus simply "the man called Jesus"; before the Pharisees he declares Him "a prophet"; finally, cast out and sought by Jesus, he falls in worship confessing, "Lord, I believe." Real faith often matures step by step under pressure (9:11; 9:17; 9:35–38).

**Plain truth outlasts learned prejudice.** An uneducated beggar holds his ground before experts in the law with one simple testimony: "Though I was blind, now I see." When people shut their ears against obvious facts, pride drives them to insults and exclusion against truth-tellers (9:24–34).

**The gravest blindness is insisting one already sees clearly.** Entering the world as its light, Jesus exposes who is truly blind. Those willing to admit their blindness receive sight, while the self-satisfied claiming "We see" remain trapped in darkness and guilt (9:39–41).

## Deeper Meaning

**Opening blind eyes marks the messianic age.** No Old Testament prophet had ever restored sight to someone blind from birth (9:32), yet Isaiah foretold that when God arrives to rescue His people, "the eyes of the blind will be opened" (Isaiah 35:5), and His Servant will be sent "to open eyes that are blind" (Isaiah 42:7).

**Making mud and washing evoke creation and obedient faith.** Kneading earth into mud to touch sightless eyes echoes God forming humanity "from the dust of the ground" at creation (Genesis 2:7), as though Jesus finishes creating eyes that never saw light. Meanwhile, sending him to wash in Siloam recalls Naaman obeying Elisha's word to dip in the Jordan River for cleansing (2 Kings 5:10).

**Siloam—"Sent"—points straight to Christ.** John explicitly translates the pool's name in 9:7 so readers recognize that the water holds no magic of its own; healing power flows from the One sent into the world by the Father (3:17; 5:36).

**Willful blindness echoes Isaiah's solemn prophecy.** Jesus' pronouncement in 9:39 that those who claim to see become blind echoes Isaiah's tragic commission to a people who "keep on looking, yet never perceive" because their hearts have grown calloused against God's word (Isaiah 6:9–10).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did making mud violate Sabbath rules?** Under detailed Pharisaic regulations elaborating Sabbath rest, mixing moisture with dirt counted as kneading, and healing non-life-threatening conditions on the Sabbath was forbidden (9:14–16). As a result, the authorities viewed a miracle purely through rule-keeping instead of recognizing God's mercy.

**Expulsion from the synagogue carried severe social and economic fallout.** The synagogue anchored Jewish communal life; expulsion cut someone off from family ties, neighbors, and livelihood. Recognizing what stood at stake fosters empathy for the parents' fearful evasion under questioning (9:20–23).

**Scholars debate the historical timing of the synagogue ban in 9:22.** Some researchers argue that formal exclusion of Jesus' followers crystallized late in the first century when John wrote, and the evangelist reflects his own era's experience. Others note that local synagogues already exercised disciplinary exclusion against perceived heretics during Jesus' lifetime.

**A minor manuscript variation appears in 9:35.** Later manuscripts read "Son of God," whereas some of the earliest manuscripts read "Son of Man." Either reading leads to the exact same climax: the healed man bows down to worship Jesus in 9:38, unmistakably affirming His deity.

**Irenaeus**, second-century bishop of Lyon, saw Jesus smearing mud on the blind man's eyes as a direct continuation of Genesis 2:7. **John Chrysostom**, fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople, praised the courage of the nameless beggar facing the Pharisees: unintimidated by status and unconfused by slick arguments, he clung to the reality he had lived. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, observed on 9:2–3 how readily people play judge whenever misfortune strikes someone, rushing to declare it deserved payback for sin. Jesus quashes that harsh instinct, teaching disciples to meet suffering with compassion and humility before God's purposes. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John, on chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Calvin critiquing hasty judgment of sufferers at 9:2–3; exact phrasing not checked -->

**Raymond Brown**, twentieth-century American Catholic New Testament scholar, hailed John 9 as a dramatic masterpiece built on two opposite trajectories. Step by step, the man born blind moves from utter darkness into full sight and worship of Christ, while the confident Pharisees sink step by step into willful blindness. *(The Gospel According to John, Anchor Bible, vol. 1, 1966)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing the paired, opposite dramatic arcs of the blind man and the Pharisees is one of Brown's signature insights -->

**J. Louis Martyn**, twentieth-century American New Testament scholar, gained renown for reading John 9 as a two-level drama. Alongside reporting a healing from Jesus' earthly ministry, he argued that the synagogue expulsion in 9:22 directly mirrors the late-first-century crisis of the Johannine church being severed from the Jewish synagogue. *(History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel, 1968)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the "two-level drama" thesis built around John 9 is the most famous contribution of Martyn's book -->
