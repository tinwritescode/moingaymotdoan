# John 10 — The Good Shepherd

## Story

Continuing His conversation with the Pharisees, Jesus declared: truly, whoever does not enter the sheepfold by the gate, but climbs in by another way, is a thief and a robber. The one who enters by the gate is the shepherd of the sheep. The gatekeeper opens to him, the sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out. Once he has brought out all his own, he walks ahead of them, and the sheep follow him because they know his voice. They will never follow a stranger, but will flee from him because they do not recognize a stranger's voice. Jesus spoke this figure of speech to them, yet they did not grasp what He was saying (10:1–6). Just moments before, in chapter 9, He had watched these leaders cast one of the sheep out of the synagogue.

So Jesus spoke again: truly, "I am the gate for the sheep." All who came before Me were thieves and robbers, yet the sheep did not listen to them. Whoever enters through Me will be saved, and will go in and out and find pasture. The thief comes only to steal, kill, and destroy; "I have come that they may have life, and have it abundantly." (10:7–10).

"I am the good shepherd; the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." The hired hand is not the shepherd and does not own the sheep; seeing the wolf approaching, he abandons the sheep and flees, and the wolf snatches and scatters them. He runs away because he is a hired hand and cares nothing for the sheep. I am the good shepherd; I know My own and My own know Me, just as the Father knows Me and I know the Father, and I lay down My life for the sheep (10:11–15).

I have other sheep that do not belong to this fold; I must bring them also. They will listen to My voice, and there will be one flock and one shepherd. This is why the Father loves Me: because I lay down My life in order to take it up again. No one takes it from Me, but I lay it down of My own accord; I have authority to lay it down and authority to take it up again—this charge I received from My Father (10:16–18). His death will never be something wrested away from Him, but a life freely given.

At these words, division broke out again among the Jewish listeners. Many said, "He has a demon and is out of his mind; why listen to him?" Others replied, "These are not the words of a demon-possessed man; can a demon open the eyes of the blind?" (10:19–21).

Then came the Feast of Dedication in Jerusalem; it was winter, and Jesus was walking in the temple courts along Solomon's Colonnade. The Jewish leaders gathered around Him and demanded: how long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Christ, tell us plainly. Jesus answered: I told you, yet you do not believe; the works I do in My Father's name testify about Me. You do not believe because you are not among My sheep. "My sheep hear My voice; I know them, and they follow Me." I give them eternal life, they shall never perish, and no one will snatch them out of My hand. My Father, who has given them to Me, is greater than all, and no one can snatch them out of the Father's hand. "I and the Father are one." (10:22–30). The Feast of Dedication commemorated cleansing and rededicating the temple after a foreign king defiled it; today Jews celebrate it as Hanukkah.

Again the Jewish opponents picked up stones to throw at Him. Jesus asked: I have shown you many good works from the Father; for which of these do you stone Me? They answered: we are not stoning you for any good work, but for blasphemy, because you, a mere man, claim to be God. Jesus replied: is it not written in your law, "I said, you are gods"? If Scripture called those to whom the word of God came "gods"—and Scripture cannot be broken—how can you accuse the One whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world of blasphemy simply because I said, "I am the Son of God"? (10:31–36).

If I am not doing the works of My Father, do not believe Me. Yet if I do them, even though you do not believe Me, believe the works, so that you may know and understand that the Father is in Me and I am in the Father. Again they sought to arrest Him, yet He slipped from their grasp. He crossed back over the Jordan to the place where John had been baptizing at the beginning, and stayed there. Many came to Him, saying: John performed no sign, yet everything John said about this man turned out to be true. And many believed in Him there (10:37–42).

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus is both the Gate to the fold and the Good Shepherd.** Unlike thieves arriving to plunder or hired hands fleeing from wolves, He enters through the proper door, calls each sheep by name, and guides them out to green pasture. His aim is unmistakable: granting His sheep "life, and life abundantly" (10:1–13).

**The Shepherd's love shows in voluntary sacrifice and a heart wide enough for all nations.** Nobody wrenches Jesus' life away; He lays it down willingly for His sheep so that He may take it up again. His gaze also reaches beyond Israel's borders to gather "other sheep," forming one single flock under one Shepherd (10:14–18).

**True sheep recognize His voice, follow Him, and rest completely secure.** The bond between Shepherd and flock mirrors the intimacy shared by Father and Son. Those who truly belong to Him receive eternal life and rest inside the grip of both Son and Father—beyond any enemy's reach (10:27–29).

**"I and the Father are one" openly affirms His deity.** During winter's Feast of Dedication, pressed to speak plainly, Jesus points to His works as ample proof. Declaring "I and the Father are one" prompts His opponents to reach for stones, yet He still urges them to weigh His good deeds from the Father and recognize that "the Father is in Me and I am in the Father" (10:22–38).

## Deeper Meaning

**God Himself searches for His scattered flock.** John 10's shepherd imagery flows directly from Ezekiel, where God rebukes Israel's selfish leaders and vows, "I Myself will search for My sheep and look after them," appointing a single shepherd from David's line to tend them (Ezekiel 34:11–12; Ezekiel 34:23). Jesus fulfills both that prophecy and the beloved confidence of the psalmist: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (Psalms 23:1).

**One flock and one Shepherd uniting Jew and Gentile.** Speaking of "other sheep not of this fold" (10:16) foretells Gentiles hearing the gospel and entering God's family. Paul later celebrates that very unity, declaring that Christ broke down the dividing wall separating Jew and Gentile to make the two into one (Ephesians 2:14–16).

**"I and the Father are one" echoes Israel's monotheistic confession.** Every Jew recited daily the foundational creed: "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Naming Himself "one" with the Father, Jesus never fractures monotheism; He opens it from within to reveal that the Son shares the one divine nature (10:30).

**Reasoning from Psalm 82 regarding the title Son of God.** Charged with blasphemy, Jesus cites "I said, you are gods" (Psalms 82:6), where Israel's judges or recipients of God's word bear that title in a representative sense. If Scripture legitimately uses such language for mortals, He reasons, how much less can anyone charge the One consecrated and sent from heaven by the Father with blasphemy for calling Himself God's Son (10:34–36)?

## Notes & Nuances

**"All who came before Me are thieves and robbers" in 10:8 does not refer to Moses or the prophets.** Jesus consistently upholds Old Testament Scripture as God's unbreakable word (10:35). "Those who came before Me" targets bogus religious guides, messianic pretenders, and self-serving shepherds who exploit and cast out the flock—like the Pharisees in chapter 9.

**The Greek gender of "one" in 10:30 safeguards Trinitarian balance.** The text uses the neuter form of "one" (one reality, one essence) rather than the masculine (one person). Jesus is not claiming that Father and Son are a single person wearing two masks, but two distinct persons completely united in nature, purpose, and power.

**The Feast of Dedication illuminates 10:36.** Dedication (Hanukkah) celebrated purifying and reconsecrating the Jerusalem temple after a foreign ruler desecrated it in the second century BC. Right inside that festival, Jesus describes Himself as the One "whom the Father consecrated," signaling that He Himself is the true temple consecrated by God for the world.

**Assurance in 10:28–29 walks hand in hand with discipleship in 10:27.** The promise that "no one will snatch them out of My hand" brings profound comfort to believers, never license for carelessness. Jesus sketches the identifying mark of His true sheep in the very preceding line: they "hear My voice" and "follow Me."

## What Commentators Say

**Athanasius**, fourth-century bishop of Alexandria and staunch defender of Christ's deity against Arianism, viewed John 10:30 as a cornerstone verse. He argued that unity between Father and Son means far more than mere agreement of willpower like two friends; it signifies unity in the one divine essence, since keeping sheep safe in the Son's hand is identical with keeping them in the Father's hand. *(Against the Arians, discourse 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Athanasius's appeal to John 10:30 to demonstrate the Son's shared essence with the Father is classic in discourse 3 -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa across the fourth and fifth centuries, drew a crisp twofold lesson from "I and the Father are one" in 10:30. The plural subject "I and the Father" refutes any heresy collapsing Father and Son into a single person, while "are one" refutes any heresy separating the Son from the Father's divine nature. *(Tractates on the Gospel of John, tractate 48)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's double refutation of opposing heresies from John 10:30 in tractate 48 is well known -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, emphasized that 10:17–18 shows Jesus' death to be completely voluntary—born of love and obedience to the Father rather than defeat by enemies. On 10:28–29, he found rock-solid peace for believers: though our own faith falters, the hand holding us fast is the almighty hand of both Father and Son. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John, on chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Calvin's emphases on voluntary sacrifice and assurance in chapter 10; exact wording not checked -->

**Raymond Brown**, twentieth-century American Catholic New Testament scholar, highlighted the seamless continuity linking chapters 9 and 10: right after Pharisee leaders expel the healed blind man from the synagogue, Jesus portrays them as hired hands abandoning the flock and declares Himself Ezekiel 34's Good Shepherd arriving to seek and gather cast-out sheep. *(The Gospel According to John, Anchor Bible, vol. 1, 1966)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: connecting the blind man's expulsion in chapter 9 directly to the Good Shepherd discourse in chapter 10 is a central insight of Brown -->

**D. A. Carson**, Canadian-American New Testament scholar, explained Jesus' citation of Psalm 82:6 in 10:34–36 as a classic rabbinic "from lesser to greater" argument. If Scripture can refer to Israel's judges as "gods" simply because God's word came to them, then the One whom the Father consecrated and sent from heaven cannot possibly commit blasphemy by calling Himself Son of God. *(The Gospel According to John, Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing Jesus' lesser-to-greater rabbinic logic on Psalm 82:6 is a central point of Carson's commentary on chapter 10 -->
