# John 2 — New Wine and a New Temple

## Story

Three days later there was a wedding at Cana in Galilee. Jesus' mother was there, and Jesus and His disciples had also been invited. Midway through the celebration, the wine ran out. His mother told Him, "They have no more wine." He replied, "Woman, what does this concern have to do with you and Me? My hour has not yet come." Still, she told the servants, "Do whatever He tells you." (2:1–5). At a village wedding, running out of wine brought deep embarrassment upon the host family.

Standing nearby were six stone water jars used for Jewish ceremonial washing, each holding roughly eighty to one hundred twenty liters. Jesus instructed the servants to fill the jars with water, and they filled them right to the brim. Then He told them to draw some out and carry it to the master of the banquet. Tasting water turned into wine, the master had no idea where it came from, though the servants who drew the water knew. Calling the bridegroom aside, he remarked that everyone serves the fine wine first and brings out the cheaper wine once guests have drunk freely, yet you have saved the best wine until now (2:6–10).

This was the first of the signs Jesus performed, at Cana in Galilee. He manifested His glory, and His disciples believed in Him. Afterward He went down to Capernaum with His mother, His brothers, and His disciples, staying there a few days (2:11–12). John does not call this act a "miracle" meant to showcase raw power, but a "sign"—a deed revealing who Jesus is. Water set aside for ritual purification turns into abundant fine wine for a wedding feast.

As the Jewish Passover drew near, Jesus went up to Jerusalem. Inside the temple courts He found merchants selling cattle, sheep, and doves, along with money changers seated at their tables. Twisting cords into a whip, He drove them all out of the temple area along with both sheep and cattle, scattered the coins of the money changers, and overturned their tables. To those selling doves He ordered: get these out of here, and "stop turning My Father's house into a marketplace!" His disciples remembered what was written: "Zeal for Your house will consume me." (2:13–17).

The Jewish authorities challenged Him: what sign can you show us to prove your right to do this? Jesus replied, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up!" They protested: this temple has been under construction for forty-six years, and you will raise it in three days? Yet the temple He spoke of was His own body. After He rose from the dead, His disciples recalled what He had said, and they believed the Scripture and the word Jesus had spoken (2:18–22). The meeting place between humanity and God is no longer a stone sanctuary, but Jesus Himself.

During the Passover festival in Jerusalem, many people saw the signs He was performing and trusted in His name. Yet Jesus did not entrust Himself to them, because He knew all people. He needed no one's testimony about humanity, for He Himself knew what was inside the human heart (2:23–25). There is a kind of faith built only on spectacle, and He knows how shallow it runs.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus brings the overflowing joy of the new era.** Six stone jars reserved for old cleansing rites brim over with finest wine. He does more than rescue a village host from awkwardness; He signals that the messianic age has dawned, bringing grace far richer than anything known before (2:1–11).

**He acts on the Father's "hour," not human pressure.** Even when His mother nudges Him about the missing wine, Jesus remains obedient to the timing His Father sets. That key word "hour" enters the Gospel here and points straight toward the cross near its end (2:4).

**The true temple is no longer a stone edifice, but His own body.** By clearing trade from the temple courts and declaring, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," Jesus reveals that humanity now meets God in Him, through His death and resurrection (2:13–22).

**Not every belief sparked by wonders runs deep.** Crowds in Jerusalem believe His name after watching His signs, yet Jesus refuses to commit Himself to them. Looking straight into the heart, He distinguishes passing fascination from genuine trust (2:23–25).

## Deeper Meaning

**Choice wine signals the banquet of the last days.** Old Testament prophets pictured God's final deliverance as a feast of "aged wine well refined" (Isaiah 25:6) where "the mountains will drip sweet wine" (Amos 9:13). The sign at Cana opens that banquet and points forward to the "wedding supper of the Lamb" at history's climax (Revelation 19:9).

**Zeal that brings opposition.** When the disciples recall "Zeal for Your house will consume me" (2:17), they quote a psalm describing a righteous sufferer whose passion for God's house draws scorn and hostility (Psalms 69:9). The verb "consume" hints that this very devotion will lead Jesus to His death.

**The Lord arriving suddenly in His temple.** Malachi foretold that "the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to His temple" to purify worship (Malachi 3:1). Zechariah likewise envisioned a day when "there will no longer be a trader" profaning the house of the Lord (Zechariah 14:21).

**From Christ's risen body to the Church.** Once Jesus rises from the dead, His body becomes the cornerstone of a new temple not built of stone. Later New Testament letters widen the picture: believers everywhere are joined together on Christ's foundation to form a spiritual house where God dwells (Ephesians 2:19–22).

## Notes & Nuances

**Addressing Mary as "Woman" was not disrespectful.** In Greek of that period, this form of address was polite and dignified—Jesus uses the exact same word from the cross when entrusting His mother to the beloved disciple (19:26). His point in 2:4 is that His mission now answers solely to the Father, no longer to family expectations.

**Why does John place the temple clearing at the start of the Gospel?** Matthew, Mark, and Luke all situate the clearing of the temple in the final week before Jesus' death. Scholars offer two main explanations: either Jesus cleared the temple twice, at the beginning and end of His public ministry, or it happened once and John moved it to the opening chapters to announce immediately that Jesus replaces the old temple.

**The whip of cords was not necessarily used to strike people.** Reading 2:15 closely in Greek, many commentators note that Jesus used the whip to herd sheep and cattle out of the courtyard, while overturning the money changers' tables and commanding dove sellers to carry their wares away. The gesture shows firm, decisive authority rather than uncontrolled rage.

**The figure of forty-six years helps date the scene.** Herod the Great launched his grand renovation of the Jerusalem temple around 20–19 BC. Counting forward forty-six years lands around AD 27–28, aligning smoothly with the opening of Jesus' ministry.

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople, explained that Jesus was not rejecting His mother when He said, "My hour has not yet come." Rather, He gently taught that miracles must follow the timetable set by the Father, rather than family sentiment or human expectation. *(Homilies on the Gospel of John, homily 21)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom treats Jesus' reply to Mary in detail in his homily on the Cana wedding -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa across the fourth and fifth centuries, read the turning of water into wine as an emblem of all Scripture. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, saw Jesus' attendance at a wedding and provision of fine wine as an unmistakable blessing upon marriage and ordinary human joy.
