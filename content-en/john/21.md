# John 21 — Breakfast by the Sea

## Story

Afterward Jesus appeared once more to His disciples beside the Sea of Tiberias. Simon Peter, Thomas called Didymus, Nathanael of Cana in Galilee, the two sons of Zebedee, and two other disciples were together. Simon Peter announced, "I am going fishing." The others replied, "We will go with you." They went out and climbed into the boat, yet throughout that entire night they caught nothing (21:1–3). After all that has happened, Peter drifts back to his old trade.

Just as dawn broke, Jesus stood on the shore, though the disciples did not realize it was Him. He called out, "Children, do you have any fish?" They answered, "No." He told them: cast the net on the right side of the boat and you will find some. They cast it, and instantly were unable to haul it in because of the sheer mass of fish. The disciple whom Jesus loved told Peter, "It is the Lord!" Hearing that, Simon Peter wrapped his outer garment around himself—for he was stripped for work—and jumped straight into the sea. The other disciples came ashore in the boat towing the full net, since they were only about ninety meters from land (21:4–8). The beloved disciple recognizes Him first; Peter cannot wait to reach Him.

Stepping onto the beach, they saw a charcoal fire burning with fish laid on it and bread beside it. Jesus told them, "Bring some of the fish you have just caught." Simon Peter climbed back aboard and dragged the net ashore, bursting with one hundred fifty-three large fish; even with so many, the net did not tear. Jesus invited them, "Come and have breakfast." None of the disciples dared ask Him, "Who are you?" because they knew it was the Lord. Jesus stepped forward, took the bread and gave it to them, and did the same with the fish. This was now the third time Jesus revealed Himself to the disciples after rising from the dead (21:9–14). The risen Lord arrives not with a lecture, but with breakfast waiting.

When they had finished eating, Jesus asked Simon Peter: "Simon, son of John, do you love Me more than these?" Peter replied, "Yes, Lord; You know that I love You." Jesus told him, "Feed My lambs." A second time He asked, "Simon, son of John, do you love Me?" He answered, "Yes, Lord; You know that I love You." Jesus told him, "Tend My sheep." (21:15–16).

A third time He asked, "Simon, son of John, do you love Me?" Peter felt grieved because Jesus asked him a third time, and answered, "Lord, You know everything; You know that I love You!" Jesus told him, "Feed My sheep." (21:17). Three denials beside a charcoal fire in the high priest's courtyard; now three confessions of love beside another charcoal fire. Peter is not merely forgiven—he is entrusted with tending his Master's flock.

Truly, Jesus added, when you were younger you dressed yourself and walked wherever you wished; yet when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, another will dress you, and lead you where you do not want to go. He said this to indicate by what kind of death Peter would glorify God. Then He told him, "Follow Me." (21:18–19). Back in chapter 13, Peter vowed to lay down his life for his Master and failed; now his Master foretells that he will truly do so.

Turning around, Peter saw the disciple whom Jesus loved following behind—the same one who had leaned back against Jesus' chest at supper to ask who was betraying Him. Seeing him, Peter asked, "Lord, what about this man?" Jesus replied: if I want him to remain until I come, what is that to you? You follow Me! Word consequently spread among the brothers that this disciple would not die. Yet Jesus did not say he would not die, only, "If I want him to remain until I come, what is that to you?" (21:20–23). Each follower walks an individual road behind Christ; there is no place for measuring one's road against another's.

This very disciple is the one testifying to these things and writing them down, and we know his testimony is true. Jesus did many other things as well; if every single one were written down, I suppose the whole world itself could not contain the books that would be written (21:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus meets His disciples right back in their familiar spot.** After the resurrection, the disciples do not launch grand enterprises; they head back to the lake and their nets, toiling all night empty-handed (21:3). Jesus does not wait for them to track Him down. Standing on shore at first light, He hails them warmly as "Children" (21:5) and turns an empty night into a bursting catch.

**He provides the meal, yet invites them to contribute.** Charcoal fire, fish, and bread already sit ready before the disciples haul in a single fish (21:9). Still He asks, "Bring some of the fish you have just caught" (21:10). The meal belongs to Him, yet He welcomes their catch into it. Human labor never replaces the Lord's work; the Lord graciously folds it in.

**Restoration never pretends failure never happened.** Three questions hurt Peter's heart (21:17) precisely because they echo his three denials. Yet Jesus asks not to rub salt in wounds. After every answer, He hands Peter a fresh charge. The leader called to shepherd the flock is not someone who never stumbled, but someone who fell, found grace, and loves his Master.

**The sheep remain His sheep.** Jesus never says *tend your sheep*; three times He specifies "My lambs" and "My sheep" (21:15–17). Every under-shepherd serves on trust, never owning the flock. And the cost of shepherding is spelled out plainly: one day another will bind Peter and lead him where he does not wish to go (21:18–19).

**The Gospel's closing call is "Follow Me."** Those were the exact words with which Jesus called His first disciples (John 1:43). John ends right where it started, now carrying far deeper weight: follow Him even unto death, without peering sideways to compare your calling with someone else's (21:19–22).

## Deeper Meaning

**The catch of fish from his first calling.** Luke records an eerily similar haul of fish when Peter was first called: an empty night of labor followed by casting the net at Jesus' command for an overwhelming catch (Luke 5:5–6). Back then Peter dropped to his knees crying, "Depart from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man" (Luke 5:8), and Jesus promised he would catch people (Luke 5:10). Here in John 21, instead of begging Jesus to stay away, Peter plunges into the water to reach Him—recommissioned at the very kind of scene where his calling began.

**Shepherds who must not feed only themselves.** Ezekiel denounced Israel's negligent rulers: "Woe to the shepherds of Israel who only feed themselves!" (Ezekiel 34:2). Jesus, the Good Shepherd of chapter 10, now entrusts His flock to Peter. Years later, Peter himself writes to church elders: "Shepherd the flock of God that is among you," not for greedy gain, awaiting the appearance of "the Chief Shepherd" (1 Peter 5:2–4).

**A net full of every kind of fish.** Ezekiel beheld a river flowing from the temple that revived the Dead Sea, drawing fishermen to cast nets along its shores for fish as varied and abundant as the great sea (Ezekiel 47:9–10). Jesus likewise compared heaven's kingdom to "a net thrown into the sea, gathering fish of every kind" (Matthew 13:47). The brimming yet untorn net (21:11) has long been read as an emblem of the Church gathering every nation without fracturing.

**Peter remembers his Master's forecast.** Jesus' words foretelling Peter's martyrdom (21:18–19) resurface in the second letter bearing Peter's name: "I know that the putting off of my body will be soon, as our Lord Jesus Christ made clear to me" (2 Peter 1:14). Early church tradition records Peter dying as a martyr in Rome under Nero, with "stretching out your hands" widely understood as crucifixion.

**The risen Lord dining with disciples.** Luke records the risen Jesus eating broiled fish right before the disciples to confirm He was no ghost (Luke 24:42–43). Here He not only shares a meal, He cooks and serves it—and His gesture of taking bread and fish to hand them (21:13) echoes feeding the five thousand back in chapter 6 (John 6:11).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was chapter 21 added later as an epilogue?** Verses 20:30–31 sound so naturally like a book conclusion that many scholars regard chapter 21 as a postscript. Verse 21:24 shifts to "we know that his testimony is true," as though a circle of companions endorses the beloved disciple's account. Some think the evangelist himself added the epilogue; others suggest his disciples appended it, perhaps shortly after his death. Either way, every surviving ancient manuscript of John includes chapter 21.

**Do the two Greek words for "love" carry different meanings?** In Greek, Jesus uses *agapaō* twice and switches to *phileō* the third time, whereas Peter uses *phileō* all three times. Some readers hear subtle pathos: Jesus steps down to Peter's humbler word to meet him where he is. Many scholars, however, point out that John uses both verbs interchangeably throughout the Gospel—even describing the Father's love for the Son with *agapaō* in one place and *phileō* in another (John 3:35; John 5:20). What grieved Peter was not a shift in vocabulary, but being asked three times.

**What does the number 153 mean?** Since the Church Fathers, readers have hunted symbolic meanings behind 153: mathematical formulas, or an ancient zoology tally representing all known species of fish (and thus all nations). Others see no hidden cipher at all—simply fishermen counting the catch of a lifetime and an eyewitness remembering the exact tally. Because John gives no explanation, every symbol remains guesswork.

**Who or what are "these" in "more than these"?** Verse 21:15 allows three readings: do you love Me more than the other disciples love Me; do you love Me more than you love these companions; or do you love Me more than these boats, nets, and fishing gear? Many favor the first reading, recalling Peter's earlier boast of surpassing everyone else's loyalty (John 13:37). Now humbled, Peter refuses to compare himself with anyone, simply answering, "Lord, You know."

**Who is the beloved disciple?** Verse 21:24 identifies him as the eyewitness who wrote these things, still without giving a name. Second-century church tradition names John son of Zebedee. Some scholars propose a distinct Jerusalem disciple outside the Twelve, while a few even speculate Lazarus, earlier called "the one You love" (John 11:3). Verse 21:23 implies that as the Gospel took final shape, this disciple had either died or neared death, prompting the community to correct rumors that Jesus promised he would never die.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, noticed that 153 equals the sum of every integer from 1 through 17. He split 17 into 10 plus 7: ten representing the Ten Commandments of the law, and seven representing the sevenfold Spirit who imparts grace. Since law cannot save apart from grace, 153 symbolizes the full company of the redeemed where law and grace meet. *(Tractates on the Gospel of John, tractate 122)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's triangular-number reading of 153 from 1 to 17 (10 + 7) is a famous section of tractate 122 -->

**Jerome**, Latin Bible translator around AD 400, explained 153 differently. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, argued on 21:15–17 that Jesus restores Peter to the apostolic office he forfeited through denial. In Calvin's reading, the passage never elevates Peter above the other apostles, since tending Christ's flock belongs to every faithful pastor—using this point to reject claims of papal supremacy drawn from these verses. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Calvin treats this passage as Peter's restoration rather than primacy and opposes papal claims here; exact phrasing unchecked -->

**Brooke Foss Westcott**, nineteenth-century English New Testament scholar and Anglican bishop, championed a meaningful distinction between the two Greek love verbs across 21:15–17. In his reading, Jesus' first two questions ask for higher, deliberate devotion, whereas Peter answers with a humbler verb of warm personal affection—reluctant after his fall to claim the loftier love. *(The Gospel according to St. John, 1881)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Westcott maintaining the semantic distinction between the two verbs here; exact wording of his definitions unchecked -->

**Raymond Brown**, American Catholic New Testament scholar, viewed chapter 21 as an epilogue appended by an editor after the original conclusion at 20:30–31. Far from an alien addition, however, the editor belonged to the evangelist's own circle and addressed two live questions: Peter's pastoral role and the death of the beloved disciple. *(The Gospel According to John, Anchor Bible, vol. 2, 1970)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: treating chapter 21 as an intra-community epilogue addressing Peter and the beloved disciple is a central claim of Brown -->

**Richard Bauckham**, British New Testament scholar, read 21:24 as a straightforward eyewitness claim identifying the beloved disciple as the Gospel's author—yet not John son of Zebedee, rather a Jerusalem disciple known to early sources as John the Elder, explaining his acquaintance with the high priest and his close focus on Jerusalem events. *(Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2006)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: identifying the beloved disciple and author as John the Elder appears across the closing chapters of Bauckham's book -->
