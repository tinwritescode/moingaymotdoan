# John 15 — The Vine and the Branches

## Story

Jesus continued: "I am the true vine, and My Father is the vinedresser." Every branch in Me that bears no fruit He cuts away, and every branch that does bear fruit He prunes so it will bear even more fruit. You are already clean because of the word I have spoken to you. Abide in Me, and I will abide in you. No branch can bear fruit by itself unless it remains in the vine; neither can you, unless you abide in Me (15:1–4). Throughout the Old Testament, Israel was pictured as the vineyard God planted. Now Jesus declares that He Himself is the true vine.

"I am the vine; you are the branches." Whoever abides in Me and I in him bears abundant fruit, "for apart from Me you can do nothing." Anyone who does not abide in Me is thrown aside like a withered branch; such branches are gathered up, tossed into the fire, and burned. If you abide in Me and My words abide in you, ask whatever you wish and it will be done for you. My Father is glorified when you bear much fruit and so prove to be My disciples (15:5–8). A branch does not strain to manufacture grapes; it simply stays connected to the vine.

Just as the Father has loved Me, so have I loved you; abide in My love. If you keep My commandments, you will abide in My love, just as I have kept My Father's commandments and abide in His love. I have spoken these things to you so that My joy may be in you, and that your joy may be complete (15:9–11).

"This is My commandment: that you love one another as I have loved you." "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." You are My friends if you do what I command you. No longer do I call you servants, because a servant does not know what his master is doing; instead I have called you friends, for everything I heard from My Father I have made known to you. You did not choose Me, but I chose you and appointed you to go and bear fruit—fruit that will remain—so that whatever you ask the Father in My name, He may give you. This is My command to you: love one another (15:12–17). He speaks of laying down one's life for friends only hours before doing exactly that.

If the world hates you, remember that it hated Me before it hated you. If you belonged to the world, the world would love its own; yet because you do not belong to the world, and I chose you out of the world, the world hates you. Remember the word I told you: a servant is not greater than his master. If they persecuted Me, they will persecute you too; if they kept My word, they will keep yours as well. They will treat you this way because of My name, since they do not know the One who sent Me (15:18–21).

If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not be guilty of sin; now, however, they have no excuse for their sin. Whoever hates Me hates My Father as well. If I had not performed among them works no one else ever did, they would have no guilt; yet now they have seen them and still hate both Me and My Father. Thus the word written in their Law comes true: "They hated Me without cause." (15:22–25).

When the Helper comes, whom I will send to you from the Father—the Spirit of truth who proceeds from the Father—He will bear witness about Me. And you also must testify, because you have been with Me from the beginning (15:26–27). Disciples never witness alone: the Holy Spirit testifies right alongside them.

## Key Takeaways

**Fruit grows from abiding, not from straining.** A branch does not grit its teeth to squeeze out grapes. It simply remains attached to the stem, and sap from the vine handles the rest. Jesus repeats "abide in Me" again and again (15:4–7). Discipleship begins not by scrambling to do more for Him, but by refusing to drift from Him, "for apart from Me you can do nothing" (15:5).

**The vinedresser prunes even fruitful branches.** Barren branches are removed; fruitful branches are trimmed back so they yield an even richer harvest (15:2). The gardener cuts out of desire for more fruit. Painful seasons of loss in a believer's life are not proof of God's abandonment, but often signs of His attentive care.

**Elevated from servants to friends.** A servant executes orders without sharing his master's confidences; a friend is invited into the master's heart. Jesus names His disciples friends because He has shared everything He received from the Father (15:15)—and reminds them He chose them first, not the reverse (15:16). That friendship is measured by the highest love of all: "laying down one's life for one's friends" (15:13).

**Following Christ means expecting opposition without shock.** Because the world hated the Master, it will hate His followers too (15:18–20). Jesus never tells disciples to provoke hostility, only not to be caught off guard when it arrives. Nor do they stand isolated: the Spirit bears witness to Him, and they testify alongside the Spirit (15:26–27).

## Deeper Meaning

**God's vineyard produced wild grapes.** Across the Old Testament, Israel stands as God's planted vine: "You brought a vine out of Egypt" (Psalms 80:8). Isaiah's vineyard song tells of an owner who tended his vines with utmost care, "expecting it to yield good grapes, yet it yielded only wild grapes" (Isaiah 5:2). Jeremiah lamented, "I planted you as a choice vine" (Jeremiah 2:21), only for it to turn degenerate. Declaring "I am the true vine," Jesus presents Himself as Israel fulfilled—the faithful vine at last bearing good fruit (15:1).

**Vine wood is fit only for the fire.** Ezekiel asks what use vine wood serves: you cannot even carve a peg from it to hang a pot; "it is given to the fire for fuel" (Ezekiel 15:3–4). A vine justifies its existence solely by producing grapes. The dried branches cast into flames in verse 6 draw straight from that prophet, echoing John the Baptist's warning that every barren tree is cut down and thrown into the fire (Matthew 3:10).

**Fruit means love and joy.** Right after the vine metaphor, Jesus speaks of love, obedience, and overflowing joy (15:9–12). Paul similarly lists "the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace," and their companion virtues (Galatians 5:22). Above external achievements, the primary fruit of the true vine is a character shaped like Jesus.

**Friends of God.** In the Old Testament, only rare figures received the title friend of God. Abraham is named "My friend" (Isaiah 41:8; James 2:23). The Lord spoke with Moses face to face, "as a man speaks with his friend" (Exodus 33:11). An intimacy once reserved for patriarch and lawgiver is now extended by Jesus to a circle of ordinary disciples.

**A love surpassing even dying for friends.** Jesus says no love exceeds laying down one's life for friends (15:13). Paul points out an even deeper wonder: God proves His love in that "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). The very companions Jesus calls friends tonight will scatter before morning—and still He dies for them.

**"They hated Me without cause" echoes the righteous sufferer.** Verse 25 quotes the Psalms, where an innocent sufferer cries out, "Those who hate me without reason outnumber the hairs of my head" (Psalms 69:4; cf. Psalms 35:19). Jesus refers to the Psalms under the broad heading "their Law," encompassing all of Scripture (15:25). David endured groundless hatred, and David's Son walks the same path.

## Notes & Nuances

**Do burned branches represent believers losing salvation?** Verse 6 sounds stark, and Christians interpret it in three main ways. The Reformed tradition identifies withered branches as superficial followers who looked attached to Christ without ever drawing true life from Him—like Judas, who just walked out. Many other traditions take it as a real warning that a believer can sever ties with Christ, mirroring Paul's caution regarding grafted branches: "Otherwise you too will be cut off" (Romans 11:22). A minority read the fire as loss of reward rather than loss of eternal life (1 Corinthians 3:15). Whatever view one holds, the central warning stays unmistakable: apart from the vine, there is no life.

**"Cuts off" or "lifts up"?** The Greek verb in verse 2 can mean either "takes away" or "lifts up." A popular devotional reading imagines a vinedresser propping trailing branches off damp soil so they bear fruit. Most scholars reject that interpretation here, because verse 6 plainly describes barren branches cast outside and burned—supporting standard translations like "cuts off" or "takes away."

**Who is "the world" that hates the disciples?** In John, "world" carries two senses: sometimes humanity loved by God—"For God so loved the world" (John 3:16)—and sometimes human society organized in resistance to Him, as here. Because the next chapter mentions expulsion from synagogues (John 16:2), for John's first readers the hostile "world" could include neighboring Jewish communities. Yet this text never equates the Jewish people with "the world." Tragically, once Christians later gained political power, they persecuted Jews—betraying the very love command Jesus gives in this chapter.

**The Spirit "proceeding from the Father" and Christendom's great schism.** Verse 26 states that the Spirit of truth "proceeds from the Father," wording echoed in the Nicene Creed. Later, Western churches added the Latin word *filioque* ("and from the Son") to the creed, a unilateral change rejected by Eastern churches and a major factor in the East–West split of 1054. Verse 26 itself holds both truths together: the Spirit proceeds from the Father, and the Son sends Him.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, deployed verse 5 against Pelagius, who taught that humans can achieve goodness on their own strength. Augustine stressed that Jesus did not say apart from Him we can do very little, but "nothing." On verse 6 he put it crisply: a vine branch belongs in only one of two places—either on the vine, or in the fire. *(Tractates on the Gospel of John, tractates 80–81)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's famous emphases on "nothing, not a little" and "either the vine or the fire" in his John 15 tractates are well remembered -->

**Cyril of Alexandria**, fifth-century bishop, read the vine imagery as teaching genuine union with Christ. Through the Holy Spirit we are grafted into Him and nourished by His life—especially in the Eucharist—just as a branch draws sap from the trunk. This union is far deeper than mere agreement of outlook between friends; it is real participation in the life of the Son. *(Commentary on John, book 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Cyril's emphasis on real participation in Christ through Spirit and Eucharist on John 15; exact book number and wording unverified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observed that verse 1 first humbles us by revealing our natural barrenness: every good fruit flows solely from Christ. Regarding the cut-off branches in verses 2 and 6, he identified them as people outwardly joined to the vine who never truly took root in Him. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John, on chapter 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain this summarizes Calvin's view of fruitless branches; exact wording not checked -->

**C. S. Lewis**, twentieth-century British author and scholar, closed his chapter on friendship in *The Four Loves* with verse 16. We imagine we select our own friends, yet to any Christian circle of friends Christ might say: you did not choose one another; I chose you for one another. Friendship is never a prize earned by our good taste, but a gift through which Christ lets us glimpse His beauty in another person. *(The Four Loves, 1960, chapter on Friendship)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: clearly recalled Lewis closing the Friendship chapter with John 15:16 -->

**Flavius Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, described the grand doorway of the Jerusalem temple's sanctuary crowned by a massive golden vine with grape clusters as tall as a person. The Mishnah also mentions that golden vine, noting worshipers donated golden leaves or clusters to hang upon it. To first-century Jews, the vine stood right over God's doorway as Israel's emblem—meaning Jesus' listeners may well have pictured that golden vine when He declared, "I am the true vine." *(The Jewish War, book 5; Mishnah, Middot, chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the golden temple vine is attested in both sources; neither comments on John 15 directly, providing historical context, with the closing link as inference -->

**Adele Reinhartz**, Canadian Jewish New Testament scholar, examined the hostility language of verses 18–25 alongside John's portrayal of "the Jews." She argued that John draws a sharp covenant boundary placing believers inside fellowship with the Father and nonbelievers outside—urging Christian readers to reckon honestly with how heavily such rhetoric weighed on Jewish communities across two millennia rather than skimming past it as harmless. *(Cast Out of the Covenant: Jews and Anti-Judaism in the Gospel of John, 2018)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain this reflects the central argument of Reinhartz's book; exact placement of her treatment of 15:18–25 not checked -->
