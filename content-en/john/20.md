# John 20 — The Empty Tomb and Resurrection Appearances

## Story

Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene went to the tomb and saw that the stone had been removed from the entrance. She ran to Simon Peter and the other disciple, the one Jesus loved, and cried, "They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid Him!" (20:1–2).

Peter and the other disciple set off toward the tomb. Both ran together, though the other disciple outran Peter and arrived first. Stooping to look in, he saw the linen strips lying there, yet did not step inside. Simon Peter came right behind him, walked straight into the tomb, and saw the linen strips lying there, while the cloth that had wrapped Jesus' head was not lying with the linen strips, but rolled up separately in a place by itself. Then the other disciple, who had reached the tomb first, also went inside; he saw and believed. Up to that point they still did not understand from Scripture that He must rise from the dead. Then the two disciples returned home (20:3–10). Grave robbers would never leave burial wrappings behind, still less fold the head cloth neatly aside.

Mary remained standing outside the tomb weeping. As she wept, she stooped to look inside and saw two angels in white seated where Jesus' body had lain, one at the head and one at the foot. They asked her, "Woman, why are you weeping?" She replied, "Because they have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid Him." Saying this, she turned around and saw Jesus standing there, though she did not realize it was Jesus (20:11–14).

Jesus asked her, "Woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you seeking?" Supposing Him to be the gardener, she said: sir, if you carried Him away, tell me where you laid Him, and I will take Him. Jesus spoke her name: "Mary!" Turning fully toward Him, she cried in Aramaic, "Rabboni!"—which means Teacher. Jesus told her: do not cling to Me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father; instead go to My brothers and tell them, "I am ascending to My Father and your Father, to My God and your God." Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, "I have seen the Lord!" and shared everything He had told her (20:15–18). She recognizes Him not by His face, but by His voice calling her name—just like the Good Shepherd calling His sheep by name in chapter 10.

On the evening of that first day of the week, with doors locked where the disciples were gathered for fear of the Jewish authorities, Jesus came, stood right among them, and said, "Peace be with you!" Saying this, He showed them His hands and His side. Seeing the Lord, the disciples were overjoyed. Again Jesus told them: peace be with you! "As the Father has sent Me, even so I am sending you." Having said this, He breathed on them and said, "Receive the Holy Spirit." If you forgive anyone's sins, they are forgiven; if you withhold forgiveness from anyone, it is withheld (20:19–23). Just as God breathed life into Adam at creation, Jesus breathes upon His disciples to launch new creation.

Now Thomas, called Didymus, one of the Twelve, was absent when Jesus appeared. The other disciples told him, "We have seen the Lord!" He replied: unless I see the nail marks in His hands, put my finger into the mark of the nails, and place my hand into His side, I will never believe (20:24–25).

Eight days later His disciples were indoors again, this time with Thomas present. Though the doors stood locked, Jesus came, stood among them, and said, "Peace be with you!" Then He turned to Thomas: put your finger here and examine My hands; reach out your hand and place it in My side; "do not disbelieve, but believe!" Thomas answered Him, "My Lord and my God!" Jesus said: have you believed because you saw Me? "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed!" (20:26–29). The most skeptical disciple utters the highest confession in the entire Gospel—and the blessing that follows belongs to every reader since.

Jesus performed many other signs in the presence of His disciples that are not recorded in this book. "But these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in His name." (20:30–31). Here John states directly why he wrote this Gospel.

## Key Takeaways

**Each person arrives at faith by a different road.** The beloved disciple sees empty linen cloths and believes before ever seeing Jesus (20:8). Mary looks straight at Him without recognition until He speaks her name (20:14–16). Thomas asks to see and touch. Jesus never forces all three down one identical track; He meets each person exactly where they stand.

**The first herald of resurrection is a woman.** Jesus appears first to Mary Magdalene and dispatches her to report to the apostles (20:17–18). Her message carries astonishing news: He calls them "My brothers" and speaks of "My Father and your Father" (20:17). On the far side of the cross, His followers stand welcomed as siblings inside His own family.

**The risen body still bears its scars.** The very first thing Jesus shows the frightened room is His hands and His side (20:20). A week later He invites Thomas to inspect those same marks (20:27). The risen Lord is no substitute figure, but the very Jesus who hung on the cross. Wounds are not erased; they become His unmistakable badge of identity.

**From locked doors to global mission.** Terrified, the disciples bar the doors (20:19). Rather than scolding them, Jesus twice speaks "Peace be with you!" then sends them forth just as the Father sent Him and breathes His Spirit upon them (20:21–22). Peace is never meant to keep us huddled indoors; it steadies us to step outside.

**This Gospel was written for readers who have never seen.** The beatitude "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed!" (20:29) stands right before John's statement of purpose (20:30–31). Believers today do not stand one rung beneath Thomas; through written eyewitness testimony we receive the exact same life in His name.

## Deeper Meaning

**The breath of new creation.** Breathing upon the disciples in 20:22 deliberately echoes God breathing "the breath of life into his nostrils" when creating Adam (Genesis 2:7), and recalls Ezekiel's valley of dry bones where breath is summoned to "breathe on these slain, that they may live" (Ezekiel 37:9). Terrified disciples, virtually lifeless inside a locked room, are breathed back to life.

**The first day of the week.** Twice John points out that these events unfold on "the first day of the week" (20:1; 20:19), echoing creation week at the start of Genesis. Fittingly, Mary mistakes Him for the gardener—for this dawn launches new creation, captured in Revelation's pledge, "Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5).

**A woman searching for her beloved.** Since ancient times, readers have placed Mary beside the bride in Song of Solomon, who combs the city in darkness until she finds "him whom my soul loves; I held him, and would not let him go" (Song of Solomon 3:4). Mary likewise seeks Him before dawn and longs to cling to Him (20:17)—yet Jesus teaches her a deeper closeness: not clutching Him tightly, but going out to proclaim Him.

**Not ashamed to call them brothers.** Hebrews marvels that Jesus "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Hebrews 2:11). Saying "My Father and your Father, My God and your God" (20:17) carries the covenant tenderness of Ruth's vow to Naomi: "Your God shall be my God" (Ruth 1:16)—the unmistakable language of adoption into one family.

**A confession rounding off the entire Gospel.** John's opening sentence declared, "The Word was God" (John 1:1). Near the Gospel's close, a human disciple looks Jesus in the eye and cries, "My Lord and my God!" (20:28), echoing the psalmist's address to Israel's Lord: "my God and my Lord!" (Psalms 35:23). What the narrator announced at page one, a believer now confesses face to face.

## Notes & Nuances

**What does "do not touch Me" mean?** Verse 20:17 puzzles readers because a week later Jesus invites Thomas to touch His wounds (20:27). The Greek phrasing actually means *stop clinging to Me*. Most interpreters understand that Mary wants to hold onto her Master on the old earthly terms, yet their relationship is entering a new phase: He is ascending to the Father and will henceforth dwell with them through the Spirit. For Thomas, touching served to confirm faith; for Mary, clinging sought to keep things as they used to be.

**Who holds authority to forgive sins?** Verse 20:23 has been read differently across Christian traditions. Catholic and Orthodox traditions see here the scriptural root of priestly absolution in confession. Protestant traditions generally read it as the Church's collective authority in heralding the gospel: whoever believes the message receives forgiveness, while whoever rejects it remains bound in sin. Both agree that God alone forgives sins; they differ on the instruments He uses—parallel to Matthew's binding and loosing (Matthew 16:19).

**Were there two outpourings of the Spirit?** John depicts Jesus imparting the Spirit on Easter evening (20:22), whereas Acts records the Spirit descending at Pentecost fifty days later (Acts 2:1–4). Some think John narrates the same gift thematically, linking it closely to resurrection. Others view Easter evening as a genuine initial endowment, followed by Pentecost as the full, public outpouring empowering global mission.

**Thomas does not deserve his caricature as uniquely doubtful.** He asked for nothing more than what the other disciples had already received—seeing Jesus' hands and side (20:20; 20:25). Nor does John say he actually touched Him; merely seeing Jesus drew forth the boldest confession of all. Earlier, it was Thomas who rallied the others to march into Judea and die alongside their Master (John 11:16).

**Was John written to spark new faith or deepen existing faith?** In 20:31 Greek manuscripts split by a single letter between *that you may come to believe* and *that you may continue believing*. Consequently some scholars see an evangelistic book aimed at seekers, while others see a pastoral book nurturing established Christians. Likely both hold true: the Gospel welcomes outsiders and feeds insiders alike.

## What Commentators Say

**Gregory the Great**, late sixth-century pope in Rome, coined an enduring tribute to Thomas: Thomas's doubt aided our faith far more than the quick faith of the other disciples, because through his seeing and touching, every lingering doubt in our own minds about Christ's bodily resurrection is healed. *(Forty Gospel Homilies, homily 26)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Gregory's famous observation that Thomas's doubt benefited us more than the other disciples' swift belief in homily 26 is widely quoted -->

**Thomas Aquinas**, thirteenth-century Dominican theologian, hailed Mary Magdalene as the "apostle to the apostles," because she was sent to carry resurrection news to the apostles themselves. He noted a striking reversal: just as a woman brought words of death to a man in Eden, now a woman brings words of life to the disciples. *(Commentary on the Gospel of John, on chapter 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the title "apostle to the apostles" is closely tied to Aquinas on John 20; less certain whether the Eve reversal stands in the exact same passage -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, argued on 20:23 that authority to forgive sins is not a private power held by clergy hearing confessions. Rather, Christ entrusts His disciples with preaching the gospel: when they proclaim pardon in His name, believers are genuinely forgiven because God Himself backs His word. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Calvin's interpretation tying 20:23 to gospel proclamation; exact wording unchecked -->

**D. A. Carson**, Canadian-American New Testament scholar, wrote an influential article on 20:31 arguing from Greek syntax that the sentence answers *Who is the Christ?* with *Jesus*. On that basis, he maintained that John's Gospel serves primarily an evangelistic purpose, directed to Jewish readers and God-fearers who had not yet recognized Jesus as Messiah. *("The Purpose of the Fourth Gospel," Journal of Biblical Literature, 1987)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the full article subtitle explicitly mentions 20:31, and this grammatical argument for an evangelistic aim is the article's core thesis -->

**Pinchas Lapide**, German Orthodox Jewish scholar, reached an extraordinary conclusion from within Judaism: he regarded Jesus' resurrection as a real historical event. Nothing else, he argued, adequately explains how a terrified band hiding behind locked doors transformed into fearless witnesses willing to die for their testimony—even though Lapide himself did not accept Jesus as Israel's Messiah. *(The Resurrection of Jesus: A Jewish Perspective, 1977)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Lapide's affirmation of a historical resurrection without accepting Jesus' messiahship is the central thesis of the book; discusses resurrection appearances broadly rather than John 20 alone -->
