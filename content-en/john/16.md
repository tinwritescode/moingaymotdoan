# John 16 — Sorrow Will Turn to Joy

## Story

Jesus continued: I have told you these things so you will not fall away. They will put you out of the synagogues. Indeed, an hour is coming when anyone who kills you will think he is offering service to God. They will do such things because they have known neither the Father nor Me. I have told you ahead of time so that when that hour arrives, you will remember I warned you. I did not tell you these things from the beginning because I was still with you (16:1–4).

Now I am going to the One who sent Me, yet none of you asks Me, "Where are You going?" Instead, because I have spoken these things to you, sorrow has filled your hearts. Still, I tell you the truth: it is to your advantage that I go away; for if I do not go away, the Helper will not come to you, whereas if I depart, I will send Him to you (16:5–7). All the disciples see is loss; Jesus insists His departure works for their good.

When He comes, He will convict the world concerning sin, righteousness, and judgment: concerning sin, because they do not believe in Me; concerning righteousness, because I am going to the Father and you will see Me no longer; concerning judgment, because the ruler of this world stands already judged (16:8–11).

I still have many things to tell you, yet you cannot bear them right now. When the Spirit of truth comes, He will guide you into all truth. He will not speak on His own, but will speak whatever He hears and declare to you things to come. He will glorify Me, because He will take from what is Mine and disclose it to you. Everything the Father has belongs to Me; that is why I said He will take from what is Mine and make it known to you (16:12–15).

"A little while, and you will see Me no longer; and again a little while, and you will see Me." Some of His disciples murmured to one another: what does He mean by "a little while"? We cannot figure out what He is talking about. Sensing they wanted to question Him, Jesus said: truly, you will weep and lament, while the world rejoices; you will be sorrowful, yet your sorrow will turn into joy (16:16–20).

When a woman is giving birth, she feels pain because her hour has come; yet once the baby is born, she remembers the anguish no longer for joy that a human being has been brought into the world. So it is with you: right now you have sorrow, yet I will see you again, your hearts will rejoice, and "no one will take your joy from you." On that day you will ask Me nothing further. Truly, whatever you ask the Father in My name, He will give you. Until now you have asked nothing in My name; ask and you will receive, so your joy may be complete (16:21–24). Pain is not bypassed, but becomes the very doorway to joy, like the pangs of childbirth.

I have spoken these things to you in figures of speech; an hour is coming when I will no longer use figurative language, but will tell you plainly about the Father. On that day you will ask in My name. I do not say I will plead with the Father on your behalf, because the Father Himself loves you, since you have loved Me and believed that I came from God. I came from the Father and entered the world; now I am leaving the world and returning to the Father (16:25–28).

His disciples said: now You are speaking plainly and not in figures! Now we realize You know everything and need no one to question You; therefore we believe You came from God. Jesus replied, "Do you now believe?" Behold, an hour is coming—indeed it has already come—when you will be scattered, each to his own home, leaving Me all alone. Yet I am not alone, because the Father is with Me. I have told you these things so that in Me you may have peace. "In the world you will have tribulation; yet take heart—I have overcome the world!" (16:29–33). Moments before arrest, He speaks of victory in the past tense, as a triumph already won.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus warns ahead of time so His disciples will not be blindsided.** He promises no smooth road. They face expulsion from synagogues, and zealots will even kill them supposing "they are offering service to God" (16:2). Forewarning them ensures that when hardship strikes, they will not assume God has abandoned them, but recognize that events unfold inside His sovereign word (16:1–4).

**His physical departure becomes a gift.** To the disciples, His leaving feels purely like bereavement. Jesus flips their outlook: "It is to your advantage that I go away" (16:7). On earth in a human body, He occupied one place with one small band; returning to the Father and sending the Spirit means the Spirit abides with every believer everywhere (16:5–7).

**The Spirit carries out a double task: exposing the world and guiding disciples.** Toward the world, He acts like a courtroom prosecutor exposing the root sin of unbelief, vindicating Jesus' righteousness despite His condemnation, and proving that the ruler of this world is the one truly condemned (16:8–11). Toward disciples, He serves as guide—never bringing a rival gospel, but drawing from Christ Himself to unveil His glory (16:13–15).

**One event reverses two reactions.** At the cross, disciples weep while "the world rejoices" (16:20). At the resurrection, the very event that broke their hearts becomes the wellspring of lasting gladness. Unlike ordinary happiness, this joy endures: "no one will take your joy from you" (16:22), because it rests on a finished historical event rather than shifting moods.

**Peace resides in Him, never in easy circumstances.** The closing verse sets two realities side by side: tribulation in the world, peace in Christ (16:33). Right after the disciples boast confidently of their faith, Jesus asks gently, "Do you now believe?" (16:31), knowing they are about to flee. Their peace rests not on their fragile loyalty, but on His completed victory (16:31–33).

## Deeper Meaning

**Persecutors convinced they serve God.** The warning in 16:2 came true swiftly, with Paul as its starkest example. Looking back, he confessed he had been "convinced that I ought to do many things opposing the name of Jesus of Nazareth" (Acts 26:9), driven by being "extremely zealous for the traditions of my fathers" (Galatians 1:14). Religious zeal divorced from truly knowing God can turn merciless (16:3).

**Conviction fulfilled at Pentecost.** Jesus promised the Spirit would "convict the world concerning sin" (16:8). Fifty days after Passover, when Peter proclaimed that God had made the crucified Jesus both Lord and Christ, the crowd was "cut to the heart" and cried out, "What shall we do?" (Acts 2:36–37). No debate won them over; the Spirit awakened their consciences.

**Zion's labor pains.** Old Testament prophets repeatedly pictured God's people awaiting redemption like a mother in labor: "like a pregnant woman about to give birth, writhing and crying out in her pains" (Isaiah 26:17). Isaiah even beheld Zion bringing forth a new nation in a single day as soon as labor began (Isaiah 66:8). Jesus applies that image to His own passion: out of those birth pangs, a new people is born.

**The ruler of this world stands condemned.** Verse 16:11 echoes His earlier announcement: "Now the ruler of this world will be cast out" (John 12:31). Paul celebrates the same triumph, declaring that on the cross Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities" (Colossians 2:15). The world assumed it was putting Jesus on trial, when in truth the world and its dark ruler were being judged.

**Faith shares in His victory.** "I have overcome the world" (16:33) rings out again in John's first letter, now applied to believers: "This is the victory that has overcome the world—our faith" (1 John 5:4). Disciples never conquer in their own strength; they overcome by trusting the One who already conquered.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 16:2 is not an indictment of the Jewish people.** Those expelling disciples from synagogues were Jews—and the disciples themselves were Jews. This reflects an internal family rift, not a verdict on an ethnic people. Many scholars believe John wrote late in the first century after some believers faced synagogue exclusion (John 9:22), while others note evidence is thin for any empire-wide ban. Tragically, in later centuries Christians committed the exact sin this verse warns against, persecuting Jews while imagining they served God.

**What does "convict the world" mean?** The Greek verb in 16:8 belongs to courtroom speech: exposing guilt beyond denial. The title Helper (*Parakletos*) also carries legal coloring—an advocate standing beside a client. Both roles fit seamlessly: toward disciples, the Spirit acts as defense counsel; toward the world, He prosecutes—yet prosecutes to spark repentance, just as happened at Pentecost.

**"All truth" does not mean encyclopedic knowledge.** The promise in 16:13 addressed first the apostles commissioned to preach and write concerning Christ. The Spirit did not teach them astronomy or secular history, but led them into full comprehension of Christ's person and work, which they could not yet bear before the cross (16:12). Verse 16:14 supplies the litmus test of the Spirit's voice: "He will glorify Me." Any supposedly new revelation pulling people away from Christ lies outside this promise.

**Does prayer guarantee anything we want?** The pledge in 16:23 hinges on "in My name"—not a lucky charm tacked onto a prayer's end, but praying as one belonging to Jesus in alignment with His will. John's first epistle spells this out plainly: "If we ask anything according to His will, He hears us" (1 John 5:14).

**Does Jesus intercede for us or not?** Verse 16:26 says He does not say He will ask the Father on their behalf, while other Scriptures affirm Christ "intercedes for us" (Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25). There is no conflict. Verses 16:26–27 dismantle any notion of a reluctant, stern Father who must be coaxed by a kinder Son, emphasizing that "the Father Himself loves you." Christ's intercession flows not from overcoming the Father's reluctance, but from Father and Son sharing one heart.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, asked why the Spirit could not arrive while Jesus stayed visibly present. He answered that the disciples clung too tightly to their Master's visible human form; as long as they leaned on sight, they had not yet learned to love Him by faith. He had to withdraw from their eyes in order to dwell within their hearts. *(Tractates on the Gospel of John, on chapter 16)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Augustine's insight on letting go of physical sight to receive the Spirit at 16:7; exact tractate number uncertain, likely around tractate 94 -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, preached extensively across John 14–16. On 16:9 he stressed that the Spirit convicts the world not primarily over isolated misdeeds, but over the root sin of unbelief in Christ. Every other sin finds pardon in Christ; only refusing Him locks the door against forgiveness. *(Sermons on the Gospel of St. John, 1537–1538)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Luther treating unbelief in 16:9 as the root of all sin; exact wording and lecture placement unchecked -->

**Raymond Brown**, twentieth-century American Catholic New Testament scholar, devoted a major appendix to the Helper (*Parakletos*). He pointed out how closely John's portrait of the Spirit mirrors Jesus Himself: both come from the Father, both teach, both bear witness, and both are rejected by the world. In Brown's reading, the Spirit is the living presence of Jesus remaining with His disciples during His physical absence. *(The Gospel According to John, Anchor Bible, vol. 2, 1970, appendix on the Paraclete)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: portraying the Spirit as Jesus' continuing presence during His absence is the core thesis of the Paraclete appendix in vol. 2 -->

**J. Louis Martyn**, American New Testament scholar, read the synagogue expulsion notices in chapters 9, 12, and 16:2 as historical fingerprints of John's own era. On his view, the Gospel simultaneously tells Jesus' story and mirrors a late-first-century community of Jewish believers recently severed from the synagogue. While widely influential, many recent scholars caution that he presses the reconstruction too confidently. *(History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel, 1968)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the synagogue expulsion thesis built on these verses is the centerpiece of Martyn's book -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the massive rabbinic collection of law and debate, relates that after the temple fell, Rabban Gamaliel asked Samuel the Lesser to compose an additional blessing against "heretics" (*minim*) for the daily synagogue prayer. Jewish and Christian scholars still debate whether that liturgy targeted followers of Jesus and whether it connects with 16:2. The Talmud does not comment on John; it simply illuminates the historical setting. *(Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 28b–29a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the tradition of Samuel the Lesser drafting the blessing against heretics in Berakhot 28b is widely cited background rather than commentary on John -->
