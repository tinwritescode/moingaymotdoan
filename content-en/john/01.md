# John 1 — The Word Became Flesh

## Story

John does not open in Bethlehem or beside the Jordan River, but at the very beginning: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." Everything that exists came into being through Him. In Him was life, and that life was the light of humanity. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it (1:1–5). The phrase "in the beginning" echoes the opening words of Genesis.

There came a man sent from God, whose name was John. He was not the light himself, but came to bear witness to the light, so that through him all might believe. The true light was coming into the world. Though the world was made through Him, the world did not recognize Him; He came to His own home, yet His own people did not receive Him. Still, to everyone who received Him—those who believed in His name—He gave the right to become children of God, born not of blood, nor of human desire or a husband's will, but born of God (1:6–13).

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth." We have beheld His glory, the glory of the one and only Son from the Father. John the Baptist bore witness concerning Him: the one coming after me ranks ahead of me, because He existed before me. Out of His fullness we have all received grace upon grace. The law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. No one has ever seen God; the one and only Son, who is at the Father's side, has made Him known (1:14–18). In verse 18, some translations read "the only Son," while many ancient manuscripts read "the only begotten God," explicitly calling the Word God just as in verse 1. This prologue unlocks the entire Gospel: whoever wants to know what God is like should look at Jesus.

Religious leaders in Jerusalem sent priests and Levites to ask John the Baptist, "Who are you?" He answered plainly: I am not the Christ. They asked: are you Elijah, then? Are you the Prophet? To each he replied no. He claimed only to be "the voice of one crying out in the wilderness," just as Isaiah wrote. The Pharisees pressed further: why then are you baptizing? He answered: I baptize with water, yet someone stands right among you whom you do not recognize; I am not worthy even to untie the strap of His sandal. All this took place at Bethany on the far side of the Jordan (1:19–28).

The next day, seeing Jesus walking toward him, John the Baptist declared, "Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" He explained that he himself had not known Him before, yet the One who sent him to baptize had given him a sign: the man on whom he saw the Spirit descend and remain is the one who baptizes with the Holy Spirit. John saw the Spirit come down from heaven like a dove and rest on Him, and therefore testified that He is the Son of God (1:29–34).

The following day, John again pointed Jesus out to two of his disciples, and both began following Jesus. Turning around, Jesus asked them, "What are you seeking?" They asked where He was staying. He replied, "Come and see." They went and spent the day with Him; it was around the tenth hour. One of the two was Andrew. He immediately found his brother Simon and told him, "We have found the Messiah." Bringing Simon to Jesus, Jesus looked intently at him and said, "You are Simon son of John; you shall be called Cephas," which translates as Peter (1:35–42). Jesus' very first question in this Gospel reaches out to every reader: what are you looking for?

The next day Jesus decided to leave for Galilee. Finding Philip, who came from Bethsaida, He told him, "Follow Me." Philip sought out Nathanael and said, "We have found the one Moses wrote about in the Law, and about whom the prophets also wrote—Jesus of Nazareth, son of Joseph." Nathanael asked back, "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" Philip answered simply, "Come and see." (1:43–46).

Seeing Nathanael approaching, Jesus remarked, "Here truly is an Israelite in whom there is no deceit." Surprised, Nathanael asked, "How do you know me?" Jesus answered, "Before Philip called you, I saw you under the fig tree." Nathanael exclaimed, "Rabbi, You are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!" Jesus replied: do you believe simply because I said I saw you under the fig tree? You will see greater things than this. You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man (1:47–51). Like the ladder Jacob beheld in his dream, Jesus Himself is the living link uniting heaven and earth.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus is the eternal Word, truly God Himself.** Before heaven and earth existed, He was with God and brought all things into being. Human life and light flow directly from Him, and no darkness can extinguish that light (1:1–5).

**God does not stand aloof, but steps straight into human life.** "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth." Humanity no longer needs to guess what God is like, because the one and only Son from the Father's side has revealed Him openly (1:14–18).

**Becoming God's child rests not on ancestry, but on receiving Him.** Even when the world and His own people turn away, whoever trusts in His name receives the right to become a child of God. That new birth springs entirely from God, not from human effort or lineage (1:10–13).

**A faithful witness knows his proper place.** Pressed with grand titles, John the Baptist declines them all, calling himself merely a voice clearing the road and pointing straight to Jesus, "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (1:19–29).

**Faith begins with a simple invitation: "Come and see."** From Andrew to Philip, those who meet Jesus do not launch long debates to win others over; they simply bring them to Him. Jesus Himself asks the piercing question, "What are you seeking?" and opens a horizon larger than anything they imagined (1:35–51).

## Deeper Meaning

**New creation opens with the same words, "In the beginning."** John's first sentence deliberately echoes the opening line of the Bible: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). John signals that Jesus' arrival is not merely another chapter of history, but God launching a new creation—a truth Paul and the letter to the Hebrews also affirm in stating that all things were made through Him (Colossians 1:15–16; Hebrews 1:3).

**Pitching a tent among His people.** The Greek verb translated "dwelt among us" in 1:14 literally means "pitched His tent" or "tabernacled," recalling the wilderness tabernacle where "the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exodus 40:34–35). Likewise, "full of grace and truth" echoes God's self-revelation to Moses on Mount Sinai as "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6).

**The wilderness voice and the slain Lamb.** By identifying himself as the voice preparing the way of the LORD (Isaiah 40:3), John the Baptist quietly affirms that the One whose road he clears is the Lord Himself visiting His people. Calling Jesus "the Lamb of God" recalls both the Passover lamb that rescued Israel and the Suffering Servant led "like a lamb to the slaughter" to bear the sins of many (Isaiah 53:7).

**Jacob's ladder is now a person.** Promising Nathanael that they will watch "the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man" (1:51), Jesus recalls Jacob's dream at Bethel, where a stairway connected earth to heaven (Genesis 28:12). No longer does a sacred site or stone sanctuary bridge heaven and earth; Jesus Himself is that bridge.

## Notes & Nuances

**The term "Word" speaks to both Jews and Greeks.** In Greek the term is *Logos*: Greek thinkers heard in it the rational principle holding the cosmos together, while Jewish readers heard God's creative spoken word in Genesis and His divine Wisdom. John takes that resonant word and points it squarely at a flesh-and-blood human being.

**Law through Moses and grace through Jesus are not opposites.** Verse 1:17 is easily misread as pitting a cold, legalistic Old Testament against a gracious New Testament. Yet verse 1:16 has just spoken of "grace upon grace," meaning one gift arriving to build on and fulfill an earlier gift: the Mosaic law was already a gift from God, and Jesus now brings that gift to fullness.

**The phrase "the Jews" in 1:19 must be read carefully in John's Gospel.** Throughout John, this phrase never condemns an entire ethnic people—Jesus, His disciples, and most early believers were themselves Jewish—but normally denotes the religious authorities in Jerusalem opposing Him. Misreading this expression across history caused tragic harm.

**The call of the first disciples differs from Matthew and Mark.** Here Andrew, Peter, and Philip meet Jesus near the Jordan River before traveling to Galilee, whereas in Matthew and Mark they leave their nets beside the Sea of Galilee. Many readers reconcile the accounts by seeing John 1 as their initial meeting, with the call to leave fishing behind happening slightly later.

## What Commentators Say

**Philo of Alexandria**, first-century Jewish philosopher living in Egypt, wrote extensively about the *Logos* as the bridge between the transcendent God and the visible cosmos. He called the *Logos* the firstborn image of God, yet in his thought the *Logos* remained a divine principle that could never take on human flesh—making John 1:14 astonishing to ancient readers. *(On the Creation of the World — De Opificio Mundi)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Philo's Logos doctrine is standard historical background cited across commentaries on John 1 -->

**Irenaeus**, second-century bishop of Lyon, drew directly on John 1 to refute Gnostic groups who claimed the material world was evil and crafted by a lesser deity. He insisted there is only one God: the very Word who made all things truly became flesh in order to redeem His own creation. *(Against Heresies, book 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Irenaeus relies heavily on John 1 in book 3 to defend the unity of creation and incarnation -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa across the fourth and fifth centuries, recounted in his memoir that before his conversion he found ideas resembling John 1:1 about the Word with God in Neoplatonic writings. Yet what he never found in philosophy books was that "the Word became flesh" and humbled Himself to die for humanity. *(Confessions, book 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's contrast between Neoplatonic books and John 1 is among the best-known passages of Confessions book 7 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century French Reformer in Geneva, focused on the "tent-pitching" verb in 1:14, arguing that Jesus' human body is the true sanctuary where the fullness of deity dwells. In his reading, 1:17 does not demean Moses, but shows that what the law sketched in shadow now stands complete in Christ. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John, on chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recalled Calvin's emphasis on continuity between Moses and Christ in chapter 1; exact phrasing in original not verified -->

**Raymond Brown**, twentieth-century American Catholic New Testament scholar, argued that the prologue in 1:1–18 carries the cadence of an early Christian hymn into which the evangelist wove lines about John the Baptist to transition smoothly into the narrative at verse 19. He also highlighted how John structures Jesus' opening week with repeated "the next day" markers as an echo of creation week. *(The Gospel According to John, Anchor Bible, vol. 1, 1966)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analysis of the prologue's hymn structure and the new-creation week is one of Brown's signature contributions in vol. 1 -->
