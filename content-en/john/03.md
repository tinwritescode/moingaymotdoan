# John 3 — Born from Above

## Story

There was a Pharisee named Nicodemus, a member of the Jewish ruling council. He came to Jesus by night and said, "Rabbi, we know you are a teacher come from God, because no one could perform the signs you do unless God were with him." Jesus answered: truly, unless a person is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God (3:1–3). Nicodemus opens with a compliment; Jesus responds with something he never expected.

Nicodemus asked: how can an old man be born? Surely he cannot re-enter his mother's womb and be born a second time! Jesus replied: unless someone is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God. What is born of flesh is flesh, and what is born of the Spirit is spirit. Do not marvel that I told you, "You must be born again." The wind blows wherever it chooses; you hear its sound, yet you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone born of the Spirit (3:4–8).

Nicodemus asked, "How can these things be?" Jesus replied: are you Israel's teacher, and yet you do not grasp this? We speak of what we know and testify to what we have seen, yet you people refuse our testimony. If I have spoken to you about earthly things and you do not believe, how will you believe if I tell you heavenly things? No one has ascended into heaven except the one who descended from heaven—the Son of Man (3:9–13).

Then He recalled an episode from the wilderness: just as Moses lifted up the bronze serpent in the desert, so the Son of Man must be lifted up, that everyone who trusts in Him may have eternal life. "For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, so that whoever believes in Him will not perish, but have eternal life." God did not send His Son into the world to condemn the world, but so that the world might be saved through Him (3:14–17). This is perhaps the most widely memorized verse in the entire Bible.

Whoever believes in the Son is not condemned, whereas whoever does not believe stands condemned already, because he has not believed in the name of God's only Son. And this is the verdict: light has entered the world, yet people loved darkness rather than light because their deeds were evil. Everyone who practices evil hates the light and keeps away from it, afraid that his deeds will be exposed. Whoever lives by the truth comes toward the light, so that it may be plainly seen that what he has done has been carried out in God (3:18–21). People are not condemned because God slams the door shut, but because they turn their backs on the light.

Afterward Jesus went with His disciples into the Judean countryside, where He stayed with them and baptized. John the Baptist was also baptizing at Aenon near Salim, because water was plentiful there, as he had not yet been put in prison. A dispute arose between John's disciples and a Jew over ceremonial washing, and they came to their teacher saying: Rabbi, the man who was with you across the Jordan—the one you testified about—is baptizing now, and everyone is going to him (3:22–26).

John answered: no one can receive anything unless it is given from heaven. You yourselves can bear witness that I declared, "I am not the Christ, but I have been sent ahead of Him." The bride belongs to the bridegroom; the bridegroom's friend stands by, listens for him, and rejoices at the sound of his voice. That joy of mine is now complete. "He must increase, but I must decrease." (3:27–30). The students worry their teacher is losing followers; the teacher rejoices because that is the very outcome he was sent to bring about.

The one coming from above is above all; the one who belongs to earth is earthly and speaks in earthly terms. He testifies to what He has seen and heard, yet few accept His testimony; whoever does accept it certifies that God is truthful. God gives Him the Spirit without measure. The Father loves the Son and has placed all things in His hands. Whoever trusts in the Son has eternal life; whoever rejects the Son will not see life, for God's wrath remains upon him (3:31–36).

## Key Takeaways

**Humanity needs more than surface reform; it needs new birth from above.** Nicodemus is an upright scholar devoted to the law, yet Jesus speaks bluntly: apart from birth by water and the Spirit, no one enters God's kingdom. Like the wind, this renewal cannot be manufactured by human willpower, yet its effects are unmistakable wherever it takes hold (3:1–8).

**The cross flows from God's love, not His anger.** Verse 3:16 captures the heart of the gospel: God loved the world so deeply that He gave His only Son, sending Him not to condemn but to rescue. The Son of Man must be lifted up like the bronze serpent of old so that all who look to Him in faith may live (3:14–17).

**Judgment arises when people turn away from the light.** Once light enters the world, every heart reveals itself by how it reacts. Those attached to wrongdoing recoil from the light to hide their actions; those pursuing truth step gladly into its glow (3:18–21).

**A servant's deepest joy is making room for Christ.** When his followers fret over crowds shifting toward Jesus, John the Baptist feels no envy. Styling himself the best man delighted to hear the bridegroom's voice, he leaves behind an unforgettable motto: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (3:27–30).

## Deeper Meaning

**The promised renewal in Ezekiel.** When Jesus speaks of being born of "water and the Spirit" (3:5) and expresses surprise that Israel's teacher fails to understand (3:10), He points back to Ezekiel's promise: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean," followed by "I will put My Spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:25–27). New birth is no unfamiliar idea; God announced it centuries earlier through the prophets.

**The bronze serpent in the wilderness.** During Israel's march from Egypt, venomous snakes struck the rebellious people; God instructed Moses to fashion a bronze serpent and raise it on a pole so that "anyone who was bitten could look at the bronze serpent and live" (Numbers 21:8–9). Jesus adopts that precise scene to foreshadow being lifted up on the cross to heal the deadly venom of sin (3:14–15).

**A Father giving His beloved only Son.** The phrase "only Son" in 3:16 echoes God's test of Abraham: "Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love," and offer him on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:2). Back then God stayed Abraham's hand and supplied a ram in Isaac's place; now God Himself does not spare His own Son for humankind (Romans 5:8; 1 John 4:9).

**The covenant bridegroom and His bride.** John the Baptist's image of the bridegroom (3:29) draws on deep Old Testament roots, where God repeatedly portrays Himself as Israel's faithful husband (Hosea 2:19–20). By naming Jesus the bridegroom, John sets Him in God's own place arriving to claim His people (Ephesians 5:25–27).

## Notes & Nuances

**Both "born again" and "wind" carry double meanings in Greek.** The Greek adverb rendered "again" in 3:3 also means "from above." Nicodemus catches only the sense "a second time" and puzzles over re-entering the womb, whereas Jesus emphasizes birth originating from heaven. Similarly, in 3:8, a single Greek word serves for both "wind" and "Spirit."

**"Water and the Spirit" in 3:5 has three main interpretations.** First, linking it to Ezekiel 36:25–27 sees water as cleansing and Spirit as inward transformation. Second, some take "water" as natural physical birth (amniotic fluid), contrasted with spiritual birth by the Spirit. Third, others connect it with water baptism and the gift of the Spirit in early Christian practice.

**Ancient Greek manuscripts lack quotation marks separating Jesus' speech from John's commentary.** Across 3:16–21, and again in 3:31–36, many interpreters sense the voice gliding seamlessly from Jesus' reply to Nicodemus into the evangelist's own theological reflection. Either way, the message stays wholly unified and sums up the chapter's heart.

**God's love in 3:16 and God's wrath in 3:36 do not contradict each other.** God takes no delight in condemning anyone; out of love He has already opened the road of rescue through His Son. Wrath simply remains on whoever deliberately refuses that rescue, like a patient pushing away the sole cure that can save his life.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople, noted that Nicodemus visited under cover of night out of fear of public opinion, yet Jesus never scolded him. Instead, He guided him patiently from admiring miracles toward grasping spiritual rebirth—and the seed planted that night grew until Nicodemus dared at the Gospel's close to care publicly for Jesus' burial. *(Homilies on the Gospel of John, homily 24)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom highlights Jesus' patience with Nicodemus's initial timidity in his homily on John 3 -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa across the fourth and fifth centuries, unpacked the image of the bronze serpent in 3:14: Israel suffered bites from venomous snakes, yet found healing by gazing at a bronze likeness that held no venom. Likewise, humanity suffers the poison of sin and finds healing by gazing at Jesus, who took the likeness of human flesh yet knew no sin. *(Tractates on the Gospel of John, tractate 12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: comparing the venomless bronze serpent to Christ's sinless flesh is a famous image from Augustine's tractate 12 -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, famously called John 3:16 the gospel in miniature. He emphasized that the verse grounds salvation completely in God's initiating love rather than human merit, and that faith alone receives that gift. *(Sermons on the Gospel of St. John)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's description of John 3:16 as the miniature gospel is widely known and frequently cited -->

**D. A. Carson**, Canadian-American New Testament scholar, argued that Ezekiel 36:25–27 unlocks the phrase "water and the Spirit" in 3:5. That explains why Jesus expects Israel's teacher Nicodemus to recognize it: water and Spirit are not two separate stages, but paired images depicting thorough cleansing and inner renewal from God. *(The Gospel According to John, Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking John 3:5 to Ezekiel 36 is a central point of Carson's exposition of chapter 3 -->

**Lesslie Newbigin**, British bishop and longtime missionary in India, observed that "the world" in John's Gospel normally signifies humanity alienated from and opposed to God. What makes 3:16 breathtaking is therefore not how vast the world is, but how broken and hostile it is—and yet God still loves it enough to give His only Son. *(The Light Has Come, 1982)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Newbigin's point about the negative sense of "world" heightening the wonder of 3:16; exact phrasing not checked -->
