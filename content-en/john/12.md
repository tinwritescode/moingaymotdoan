# John 12 — The Hour Has Come

## Story

Six days before Passover, Jesus arrived in Bethany, home of Lazarus. A dinner was given in His honor; Martha served, while Lazarus reclined at table with Him. Mary took a pound of pure, costly spikenard ointment, anointed Jesus' feet, and wiped them with her hair, filling the entire house with fragrance. Judas Iscariot, the disciple who would later betray Him, objected: why wasn't this perfume sold for three hundred denarii and the money given to the poor? He spoke not out of care for the poor, but because he was a thief; in charge of the money bag, he used to help himself to what was put into it (12:1–6).

Jesus answered: leave her alone; she kept this perfume for the day of My burial. You will always have the poor among you, yet you will not always have Me. Meanwhile a large crowd of Jews learned He was there and came—not solely for Jesus, but also to see Lazarus, whom He had raised from the dead. So the chief priests made plans to put Lazarus to death as well, because on account of him many Jews were leaving them and believing in Jesus (12:7–11). Even the man brought back to life becomes a target; living proof had to be silenced.

The next day, the large festival crowd heard Jesus was approaching Jerusalem, took palm branches, and went out to greet Him shouting, "Hosanna! Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord, the King of Israel!" Finding a young donkey, Jesus sat on it, just as Scripture says: "Fear not, daughter of Zion; behold, your king is coming, seated on a donkey's colt." At first His disciples did not grasp all this; only after Jesus was glorified did they remember these words had been written about Him and that people had done these very things to Him (12:12–16).

Those who had stood by when He called Lazarus out of the tomb kept testifying about it, and because the crowd heard He had performed that sign, they poured out to welcome Him. The Pharisees murmured to one another: look, we are getting nowhere; the whole world has gone after him! (12:17–19).

Among those who had gone up to worship at the festival were some Greeks. They approached Philip, from Bethsaida in Galilee, with a request: "Sir, we would like to see Jesus." Philip went and told Andrew, and together the two told Jesus. He replied, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." Truly, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; yet if it dies, it bears much fruit. Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life. Whoever serves Me must follow Me, and where I am, My servant will be too; My Father will honor anyone who serves Me (12:20–26). Across the whole Gospel He kept saying "My hour has not yet come"; the moment Gentiles come seeking Him, He announces that the hour has arrived.

Now My soul is troubled, He continued; what shall I say? "Father, save Me from this hour"? Yet for this very purpose I came to this hour. "Father, glorify Your name!" A voice sounded from heaven: "I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again!" The crowd standing nearby said it had thundered; others said an angel had spoken to Him. Jesus explained: this voice came not for My sake, but for yours. Now is the judgment of this world; now the ruler of this world will be cast out. "And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to Myself." He said this to indicate the kind of death He was about to die (12:27–33).

The crowd replied: we have heard from the Law that the Christ remains forever, so how can you say the Son of Man must be lifted up? Who is this Son of Man? Jesus told them: the light stays among you a little longer. Walk while you have the light, before darkness overtakes you; whoever walks in the dark does not know where he is going. While you have the light, believe in the light, so you may become children of light. Having said this, Jesus departed and hid Himself from them (12:34–36).

Even after He had performed so many signs right before their eyes, they still would not believe in Him—fulfilling Isaiah's word: "Lord, who has believed our report, and to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?" They could not believe, for Isaiah also declared that God blinded their eyes and hardened their hearts so they would not see with their eyes or understand with their hearts and turn to be healed. Isaiah spoke these words because he saw His glory and spoke about Him. Nevertheless, even among the rulers many believed in Him, yet for fear of the Pharisees they would not confess it openly lest they be expelled from the synagogue—for they loved human praise more than praise from God (12:37–43).

Jesus cried out: whoever believes in Me believes not only in Me, but in the One who sent Me; whoever sees Me sees the One who sent Me. "I have come as light into the world, so that whoever believes in Me may not remain in darkness." If anyone hears My words and does not keep them, I do not judge him, because I came not to judge the world, but to save the world. Whoever rejects Me and refuses My words already has a judge: the very word I have spoken will judge him on the last day. For I have not spoken on My own authority, but the Father who sent Me commanded Me what to say and speak, and I know His command is eternal life (12:44–50). These are the final words Jesus speaks publicly to the crowds in John's Gospel.

## Key Takeaways

**True love often looks extravagant.** A pound of pure spikenard cost three hundred denarii, roughly a full year's wages for a laborer. Mary pours the entire jar over Jesus' feet. Judas tallies coins; Mary refuses to calculate. Jesus defends her and reveals her act reaches deeper than she realizes: she has anointed His body ahead of time for burial (12:3–8).

**The King riding a donkey's colt.** Crowds wave palm fronds and cheer "King of Israel." Palms carried nationalist overtones, with many longing for a monarch to expel Roman troops. Jesus answers with a quiet action: rather than mounting a warhorse, He rides a young donkey. He is indeed King—a King of peace (12:12–15).

**Glory passes through death.** As Greek seekers ask to see Him, Jesus recognizes that His hour has struck. He points to a kernel of wheat: hoarded, it remains "alone"; planted and dying, it yields "much fruit." That is His path, and it sets the pattern for every follower: clinging to one's life means losing it (12:20–26).

**Troubled in soul, yet steadfast in obedience.** Jesus does not stride toward the cross immune to fear. He admits openly that His soul is deeply troubled, then chooses His Father's purpose: "Father, glorify Your name!" And He promises that once lifted up, He will draw all peoples to Himself (12:27–33).

**Witnessing miracles does not guarantee belief, and believing does not guarantee courage.** After countless signs, crowds still turn away. Even among leaders who do believe, fear of synagogue expulsion keeps them silent. John exposes the root directly: they "loved human praise more than praise from God" (12:37–43).

## Deeper Meaning

**"Hosanna" is a plea for rescue.** The crowd sings from Psalm 118, a pilgrim hymn chanted on the climb up to the temple: "LORD, save us!" and "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD!" (Psalms 118:25–26). Literally "Hosanna" means "Save now." They shout a cry for rescue straight into the face of their Savior, who will rescue them in a way they never anticipated.

**Zechariah sketched this very King.** The prophet foresaw a king arriving in Jerusalem "humble and riding on a donkey," who in the very next line banishes war chariots and battle horses and "proclaims peace to the nations" (Zechariah 9:9–10). John trims the quote and opens with "Fear not" (12:15). Choosing the donkey announces the kind of kingdom He establishes.

**A seed must die to bear life.** Paul uses the exact same image to explain resurrection: "What you sow does not come to life unless it dies" (1 Corinthians 15:36). For Jesus, He Himself is the seed; the harvest is people from every nation, previewed by the Greeks waiting outside. God's ancient promise to Abraham—that all families of the earth would be blessed through him—begins coming to bloom (Genesis 12:3).

**This is John's Gethsemane.** John does not recount Jesus praying in the garden on the night of His arrest as the other Gospels do. Yet verses 27–28 capture the same agony: a troubled soul, a fleeting plea for rescue from the hour, and full surrender to the Father's will—mirroring Matthew's "Yet not as I will, but as You will" (Matthew 26:39). Hebrews likewise remembers Jesus offering prayers "with loud cries and tears" (Hebrews 5:7).

**"Lifted up" echoes the Servant Song.** Isaiah's song opens by declaring that God's Servant "will be raised and lifted up and highly exalted" (Isaiah 52:13). Just verses later, John quotes the opening line of the very next chapter of that song: "Who has believed our report?" (12:38; Isaiah 53:1). In John's eyes, being lifted on a cross and being exalted in glory are one single event: the despised Servant is the glorified Lord—a double meaning Jesus already introduced when comparing Himself to Moses' bronze serpent lifted up in the desert (John 3:14).

**Whose glory did Isaiah behold?** John writes that Isaiah spoke these words "because he saw His glory" (12:41). Isaiah 6 recounts the prophet seeing "the Lord seated on a high and lofty throne" inside the temple (Isaiah 6:1). John identifies the glory Isaiah beheld that day as the glory of Jesus Himself—one of the Gospel's boldest affirmations of His deity.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does "they could not believe" mean God forced them into unbelief?** Verses 39–40 feel unsettling. In Isaiah, God sends the prophet to preach to a people who have already turned away, warning that his message will harden them further (Isaiah 6:10). Interpreters approach this in several ways: some stress God's sovereignty in handing over or hardening hearts that reject Him; others stress that Scripture foretells the outcome rather than causing it. Notice that verse 37 states first that they themselves refused to believe despite witnessing His signs—and verse 42 immediately adds that many rulers did believe, proving hardening is no blanket sentence on an entire people.

**"You always have the poor" never excuses neglecting them.** Jesus echoes a line from Mosaic law that closes with a firm command: "Open your hand wide to your poor and needy brother in your land" (Deuteronomy 15:11). His point is that His earthly time with them is almost over, whereas opportunities to care for the poor remain always. Ironically, the sole voice objecting to Mary's gift belongs to the man stealing from the poor fund (12:6).

**How many anointing stories are there?** Mark and Matthew tell of an unnamed woman pouring ointment on Jesus' head at Bethany in the home of Simon the leper (Mark 14:3–9). Luke tells of a sinful woman anointing His feet in Galilee (Luke 7:36–50). Most commentators regard John and Mark as accounts of the same Bethany meal, with Luke narrating an earlier, separate event. Mary of Bethany is also distinct from Mary Magdalene; from the sixth century onward, Western church tradition conflated all three women, saddling Mary Magdalene with an unbiblical reputation as a prostitute for centuries.

**"The ruler of this world" is the devil.** Verse 31 declares that he "will be cast out." John mentions him twice more during Jesus' farewell discourse (14:30; 16:11). Calvary looks on the surface like Satan's triumph, yet John insists that is the precise hour he is overthrown while the crucified Lord draws humanity to Himself.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, paused over Judas carrying the money bag, asking why Jesus knew he was a thief yet kept him among the Twelve. He reasoned that Christ did so to teach the Church patience in bearing with unworthy members in its midst until final judgment, rather than splintering away in pursuit of spotless purity. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, weighed verse 27 carefully. The cry "Save Me from this hour" springs from the natural human dread of death in a truly human Savior, instantly brought under obedience to the Father. Calvin saw zero sin here: feeling fear is not wrong in itself; what counts is refusing to let fear take command, and Christ shows us how to keep fear yielded to God. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John, on chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Calvin's overall interpretation of verse 27; exact wording not checked -->

**Raymond Brown**, twentieth-century American Catholic New Testament scholar, organized John's Gospel into two grand halves: the Book of Signs (chapters 1–12) and the Book of Glory (chapters 13–20). Chapter 12 serves as the hinge, with verses 37–50 closing the first half on a somber summary of unbelief despite abundant signs. He also treated verses 27–28 as John's counterpart to the Gethsemane prayer. *(The Gospel According to John, Anchor Bible, vol. 1, 1966)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the two-part structure Book of Signs and Book of Glory frames Brown's entire commentary; Gethsemane parallel recalled though exact page unchecked -->

**Richard Bauckham**, British New Testament scholar, demonstrated how verses 38–41 knit together two Isaiah passages sharing the same verbs "high" and "lifted up": the Lord's throne in Isaiah 6 and the exalted Servant in Isaiah 52–53.
