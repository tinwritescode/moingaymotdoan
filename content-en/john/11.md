# John 11 — The Raising of Lazarus

## Story

A man named Lazarus fell ill in Bethany, the village of the sisters Mary and Martha. Mary was the one who would later anoint the Lord with perfume and wipe His feet with her hair. The sisters sent word to Jesus: "Lord, the one you love is sick." Hearing this, He remarked: this sickness will not end in death, but is for God's glory, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it. Jesus loved Martha, her sister, and Lazarus. Even so, when He heard Lazarus was sick, He stayed where He was two more days (11:1–6). Loving someone and still waiting—that stands as the hardest riddle in the story.

Then He told His disciples, "Let us go back to Judea." They protested: Rabbi, just recently the Jewish leaders tried to stone you, and you are going back there? He answered: aren't there twelve hours of daylight? Whoever walks in the daytime does not stumble, because he sees this world's light; whoever walks at night stumbles, because he has no light. Then He added: our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, and I am going to wake him up. The disciples replied: Lord, if he has fallen asleep, he will recover. Jesus meant his death, while they thought He meant natural sleep. So He told them plainly, "Lazarus is dead." For your sake I am glad I was not there, so that you may believe; let us go to him. Thomas, called Didymus, said to the other disciples, "Let us also go, that we may die with him!" (11:7–16).

When Jesus arrived, Lazarus had already lain in the tomb four days. Because Bethany sat only about three kilometers from Jerusalem, many Jews had come to comfort Martha and Mary. Hearing Jesus was approaching, Martha went out to meet Him, while Mary remained seated at home. Martha told Him: Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died; yet even now I know that whatever you ask of God, God will give you. Jesus told her, "Your brother will rise again." Martha replied, "I know he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day." (11:17–24).

Jesus declared, "I am the resurrection and the life; whoever believes in Me, though he die, yet shall he live." Everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die. "Do you believe this?" Martha replied, "Yes, Lord; I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world." (11:25–27). Martha believed resurrection belonged to the distant end of time; Jesus told her resurrection was standing right in front of her.

After saying this, Martha went back and privately called Mary: the Teacher is here and is asking for you. Hearing that, Mary rose quickly and hurried out to Him. Jesus had not yet entered the village, still waiting where Martha had met Him. The Jews consoling Mary in the house saw her get up hastily and followed her, thinking she was going to the tomb to weep. Reaching Jesus, Mary fell at His feet crying, "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died!" (11:28–32).

Seeing Mary weeping and the Jews who accompanied her weeping too, Jesus was deeply stirred in spirit and troubled, and asked, "Where have you laid him?" They told Him, "Lord, come and see." "Jesus wept." The Jews remarked, "See how he loved him!" Yet others asked, "Could not the one who opened the eyes of the blind man have kept this man from dying?" (11:33–37). The shortest verse in the Bible shows that the very Lord about to summon a dead man back to life still weeps alongside grieving friends.

Deeply moved once more, Jesus approached the tomb—a cave with a stone laid across its opening. He commanded, "Roll away the stone." Martha objected: Lord, by now there is an odor, for he has been dead four days. Jesus answered: did I not tell you that if you believed, you would see the glory of God? So they rolled the stone aside. Lifting His eyes toward heaven, Jesus prayed: Father, I thank You that You have heard Me; I knew You always hear Me, yet I said this for the sake of the crowd standing around, so they may believe You sent Me. Then He cried out in a loud voice, "Lazarus, come out!" The dead man stepped out, hands and feet still bound in burial strips, his face wrapped in a cloth. Jesus instructed them, "Unbind him and let him go." (11:38–44).

Many of the Jews who had come to visit Mary saw what He did and put faith in Him. Some, however, went to report to the Pharisees. The chief priests and Pharisees convened the Sanhedrin: this man performs so many signs—what are we to do? If we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our temple and our nation. Caiaphas, who was high priest that year, declared: you know nothing at all! It is better for you that one man die for the people than for the whole nation to perish. He did not speak merely on his own; serving as high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation—and not for the nation alone, but to gather into one the scattered children of God. From that day forward they resolved to put Him to death (11:45–53). Giving one man back his life seals the verdict for His own death.

Consequently Jesus stopped moving about openly among the Judeans and withdrew to a region near the wilderness, to a town called Ephraim, where He stayed with His disciples. As Passover drew close, many people went up from the countryside to Jerusalem ahead of the feast to purify themselves. Standing in the temple courts, they kept looking for Jesus and asking one another: what do you think—will he come to the festival at all? Meanwhile the chief priests and Pharisees had issued orders that anyone who knew where He was must report it so they could arrest Him (11:54–57).

## Key Takeaways

**God's love does not always arrive on our timetable.** John explicitly states that Jesus loved all three siblings, and immediately follows with "so when He heard Lazarus was sick, He stayed two more days." To Martha and Mary, those two days felt like silence and loss. To Jesus, they made space for God's glory to shine and for disciples to believe (11:4–6; 11:15). Waiting never means being forgotten.

**Jesus does more than promise resurrection; He is the resurrection.** Martha held the orthodox hope of devout Jews: the dead will rise on the last day. Jesus pulls that hope out of the distant future and sets it directly before her: "I am the resurrection and the life." Eternal life is not merely a future event, but communion with a Person beginning right now (11:23–27).

**The One about to call the dead back still weeps.** Jesus knows that within minutes Lazarus will walk out alive. Even so, He trembles with emotion, asks where the grave lies, and lets His tears fall (11:33–35). Hope never cancels grief. Believers have full permission to weep beside the graves of those they love, just as the Lord wept.

**Lazarus's life is bought at the cost of Jesus' death.** The climactic miracle of John's Gospel triggers the Sanhedrin's formal decision to execute Him. Caiaphas calculates strictly in political terms: sacrifice one man to save the nation. Yet John hears the very gospel inside his cynical line: one Man dying for the people to gather God's scattered children into one family (11:47–53).

## Deeper Meaning

**Sleeping in the dust and waking up.** Martha's hope rested on Old Testament promise: "Multitudes who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2). Jesus likewise refers to Lazarus's death as sleep and goes "to wake him up" (11:11). Paul borrows that exact vocabulary to comfort believers so they do not grieve "like others who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).

**The shout before the tomb previews the final day.** Through Ezekiel God promised, "I will open your graves" (Ezekiel 37:12). Back in chapter 5, Jesus announced an hour when "all who are in the tombs will hear His voice and come out" (John 5:28). At Bethany that future promise breaks into the present for one man: one shout, and the dead walks out (11:43–44).

**Lazarus emerges wrapped in grave clothes; Jesus leaves His behind.** Lazarus returns to ordinary mortal life and will die again someday; others have to unwind his burial strips. When Jesus rises, Peter finds the linen cloths and face wrap left neatly inside the tomb (John 20:6–7). Jesus does not return to mortal life, but steps into resurrection life over which death holds no mastery (Romans 6:9). Thus Christ is the "firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20), while Lazarus serves as an advance sign pointing to that harvest.

**The One who weeps is the One who will wipe every tear.** Isaiah promised that the Lord "will wipe away tears from all faces" (Isaiah 25:8), a pledge Revelation repeats for the new creation (Revelation 21:4). Before wiping away others' tears, Jesus let His own tears fall. Hebrews assures us we have a high priest able to "sympathize with our weaknesses" (Hebrews 4:15).

**Even when someone rises from the dead, hardened hearts still refuse belief.** In Jesus' parable in Luke, a poor man also named Lazarus appears, and Abraham remarks that even if someone rises from the dead, hardened hearts will remain unconvinced (Luke 16:31). John 11 turns that warning into history: while many believe, others hurry to inform plots against Jesus' life, and chief priests soon plot to kill Lazarus as well (11:45–46; 12:10–11).

## Notes & Nuances

**"The Jews" in John never means the entire Jewish nation.** In this chapter, it is "the Jews" who come to console Martha and Mary, weep beside them, and put faith in Jesus (11:19; 11:31; 11:45). Only a handful report Him to the Pharisees, and the plot belongs to the Sanhedrin (11:46–53). John typically uses the term for Judeans or Jerusalem's leadership. Jesus, His disciples, and the three siblings in Bethany were all Jewish. Using John's language across later centuries to blame or persecute the Jewish people was a tragic misreading of the Gospel.

**Jesus being "deeply moved" may express fierce anger.** The Greek verb in verses 33 and 38 ordinarily conveys indignation or anger, even echoing a horse's snort. Three readings stand out: anger against death itself as an enemy ravaging those He loves; anger at the unbelief around Him; or overwhelming inward distress that shakes Him deeply. Many commentators favor the first reading, noting that the same verb recurs as He faces the tomb itself (11:38).

**The two-day delay did not cause Lazarus's death.** By the time Jesus arrives, Lazarus has lain buried four days (11:17). Allowing one day for the messenger to reach Jesus, two days waiting, and one day traveling back implies Lazarus likely died soon after the messenger departed. Jesus did not allow His friend to die through tardiness; waiting ensured no one could dismiss Lazarus as merely fainting.

**Caiaphas prophesies unwittingly.** John notes he spoke not merely on his own, but as high priest "that year" (11:51). High priests did not rotate annually—Caiaphas held office nearly twenty years—so "that year" highlights that decisive, fateful year in which Jesus died. God overrules the speech of a man plotting His Son's execution to proclaim His own saving intent without the speaker realizing it.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, read Lazarus as an emblem of a soul buried beneath entrenched sinful habit: sin hardens like a heavy stone and begins to stink. Only Christ's voice calls such a person back to life. **John Chrysostom**, fourth-century archbishop of Constantinople, asked why One performing a miracle by His own authority prayed aloud before the tomb. Following verse 42, he answered that Jesus prayed for the sake of a wavering crowd so they would recognize He worked in union with the Father rather than against God. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, drew on Jesus' tears and deep distress in verses 33–35 to refute Stoic philosophy, which equated wisdom with emotionlessness. Because Christ took on true human emotions, grief itself is no sin; what matters is keeping our emotions governed by obedience to God. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John, on chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: clearly recalled Calvin refuting Stoicism at this passage; exact phrasing not checked -->

**Rudolf Bultmann**, twentieth-century German New Testament scholar, placed verses 25–26 at the chapter's heart. In his reading, John deliberately reshapes Martha's expectation: resurrection is not merely an occurrence postponed to the end of time, but a present reality breaking in whenever someone encounters Jesus and believes. The miracle serves as a pointer to that truth rather than the ultimate reality itself. *(Das Evangelium des Johannes, 1941; English: The Gospel of John: A Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain this matches Bultmann's overarching reading of realized eschatology in John; exact phrasing at this passage not checked -->

**Midrash Genesis Rabbah**, a Jewish commentary compiled around the fifth century, preserves a rabbinic belief that for three days after death the soul hovers near the body hoping to re-enter, departing for good only once the face begins to change.
