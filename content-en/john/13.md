# John 13 — The Master Washes His Disciples' Feet

## Story

Before the Passover festival, Jesus knew that His hour had come to depart from this world and go to the Father. Having loved His own who were in the world, He "loved them to the very end." During supper, when the devil had already put it into the heart of Judas Iscariot, son of Simon, to betray Him, Jesus—knowing that the Father had placed all things into His hands, and that He had come from God and was returning to God—rose from the table, laid aside His outer garment, and tied a towel around His waist. He poured water into a basin and began washing the disciples' feet and drying them with the towel wrapped around Him (13:1–5). Washing guests' feet was the chore of the lowest servant in a household. The One holding everything in His hands used those very hands for that task.

When He reached Simon Peter, Peter exclaimed, "Lord, are You going to wash my feet?" Jesus answered: right now you do not understand what I am doing, yet afterward you will understand. Peter protested, "You shall never wash my feet!" Jesus replied, "Unless I wash you, you have no share with Me." Simon Peter said, "Lord, then not only my feet, but also my hands and my head!" Jesus answered: whoever has bathed needs only to wash his feet to be completely clean; and you are clean—though not every one of you. He knew who was going to betray Him, which is why He said not all were clean (13:6–11).

When He finished washing their feet, He put His outer garment back on, resumed His place at the table, and asked: do you understand what I have done for you? You address Me as Teacher and Lord, and rightly so, for that is what I am. If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. I have given you an example so that you should do just as I have done for you. A servant is no greater than his master, nor is a messenger greater than the one who sent him. Once you know these things, blessed are you if you practice them (13:12–17).

I am not speaking about all of you; I know those I have chosen. Yet the Scripture must be fulfilled: "The one who ate My bread has lifted his heel against Me." I am telling you now before it happens, so that when it does happen you may believe that I am He. Whoever receives anyone I send receives Me, and whoever receives Me receives the One who sent Me (13:18–20). He washed Judas's feet too, knowing full well Judas was about to hand Him over.

Having said this, Jesus was troubled in spirit and testified openly: truly, one of you is going to betray Me. The disciples stared at one another, uncertain whom He meant. Reclining close to Jesus' chest was one of His disciples, the one whom Jesus loved. Simon Peter motioned to him to ask whom the Teacher was talking about. Leaning back against Jesus' chest, he asked, "Lord, who is it?" Jesus answered: it is the one to whom I give this piece of bread after I dip it. Dipping the morsel, He handed it to Judas Iscariot, son of Simon (13:21–26). The disciple "whom Jesus loved" appears for the first time here; this Gospel never names him.

As soon as Judas took the morsel, Satan entered into him. Jesus told him, "What you are going to do, do quickly." No one else at the table understood why He said this. Because Judas kept the money bag, some supposed Jesus was instructing him to buy supplies for the festival or give something to the poor. After taking the bread, Judas went out immediately. "And it was night." (13:27–30). Offering a dipped piece of bread was a host's gesture of friendship; Judas accepted it and walked straight out into the dark.

Once Judas had gone, Jesus declared: now the Son of Man is glorified, and God is glorified in Him. Little children, I am with you only a little while longer. You will look for Me, and just as I told the Jewish leaders, where I am going you cannot come. "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; just as I have loved you, so you also must love one another." By this everyone will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another (13:31–35). The command to love was nothing new; what is new is the measure—"just as I have loved you," like the Master who has just knelt to wash their feet.

Simon Peter asked, "Lord, where are You going?" Jesus replied: where I am going you cannot follow now, yet you will follow afterward. Peter asked: Lord, why cannot I follow You now? "I will lay down my life for You!" Jesus answered: will you really lay down your life for Me? Truly, before the rooster crows, you will deny Me three times (13:36–38).

## Key Takeaways

**The One holding all things in His hands uses those hands to wash feet.** John intentionally links two facts together: Jesus knows the Father placed everything under His authority, and out of that very certainty He rises to pick up towel and basin (13:3–5). True greatness never scrambles for the highest seat; it stoops to perform the chore nobody else at the table wants to touch.

**To share in Christ, we must first let Him wash us.** Peter refuses because he feels unworthy for his Master to serve him. It sounds humble, yet underneath lies an insistence on dictating what the Lord may do for him. Jesus speaks plainly: "Unless I wash you, you have no share with Me" (13:6–10). Before serving anyone else, a disciple must consent to be served by the Lord.

**Jesus loves even the man betraying Him.** Fully aware of Judas's plot, Jesus still washes his feet and still hands him the dipped morsel—a host's token of honor to a favored guest (13:11; 13:26). Nobody at table even suspects Judas, because Jesus treats him no differently from the rest. His love never waits for us to prove worthy.

**A new command brings a new standard.** "Just as I have loved you, so you also must love one another" (13:34). The benchmark is no longer "as yourself," but as the Lord who just knelt with a basin and is about to die for them. The world will recognize His disciples not primarily by creed or ceremony, but by how they love one another (13:35).

**The Lord knows our weakness better than we do, yet never abandons us.** Peter pledges sincerely to die for his Master, while Jesus sees that before dawn Peter will deny Him three times (13:37–38). Even so, He also promises: you cannot follow now, "yet you will follow afterward" (13:36). Warning of failure walks side by side with the promise of restoration.

## Deeper Meaning

**Foot-washing tells the entire story of Jesus in miniature.** He rises from table, lays aside His garment, takes up a servant's task, then puts His garment back on and resumes His place (13:4–5; 13:12). Paul tells that exact same story on a cosmic canvas: the One in the form of God "took the form of a servant," humbled Himself to death on a cross, and was then highly exalted by God (Philippians 2:7–9).

**David's lament becomes the voice of Jesus.** Verse 18 quotes the Psalms: "Even my close friend, whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me" (Psalms 41:9). David suffered betrayal by his trusted adviser Ahithophel (2 Samuel 15:31). When his counsel failed, Ahithophel went home and hanged himself (2 Samuel 17:23); Judas likewise "went away and hanged himself" (Matthew 27:5). King David's grief repeats itself in David's greater Son.

**Old yet brand new.** The Law of Moses already commanded, "Love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18). Later John writes that this command is simultaneously old and new (1 John 2:7–8). Its newness lies in love visibly embodied before our eyes: "He laid down His life for us, and we ought to lay down our lives for our brothers" (1 John 3:16).

**At the very same meal, the disciples argued over greatness.** Luke notes that on this very evening a quarrel erupted over which disciple ranked greatest (Luke 22:24), prompting Jesus to reply, "I am among you as one who serves" (Luke 22:27). John omits the argument itself and narrates the action that answered it—embodying Mark's summary that the Son of Man came "not to be served, but to serve" (Mark 10:45).

**"And it was night."** Those brief words convey more than clock time. From page one John announced that "the light shines in the darkness" (John 1:5) and that people "loved darkness rather than light" (John 3:19). Turning away from the Light of the World, Judas steps out into literal and spiritual night (13:30).

**Peter will follow, though not yet.** At the end of the Gospel, Jesus restores Peter after his three denials, foretells the death by which he will glorify God, and commands, "Follow Me" (John 21:18–19). Luke adds that Jesus had already prayed for him "that your faith may not fail" (Luke 22:32).

## Notes & Nuances

**Must Christians literally wash one another's feet?** Some churches—such as Mennonites, Brethren, and Seventh-day Adventists—practice literal foot-washing as an ordinance. Catholic and many other churches perform it on the Thursday before Easter; the English name Maundy Thursday derives from the Latin word for "commandment," drawn from verse 34. Most other Christians treat it as a vivid pattern for humble service of every kind. Since 1 Timothy lists "washing the feet of the saints" among the good deeds of faithful women, early churches likely practiced it literally as part of hospitality (1 Timothy 5:10).

**What does "whoever has bathed needs only to wash his feet" mean?** The classic reading sees bathing as once-for-all cleansing upon coming to Christ, while foot-washing stands for ongoing forgiveness of daily sins picked up like road dust. Jesus also tells the disciples elsewhere that they are already clean through His word (15:3). Several ancient manuscripts omit "except for his feet" in verse 10; on that shorter reading, the foot-washing itself symbolizes the complete cleansing He is about to win on the cross.

**If Satan entered Judas, is Judas still responsible?** John affirms both realities. The devil puts the scheme into Judas's heart and then enters him (13:2; 13:27), yet John has already noted Judas pilfering from the common purse (12:6), and right up to the final moment Jesus extends the bread as a gesture of appeal. Prophecy foresees betrayal without turning Judas into a puppet; he opened his own heart to the adversary.

**Who is the disciple "whom Jesus loved"?** John never supplies a name, identifying him only as the one reclining next to Jesus at supper, standing beneath the cross, and bearing witness to write this Gospel (13:23; 19:26; 21:24). Church tradition since the second century identifies him as the apostle John, son of Zebedee. Others have suggested Lazarus, described earlier as "the one You love" (11:3), or an unnamed Jerusalem disciple outside the Twelve. Because guests reclined on low couches at formal dinners, the companion beside Jesus could easily lean back against His chest to whisper a question.

## What Commentators Say

**Ambrose**, late fourth-century bishop of Milan, noted that the church in Milan washed the feet of newly baptized believers even though the church in Rome did not. He defended the custom using Jesus' reply to Peter in verse 8, and interpreted verse 10 symbolically: baptism cleanses sin, while foot-washing heals the heel bitten by the serpent back in Adam's day, helping believers stand firmer against temptation. *(On the Sacraments, book 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: clearly recalled Ambrose contrasting Milan's custom with Rome's; the serpent-and-heel motif recalled in substance though exact phrasing unverified -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, explained verse 10 this way: anyone washed in baptism is clean overall, yet while walking through earthly life our feet pick up dust again. We wash our feet daily whenever we pray, "Forgive us our debts" (Matthew 6:12). Washing one another's feet means forgiving one another and praying for one another. *(Tractates on the Gospel of John, around tractates 56–58)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Augustine's themes on road dust and mutual forgiveness; exact tractate numbers not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, sharply criticized the annual papal ceremony of washing the feet of twelve poor men. To him it amounted to hollow theater: washing feet one afternoon while despising one's neighbors the rest of the year. Jesus was not instituting a ritual here, but calling believers to a way of life ready to shoulder even the lowliest service for brothers and sisters. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John, on chapter 13 verse 14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: clearly recalled Calvin calling the annual papal foot-washing ceremony a theatrical imitation here; exact wording not checked -->

**Richard Bauckham**, British New Testament scholar, argued that the beloved disciple was indeed an eyewitness and the Gospel's author, yet not John son of Zebedee. Instead, he identified him with a Jerusalem disciple known in second-century sources as John the Elder—which, in his view, explains why John's Gospel highlights Judean and Jerusalem episodes bypassed by the other Gospels. *(Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2006)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this identification forms the central thesis of the concluding chapters of Bauckham's book -->
