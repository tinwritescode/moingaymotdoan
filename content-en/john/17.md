# John 17 — The Prayer of the Son

## Story

After speaking these words, Jesus lifted His eyes toward heaven and prayed: "Father, the hour has come; glorify Your Son, that the Son may glorify You." You granted Him authority over all humanity so that He might give eternal life to all whom You have given Him. "And this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent." I have glorified You on earth by completing the work You gave Me to do. Now, Father, glorify Me in Your own presence with the glory I shared with You before the world existed (17:1–5). Eternal life is not merely endless duration, but knowing God.

Next He prays for His disciples. I have revealed Your name to the people You gave Me out of the world. They were Yours, You gave them to Me, and they have kept Your word. Now they know that everything You gave Me comes from You, because I have passed on to them the words You gave Me; they accepted them, truly understood that I came from You, and believed that You sent Me (17:6–8).

I pray for them—not for the world, but for those You have given Me, because they belong to You. All that is Mine is Yours, and all that is Yours is Mine, and I am glorified in them. I am no longer remaining in the world, yet they stay in the world while I come to You. Holy Father, protect them in Your name, the name You gave Me, so that they may be one just as We are one. While I was with them, I guarded them, and not one of them was lost except the son of destruction, so that Scripture would be fulfilled. Now I am coming to You, and I speak these things while still in the world so they may have My joy fulfilled within themselves (17:9–13).

I have given them Your word, and the world hated them because they do not belong to the world, just as I do not belong to the world. "I do not ask that You take them out of the world, but that You keep them from the evil one." "Sanctify them in the truth; Your word is truth." Just as You sent Me into the world, so I have sent them into the world. For their sake I consecrate Myself, so that they too may be sanctified in truth (17:14–19). He does not ask to pull disciples out of the world, but to guard them as they are sent right into it.

Then He prays for future generations. I do not pray for these alone, but also for those who will believe in Me through their message, so that all of them may be one—just as You, Father, are in Me and I in You, that they also may be in Us, so the world may believe that You sent Me. The glory You gave Me I have given them, so they may be one as We are one: I in them and You in Me, that they may be brought to complete unity, letting the world know You sent Me and loved them even as You loved Me (17:20–23). Every believer reading these words today stands inside that prayer.

Father, I desire that those You have given Me may be with Me where I am, to behold My glory which You gave Me because You loved Me before the foundation of the world. Righteous Father, though the world does not know You, I know You, and these know that You sent Me. I have made Your name known to them and will continue making it known, so that the love with which You loved Me may live in them, and I Myself in them (17:24–26). His final prayer before Calvary closes on the word "love."

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus' glory shines through the cross.** "The hour has come" (17:1) means the hour of His death. Asking the Father to glorify Him is no plea to escape that hour, but a prayer that His approaching death would unveil who the Father truly is. He even speaks as though the task already stands finished: "I have completed the work You gave Me to do" (17:4). For John, the cross never hides God's glory; it displays it at its brightest.

**In the world, yet not of the world.** Jesus neither asks to evacuate His disciples from earth nor permits them to blend seamlessly into its values. They stay in the world just as He did, facing hatred just as He did, while kept safe by the Father from the evil one (17:14–16). This prayer rules out both tempting shortcuts: retreating from the world, or conforming to it.

**Set apart in order to be sent out.** He asks the Father to sanctify His disciples through truth—God's word—and adds immediately, "As You sent Me into the world, so I have sent them into the world" (17:17–18). Sanctification here is not hiding away in spotless isolation, but being consecrated for a mission, just as Jesus consecrates Himself for the cross (17:19).

**Unity serves a mission.** Jesus prays that all believers be united after the pattern of Father in Son and Son in Father (17:21). Such unity must be visible because it carries a task: "so that the world may believe that You sent Me." When believers fracture, the world struggles to believe; when they love in unity, the world sees the Father (17:21–23).

**The final goal is being with Him.** Above earthly success, Jesus' climactic desire is for His people to be with Him and gaze upon His glory (17:24). Beneath every petition flows the Father's love for the Son "before the foundation of the world," now poured into believers' hearts (17:24–26).

## Deeper Meaning

**A high priest carrying His people's names upon His chest.** On the Day of Atonement, Israel's high priest made atonement "for himself, for his household, and for the whole assembly of Israel" (Leviticus 16:17). John 17 moves in that exact three-part sweep: for Himself, for His immediate disciples, and for all future believers. Just as Aaron bore the names of the twelve tribes on his breastpiece entering the sanctuary (Exodus 28:29), Jesus carries the names of those given to Him as He returns to the Father—a ministry Hebrews says continues without ceasing: "He always lives to intercede for them" (Hebrews 7:25).

**Making the Father's name known.** On Mount Sinai, God "proclaimed the name of the LORD" before Moses, declaring Himself "compassionate and gracious, slow to anger" (Exodus 34:5–6). In biblical thought, a person's name expresses their true character. Saying "I have revealed Your name" (17:6) means that through His life and death, people finally see who God truly is.

**Glory before the world began.** Verse 17:5 loops back to the Gospel's prologue: the eternal Word "became flesh," and "we beheld His glory" (John 1:14). Paul likewise writes that the One "in very nature God" humbled Himself unto death on a cross (Philippians 2:6–8). Jesus does not ask for a brand-new honor, but to re-enter the glory eternally His.

**The son of destruction.** The Scripture fulfilled in 17:12 is the verse Jesus already cited in chapter 13: "The one who ate My bread has lifted his heel against Me" (John 13:18; Psalms 41:9). Originally David's lament over a trusted friend's treachery, Jesus reads David's wound as a foreshadowing of His own.

**One body and one Spirit.** Paul urges churches to "make every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace," because "there is one body and one Spirit" (Ephesians 4:3–4). To quarreling Corinthians he demands, "Is Christ divided?" (1 Corinthians 1:13). Unity is not something Christians invent from scratch; it already exists in Christ, and the Church is summoned to guard and display it.

## Notes & Nuances

**"I am not praying for the world" does not mean indifference toward the world.** In 17:9 Jesus focuses His intercession specifically on His disciples at that moment. Yet this same Gospel proclaims "God so loved the world" (John 3:16), and twice inside this very prayer Jesus sets the world's faith and knowledge as the ultimate aim (17:21; 17:23). He prays for His disciples so that through them the world may meet the Father.

**Was Judas predestined to perish?** The phrase "son of destruction" (17:12) is a Hebrew idiom meaning someone headed for ruin, just like "children of wrath" denotes those under judgment (Ephesians 2:3). Many interpreters understand Scripture as foreseeing what Judas freely chose rather than forcing his hand—prophecy describes what will happen without coercing it. Others, standing in the Augustinian and Reformed stream, emphasize God's sovereign plan more strongly while still insisting Judas bears full moral responsibility for his act.

**Does verse 17:3 rank Jesus below God?** Ancient Arians and some modern groups seize on "the only true God" to restrict full deity to the Father alone. Yet the very same sentence links Jesus Christ directly beside the Father as the object of saving knowledge, and two verses later Jesus speaks of sharing glory with the Father before creation existed (17:5). Most interpreters read the verse as affirming strict monotheism—one true God—while uniting the Son inseparably with the Father.

**What kind of unity is envisioned?** Verse 17:21 became the banner text of the twentieth-century ecumenical movement. Some believe Jesus envisions visible structural reunion among churches into a single institution; others see spiritual unity in heart and truth across distinct denominations. Both sides agree on two essentials: the pattern is the communion of Father and Son, and the unity must shine plainly enough for the world to notice.

**The title "High Priestly Prayer."** John's Gospel itself assigns no heading to this chapter. As early as Cyril of Alexandria, readers recognized Jesus here as interceding High Priest, though the familiar label is generally traced to sixteenth-century Lutheran theologian David Chytraeus. The title fits naturally with Jesus "consecrating Myself" (17:19), like a priest setting himself apart before offering sacrifice.

## What Commentators Say

**Cyril of Alexandria**, fifth-century bishop, read John 17 as the intercession of Christ our High Priest. On 17:21 he taught that believers become one in two intertwined ways: inwardly through one Holy Spirit dwelling in all, and bodily by partaking together of Christ's one body in the Eucharist—eating one bread makes them one body. *(Commentary on John, book 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Cyril's dual emphasis on Spirit and Eucharist in commenting on 17:21 is widely noted; exact chapter inside book 11 unverified -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century preacher of Antioch and archbishop of Constantinople, interpreted "for their sake I consecrate Myself" (17:19) as offering Himself in sacrifice. Because Old Testament sacrifices were called consecrated or holy offerings, Jesus here announces He is about to offer His own life on behalf of His disciples. *(Homilies on John)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Chrysostom reading "consecrate" in 17:19 in sacrificial terms; exact homily number not recalled -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observed on 17:9 that Jesus here intercedes specifically for those the Father has given Him. Even so, Calvin stressed this never allows believers to limit their prayers to their own circle: because we cannot know whom God will draw to Himself, we must pray for all people. *(Commentary on the Gospel according to John)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Calvin balancing particular intercession in 17:9 with our duty to pray for all; exact wording not checked -->

**Pope John Paul II**, head of the Catholic Church from 1978 to 2005, named his landmark encyclical on Christian unity after the Latin words of 17:21 (*Ut Unum Sint*—"That They May Be One"). Calling Christian division contrary to Christ's will and a stumbling block to the world, he even invited other churches to enter dialogue over how the papacy itself might be exercised. *(Encyclical Ut Unum Sint, 1995)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the encyclical title comes directly from John 17:21, and its closing invitation to discuss papal ministry is widely noted -->

**Lesslie Newbigin**, British missionary in India and an architect of the Church of South India, insisted that unity and mission can never be pulled apart.
