# John 5 — The Invalid at the Pool of Bethesda

## Story

Afterward there was a Jewish festival, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem. Near the Sheep Gate sits a pool called Bethesda in Hebrew, bordered by five covered colonnades. Crowds of sick people—the blind, the lame, and the paralyzed—lay there waiting for the water to stir. People believed that from time to time an angel came down to stir the pool, and whoever stepped in first after the water moved was healed of whatever affliction he had (5:1–4). The end of verse 3 and all of verse 4, describing the angel stirring the water, do not appear in the earliest manuscripts; many scholars regard them as a later explanatory note reflecting popular belief around the pool.

Among them lay a man who had been an invalid for thirty-eight years. Seeing him lying there and knowing he had suffered a long time, Jesus asked, "Do you want to be made well?" Rather than saying yes or no, the man poured out his frustration: sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water stirs; as soon as I try to get down, someone else steps in ahead of me. Jesus told him, "Get up, pick up your mat, and walk." Instantly the man was healed, picked up his mat, and walked. That day was the Sabbath (5:5–9). Thirty-eight years waiting for a chance that never came; one word from Jesus was enough.

The Jewish leaders confronted the healed man: today is the Sabbath, and it is not lawful for you to carry your mat. He answered: the man who made me well told me, "Pick up your mat and walk." They asked who had told him that, yet he did not know, because Jesus had slipped away into the crowd. Later Jesus found him in the temple and said: see, you are well; stop sinning, or something worse may happen to you. The man went and reported to the Jewish leaders that it was Jesus who had healed him (5:10–15).

Because He did such things on the Sabbath, the Jewish leaders began persecuting Jesus. He answered them, "My Father is always working until now, and I too am working." For this reason they tried all the harder to kill Him—not only because He was breaking the Sabbath, but also because He called God His own Father, making Himself equal with God (5:16–18). They grasped the full weight of His claim.

Jesus answered them: the Son can do nothing on His own, only what He sees the Father doing; whatever the Father does, the Son does in just the same way. The Father loves the Son and shows Him everything He Himself does. Just as the Father raises the dead and gives life, so too the Son gives life to whomever He pleases. The Father has entrusted all judgment to the Son, so that all may honor the Son just as they honor the Father; whoever does not honor the Son fails to honor the Father who sent Him (5:19–23).

Truly, whoever hears My word and believes Him who sent Me has eternal life and will not come under judgment, but has crossed over from death to life. An hour is coming, and is already here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live. For just as the Father has life in Himself, so He has granted the Son to have life in Himself, and He has given Him authority to execute judgment because He is the Son of Man. Do not marvel at this: an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear His voice and come out—those who have done good to a resurrection of life, and those who have practiced evil to a resurrection of judgment. I can do nothing on My own; I judge as I hear, and My judgment is just, because I seek not My own will but the will of Him who sent Me (5:24–30).

Then He summoned witnesses on His behalf. If I testify about Myself alone, My testimony is not valid. You sent emissaries to John, and he testified to the truth; he was a burning and shining lamp, and for a while you enjoyed rejoicing in his light. Yet I hold a testimony weightier than John's: the very works the Father gave Me to finish testify that the Father has sent Me. The Father Himself has borne witness to Me, though you have never heard His voice nor seen His form, and His word does not abide in you because you refuse to believe the One He sent (5:31–38).

"You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about Me." Even so, you refuse to come to Me to have life. I do not accept glory from human beings. I have come in My Father's name, and you do not receive Me; if another comes in his own name, him you will receive. How can you believe when you accept glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God? Do not imagine that I will accuse you before the Father; your accuser is Moses, on whom your hopes are set. If you believed Moses, you would believe Me, for he wrote about Me. But if you do not believe his writings, how will you believe My words? (5:39–47). It is possible to study Scripture thoroughly and still miss the very Person Scripture points to.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus heals a helpless man on the Sabbath, sparking open conflict.** After thirty-eight years lying paralyzed waiting for stirred water with no one to help, a single word from Jesus lifts him up carrying his mat. That merciful healing ignites the plot to kill Him because He declares Himself working alongside His Father (5:5–18).

**The Son and the Father are wholly united in obedience and authority.** Jesus never acts independently or in rivalry with God; the Son does only what He sees the Father doing, and does it identically. Both uniquely divine prerogatives—giving life and judging humanity—are placed wholly in the Son's hands so that everyone may honor the Son as they honor the Father (5:19–23).

**Eternal life does not wait until the end of history to begin.** Whoever hears Jesus' word and trusts the One who sent Him has already "crossed over from death to life." At the same time, a final hour still approaches when all in the graves will hear His voice and rise for righteous judgment (5:24–30).

**Studying Scripture without coming to Jesus misses Scripture's entire aim.** Jesus lays out an array of witnesses: John the Baptist, His own works, the Father, and the Scriptures themselves. The sharpest peril for devout people is craving human applause so deeply that they cling to every letter of Moses while turning away from the One Moses wrote about (5:31–47).

## Deeper Meaning

**The Son of Man's authority to judge in Daniel.** When Jesus explains that the Father entrusted judgment to the Son "because He is the Son of Man" (5:27), He points directly to Daniel's vision of "one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven" to whom the Ancient of Days grants everlasting dominion and kingdom (Daniel 7:13–14).

**Spiritual resurrection and bodily resurrection.** Jesus' words about a day when everyone in the tombs rises—some to life, some to judgment (5:28–29)—echo Daniel's prophecy: "Multitudes who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake: some to everlasting life, others to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2). Meanwhile, the spiritually dead hearing His voice and coming alive right now (5:24–25) connects with Paul's description of people once "dead in your trespasses and sins" made alive in Christ (Ephesians 2:1).

**The Mosaic rule of two or three witnesses.** The law stipulated that "a matter must be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15). Jesus respects that legal standard across 5:31–39, refusing to rely on self-testimony alone and calling a sequence of valid witnesses: John the Baptist, the works the Father assigned Him, the Father Himself, and the Scriptures.

**Moses wrote about Him.** Jesus' declaration that "he wrote about Me" (5:46) recalls Moses' farewell promise that God would raise up a prophet like himself from among the people to whom they must listen (Deuteronomy 18:15), as well as the entire covenant and sacrificial system of the Old Testament pointing forward to Christ.

## Notes & Nuances

**The passage about the angel stirring the water at the end of verse 3 and verse 4 is absent from the earliest manuscripts.** The oldest and most reliable Greek manuscripts of John omit these lines, leading most scholars to conclude that a later copyist inserted a marginal note explaining popular folklore at Bethesda so readers would understand the invalid's complaint in verse 7. Omitting or including the note changes nothing about Jesus' miracle.

**The warning "stop sinning" in 5:14 does not mean every illness stems from personal sin.** Jesus Himself explicitly rejects that rigid link when encountering the man born blind in chapter 9 (9:2–3). Here He is not diagnosing the medical cause of the man's paralysis, but urging someone healed physically not to stumble into the far graver disaster of final judgment.

**Jewish context explains why 5:17 sounded blasphemous.** Jewish teachers of the day acknowledged that God continually sustains the cosmos, grants life, and renders judgment even on the Sabbath without violating Sabbath rest. By declaring, "My Father is always working until now, and I too am working," Jesus stepped directly into God's exclusive prerogative, which is why His hearers instantly understood Him to be claiming equality with God (5:18).

**Verse 5:29 on deeds does not clash with salvation by faith in 5:24.** John never divorces faith from how a person lives: anyone who genuinely trusts the Son steps into the light and practices truth (3:21). Deeds at the final judgment therefore display outwardly whether a heart truly embraced or rejected Christ.

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa across the fourth and fifth centuries, distinguished two resurrections in chapter 5: verse 5:25 describes the first resurrection taking place here and now, when a soul dead in sin hears the Son of God and comes alive through faith, whereas 5:28–29 describes the second resurrection of physical bodies on the last day. *(Tractates on the Gospel of John, tractate 19)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: distinguishing the present spiritual resurrection from the final bodily resurrection is Augustine's classic exposition of John 5 -->
