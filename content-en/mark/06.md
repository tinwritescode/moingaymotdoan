# Mark 6 — Rejected at Home, the Death of John, and Bread for Five Thousand

## Story

Jesus returned to His hometown accompanied by His disciples. On the Sabbath He began teaching in the synagogue. Many who heard Him were astonished: where did this man get these things? What is this wisdom given to Him, and what are these miracles performed by His hands? "Isn't this the carpenter, the son of Mary," the brother of James, Joses, Judas, and Simon? Aren't His sisters right here among us? And they took offense at Him (6:1–3).

He replied: a prophet is not without honor except in his hometown, among his relatives, and in his own household. He could not do any mighty work there, except lay His hands on a few sick people and heal them, and He marveled at their unbelief. Then He went around teaching from village to village (6:4–6).

Calling the Twelve to Him, He began sending them out two by two, giving them authority over unclean spirits. He instructed them to take nothing for the journey except a staff—no bread, no bag, no money in their belts—to wear sandals, and not to put on two tunics. Whenever you enter a house, stay there until you leave that place. If any place will not welcome you or listen to you, shake the dust off your feet as you leave as a testimony against them. They went out preaching repentance, driving out many demons, and anointing many sick people with oil and healing them (6:7–13).

King Herod heard about Jesus' fame. Some were saying John the Baptist had risen from the dead, others said Elijah, still others a prophet like the prophets of old. Yet Herod declared: John, whom I beheaded, has been raised from the dead! (6:14–16).

Mark flashes back to what had happened. Herod had arrested John and bound him in prison because of Herodias, his brother Philip's wife, whom Herod had married. John had been telling Herod, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife." Herodias nursed a grudge against John and wanted him dead, yet could not manage it because Herod feared John, knowing him to be a righteous and holy man, and protected him. Listening to John left Herod deeply perplexed, yet he liked listening to him anyway (6:17–20).

Herodias got her chance on Herod's birthday, when he threw a banquet for his nobles, military commanders, and leading men of Galilee. Herodias's daughter came in and danced, pleasing Herod and his guests. The king told the girl: ask me for anything you want, and I will give it to you. He even vowed: whatever you ask, up to half my kingdom, I will give you. Stepping outside, she asked her mother: what should I ask for? The head of John the Baptist, her mother replied. She hurried right back in: I want you to give me right now the head of John the Baptist on a platter (6:21–25).

The king was deeply distressed, yet because of his oaths and his dinner guests he did not want to refuse her. He sent an executioner with orders to bring John's head. The soldier went, beheaded John in prison, brought his head on a platter, and gave it to the girl, who handed it to her mother. Hearing of it, John's disciples came, took his body, and laid it in a tomb (6:26–29).

The apostles gathered back around Jesus and reported everything they had done and taught. He told them: come away by yourselves to a quiet place and rest a while—for so many people were coming and going that they did not even have time to eat. They went off by boat to a solitary place, yet many recognized them and ran ahead on foot from all the towns, arriving before them (6:30–33).

Stepping ashore and seeing a vast crowd, He felt deep compassion for them, because they were "like sheep without a shepherd," and He began teaching them many things. By late afternoon the disciples urged Him to send the people away to surrounding farms and villages to buy food. He answered, "You give them something to eat." They asked: should we go spend two hundred denarii on bread to feed them? He asked: how many loaves do you have? Go look. Five loaves and two fish, they reported (6:34–38).

He directed them to have everyone sit down in groups on the green grass, by hundreds and fifties. Taking the five loaves and two fish, looking up to heaven, He gave thanks, broke the loaves, and handed them to the disciples to distribute, dividing the two fish among them all as well. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and they picked up twelve basketfuls of leftover broken pieces and fish. Those who ate numbered five thousand men (6:39–44).

Immediately He made His disciples get into the boat and head ahead toward Bethsaida while He dismissed the crowd. After saying goodbye to them, He went up on a mountainside to pray. When evening came, the boat was out in the middle of the lake and He was alone on land. Seeing them straining at the oars against a headwind, about the fourth watch of the night He came toward them walking on the sea—and He meant to pass by them (6:45–48).

Seeing Him walking on the water, they thought He was a ghost and cried out, for they all saw Him and were terrified. Immediately He spoke to them: "Take courage! It is I. Do not be afraid." Climbing into the boat with them, the wind died down. They were utterly astonished, for they had not understood about the loaves—their hearts were hardened (6:49–52).

Crossing over, they landed at Gennesaret and moored the boat. People recognized Him at once, ran through the entire region, and carried the sick on mats wherever they heard He was. Wherever He went—villages, towns, or countryside—they laid the sick in the marketplaces and begged just to touch the fringe of His cloak; and everyone who touched it was healed (6:53–56).

## Key Takeaways

**Familiarity can make people blind.** Nazareth watched Jesus grow up: the local carpenter, Mary's son, brother of familiar neighbors (6:3). Convinced they already had Him figured out, they refused to see who He truly was. Even growing up familiar with religion can quietly harden a heart.

**The apostles are sent out empty-handed.** Jesus forbids packing bread, travel bags, or money (6:8). They must depend on others' hospitality and on God's care. God's mission never launches on full budgets; it starts with obedience.

**Saving face silences conscience.** Herod respects John as a holy man and enjoys listening to him (6:20). Yet a reckless boast and fear of looking weak before banquet guests push him straight into murder (6:26). Terrible wrongs often happen not from fierce hatred, but from cowardice refusing to say no.

**Jesus is the true Shepherd.** Looking at the crowds, He feels compassion because they are "like sheep without a shepherd" (6:34). Mark sets two banquets side by side in this chapter: King Herod's feast ends with a severed head on a platter, while Jesus' feast on green grass fills five thousand hungry people.

**He comes to His disciples against a headwind.** From the mountain, He spots them straining at the oars (6:48). Stepping across the waves, He calls out, "Take courage! It is I. Do not be afraid" (6:50). Christ sees His people struggling even when they cannot yet see Him.

## Deeper Meaning

**Sheep without a shepherd.** Before dying, Moses asked God to appoint a successor "so that the LORD's congregation will not be like sheep without a shepherd" (Numbers 27:17). God chose Joshua. Jesus—whose Hebrew name is the exact same name as Joshua—arrives as that promised Shepherd.

**Green pastures.** The detail of "green grass" appears uniquely in Mark (6:39), whispering Psalms 23: "He makes me lie down in green pastures" (Psalms 23:2). The Good Shepherd leads His flock to rest and feeds them abundantly.

**Elisha feeding a hundred men.** Elisha once fed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves while his servant protested, "How can I set this before a hundred men?" Elisha answered, "They will eat and have some left over" (2 Kings 4:43–44). Jesus outstrips Elisha: five loaves feeding five thousand.

**Groups of hundreds and fifties.** In the wilderness, Moses organized Israel into divisions of "thousands, hundreds, fifties" (Exodus 18:21). Seating the crowds by hundreds and fifties (6:40) pictures a renewed Israel in the desert, fed by God just as He once fed them manna.

**Walking on the waves.** Job declares of God, "He alone stretches out the heavens and treads on the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8). Walking across the lake, Jesus performs what Scripture says belongs to God alone.

**"It is I" and "pass by."** In Greek, "It is I" literally reads "I AM," echoing God's self-revelation to Moses: "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14). Likewise, intending to "pass by them" (6:48) echoes God letting His glory "pass by" Moses on Mount Sinai (Exodus 33:22).

## Notes & Nuances

**Could Jesus really do no miracles in Nazareth?** Mark says He could do no mighty work there aside from healing a few sick people (6:5). That does not mean His divine power ran dry. Matthew explains He did not do many miracles there because of their unbelief. Jesus never forces miracles upon shut hearts; His signs are invitations to trust, never theatrical stunts.

**Why call Him "son of Mary"?** Men were normally identified by their father's name, making "son of Mary" striking. Joseph may already have died by then, or locals may have been dropping a veiled slur questioning Jesus' parentage. Matthew alters the phrase to "the carpenter's son" (Matthew 13:55).

**Herod was not strictly a king.** This is Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, granted only the lesser title tetrarch of Galilee and Perea by Rome. Mark uses "king" in the casual way locals spoke. Under Mosaic law, marrying a living brother's wife was explicitly forbidden (Leviticus 18:16), prompting John's rebuke.

**Does a reckless vow oblige someone to do evil?** Herod kept his oath purely out of pride. Offering "up to half my kingdom" sounds like the Persian king speaking to Esther (Esther 5:3). No oath ever obligates a person to commit murder. Herod sinned twice: making a foolish vow, and keeping it.

**Anointing the sick with oil.** The apostles anointed many sick people with oil and healed them (6:13). Later James instructs church elders to pray over the sick, "anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14). Churches continue this practice today in varied ways.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, the third-century Alexandrian theologian, answered the pagan critic Celsus, who sneered at Jesus for being a mere carpenter. Origen replied that none of the Gospels used in the churches ever calls Jesus Himself a carpenter—likely because Origen's copy of Mark read "son of the carpenter" like Matthew. **The Talmud**, the collection of rabbinic commentary, insists every father must teach his son a trade; Rabbi Judah even warned that failing to teach a son a trade teaches him to become a bandit. Numerous revered rabbis worked with their hands. Nazareth stumbled not because carpentry was shameful, but because a familiar hometown neighbor dared speak and act like a prophet. *(Babylonian Talmud, Kiddushin 29a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the duty to teach a son a trade and Rabbi Judah's saying appear in Kiddushin 29a; the link to Mark 6:3 is editorial -->

**Richard Hays**, a contemporary American New Testament scholar, observes that Mark seldom states outright that Jesus is God, choosing instead to reveal it through Old Testament echoes. Walking on the sea evokes Job 9:8, "I AM" evokes the divine name, and "meant to pass by" evokes God passing before Moses. Scripture-saturated readers immediately recognize Jesus portrayed with imagery belonging solely to the Lord. *(Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels, 2016)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Hays analyzes Mark 6:45–52 through Job 9:8 and Exodus 33–34 in his chapter on Mark -->
