# Mark 9 — Transfiguration, the Demon-Possessed Boy, and Who Is Greatest

## Story

Jesus tells His disciples: truly some standing here will not taste death before they see the kingdom of God come with power (9:1).

Six days later, Jesus took Peter, James, and John up a high mountain by themselves, and He was transfigured before them. His clothes became dazzling white, whiter than any launderer on earth could bleach them. Elijah and Moses appeared, talking with Him. Peter spoke up: Rabbi, it is good for us to be here; let us put up three shelters—one for You, one for Moses, and one for Elijah. He did not know what to say, for they were all terrified (9:2–6).

A cloud overshadowed them, and a voice came from the cloud: "This is My beloved Son. Listen to Him!" Suddenly, looking around, they saw no one with them anymore except Jesus alone (9:7–8).

Coming down the mountain, He ordered them not to tell anyone what they had seen until the Son of Man had risen from the dead. They kept the matter to themselves, discussing what "rising from the dead" meant. They asked Him: why do the scribes say Elijah must come first? He answered: Elijah indeed comes first and restores all things; yet how then is it written that the Son of Man must suffer much and be rejected? I tell you, Elijah has already come, and they did to him whatever they wished, just as it is written about him (9:9–13).

Rejoining the other disciples, they saw a large crowd around them and scribes arguing with them. A father spoke up: Teacher, I brought You my son, who is possessed by a spirit that makes him mute; whenever it seizes him it throws him down, he foams at the mouth, grinds his teeth, and becomes rigid. I asked Your disciples to drive it out, yet they could not (9:14–18).

He replied: O unbelieving generation, how long shall I stay with you? Bring the boy to Me. The instant the spirit saw Jesus, it convulsed the boy, throwing him to the ground foaming at the mouth. Jesus asked the father: how long has he been like this? From childhood, he replied; it has often thrown him into fire or water to kill him—if You can do anything, take pity on us and help us! Jesus responded: "'If You can'? Everything is possible for one who believes." Immediately the father cried out, "I do believe; help my unbelief!" (9:19–24).

Seeing a crowd running together, Jesus rebuked the unclean spirit: deaf and mute spirit, I command you, come out of him and never enter him again! Shrieking and convulsing him violently, the spirit came out. The boy lay like a corpse, so that many said: he is dead. Yet Jesus took him by the hand and lifted him up, and he stood. Later inside the house, His disciples asked privately: why couldn't we drive it out? He answered: this kind can come out only by prayer (9:25–29).

Passing through Galilee, He did not want anyone to know, because He was teaching His disciples: the Son of Man is going to be delivered into the hands of men; they will kill Him, and after three days He will rise. Yet they did not understand what He meant and were afraid to ask Him (9:30–32).

Arriving in Capernaum, inside the house, He asked them: what were you arguing about on the road? They stayed silent, because along the way they had been arguing about who was the greatest. Sitting down, He summoned the Twelve: "If anyone wants to be first, he must be the very last, and the servant of all." Taking a little child, He set the child among them, gathered him into His arms, and said: whoever welcomes one of these little children in My name welcomes Me; and whoever welcomes Me does not welcome Me, but the One who sent Me (9:33–37). Right after His second announcement of the cross, the disciples are jockeying for rank.

John said: Teacher, we saw someone driving out demons in Your name and we tried to stop him, because he does not follow us. Do not stop him, Jesus replied, for no one who performs a miracle in My name can turn around and speak evil of Me. "For whoever is not against us is for us." Anyone who gives you a cup of water in My name because you belong to Christ will certainly not lose his reward (9:38–41).

Yet if anyone causes one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble, it would be better for him to have a heavy millstone hung around his neck and be cast into the sea. If your hand causes you to sin, cut it off; better to enter life maimed than with two hands go into hell, where the worm does not die and the fire is not quenched. The same goes for your foot or your eye. Everyone will be salted with fire. Salt is good, yet if it loses its saltiness, how can you season it again? Have salt in yourselves, and live at peace with one another (9:42–50).

## Key Takeaways

**Glory shines right along the road to the cross.** Six days after announcing His coming suffering, Jesus unveils His splendor to three disciples (9:2). They need this memory to keep hope alive when they later watch Him crucified. Glory and cross are not opposites; both belong to the same Lord.

**"Listen to Him!"** Peter wants three equal shelters for Jesus, Moses, and Elijah (9:5). The heavenly voice gently corrects him: only one is the beloved Son, and disciples must listen to Him (9:7). Once the cloud lifts, "they saw no one… except Jesus alone" (9:8).

**Faltering faith is still real faith.** The desperate father cannot muster spotless certainty. All he can cry is, "I do believe; help my unbelief!" (9:24). Jesus does not wait for him to conquer every doubt before stepping in. He honors that raw honesty and heals his son.

**Greatness looks like serving.** Minutes after Jesus predicts His murder, His followers argue over who ranks highest (9:34). Wrapping His arms around a child, Jesus tells them: whoever wants to be first must become last of all and servant of all (9:35). In God's kingdom, greatness is measured by serving the smallest.

**Guard others from stumbling—and deal ruthlessly with sin in yourself.** Jesus uses startling imagery: millstones, severed hands and feet, plucked eyes (9:42–47). He never commands self-harm; He warns that sin is deadly serious, demanding decisive cuts with whatever pulls us or others toward ruin.

## Deeper Meaning

**Six days and the cloud.** At Mount Sinai, "for six days the cloud covered the mountain, and on the seventh day the LORD called to Moses from within the cloud" (Exodus 24:16). Mark opens likewise with "After six days" (9:2), a high peak, a cloud, and God's voice. A new Sinai is unfolding.

**A prophet like Moses.** Moses promised God would raise up "a prophet like me… you must listen to him" (Deuteronomy 18:15). The command "Listen to Him!" (9:7) confirms Jesus is that promised Prophet, with Moses himself standing witness.

**Elijah coming first.** Malachi promised, "I will send you the prophet Elijah before that great and dreadful day of the LORD comes" (Malachi 4:5). Jesus reveals that Elijah has already arrived in John the Baptist, treated however his enemies pleased (9:13). Ancient Elijah was hunted by Queen Jezebel (1 Kings 19:2); John was murdered by Queen Herodias.

**Peter remembers the holy mountain.** Decades later, 2 Peter testifies, "We ourselves heard this voice that came from heaven when we were with Him on the sacred mountain" (2 Peter 1:18). What they had to keep quiet coming down the slope eventually became their public witness.

**Would that all God's people were prophets.** When two men prophesied inside Israel's camp, Joshua urged Moses, "Stop them!" Moses replied, "I wish that all the LORD's people were prophets" (Numbers 11:28–29). John tries to stop an outsider casting out demons in Jesus' name (9:38), and Jesus answers in Moses' exact spirit.

**Undying worm and unquenched fire.** The imagery of hell in 9:48 draws on the closing verse of Isaiah, depicting rebels whose "worm will not die, nor will their fire be quenched" (Isaiah 66:24). The word Jesus uses for "hell" is *Gehenna*, the Jerusalem valley once infamous for pagan child sacrifices.

## Notes & Nuances

**When would they see the kingdom "come with power"?** Verse 9:1 puzzles readers since all those disciples died long before Christ's final return. Because Mark places this saying immediately before the Transfiguration, many understand the three disciples glimpsed that coming power on the mountain. Others tie it to the resurrection, Pentecost, or the rapid spread of the gospel.

**By prayer—or by prayer and fasting?** The earliest manuscripts of 9:29 read simply "only by prayer," while many later manuscripts add "and fasting." Either way, the lesson is unmistakable: the disciples tried to lean on their own knack, forgetting that power belongs strictly to God.

**Verses 9:44 and 9:46 are absent from many ancient manuscripts.** Both verses repeat 9:48 word for word. Because the earliest Greek copies include only 9:48, most modern translations place 9:44 and 9:46 in footnotes.

**Literally cut off a hand?** No. Jesus uses vivid Jewish hyperbole to drive home urgency. Hands, feet, and eyes cannot sin on their own; sin springs from the heart (7:21). He calls for decisive breaks with habits, ties, or situations that keep dragging us down.

**What does "salted with fire" mean?** Verses 9:49–50 rank among Mark's trickiest lines. Mosaic law required every grain offering to be seasoned with salt, "the salt of the covenant" (Leviticus 2:13). Many understand disciples as being purified through fiery trials just as sacrifices were salted. The final call is crystal clear: keep what sets you apart, and live in peace with one another—healing the rivalry that sparked the chapter's argument.

## What Commentators Say

**Thomas Aquinas**, the thirteenth-century Italian Catholic theologian, asked why Jesus was transfigured. Anyone walking a grueling road, he answered, needs a glimpse of the destination ahead. **The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, records rabbinic debates over what Elijah will accomplish upon returning. Some sages said he would settle disputes over family lineage; others insisted he comes neither to drive away nor draw near, but to bring peace to the world in line with Malachi's promise of turning fathers' hearts back to children. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, meditated on the father's cry in 9:24. The man simultaneously believes and disbelieves—and that, Calvin writes, describes every believer in this life. Faith never grows so flawless that every shadow of doubt vanishes; therefore we continually ask God to strengthen our weak faith. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin emphasizes that faith in this life always remains mingled with unbelief on this verse; uncertain of exact wording -->

**John Wesley**, eighteenth-century founder of Methodism in England, preached a famous sermon on 9:38–39 titled "A Caution against Bigotry." He warned Christians never to obstruct or belittle others doing God's genuine work simply because they belong to a different group.
