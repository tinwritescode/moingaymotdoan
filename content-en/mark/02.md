# Mark 2 — A Torn Roof, Dining with Sinners, and the Sabbath

## Story

A few days later, Jesus returned to Capernaum. When word got out that He was home, so many gathered that there was no room left even outside the door, and He preached the word to them (2:1–2).

Four men arrived carrying a paralyzed man. Unable to reach Him because of the crowd, they dug through the roof directly above where He sat, made an opening, and lowered the mat the paralyzed man lay on (2:3–4). Seeing their faith, Jesus said to the paralytic, "Son, your sins are forgiven" (2:5).

Some scribes sitting there questioned in their hearts: why does this man talk like that? He is blaspheming! "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (2:6–7). They got one thing completely right: only God can forgive sins.

Perceiving their thoughts at once, He asked: which is easier to say to the paralytic—"Your sins are forgiven," or "Get up, pick up your mat, and walk"? So that you may know the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, He told the paralytic: get up, take your mat, and go home. The man rose, picked up his mat, and walked out in full view of everyone. They were all amazed and praised God: we have never seen anything like this (2:8–12).

He went out again beside the lake, crowds came to Him, and He taught them. Passing by, He saw Levi son of Alphaeus sitting at the tax booth, and said to him, "Follow Me." Levi stood up and followed Him (2:13–14).

Later, as Jesus dined at Levi's house, many tax collectors and sinners ate with Him and His disciples, for many such people followed Him. Scribes of the Pharisees asked His disciples: why does He eat with people like that? Hearing them, Jesus replied: it is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick; I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners (2:15–17).

John's disciples and the Pharisees were fasting. Some came and asked: why do their disciples fast while Yours do not? He replied: how can the bridegroom's friends fast while the bridegroom is still with them? The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them; on that day they will fast. Nobody sews an unshrunk patch on an old garment, nor pours new wine into old wineskins, or the wine will burst the skins and ruin both wine and skins; new wine belongs in fresh wineskins (2:18–22).

One Sabbath, walking through grainfields, His disciples began plucking heads of grain along the way. The Pharisees asked: why are they doing what is unlawful on the Sabbath? He reminded them what David did when hungry—entering the house of God in the days of Abiathar the high priest, eating the consecrated bread lawful only for priests, and sharing it with his companions (2:23–26).

Then He declared, "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." So the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath (2:27–28).

## Key Takeaways

**The deepest human need is forgiveness.** Four friends carried a man to get his legs fixed, yet Jesus starts with "your sins are forgiven" (2:5). He sees what the paralyzed man needs even more urgently than walking.

**Jesus exercises God's own prerogative.** The scribes are right: only God forgives sins (2:7). Jesus does not dispute them. He raises the paralyzed man to his feet to verify that He holds authority to forgive (2:10–11). This chapter launches the central question Mark's whole Gospel answers: who is this Man?

**God notices the faith of friends.** Mark writes that Jesus saw "*their* faith" (2:5)—the faith of the four carriers. They tore open a roof to bring their friend to Jesus. Sometimes our calling is carrying someone to Christ who cannot walk there on their own.

**Jesus came to invite sinners.** He recruits a tax collector and shares a table with people everyone else writes off (2:14–15). Like a doctor, He goes where the sick are (2:17). People convinced they are already healthy never knock on a doctor's door.

**New life calls for new forms.** The bridegroom has arrived, so it is no time to mourn (2:19). New wine needs fresh wineskins (2:22). The Sabbath exists to bless humanity (2:27). Jesus does not destroy God's Law; as its Lord, He restores its true heart.

## Deeper Meaning

**The One who forgives and heals.** Psalms 103 praises the Lord "who forgives all your iniquities and heals all your diseases" (Psalms 103:3). Isaiah records God declaring, "I, even I, am He who blots out your transgressions" (Isaiah 43:25). Inside a crowded Capernaum house, Jesus does both.

**God the Bridegroom.** The prophets often picture God as Israel's bridegroom. Hosea promises, "I will betroth you to Me forever" (Hosea 2:19). Isaiah sings, "As a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so will your God rejoice over you" (Isaiah 62:5). Calling Himself the bridegroom (2:19), Jesus steps into God's own metaphor.

**David and the sacred loaves.** Jesus recalls David fleeing Saul and receiving "the consecrated bread" from the priest (1 Samuel 21:6). Human hunger outweighed ceremonial restriction. If David acted lawfully, the Son of Man—greater than David—holds even higher authority.

**Plucking grain was not stealing.** The Law of Moses specifically allowed passersby in a neighbor's field "to pluck the heads of grain with your hands" (Deuteronomy 23:25). The Pharisees did not complain about eating; they objected to working on the Sabbath, classifying grain-plucking as harvesting.

**Sabbath as liberation.** Deuteronomy anchors Sabbath rest in remembering "that you were a slave in Egypt" and God brought you out (Deuteronomy 5:15). The Sabbath was God's gift to former slaves. Turning it into a fresh set of chains betrays its very reason for existing.

## Notes & Nuances

**Abiathar or Ahimelech?** Mark notes that David ate the sacred bread "when Abiathar was high priest" (2:26). Yet 1 Samuel identifies the priest on duty as Ahimelech, Abiathar's father (1 Samuel 21:1). Proposed explanations include reading the Greek phrase as "in the Scripture passage about Abiathar," noting that Abiathar was present and became far more famous later, or acknowledging a genuine historical difficulty. Tellingly, both Matthew and Luke omit the priest's name when retelling the episode.

**Who is "the Son of Man"?** For the first time in Mark, Jesus calls Himself "the Son of Man" (2:10)—His favorite self-designation. The phrase can simply mean "a human being," yet also evokes Daniel's heavenly figure receiving everlasting kingship from God. Jesus uses it both to veil and to reveal His identity.

**Jesus does not belittle the Sabbath.** Saying the Sabbath was made for humanity (2:27) never means rest doesn't matter or anything goes. It affirms that the Sabbath is God's gracious gift, and meeting real human need honors rather than breaks it.

**Sickness and sin.** Forgiving the man's sins before healing him does not mean every illness stems from personal sin—a notion Jesus explicitly rejects elsewhere (John 9:3). Here He simply shows that sin goes deeper than physical affliction.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mekhilta**, an early rabbinic commentary on Exodus, preserves a remark by Rabbi Simeon ben Menasya: the Sabbath has been delivered over to you, not you to the Sabbath. That wording strikingly resembles 2:27 and occurs in the rabbinic discussion explaining why saving a human life overrides Sabbath prohibitions. *(Mekhilta, on Exodus 31)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Rabbi Simeon ben Menasya's saying in Mekhilta on Exodus 31:13–14 is routinely set beside Mark 2:27 -->

**Tertullian**, a theologian in Carthage, North Africa, around AD 200, wrote against Marcion, who claimed the Old Testament God differed from Jesus' Father. Analyzing the grainfield episode, Tertullian argued that Jesus was not abolishing the Sabbath at all; by appealing to David's Old Testament precedent, Jesus stood squarely inside the Law rather than against it. *(Against Marcion, book 4, chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Tertullian cites the Sabbath grainfield scene against Marcion; recalled as book 4 chapter 12, though chapter number is unverified -->

**William Lane**, a twentieth-century American New Testament scholar, examines the mention of Abiathar in 2:26. Pointing out that Mark uses a parallel phrasing in 12:26—"in the passage about the bush"—to locate a Scripture section, Lane suggests 2:26 can likewise mean "in the passage about Abiathar." *(The Gospel according to Mark, 1974)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Lane presents the "in the passage about Abiathar" reading compared with 12:26; uncertain whether he endorses it as the sole best solution -->

**Bart Ehrman**, a contemporary American New Testament scholar, recounts that as a seminary student he wrote a lengthy paper trying to prove Mark made no mistake in 2:26. His professor wrote back a brief comment: maybe Mark just made a mistake. Ehrman says that remark sparked his reexamination of biblical inerrancy—showing how small textual puzzles can carry huge weight for readers. *(Misquoting Jesus, 2005, introduction)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the story of his Abiathar term paper and his professor's comment appears in the introduction of Misquoting Jesus -->
