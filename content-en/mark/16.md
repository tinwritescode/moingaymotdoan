# Mark 16 — The Empty Tomb

## Story

When the Sabbath was over, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome bought spices so they might go and anoint Jesus' body. Very early on the first day of the week, just after sunrise, they went to the tomb, asking one another along the way: who will roll away the stone from the entrance of the tomb for us? Looking up, however, they saw that the stone—though very large—had already been rolled back (16:1–4).

Entering the tomb, they saw a young man dressed in a white robe sitting on the right side, and they were alarmed. He said to them: do not be alarmed. You are looking for Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. "He has risen! He is not here!" See the place where they laid Him. Now go, tell His disciples and Peter that He is going ahead of you into Galilee; there you will see Him, just as He told you (16:5–7). Peter, who denied Him three times, is singled out by name.

Going out, the women fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had seized them. And they said nothing to anyone, because they were afraid (16:8). The oldest surviving manuscripts of Mark end right here; the verses that follow (16:9–20) appear in the majority of later copies.

Rising early on the first day of the week, Jesus appeared first to Mary Magdalene, out of whom He had driven seven demons. She went and told those who had been with Him, who were mourning and weeping. Yet when they heard He was alive and had been seen by her, they did not believe it. Afterward He appeared in a different form to two of them walking into the countryside. These went back and told the others, yet they did not believe them either (16:9–13).

Finally He appeared to the Eleven as they reclined at table, rebuking them for their unbelief and stubbornness of heart because they had refused to believe those who saw Him after He had risen. He told them, "Go into all the world and preach the gospel to every creature." Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved, while whoever does not believe will be condemned. These signs will accompany those who believe: in My name they will drive out demons, speak in new tongues, pick up snakes with their hands, drink anything deadly without harm, and lay hands on the sick and they will recover (16:14–18).

After speaking to them, the Lord Jesus was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God. The disciples went out and preached everywhere, while the Lord worked with them and confirmed the message by the signs that accompanied it (16:19–20).

## Key Takeaways

**An empty tomb and a living announcement.** The women came expecting to embalm a corpse, yet the messenger inside declares, "He has risen! He is not here!" (16:6). The One "who was crucified" is the exact same Jesus—only now alive. The cross does not get the final word.

**"And Peter."** The message goes out to "His disciples and Peter" (16:7). The disciple who denied Him three times gets called personally by name. Resurrection is not solely good news about Jesus; it is concrete forgiveness for people who blew it.

**He goes ahead to Galilee.** Jesus schedules their reunion back in Galilee, where everything began. He still walks out front, and they are still invited to follow just as on day one. Human failure never cancels His call.

**Fear and disbelief come first.** The women run away trembling (16:8). The apostles disbelieve Mary Magdalene, disbelieve the two travelers, and earn Jesus' rebuke for hard hearts (16:14). Resurrection faith did not come easily—even to those closest to Him.

**From the grave out to the whole world.** Jesus commissions them: "Go into all the world and preach the gospel to every creature" (16:15). Mark opened with "the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ" (1:1) and closes as that gospel bursts out everywhere. Ascended to God's right hand, the Lord still works right alongside His people.

## Deeper Meaning

**A promise spoken ahead of time.** On the very night of His arrest, Jesus promised, "after I have risen, I will go ahead of you into Galilee" (Mark 14:28). Inside the tomb the messenger simply reminds them, "just as He told you" (16:7). Promises spoken before death stand unshaken on the far side of death.

**The gospel Paul received.** Paul summarizes the earliest creed: Christ "was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:4) and "appeared to Cephas" (1 Corinthians 15:5)—Peter. That special mention of Peter at the tomb fits seamlessly with the church's oldest testimony.

**Women's testimony dismissed as nonsense.** Luke writes that when the women reported the empty tomb, "their words seemed like nonsense to them, and they did not believe the women" (Luke 24:11). Mark likewise shows the disciples disbelieving Mary Magdalene (16:11). The very first heralds of the Resurrection were witnesses whose testimony first-century courts routinely discounted.

**The Servant sees His offspring.** Isaiah foretold that after the Suffering Servant offers His life as a guilt offering, "He will see His offspring and prolong His days" (Isaiah 53:10). Raised from death, He watches His followers carry His life across the world.

**Taken up into heaven.** Acts describes the same moment: "He was lifted up before their very eyes, and a cloud hid Him from their sight" (Acts 1:9). Mark sums it concisely: He "was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God" (16:19)—fulfilling His exact courtroom claim (14:62).

**Unharmed by a viper.** The promise of 16:18 plays out in Paul's ministry: bitten by a venomous snake on Malta, Paul "shook the creature off into the fire and suffered no ill effects" (Acts 28:5). It pledges God's protection during gospel mission, never reckless stunts.

## Notes & Nuances

**Where did Mark originally end?** The two oldest and most reliable manuscripts of Mark stop cold at 16:8. Verses 16:9–20 appear in the vast majority of later copies, though their vocabulary and literary flow differ noticeably from the rest of Mark; a few manuscripts also include an alternative two-sentence "shorter ending." Most scholars think 16:9–20 was added very early—probably to summarize resurrection appearances from the other Gospels—and the church has honored it within canonical Scripture for centuries.

**Ending on fear.** If Mark genuinely stopped at 16:8, his closing sentence leaves the women running off silently "because they were afraid." Some suspect an original final leaf tore off and was lost; others think Mark ended abruptly on purpose, handing the baton to readers: the promise has been announced—will *you* go tell it? The other Gospels confirm the women's initial stunned silence quickly gave way to proclamation.

**Baptism and salvation.** Verse 16:16 states, "Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved, but whoever does not believe will be condemned." In the early church, faith and baptism went immediately hand in hand. Notice, however, that the second half assigns condemnation solely to unbelief, not to lacking baptism. Christian traditions weigh baptism differently while agreeing faith is essential.

**Handling snakes and drinking poison are not commands to test God.** Sadly, a few fringe groups have used 16:18 to handle rattlesnakes in worship services, resulting in tragic deaths. These words promise protection against unintended dangers encountered on mission—like Paul on Malta—never permission to court danger. Jesus Himself declared, "Do not put the Lord your God to the test" (Matthew 4:7).

## What Commentators Say

**Irenaeus**, late second-century bishop of Lyon, explicitly quotes 16:19 and cites it as Mark's closing words in his Gospel—proving that the longer ending (16:9–20) was already circulating and treated as authoritative Scripture well within the second century. *(Against Heresies, book 3, chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Irenaeus quotes Mark 16:19 as the end of Mark's Gospel in Against Heresies book 3 chapter 10 -->

**Eusebius**, early fourth-century bishop of Caesarea, replied to a correspondent puzzled by differences between Matthew and Mark on the hour of the Resurrection. Eusebius observed that nearly all the most accurate copies of Mark end with the women's fear at 16:8, while the longer section appeared only in certain copies—suggesting readers could either set the extra verses aside or keep and harmonize them. *(Questions to Marinus)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Eusebius's statement that the most accurate copies end at 16:8 appears in his letter to Marinus; exact question number omitted as unverified -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, notes that Jewish legal custom did not accept women's testimony as admissible proof in court. Modern commentators frequently point to this fact: anyone fabricating a resurrection story in the first century would never have cast women as the primary witnesses. That every Gospel puts women at the tomb first signals faithful reporting of what truly occurred, even when it ran against cultural expectations. *(Antiquities of the Jews, book 4, chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus's statement on the inadmissibility of women's testimony appears in Antiquities book 4 chapter 8; the apologetic inference is standard among modern commentators -->

**Bruce Metzger**, the eminent American New Testament textual critic, weighed all manuscript evidence and concluded that 16:8 marks the earliest recoverable ending of Mark. The longer ending was appended very early, likely in the second century, to supply a fuller conclusion, while still preserving valuable ancient church tradition. *(A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Metzger's detailed discussion of Mark's ending is a famous entry in his Textual Commentary -->

**Andrew Lincoln**, a British New Testament scholar, argues that Mark intentionally stopped at 16:8, deliberately juxtaposing God's promise in 16:7 with human failure in 16:8. Mark invites readers to see that Christ's faithfulness towers above human weakness: the women freeze in fear and the disciples scatter, yet Jesus still goes ahead of them to Galilee. *("The Promise and the Failure," Journal of Biblical Literature, 1989)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident the central thesis contrasts promise and failure; exact publication year and full title unverified -->
