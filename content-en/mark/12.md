# Mark 12 — Questions in the Temple and the Widow's Coins

## Story

Jesus began speaking to them in parables. A man planted a vineyard, built a wall around it, dug a winepress, erected a watchtower, leased it to tenant farmers, and traveled abroad. At harvest time he sent a servant to collect his share of the crop. They seized him, beat him, and sent him away empty-handed. He sent another servant; they struck him on the head and treated him shamefully. He sent still another, and they killed him—followed by many more, some beaten and some killed (12:1–5).

He had one left to send—a beloved son. He sent him last of all, saying, "They will respect my son." Yet those tenants told one another: this is the heir; come, let's kill him, and the inheritance will be ours! So they seized him, killed him, and threw him out of the vineyard. What then will the owner of the vineyard do? He will come and destroy those tenants and give the vineyard to others. Have you never read this Scripture: "The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone"? (12:6–11).

Realizing He had told this parable against them, the religious leaders looked for a way to arrest Him; yet fearing the crowd, they left Him and walked away (12:12).

They sent some Pharisees and Herodians to trap Him in His words. They opened with flattery: Teacher, we know You are truthful, show partiality to no one, and teach the way of God in truth. Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar or not? Should we pay or shouldn't we? Seeing through their hypocrisy, He asked: why are you testing Me? Bring Me a denarius to look at. Whose image and inscription is this? Caesar's, they answered. "Give to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's." And they were utterly amazed at Him (12:13–17).

Next came the Sadducees, who say there is no resurrection, with a riddle. Moses wrote that if a man dies childless, his brother must marry the widow to raise up offspring for him. Seven brothers took turns marrying the same woman and each died childless down to the seventh; finally the woman died too. At the resurrection, whose wife will she be? (12:18–23).

Jesus replied: are you not in error because you know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God? When the dead rise, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven. Now about the dead rising—have you not read in the book of Moses, in the passage about the bush, how God told him: I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob? "He is not the God of the dead, but of the living!" You are badly mistaken (12:24–27).

One of the scribes heard them debating and, noticing how well Jesus answered, came up and asked: of all the commandments, which is the most important? Jesus replied: the foremost is, "Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One." Love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength. The second is this: "Love your neighbor as yourself." No other commandment is greater than these (12:28–31).

Well said, Teacher, the scribe replied: You have spoken truthfully that God is One and there is no other besides Him; loving Him with all heart, understanding, and strength, and loving your neighbor as yourself, is far more important than all burnt offerings and sacrifices. Seeing he had answered wisely, Jesus told him, "You are not far from the kingdom of God." And after that no one dared ask Him any more questions (12:32–34).

While teaching in the temple, Jesus posed a question of His own: how do the scribes say that the Christ is the son of David? David himself, speaking by the Holy Spirit, declared: the Lord said to my Lord, sit at My right hand until I put your enemies under your feet. David himself calls Him "Lord"—how then can He be his son? The large crowd listened to Him with delight (12:35–37).

In His teaching He warned: watch out for the scribes, who like walking around in long robes, being greeted in marketplaces, taking the chief seats in synagogues and places of honor at banquets. They devour widows' houses and make lengthy prayers for show. They will receive greater condemnation (12:38–40).

Jesus sat down opposite the treasury and watched the crowd dropping money into the offering box. Many rich people put in large sums. Then a poor widow came and dropped in two tiny copper coins worth a fraction of a penny. Summoning His disciples, He declared: truly this poor widow has put in more than everyone else. All the others gave out of their surplus, yet she, out of her poverty, put in everything she had—all she had to live on (12:41–44). Immediately after condemning those who "devour widows' houses," a widow gives away her last coin.

## Key Takeaways

**God patiently sends messengers—and finally His own Son.** The vineyard owner sends servant after servant despite beatings and murders (12:2–5), and finally sends his "beloved son" (12:6). Israel's whole story is a portrait of God's long patience looking for fruit, with Jesus as His ultimate appeal.

**The rejected stone becomes the cornerstone.** The tenants murder the son and toss him outside (12:8). Yet Jesus quotes the Psalms: the stone rejected by builders becomes the cornerstone (12:10). Human rejection cannot derail God's design; God turns rejection itself into the foundation of His new work.

**Human beings belong to God.** Coins carry Caesar's portrait, so return them to Caesar; human beings carry God's image, so return your entire self to God (12:16–17). Jesus neither dodges their trap nor settles for politics—He lifts the question to a far deeper plane.

**Loving God and loving neighbor sit at the Law's heart.** Jesus replies with Israel's daily prayer, "Hear, O Israel" (12:29). The scribe grasps that loving God and neighbor surpasses all sacrifices, and Jesus affirms he is "not far from the kingdom of God" (12:34). Not every scribe stood against Him.

**God measures giving by the heart, not the dollar amount.** Wealthy donors drop in big gifts while keeping plenty; the widow gives two tiny coins that represent "all she had to live on" (12:44). God does not tally how much we drop in, but how much we hold back.

## Deeper Meaning

**The vineyard song.** Jesus' parable opens with details lifted straight from Isaiah 5: wall, winepress, watchtower (Isaiah 5:2). Isaiah spelled it out plainly: "the vineyard of the LORD of Hosts is the house of Israel" (Isaiah 5:7). Every listener immediately caught His meaning.

**One beloved son.** Calling him the owner's "beloved son" echoes God speaking to Abraham: "Take your son, your only son, whom you love—Isaac" (Genesis 22:2), as well as the Father's voice at Jesus' baptism (1:11) and Transfiguration (9:7).

**The cornerstone is the Lord's doing.** The psalm continues that making the discarded stone the cornerstone "is from the LORD, and it is marvelous in our eyes" (Psalms 118:22–23). Jesus' resurrection is that marvelous act.

**Love above sacrifice.** The scribe's insight in 12:33 rings with the prophets. Samuel declared, "To obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Samuel 15:22), and Hosea proclaimed, "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6). This scribe read the Law the exact way the prophets did.

**David's Lord.** Jesus cites Psalms 110, where David calls the coming One Lord: "The LORD said to my Lord: 'Sit at My right hand'" (Psalms 110:1). The Messiah is not merely David's descendant; He stands greater than David.

**The widow and the God of widows.** The Law commanded, "You must not mistreat any widow or orphan" (Exodus 22:22). The widow of Zarephath similarly had only "a handful of flour in a jar and a little olive oil in a jug," yet shared with Elijah (1 Kings 17:12). Paul teaches that God welcomes a gift "according to what one has, not according to what one does not have" (2 Corinthians 8:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**The vineyard parable does not declare God finished with Jewish people.** The parable targets the abusive tenants—the corrupt leadership—as they themselves recognized (12:12). The vineyard itself is never destroyed, only entrusted to faithful stewards. Reading it as God rejecting Israel stretches it past what it says.

**Praise for the widow—or lament over her exploitation?** Most readers hear Jesus commending her sacrificial trust. Yet directly beforehand He condemned scribes who "devour widows' houses" (12:40), and directly afterward He predicts the temple's collapse (13:2). Some readers therefore also hear heartbreak: a corrupt religious establishment draining a destitute widow down to her final coins. Both insights can stand side by side.

**Questioning David's sonship does not deny Jesus' descent from David.** Back in chapter 10, Bartimaeus hailed Him as "Son of David" and Jesus welcomed it. The question in 12:35–37 does not reject Davidic ancestry; it shows that title alone is too small. The Messiah is both David's son and David's Lord.

**The tax question was a deadly snare.** Say "pay," and Jesus alienates crowds bitter under Roman rule. Say "don't pay," and He gets reported for treason. Rival Pharisees and Herodians joined forces specifically to spring this trap (12:13). Jesus slips straight through without dodging truth.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, opens on its very first line with the question: from what hour may one recite the *Shema*—"Hear, O Israel"—in the evening? Devout Jews prayed the *Shema* morning and night. Answering the scribe with those exact opening words, Jesus anchored His summary in His people's most cherished daily prayer. *(Mishnah, Berakhot 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Mishnah Berakhot opens immediately with the question of when to recite the evening Shema -->

**The Gospel of Thomas**, a collection of sayings attributed to Jesus discovered in Egypt in 1945, preserves a shorter version of the vineyard parable in which the owner sends two servants and then his son, who is killed—omitting the Isaiah 5 touches of wall, winepress, and watchtower—followed directly by the rejected cornerstone saying. Scholars continue debating whether this spare version is earlier or an abridgment of the canonical Gospels. *(Gospel of Thomas, sayings 65–66)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the vineyard parable and cornerstone saying in Thomas 65–66 are widely compared with Mark 12 -->

**Cyprian**, mid-third-century bishop of Carthage, wrote an exhortation on almsgiving rebuking a wealthy woman who came to the Lord's Supper empty-handed and even ate from the offerings brought by the poor. He pointed her straight to the Gospel widow dropping in her two tiny coins and told her to feel ashamed. *(On Works and Almsgiving, chapter 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Cyprian cites the widow to rebuke rich non-givers; recalled as De opere et eleemosynis chapter 15, though chapter number is unverified -->

**Addison Wright**, an American Catholic biblical scholar, wrote a widely influential article asking whether the widow's offering is praise or lament.
