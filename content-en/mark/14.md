# Mark 14 — The Alabaster Jar, the Last Supper, Gethsemane, and Peter's Denial

## Story

It was two days before Passover and the Festival of Unleavened Bread. The chief priests and scribes were scheming how to arrest Jesus by stealth and put Him to death. Yet they said: not during the festival, or the people may riot (14:1–2).

In Bethany, at the house of Simon the leper, while Jesus reclined at table, a woman came with an alabaster jar of very expensive pure nard perfume. She broke the jar and poured the perfume over His head. Some present were indignant, remarking to one another: why this waste of perfume? It could have been sold for more than three hundred denarii and given to the poor. And they scolded her harshly (14:3–5).

Jesus said: leave her alone; why are you troubling her? "She has done a beautiful deed to Me." The poor you will always have with you, and you can help them whenever you want; yet you will not always have Me. She did what she could: she anointed My body beforehand for burial. Truly, wherever the gospel is preached throughout the whole world, what she has done will also be told in memory of her (14:6–9).

Then Judas Iscariot, one of the Twelve, went to the chief priests to betray Him to them. Delighted to hear it, they promised to give him money. He began watching for an opportunity to hand Him over (14:10–11). One person pours out everything for Him; another sells Him for cash.

On the first day of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover lamb was sacrificed, His disciples asked: where do You want us to prepare for You to eat the Passover? He sent two disciples into the city: you will meet a man carrying a jar of water; follow him, and say to the owner of the house he enters: the Teacher asks, where is My guest room where I may eat the Passover with My disciples? He will show you a large upper room, furnished and ready. They went, found everything just as He had told them, and prepared the Passover (14:12–16).

When evening came, He arrived with the Twelve. While they reclined eating, He said: truly one of you eating with Me will betray Me. Grieved, they began asking Him one by one, "Surely not I?" He replied: it is one of the Twelve—the one dipping bread into the bowl with Me. The Son of Man goes just as it is written about Him, yet woe to that man who betrays the Son of Man! It would be better for him had he never been born (14:17–21).

While they were eating, He took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to them: "Take it; this is My body." Then He took a cup, gave thanks, and handed it to them, and they all drank from it. He told them, "This is My blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many." I will not drink again of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new in the kingdom of God (14:22–25).

After singing a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives. He told them: you will all fall away, for it is written: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered. Yet after I have risen, I will go ahead of you into Galilee. Peter declared: even if everyone else falls away, I will not. Jesus answered: truly this very night, before the rooster crows twice, you will deny Me three times. Peter insisted emphatically, "Even if I have to die with You, I will never deny You." And all the others said the same (14:26–31).

They reached a place called Gethsemane. Telling the disciples to sit while He prayed, He took Peter, James, and John with Him. He began to be deeply distressed and troubled, saying: "My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death"; stay here and keep watch. Going a little farther, He fell to the ground and prayed that if possible the hour might pass from Him: "Abba, Father, all things are possible for You. Take this cup from Me. Yet not what I will, but what You will" (14:32–36).

Returning, He found them sleeping and said to Peter: Simon, are you asleep? Couldn't you keep watch for one hour? Watch and pray so that you will not enter temptation; "the spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak." Going away again, He prayed the same words. Returning once more, He found them asleep because their eyes were heavy, and they did not know what to say to Him. Returning the third time, He said: are you still sleeping and resting? Enough! The hour has come; the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners. Rise, let us go; My betrayer is approaching (14:37–42). The command "Watch!" closing the Olivet Discourse faces its immediate test—and the disciples fail.

While He was still speaking, Judas arrived, accompanied by a crowd armed with swords and clubs sent from the chief priests, scribes, and elders. The betrayer had arranged a signal: the one I kiss is the man; arrest him and lead him away under guard. Going straight up to Jesus, he said, "Rabbi!" and kissed Him. They seized Him and arrested Him. One of those standing nearby drew his sword, struck the high priest's servant, and cut off his ear (14:43–47).

Jesus asked them: have you come out with swords and clubs to capture Me like a bandit? Day after day I was with you teaching in the temple courts, and you did not arrest Me. Yet this happens so the Scriptures may be fulfilled. "Then everyone deserted Him and fled." A young man wearing nothing but a linen cloth was following Jesus; when they grabbed him, he left the linen cloth behind and ran away naked (14:48–52).

They led Jesus to the high priest, where all the chief priests, elders, and scribes had assembled. Peter followed at a distance right into the courtyard of the high priest and sat warming himself by the fire with the guards (14:53–54).

The chief priests and the whole Sanhedrin hunted for testimony against Jesus to put Him to death, yet found none. Many gave false testimony, yet their statements did not agree. Some stood up and claimed: we heard Him say, "I will destroy this temple made with hands, and in three days build another not made with hands." Even then their testimonies did not match (14:55–59).

The high priest stood up in the center and questioned Jesus: are You not going to answer? What is this testimony these men bring against You? He remained silent. Again the high priest asked Him: are You the Christ, the Son of the Blessed One? Jesus answered, "I am"; and you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven (14:60–62). All through Mark He silenced everyone from broadcasting His identity; now, standing before the court about to condemn Him, He states it outright.

The high priest tore his garments: why do we need any more witnesses? You have heard the blasphemy; what is your decision? They all condemned Him as worthy of death. Some began spitting on Him, blindfolding Him, striking Him with their fists, and jeering: prophesy! And the guards took Him and beat Him (14:63–65).

Below in the courtyard sat Peter. One of the high priest's servant girls saw him warming himself, looked closely at him, and said: you also were with Jesus of Nazareth! He denied it: "I do not know or even understand what you are talking about." Walking out into the entryway, a rooster crowed. Seeing him again, the servant girl told bystanders: this man is one of them. Again he denied it. A little while later bystanders told Peter: surely you are one of them, for you are a Galilean (14:66–70).

He began invoking curses and swearing, "I do not know this man of whom you speak!" Immediately the rooster crowed a second time. Peter remembered what Jesus had told him: before the rooster crows twice, you will deny Me three times. And he broke down and wept (14:71–72). At the exact same moment upstairs, Jesus speaks the truth about Himself and gets condemned; downstairs in the courtyard, Peter lies about knowing Him to save his own skin.

## Key Takeaways

**One pours out everything; another sells His Master for cash.** Mark places two scenes shoulder to shoulder: an unnamed woman smashes a priceless perfume jar over Jesus, and Judas immediately visits the chief priests to betray Him (14:3–11). Jesus honors her: "She has done a beautiful deed to Me" (14:6). What calculating minds dismiss as waste, He recognizes as love that knows the hour.

**The Last Supper seals a covenant.** Inside the Passover meal, Jesus takes bread and cup, speaking of His body and blood: "This is My blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many" (14:24). Ahead of time He interprets His impending death—no senseless tragedy, but an offering inaugurating a new covenant.

**In Gethsemane, the Son obeys amid raw terror.** Jesus does not wear stoic armor. Terrified and deeply grieved, He falls face-first to the earth begging the cup to pass—yet concludes, "not what I will, but what You will" (14:36). Real obedience does not mean feeling numb; it means choosing the Father's will right inside agony.

**The disciples collapse completely.** They sleep when told to watch, scatter when He is arrested (14:50), and the boldest among them disowns Him three times. Mark hides none of it. The gospel never rests on the disciples' steadiness, but on Jesus' faithfulness.

**His clearest self-declaration happens on trial.** Asked directly if He is the Christ, Jesus replies, "I am" (14:62). Telling the truth condemns Him to death upstairs, at the very moment Peter lies downstairs to stay safe.

## Deeper Meaning

**Covenant blood at Sinai.** Sealing God's covenant with Israel, Moses sprinkled blood over the people saying, "This is the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you" (Exodus 24:8). Jesus' words over the cup deliberately echo Sinai: His blood seals God's covenant anew.

**Jeremiah's new covenant.** Jeremiah foretold coming days when the Lord would "make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah" (Jeremiah 31:31). At the Last Supper, that promise steps into reality.

**Strike the shepherd, scatter the sheep.** Jesus quotes Zechariah: "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (Zechariah 13:7). The disciples' flight does not wreck God's plan; Scripture anticipated it—and Jesus already pledges to meet them ahead in Galilee (14:28).

**The friend sharing bread.** The psalmist grieved, "Even my close friend… he who shared my bread, has lifted up his heel against me" (Psalms 41:9). Naming His betrayer as "the one dipping bread into the bowl with Me" (14:20), Jesus gathers the suffering righteous psalmist's pain into His own.

**Seated at the right hand and coming on clouds.** Jesus' courtroom answer welds together two Scriptures: "Sit at My right hand" (Psalms 110:1) and "one like a son of man, coming with the clouds of heaven" (Daniel 7:13). Standing as the accused, He quietly informs His judges that He will be their Judge.

**A prayer truly heard.** Hebrews remembers Gethsemane: Christ "offered up prayers and petitions with loud cries and tears… and He was heard because of His reverence" (Hebrews 5:7). The cup was not removed, yet the prayer was still answered—the Father saved Him *through* death rather than *around* death.

## Notes & Nuances

**"The poor you will always have with you" never excuses neglecting the poor.** Jesus is echoing Deuteronomy's command: since there will always be poor people in the land, keep your hand open wide to help them (Deuteronomy 15:11). He is not downgrading charity, but recognizing that this was the sole unrepeatable moment to honor His burial. Three hundred denarii equaled nearly a full year's wages for a laborer.

**The young man fleeing naked.** Only Mark preserves this striking detail (14:51–52). Many since antiquity have wondered if it was Mark signing his own cameo into the story, though the text never says so. Others hear an echo of Amos warning that even the bravest warrior "will flee naked on that day" (Amos 2:16). Whoever he was, the scene underscores total abandonment—even the last straggler leaves everything behind to escape.

**The rooster crowing twice.** Matthew, Luke, and John mention the rooster crowing once; Mark specifies twice (14:30; 14:72). Rather than a contradiction, Mark supplies sharper detail—likely straight from Peter's own indelible memory of that night.

**A midnight capital trial.** Later rabbinic rules recorded in the Mishnah prohibited trying capital offenses at night or issuing death verdicts on the same day. This hearing violates several such safeguards, though scholars note we cannot be certain those exact procedures were already enforced in Jesus' day, cautioning against overstating the Sanhedrin's procedural guilt.

**This story never justifies blaming the Jewish people.** Those condemning Jesus were a small circle of leaders on a single night—never an entire people. Even His own Jewish followers betrayed, denied, and abandoned Him. Mark shows every human heart failing, and Christ dying for all.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, outlines the Passover meal liturgy: four cups of wine, explanations of unleavened bread and bitter herbs, and singing the *Hallel* psalms before and after the meal. **Joachim Jeremias**, the German New Testament scholar, argued that Jesus' address to God as *Abba* was strikingly unprecedented. In surviving Jewish liturgical prayers of that era, worshipers did not address God with the intimate household term children used for their fathers—marking *Abba* as an authentic fingerprint of Jesus' voice and His singular intimacy with the Father. *(The Prayers of Jesus, 1967)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the Abba thesis is the centerpiece of The Prayers of Jesus -->

**James Barr**, a Scottish Old Testament scholar, pushed back against the popular claim that *Abba* meant "Daddy" in baby talk. Adult sons and daughters used *Abba* just as readily to address their fathers, making "Daddy" an overstatement; the term conveyed warm family closeness paired with respect rather than childishness. *("Abba Isn't 'Daddy,'" Journal of Theological Studies, 1988)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the title and thesis of Barr's 1988 JTS article are well known -->

**Ambrose**, fourth-century bishop of Milan, composed a famous morning hymn celebrating the rooster's crow. In its verses, when the cock crows, Peter himself—the very rock of the church—washes away his sin with tears. For Ambrose, the rooster's call did not merely expose Peter's failure, it woke him to repentance, just as every dawn calls believers awake to grace. *(Hymn Aeterne rerum conditor)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the stanza about Peter washing away his sin at cockcrow belongs to Ambrose's hymn Aeterne rerum conditor -->
