# Mark 11 — Entering Jerusalem, the Fig Tree, and the Temple

## Story

Approaching Jerusalem near Bethphage and Bethany at the Mount of Olives, Jesus sent two disciples ahead: go into the village opposite you, and as soon as you enter it you will find a colt tied there that no one has ever ridden; untie it and bring it here. If anyone asks why you are doing this, say, "The Lord needs it," and they will send it back right away (11:1–3).

They went, found a colt tied outside a doorway in the street, and untied it. Some people standing there asked: what are you doing untying that colt? They answered just as Jesus had instructed, and the people let them go. Bringing the colt to Jesus, they threw their cloaks over it, and He sat on it (11:4–7).

Many spread their cloaks on the road, while others spread leafy branches cut in the fields. Those going ahead and those following shouted, "Hosanna! Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!" Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David! Hosanna in the highest! (11:8–10).

He entered Jerusalem, went into the temple courts, and looked around at everything. Since it was already late, He went back out to Bethany with the Twelve (11:11). Mark ends the triumphal entry on a quiet, watchful note: He simply inspects everything, then leaves.

The next morning, leaving Bethany, He was hungry. Spotting a leafy fig tree in the distance, He walked over to see if it had any fruit; reaching it, He found nothing but leaves, because it was not the season for figs. He spoke to the tree: may no one ever eat fruit from you again! And His disciples heard Him say it (11:12–14).

Arriving in Jerusalem, He entered the temple courts and began driving out those buying and selling there, overturning the tables of the money changers and the benches of those selling doves, and refusing to allow anyone to carry merchandise through the temple courts. He taught them: is it not written, "My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations"? Yet you have made it a den of robbers! Hearing this, the chief priests and scribes began looking for a way to kill Him, for they feared Him because the whole crowd was amazed at His teaching. When evening came, He and His disciples left the city (11:15–19). The fig tree story frames the temple story like bookends: both are covered in leaves yet completely barren of fruit.

The next morning, walking past, they saw the fig tree withered from the roots. Peter remembered: Rabbi, look! The fig tree You cursed has withered. Jesus answered, "Have faith in God." Truly, if anyone tells this mountain, "Go, throw yourself into the sea," without doubting in their heart, believing that what they say will happen, it will be done for them. Therefore, whatever you ask for in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours. And whenever you stand praying, if you hold anything against anyone, forgive them, so that your Father in heaven may forgive your sins too (11:20–26).

They entered Jerusalem again. As He walked in the temple courts, the chief priests, scribes, and elders approached and demanded, "By what authority are You doing these things?" Who gave You authority to do this? Jesus answered: I will ask you one question; answer Me and I will tell you. John's baptism—was it from heaven or from men? (11:27–30).

They conferred among themselves: if we say from heaven, He will ask why we didn't believe him; yet if we say from men—they feared the crowd, for everyone truly regarded John as a prophet. We do not know, they answered. Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things, Jesus replied (11:31–33).

## Key Takeaways

**A King arrives on an unbroken colt.** Jesus chooses His own entrance: not astride a warhorse, but riding a young colt nobody has ever sat on (11:2). He accepts the crowd's royal welcome while signaling He is a gentle King arriving to rescue rather than crush.

**Thick foliage without fruit.** From a distance the leafy fig tree promised fruit; up close it held nothing (11:13). The temple looked identical: bustling, loud, packed with rituals, yet empty of genuine prayer. God looks for fruit, not leaves.

**God's house must stay open to all nations.** Buying and selling filled the outer Court of the Gentiles—the one courtyard where non-Jews could come pray. Jesus quotes God's promise: "My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations" (11:17). Turning other people's place of prayer into a marketplace slams the door on those God longs to welcome.

**Faith, prayer, and forgiveness belong together.** As the stone temple nears its end, Jesus points His disciples to a fresh life of prayer: "Have faith in God" (11:22). Yet believing prayer can never be divorced from forgiving others (11:25).

**Jesus' authority comes straight from heaven.** Religious leaders challenge His credentials (11:28). When He asks about John, they dodge the question. They were hunting for ammo, not truth—and hearts refusing truth receive no answer.

## Deeper Meaning

**The King riding a donkey.** Zechariah promised, "See, your king comes to you, righteous and victorious, gentle and riding on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9). When David crowned Solomon, Solomon rode the royal mule (1 Kings 1:33). Jacob's blessing on Judah even spoke of tying "his colt to the choicest branch" (Genesis 49:11).

**The Mount of Olives.** Zechariah declared that on the day the Lord comes, "His feet will stand on the Mount of Olives" (Zechariah 14:4). Mark intentionally launches Jesus' entry from the Mount of Olives (11:1)—signaling to Scripture-literate readers that the Lord Himself is visiting His city.

**House of prayer and den of robbers.** Jesus joins two prophets. Isaiah envisioned Gentiles welcomed onto God's holy mountain, "for My house will be called a house of prayer for all nations" (Isaiah 56:7). Jeremiah stood at the temple gates confronting Israel: "Has this house… become a den of robbers to you?" (Jeremiah 7:11)—shortly before the first temple fell.

**No figs on the fig tree.** Prophets repeatedly pictured Israel as a fig tree. Hosea recalled God finding Israel's ancestors "like early fruit on the fig tree" (Hosea 9:10). Jeremiah warned that when God came searching, "there will be no figs on the tree, and their leaves will wither" (Jeremiah 8:13). Cursing the fig tree acts out that prophecy in real time.

**No traders left in God's house.** The closing line of Zechariah envisions a day when every pot in Jerusalem is sacred and "there will no longer be a merchant in the house of the LORD of Hosts" (Zechariah 14:21)—where the Hebrew word for "Canaanite" also means trader. Driving out merchants announces that day has dawned.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why curse a fig tree out of season?** Mark candidly remarks "it was not the season for figs" (11:13), making the curse sound bizarre if read alone. Yet Mark sandwiches the temple action right inside the two halves of the fig tree scene so each explains the other. Jesus was not venting anger at a tree; He enacted a prophetic sign. Botanists also note that Palestinian fig trees normally produce edible early buds alongside or ahead of spring leaves, meaning a leafy tree without buds would produce no summer crop either.

**Temple reform or temple judgment?** Some read the overturned tables as an effort to purify temple worship. Others read it as a prophetic verdict that the temple would soon be destroyed—supported by the fig tree withering straight to its roots and Jesus' plain prediction in chapter 13 that not one stone will stay standing.

**Does "believe you have received it" mean anything goes?** Verse 11:24 is a staggering promise, yet it stands framed by faith "in God" and forgiving others. Notice "this mountain" likely pointed right at the Temple Mount looming before them. Rather than turning God into a vending machine for our whims, the promise invites bold trust in God's kingdom purposes.

**Verse 11:26 is absent from many early manuscripts.** The warning that refusing to forgive blocks the Father's forgiveness closely matches Matthew 6:15 and is missing from the oldest copies of Mark, so modern translations usually place it in a footnote.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, sets rules for reverence on the Temple Mount: nobody may enter carrying a staff, sandals, or money belt, nor use the temple courts as a shortcut. **Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa in the late fourth century, preached on the fig tree. A tree has no moral fault, he noted, since trees lack reason. Jesus cursed it strictly as a teaching sign, just as earlier prophets preached through symbolic deeds: lush leaves with zero fruit picture pious talk stripped of obedient action. *(Sermon 89)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine treats the fig tree as a symbolic sign of words without deeds; recalled as Sermon 89, uncertain of exact number -->

**William Telford**, a British New Testament scholar, wrote a dedicated study of Mark's fig tree episode. He demonstrated how intentionally Mark brackets the temple action between the two halves of the fig tree narrative, drawing on rich Old Testament fig-tree imagery of blessing and judgment to declare that the fruitless temple stood under God's verdict. *(The Barren Temple and the Withered Tree, 1980)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this is the central thesis of Telford's monograph -->
