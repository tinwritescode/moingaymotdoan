# Mark 4 — The Seed, the Lamp, and the Storm

## Story

Jesus began teaching again beside the sea. Such a huge crowd gathered around Him that He climbed into a boat and sat on the water while the entire crowd stood along the shore. He taught them many things in parables (4:1–2).

"Listen! A farmer went out to sow his seed." Some seed fell along the path, and birds ate it up. Some fell on rocky ground, sprouted quickly, yet was scorched by the sun and withered because it had no root. Some fell among thorns, which grew up and choked it so it bore no grain. Still other seed fell on good soil, sprang up, grew, and yielded a crop—thirty, sixty, and a hundred times what was sown. "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (4:3–9).

When He was alone, those around Him along with the Twelve asked Him about the parables. He replied: the mystery of the kingdom of God has been given to you, while to those outside everything comes in parables, so that seeing they may not perceive, and hearing they may not understand (4:10–12).

He asked them: do you not understand this parable? How then will you understand any of the parables? Then He explained: the sower sows the word. Seed along the path represents those who hear, and right away Satan comes and snatches away the word sown in them. Rocky soil represents those who welcome the word with joy right away yet have no root; when trouble or persecution arises because of the word, they quickly fall away. Thorny ground represents those who hear, yet the worries of this life, the deceitfulness of wealth, and desires for other things crowd in and choke the word. Good soil represents those who hear the word, accept it, and produce fruit (4:13–20).

He continued: does anyone bring in a lamp to put it under a basket or under a bed? Isn't it set on a stand? Nothing is hidden except to be disclosed. Pay close attention to what you hear: with the measure you use, it will be measured to you—and even more. Whoever has will be given more; whoever does not have, even what he has will be taken away (4:21–25).

Then He tells a parable recorded only by Mark: the kingdom of God is like a man scattering seed on the ground. Night and day, whether he sleeps or gets up, the seed sprouts and grows—though he does not know how. All by itself the earth produces grain: first the stalk, then the head, then the full kernel in the head. As soon as the grain is ripe, he puts the sickle to it, because the harvest has come (4:26–29).

He asks: what shall we compare the kingdom of God to? It is like a mustard seed, the smallest of all seeds on earth; yet once planted, it grows larger than all garden plants and puts out big branches so the birds of the air can perch in its shade (4:30–32). With many similar parables He spoke the word to them, as much as they were able to hear; in private He explained everything to His own disciples (4:33–34).

That evening He told His disciples: let us cross to the other side. Leaving the crowd behind, they took Him along just as He was in the boat, with other boats accompanying them. A furious squall arose, and waves crashed into the boat until it was nearly swamped. Meanwhile Jesus was in the stern, sleeping on a cushion. The disciples woke Him: "Teacher, don't You care that we are perishing?" (4:35–38).

He woke up, rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, "Silence! Be still!" The wind died down, and there was a complete calm. He asked His disciples, "Why are you so afraid? Do you still have no faith?" Filled with great awe, they asked one another, "Who is this? Even the wind and the sea obey Him!" (4:39–41). Their terror of the storm gives way to a deeper awe of the Man sitting inside their boat.

## Key Takeaways

**Same seed, different soil.** The farmer scatters the exact same seed, yet outcomes vary dramatically depending on where it lands (4:3–8). God's word never lacks life. What decides the harvest is the hearer's heart: packed hard like a path, shallow like rocky ground, tangled like thorns, or receptive like good soil.

**Watch how you listen.** The parable opens with "Listen!" and closes with "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (4:3; 4:9). Open-hearted listeners keep gaining deeper insight; careless listeners eventually lose even what they thought they understood (4:24–25).

**God's kingdom grows in God's own way.** Whether the farmer sleeps or wakes, the seed grows "though he does not know how" (4:27). The tiniest mustard seed becomes the largest plant in the garden (4:31–32). God's work typically starts unnoticed and expands quietly without human panic or force.

**Jesus rides right inside the storm-tossed boat.** He does not keep the squall from hitting; He stays inside the boat with His disciples while waves crash over the bow (4:37–38). He sleeps peacefully because He trusts the Father. Asking "Do you still have no faith?" (4:40) is not mocking their fear, but reminding them who is sitting right next to them.

**"Who is this?"** The disciples' stunned question at the end of the chapter drives all of Mark's Gospel (4:41). Only God commands wind and sea. Slowly the disciples begin to see their Teacher is far more than a teacher.

## Deeper Meaning

**Hearing without understanding.** Jesus echoes Isaiah: "Be ever hearing, but never understanding; be ever seeing, but never perceiving" (Isaiah 6:9–10). Isaiah was commissioned to preach to a stubborn people refusing to listen. Jesus meets the same reality: parables open truth to hungry seekers while leaving closed hearts in the dark.

**A great tree sheltering birds.** Ezekiel foretold that God would plant a tender sprig on a high mountain that would grow into a noble cedar where "birds of every kind will nest in it" (Ezekiel 17:23). That image depicts God's kingdom welcoming every nation—and Jesus daringly transfers it onto a humble mustard bush.

**God gives the growth.** Paul writes, "I planted the seed and Apollos watered it, but God made it grow" (1 Corinthians 3:6). That captures the heart of the growing-seed parable. Servants sow and wait; life itself is God's work.

**Harvest time.** The closing line of the growing-seed parable—"puts the sickle to it, because the harvest has come" (4:29)—draws directly from Joel: "Swing the sickle, for the harvest is ripe" (Joel 3:13). Quiet, hidden growth leads inevitably to an unmistakable harvest.

**Jonah sleeping in a storm.** Jonah likewise slept fast below deck in a tempest until panicked sailors shook him awake (Jonah 1:5–6). Yet Jonah was running from God, whereas Jesus is obeying the Father. Jonah had to be thrown overboard; Jesus simply speaks a word.

**The Lord who commands the sea.** In the Old Testament, mastery over the sea belongs strictly to God. He tells the ocean, "This far you may come and no farther" (Job 38:11). The psalmist sings, "He calmed the storm to a whisper, and the waves of the sea were hushed" (Psalms 107:29). The disciples just watched Jesus do what Scripture says only God does.

## Notes & Nuances

**Do parables hide truth or reveal it?** Verse 4:12 sounds as if Jesus tells parables purposefully so outsiders won't understand or be forgiven. Some read this as judicial hardening against already stubborn hearts. Others point out the Greek phrasing can express result rather than purpose: people hear yet fail to grasp it, just as Isaiah foresaw. Verse 4:33 confirms He taught "as much as they were able to hear"—He genuinely wanted them to understand.

**Mustard seed is not botanically the smallest seed.** Smaller seeds exist in nature. Yet in first-century Palestine, mustard seed was the universal proverb for tiny size. Jesus speaks everyday language to His listeners rather than giving a botany lecture.

**Who is the sower?** The parable leaves the sower unnamed—it can be Jesus Himself or anyone proclaiming God's word. The spotlight rests not on the sower, but on the seed and the soil. Preachers need not lose heart when much seed fails: what hits good ground multiplies abundantly.

**Why do the disciples still lack faith?** They had already watched Him heal diseases and cast out demons, yet still panicked. Mark never polishes the disciples into instant heroes. He shows faith growing slowly through stumbles—and a Master wonderfully patient with them.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, celebrated the unstoppable power of God's word in his 1522 Wittenberg sermons. He remarked that he simply taught, preached, and wrote God's word and did nothing else—while he slept or drank Wittenberg beer with his friends, the Word quietly shook the papacy more than any prince could. **Joachim Jeremias**, the twentieth-century German New Testament scholar, observed that Mark 4:12 ends with "be forgiven." While the Hebrew of Isaiah 6:10 reads "be healed," the Aramaic Targum read aloud in Jewish synagogues rendered it "be forgiven."
