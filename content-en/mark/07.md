# Mark 7 — Traditions, the Syrophoenician Woman, and "Ephphatha"

## Story

Pharisees and some scribes from Jerusalem gathered around Jesus and noticed a few of His disciples eating with "unclean"—that is, ceremonially unwashed—hands (7:1–2). Mark pauses to explain for non-Jewish readers: the Pharisees and Jews observe the tradition of the elders, refusing to eat without carefully washing their hands; returning from the marketplace they wash before eating; and they observe many other customs like rinsing cups, pitchers, and copper kettles (7:3–4).

They questioned Him: why do Your disciples not walk according to the tradition of the elders? He replied: you hypocrites! Isaiah prophesied rightly about you: "These people honor Me with their lips," while their hearts are far from Me. "You have disregarded the commandment of God to keep the tradition of men!" (7:5–8).

He gives a sharp example: Moses said to honor your father and mother, and anyone who curses father or mother must be put to death. Yet you say that if someone tells their parents, "Whatever help you might have received from me is *Corban*"—that is, dedicated to God—then you no longer permit them to do anything for their father or mother. Thus you nullify the word of God by the tradition you have handed down, and you do many similar things (7:9–13).

Summoning the crowd again, He declared: listen to Me, all of you, and understand. Nothing outside a person can defile them by going into them; rather, what comes out of a person is what defiles them (7:14–16).

Once He entered a house away from the crowd, His disciples asked Him about this parable. He answered: are you still so dull? Whatever enters from outside cannot enter the heart; it goes into the stomach and passes out of the body. Mark adds a parenthetical comment: in saying this, Jesus declared all foods clean (7:17–19). What comes out of a person, Jesus continued, is what defiles them—for from within, out of the human heart, come evil thoughts, sexual immorality, theft, murder, adultery, greed, malice, deceit, lewdness, envy, slander, arrogance, and folly. All these evils come from inside and defile a person (7:20–23).

Leaving there, He went to the vicinity of Tyre and Sidon. Entering a house, He did not want anyone to know He was there, yet He could not stay hidden. A woman whose little daughter had an unclean spirit heard about Him right away and came and fell at His feet. She was a Greek, born in Syrian Phoenicia, and begged Him to drive the demon out of her daughter (7:24–26).

He told her: first let the children eat all they want, for it is not right to take the children's bread and toss it to the dogs. She replied, "Yes, Lord, but even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs." He said to her: for such a reply you may go; the demon has left your daughter. Returning home, she found her child lying on the bed, and the demon gone (7:27–30). Right on the heels of His teaching on clean and unclean food, someone labeled "unclean" receives bread.

Leaving the region of Tyre, Jesus went through Sidon down to the Sea of Galilee and into the region of the Decapolis. People brought Him a man who was deaf and could hardly speak, begging Him to lay His hand on him. Taking him aside privately away from the crowd, Jesus put His fingers into the man's ears, spit, and touched his tongue. Looking up to heaven with a deep sigh, He said to him, "Ephphatha!"—which means, "Be opened!" (7:31–34).

Instantly the man's ears were opened, his tongue was loosed, and he spoke plainly. Jesus ordered them not to tell anyone, yet the more He ordered them, the more widely they proclaimed it. People were overwhelmed with amazement: "He has done everything well! He makes even the deaf hear and the mute speak" (7:35–37).

## Key Takeaways

**Human traditions can crowd out God's word.** The tradition of the elders began as a protective fence around the Law. Gradually, however, the fence received fiercer loyalty than the commandments it was meant to guard (7:8). Every religious tradition—inside the church too—must stay answerable to Scripture.

**Hypocrisy can weaponize generosity itself.** Declaring property *Corban*—devoted to God—became a pious excuse to dodge supporting aging parents (7:11–12). A sacred-sounding vow overrode one of God's clearest commands. Jesus never accepts devotion to God that harms our neighbors.

**True uncleanness starts inside the heart.** Food passes through the stomach; evil springs from the human heart (7:19–23). No ceremonial handwashing can scrub away greed, envy, or arrogance. What humanity needs is a heart made new by God.

**Faith that is both humble and bold.** The foreign mother neither bristles at the household-dog metaphor nor walks away defeated: even dogs under the table eat crumbs (7:28). Claiming no entitlement, she trusts that God's table overflows so richly that crumbs are plenty.

**Jesus heals tenderly and personally.** He draws the deaf man away from staring eyes, touches his ears and tongue, looks to heaven, and sighs (7:33–34). For someone who cannot hear words, He communicates through touch he can understand. He does not merely fix a symptom; He meets the person.

## Deeper Meaning

**Near with lips, far in heart.** Jesus quotes Isaiah: this people "honor Me with their lips, while their hearts are far from Me" (Isaiah 29:13). The prophets had long insisted God wants real hearts, not polished words.

**Honoring parents.** The Law of Moses treated honoring parents so seriously that "whoever curses his father or mother must surely be put to death" (Exodus 21:17). Using a *Corban* vow to cut off aging parents amounted to cursing them dressed up in pious vocabulary.

**All foods declared clean.** Mark's aside in 7:19 opens a door the early church took years to walk through. Peter heard a voice in his vision say, "Do not call anything impure that God has made clean" (Acts 10:15). Paul later wrote, "nothing is unclean in itself" (Romans 14:14). The wall separating Jew and Gentile begins to crumble.

**The heart as sin's fountainhead.** Jeremiah declared, "The heart is deceitful above all things and beyond cure" (Jeremiah 17:9). Jesus' catalog of thirteen vices in 7:21–22 confirms the prophets' diagnosis: humanity's crisis lives inside the heart, not on the dinner plate.

**First to the Jew.** Notice the single word "first" in Jesus' reply to the woman: "First let the children eat all they want" (7:27). Paul writes similarly that the gospel is God's saving power "first for the Jew, then for the Greek" (Romans 1:16). Saying "first" promises a "next"—the Gentiles' turn is coming.

**Deaf ears opened, mute tongues singing.** Isaiah foretold the day of God's rescue: "the ears of the deaf will be unstopped," and "the mute tongue will shout for joy" (Isaiah 35:5–6). And the crowd's cry in 7:37—"He has done everything well!"—echoes the first page of Scripture, when God saw all He had made and "it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). Jesus is renewing creation itself.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Jesus really call the woman a dog?** Verse 7:27 feels jarringly blunt. Some note the Greek word refers to small household pet dogs rather than street scavengers, softening the image slightly. Others suggest Jesus was testing her faith, or deliberately voicing prevailing Jewish assumptions so He could step past them. Either way, the ending is decisive: He praises her answer and heals her child.

**Did Jesus abolish kosher food laws on the spot?** Traditional Bibles phrase 7:19 smoothly into the sentence, while modern translations rightly highlight Mark's editorial comment: "Thus He declared all foods clean." Yet the early church still wrestled intensely over food laws for years afterward (Acts 10 and 15; Romans 14). Even the disciples likely grasped the full implications of His words only in hindsight.

**Were the Pharisees wrong to wash their hands?** Handwashing itself was harmless, and pursuing ritual purity reflected a sincere longing for everyday holiness. Jesus never opposed clean hands; He opposed turning human traditions into mandatory laws equal to Scripture and wielding them to condemn others.

**Verse 7:16 is missing from many early manuscripts.** "If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear" appears in traditional translations, yet is absent from the earliest Greek copies of Mark, likely copied in by scribes from 4:9 and 4:23.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, devotes an entire tractate to ritual handwashing—specifying exact volumes of water, how far up the hand to pour, and which vessels qualify. **Morna Hooker**, a British New Testament scholar, emphasizes that Jesus' reply to the Syrophoenician woman is not a closed door, but a statement of sequence. The children eat first—and that word "first" already hints that Gentiles will eat too. Catching that opening immediately, the woman claims the crumbs right now. Placed directly after Jesus' teaching on clean foods, her story shows old purity boundaries dissolving. *(The Gospel according to Saint Mark, 1991)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Hooker stresses "first" in 7:27 as sequence; the structural link to clean foods is widely noted by commentators rather than uniquely hers -->

**James Dunn**, a late twentieth-century British New Testament scholar, interprets 7:15 as classic Semitic contrast—stressing that what flows from the heart matters far more than food, rather than explicitly annulling kosher laws on the spot.
