# Mark 3 — The Withered Hand, the Twelve Apostles, and True Family

## Story

Another time Jesus entered the synagogue, where a man with a withered hand was sitting. People watched closely to see if He would heal on the Sabbath so they could accuse Him. Jesus told the man with the withered hand: stand up in front of everyone (3:1–3).

Then He asked them: which is lawful on the Sabbath—to do good or to do evil, to save life or to kill? They stayed completely silent. Looking around at them in anger, deeply grieved by their stubborn hearts, He said to the man: stretch out your hand. He stretched it out, and his hand was restored (3:4–5). The Pharisees marched out and immediately began plotting with the Herodians how to kill Him (3:6). Two rival factions who normally couldn't stand each other suddenly team up against Him.

Jesus withdrew with His disciples to the lake. A huge crowd followed Him—not only from Galilee, but also from Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea, beyond the Jordan, and the regions around Tyre and Sidon. Because of the pressing crowd, He told His disciples to keep a small boat ready. Everyone with diseases pushed forward to touch Him. Whenever unclean spirits saw Him, they fell down before Him shouting, "You are the Son of God!" Yet He strictly ordered them not to make Him known (3:7–12).

He went up on a mountainside, called to Him those He wanted, and they came to Him. He appointed twelve—designating them apostles—"that they might be with Him and that He might send them out to preach," giving them authority to drive out demons (3:13–15). They were Simon, whom He named Peter; James and John sons of Zebedee, whom He nicknamed Boanerges, meaning "Sons of Thunder"; Andrew, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot, who betrayed Him (3:16–19).

He entered a house, and once again a crowd gathered so tightly that He and His disciples could not even eat. Hearing of this, His own family came out to seize Him, for people were saying He had lost His mind (3:20–21).

Meanwhile scribes arriving from Jerusalem charged: he is possessed by Beelzebul, and drives out demons by the prince of demons (3:22). Summoning them, Jesus spoke in parables: "How can Satan drive out Satan?" A kingdom or household divided against itself cannot stand. If Satan has risen against himself, he is finished. Nobody can enter a strong man's house to plunder his property unless he first ties up the strong man (3:23–27).

He gives a solemn warning: all sins and blasphemies people utter will be forgiven, yet whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven, but is guilty of an eternal sin. He said this because they claimed He had an unclean spirit (3:28–30).

His mother and brothers arrived, standing outside and sending word to call Him. The crowd seated around Him reported: Your mother and brothers are outside looking for You. He asked, "Who are My mother and My brothers?" Looking at those seated in a circle around Him, He declared, "Here are My mother and My brothers!" Whoever does the will of God is My brother and sister and mother (3:31–35).

## Key Takeaways

**Doing good never violates God's day.** Jesus asks: on the Sabbath should we do good or harm, save a life or kill? (3:4). The question exposes bitter irony: while He heals a man, His critics leave to plot a murder (3:6).

**Jesus feels real anger.** Mark alone notes that Jesus looked at them "in anger, deeply grieved by their stubborn hearts" (3:5). His anger is never bruised ego; it burns when people prize technical rules above a hurting human being.

**Disciples are called first simply to be with Him.** The Twelve are appointed "that they might be with Him and that He might send them out to preach" (3:14). Being with Jesus comes ahead of working for Jesus. Anyone who stops staying with Him runs out of anything real to carry.

**Jesus binds the strong man.** The scribes accuse Him of partnering with Satan. Jesus flips their charge on its head: Satan never fights himself; rather, Someone stronger has stepped into his house and tied him up (3:27). Every exorcism is a rescue raid pulling captives free from Satan's grip.

**A brand-new family.** His biological family thinks He is out of His mind (3:21), while strangers sit around Him drinking in His words. Jesus calls everyone who does God's will His brother, sister, and mother (3:35). God's family is defined by obedience, not bloodlines.

## Deeper Meaning

**Hard hearts and new hearts.** "Stubborn hearts" recalls Pharaoh hardening his heart against sign after sign. Ezekiel promised a day when God would "remove from you your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 36:26). The Pharisees did not lack miracles; they needed new hearts.

**Twelve men for twelve tribes.** Israel consisted of "twelve tribes" descended from Jacob's twelve sons (Genesis 49:28). Choosing exactly twelve apostles signals that Jesus is reconstituting God's people from a fresh beginning.

**Plundering the mighty warrior.** Isaiah asked: can plunder be taken from a mighty warrior? And the Lord answered, "The plunder of the fierce will be retrieved… I Myself will save your children" (Isaiah 49:24–25). The bound strong man of 3:27 draws directly on that promise. John puts it plainly: "The Son of God appeared for this purpose: to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8).

**His brothers eventually believed.** John notes that during His ministry "even His own brothers did not believe in Him" (John 7:5). Yet after rising from the dead, Jesus appeared to James (1 Corinthians 15:7), and His mother and brothers joined the believers praying for the Spirit (Acts 1:14). Those standing outside in 3:31 eventually stepped inside.

## Notes & Nuances

**What is blasphemy against the Holy Spirit?** Many tender consciences worry they might accidentally commit an unforgivable sin. Mark explicitly states the context: Jesus spoke these words "because they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (3:30). It means staring squarely at the Spirit's clear saving work and willfully branding it demonic—locking out the very Spirit who awakens repentance. Commentators routinely reassure anxious readers: anyone worried about having committed this sin has not committed it, because their heart is not hardened.

**His family thought He was out of His mind.** Some older translations render 3:21 as "His friends," though the Greek phrase naturally means family members or close relatives—confirmed when His mother and brothers arrive in 3:31. Mark refuses to sanitize the story: even Jesus' own household misunderstood Him at first.

**Who are Jesus' "brothers"?** Christian traditions answer differently: children born to Mary and Joseph after Jesus; Joseph's children from a prior marriage; or cousins. This passage itself does not settle the question.

**Jesus is not scorning His family.** Asking "Who are My mother and My brothers?" (3:33) sounds abrupt at first hearing. Yet He never rejects His mother; He widens His family circle to embrace everyone who obeys God. Even from the cross, John records Him tenderly providing for His mother.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa in the late fourth century, reflected on "whoever does the will of God is… My mother." Mary was more blessed by receiving Christ in faith, he wrote, than by conceiving Him in her womb. Physical motherhood would have profited her nothing had she not first carried Christ inside her heart before carrying Him in her body. *(On Holy Virginity, chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's remark that Mary was more blessed by faith than physical conception in De sancta virginitate chapter 3 is widely cited -->

**The Talmud**, the collection of rabbinic commentary, includes a passage about a man named Yeshu executed on the eve of Passover for practicing sorcery and leading Israel astray. Many scholars see an ancient Jewish memory of Jesus here. Crucially, it never denies that He performed extraordinary deeds, instead dismissing them as magic—echoing the scribes' exact accusation in 3:22. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 43a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the Yeshu passage on sorcery and Passover eve in Sanhedrin 43a is well known, though scholars continue debating whether it directly refers to Jesus -->

**Ched Myers**, an American theologian and activist, titled his Mark commentary directly after 3:27: *Binding the Strong Man*. He reads Mark as Jesus confronting entrenched powers oppressing the poor—both Jerusalem's religious establishment and imperial Rome. Calling Jesus demon-possessed, Myers argues, reflects how ruling elites routinely stigmatize anyone threatening their authority. *(Binding the Strong Man, 1988)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the title from Mark 3:27 and Myers's political reading are well established; the specific point on labeling challengers demonic reflects his general treatment of chapter 3 -->

**James R. Edwards**, an American New Testament scholar, wrote an influential article on Mark's signature "sandwich" storytelling technique. Mark frequently opens one story, inserts a second story in the middle, and then returns to wrap up the first. Here, Jesus' family coming to seize Him (3:21; 3:31–35) brackets the scribes accusing Him of demon possession (3:22–30). Both episodes illuminate one another: both insiders and religious authorities profoundly misread who Jesus is. *(Markan Sandwiches, 1989)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Edwards's 1989 Novum Testamentum article "Markan Sandwiches" highlights Mark 3:20–35 as a prime example -->
