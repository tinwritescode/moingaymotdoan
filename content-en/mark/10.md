# Mark 10 — Marriage, Little Children, the Rich Man, and Bartimaeus

## Story

Leaving there, Jesus entered the region of Judea and across the Jordan. Crowds gathered around Him again, and as was His custom, He taught them (10:1).

Pharisees came to test Him: is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife? He asked them back: what did Moses command you? Moses permitted a man to write a certificate of divorce and send her away, they answered. Jesus replied: it was because your hearts were hard that Moses wrote that commandment. Yet from the beginning of creation, God made them male and female; for this reason a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two will become one flesh. "Therefore what God has joined together, let man not separate" (10:2–9).

Inside the house, the disciples asked Him about this again. He told them: whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her; and if a woman divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery (10:10–12).

People were bringing little children to Him for Him to touch them, yet the disciples rebuked them. Seeing this, Jesus was indignant: "Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them," for the kingdom of God belongs to such as these. Anyone who will not receive the kingdom of God like a little child will never enter it. Taking the children in His arms, He laid His hands on them and blessed them (10:13–16).

As He started out on the road, a man ran up, knelt before Him, and asked, "Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" Jesus answered: why do you call Me good? No one is good except God alone. You know the commandments: do not murder, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not give false testimony, do not defraud, honor your father and mother. Teacher, the man replied, all these I have kept from my youth (10:17–20).

"Jesus looked at him, loved him," and said: one thing you lack. Go, sell everything you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow Me. At those words the man's face fell, and he went away sorrowful, because he had great wealth (10:21–22). He is the only individual in Mark's Gospel explicitly described as looked upon with love by Jesus—and he walks away.

Looking around, Jesus told His disciples: how hard it is for the wealthy to enter the kingdom of God! The disciples were amazed. He repeated: children, how hard it is to enter the kingdom of God! "It is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God." Even more astounded, they asked: who then can be saved? Looking at them, He replied: with man this is impossible, but not with God, "for all things are possible with God" (10:23–27).

Peter spoke up: look, we have left everything to follow You! Jesus answered: no one who has left home or brothers or sisters or mother or father or children or fields for Me and for the gospel will fail to receive a hundredfold in this present age—along with persecutions—and in the age to come eternal life. Yet many who are first will be last, and the last will be first (10:28–31).

They were on the road going up to Jerusalem, with Jesus striding ahead; the disciples were amazed, and those following were afraid. Taking the Twelve aside again, He foretold a third time what would happen to Him: the Son of Man will be delivered over to the chief priests and scribes; they will condemn Him to death and hand Him over to the Gentiles, who will mock Him, spit on Him, flog Him, and kill Him; three days later He will rise (10:32–34).

James and John, the two sons of Zebedee, approached Him: Teacher, grant that one of us may sit at Your right and the other at Your left in Your glory. You do not know what you are asking, He answered. Can you drink the cup I drink or be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with? We can, they replied. You will indeed drink the cup I drink and be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with, Jesus told them; yet sitting at My right or left is not Mine to grant—those places belong to those for whom they have been prepared (10:35–40).

Hearing this, the other ten grew indignant with James and John. Calling them together, Jesus said: you know that those regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their high officials exercise authority over them. Not so with you: whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wants to be first must be slave of all. "For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many" (10:41–45).

Arriving in Jericho, as He left town with His disciples and a large crowd, a blind beggar named Bartimaeus, son of Timaeus, sat by the roadside. Hearing it was Jesus of Nazareth, he began shouting, "Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" Many scolded him to stay quiet, yet he shouted all the louder (10:46–48).

Jesus stopped: call him. People cheered him on: take courage, get up, He is calling you! Throwing aside his cloak, he sprang up and came to Jesus. Jesus asked him, "What do you want Me to do for you?"—the exact same question He had just asked James and John. Rabbi, he answered, I want to see. Go, Jesus said, your faith has healed you. Immediately he received his sight and followed Jesus along the road (10:49–52).

## Key Takeaways

**Marriage is joined together by God.** The Pharisees ask what the legal loopholes allow. Jesus takes them back to God's original creation design: one man and one woman becoming one flesh (10:6–8). Divorce certificates existed because of human hardheartedness, whereas God's desire remains: "what God has joined together, let man not separate" (10:9).

**God's kingdom is received as a gift.** The disciples push children away, and Jesus grows indignant (10:14). Children bring zero achievements to bargain with; they simply receive. Entering the kingdom requires receiving it like a child (10:15), not marching in convinced you earned it.

**Jesus loves honestly without lowering the bar.** The rich man kept the commandments since childhood, and "Jesus looked at him, loved him" (10:21). Precisely out of love, Jesus puts His finger on the one idol clutching his heart: his wealth. The man walks away grieved, and Jesus does not chase him down to soften the terms.

**Impossible for humans, possible with God.** A camel squeezing through a needle's eye cannot happen (10:25). Nobody—rich or poor—can save themselves. Yet "all things are possible with God" (10:27). Salvation is God's doing from beginning to end.

**The Son of Man came to serve and give His life.** James and John lobby for thrones while the other ten fume with jealousy. Jesus sets Himself forward as the pattern: coming to serve "and to give His life as a ransom for many" (10:45). That single sentence unlocks the entire Gospel of Mark.

## Deeper Meaning

**From the beginning of creation.** Jesus joins two verses from Genesis: God created male and female, and "a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and they will become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Moses' divorce allowance (Deuteronomy 24:1) was a concession, never God's original plan. Malachi likewise declared God's hatred of treacherous divorce (Malachi 2:16).

**Like a weaned child.** The psalmist writes, "I have stilled and quieted my soul; like a weaned child with its mother" (Psalms 131:2). Childlike faith is not naivety, but trusting rest in God's arms.

**Do not set your heart on riches.** The Psalms counsel, "Though your riches increase, do not set your heart upon them" (Psalms 62:10). Paul warns, "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10). The man in 10:22 was not condemned simply for owning wealth, but because wealth owned his heart.

**Nothing is too hard for the Lord.** When Sarah laughed at the thought of bearing a child in old age, God asked, "Is anything too difficult for the LORD?" (Genesis 18:14). Jesus' assurance in 10:27 rings with that same promise. A rich person entering God's kingdom is as much a miracle as a barren elder giving birth.

**A ransom for many.** Verse 10:45 echoes Isaiah's Suffering Servant, who makes "His life an offering for sin" (Isaiah 53:10) and bears the sin of many. Paul similarly says Christ "emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:7). The highest Lord becomes the lowliest servant.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does Jesus forbid divorce under every circumstance?** Mark records Jesus' teaching with no exception clause (10:11–12), whereas Matthew adds "except for sexual immorality" (Matthew 19:9) and Paul addresses abandonment by an unbelieving spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15). Churches differ in their guidelines on divorce and remarriage. All agree on the core vision: marriage is a lifelong covenant God desires honored, and breaking it always inflicts real pain.

**Mark includes a wife divorcing her husband.** Under Jewish law of that period, normally only husbands initiated divorce. Verse 10:12 mentions a wife divorcing her husband—something permitted under Roman law—suggesting Mark wrote for believers in the wider Roman world and applied Christ's standard equally to both spouses.

**Must every wealthy believer sell everything?** Jesus did not issue this identical demand to every well-off follower; elsewhere wealthy disciples followed Him without liquidating everything. Here He diagnosed the specific grip holding this particular man back. Yet nobody should use that observation to relax comfortably—the real question for every reader is: what single thing is keeping my heart from following Him?

**Was the "eye of a needle" a small city gate?** A popular tale claims Jerusalem had a cramped gate called the Eye of the Needle that camels squeezed through on their knees. No historical evidence supports any such gate; the story arose centuries later. Jesus deliberately painted sheer impossibility—which is exactly why the disciples gasped, "Who then can be saved?"

**Was Jesus denying His own goodness?** Asking "Why do you call Me good?" (10:18) is no confession of sin. Jesus challenges the man's casual flattery: if God alone is truly good, whom are you really addressing? The man called Jesus "Good Teacher" while refusing to obey Him as Lord.

## What Commentators Say

**Clement of Alexandria**, a late second-century theologian, devoted an entire treatise to the rich man in Mark 10, asking *Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?* Jesus was not demanding abandoning money as a physical object, Clement argued, but uprooting greed and attachment from the heart. Wealth is a tool; whoever uses it generously for others can be saved—whereas a poor person consumed by greed has surrendered nothing. *(Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Clement's treatise Quis dives salvetur directly expounds Mark 10:17–31 along these exact lines -->

**Basil the Great**, fourth-century bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, preached sharply on this rich young man. The man claimed he had kept the command to love his neighbor as himself—yet how could that be true, Basil demanded, while he hoarded surplus wealth beside starving neighbors? Surplus wealth kept back in our barns, Basil declared, belongs to the poor. *(Homily to the Rich)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Basil addresses the rich man and loving one's neighbor in his Homily to the Rich; uncertain of exact phrasing -->

**The Talmud**, the collection of rabbinic commentary, uses an elephant passing through the eye of a needle as its proverb for impossibility—noting nobody ever dreams of a golden palm tree or an elephant threading a needle. Jesus used that exact proverbial figure, simply swapping in the camel, the largest animal familiar in Palestine. *(Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 55b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the elephant-through-a-needle proverb in Berakhot 55b is widely cited in commentary on Mark 10:25 -->

**Gregory of Nyssa**, fourth-century Cappadocian bishop, explored to whom the "ransom" in 10:45 was paid, offering the famous fishhook metaphor. Seeing Jesus' humanity, the devil thought he could swallow Him like any other mortal—yet hidden inside that human flesh was divinity like a hook concealed in bait, trapping death itself. Many later theologians felt the metaphor pushed too far, yet it shows how seriously the early church took Christ's ransom. *(Great Catechism, chapter 24)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Gregory of Nyssa's fishhook-and-bait imagery in chapter 24 of the Catechetical Oration is famous -->
