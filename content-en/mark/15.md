# Mark 15 — Before Pilate, the Cross, and the Tomb

## Story

As soon as morning broke, the chief priests consulted with the elders, scribes, and the whole Sanhedrin. They bound Jesus, led Him away, and handed Him over to Pilate. Pilate asked Him: are You the King of the Jews? He replied, "You have said so." The chief priests accused Him of many things. Pilate asked Him again: aren't You going to answer? Look how many charges they are bringing against You! Still Jesus made no further reply, leaving Pilate amazed (15:1–5).

At each festival it was the governor's custom to release one prisoner requested by the crowd. A man named Barabbas was in prison with insurrectionists who had committed murder in the uprising. The crowd came up asking Pilate to do as he normally did. Pilate asked: do you want me to release the King of the Jews? For he realized the chief priests had handed Him over out of envy. Yet the chief priests stirred up the crowd to have him release Barabbas instead (15:6–11).

Pilate asked again: what then shall I do with the one you call the King of the Jews? They shouted, "Crucify Him!" Why—what evil has He done? Pilate asked. They shouted all the louder: crucify Him! Wanting to satisfy the crowd, Pilate released Barabbas to them, had Jesus flogged, and handed Him over to be crucified (15:12–15). A murderer walks out free; the innocent Man dies in his place.

Soldiers led Him inside the palace courtyard and assembled the whole cohort. They dressed Him in a purple cloak, twisted a crown of thorns and pressed it on His head, and began saluting Him, "Hail, King of the Jews!" They kept striking His head with a staff, spitting on Him, and kneeling in mock homage. When they had finished mocking Him, they stripped off the purple robe, put His own clothes back on, and led Him out to crucify Him (15:16–20).

They forced a passerby coming in from the countryside—Simon of Cyrene, the father of Alexander and Rufus—to carry His cross. Bringing Him to Golgotha, meaning Place of the Skull, they offered Him wine mixed with myrrh, yet He did not take it. They crucified Him and divided His clothes, casting lots to see what each would get. It was the third hour. The written notice of the charge against Him read: "THE KING OF THE JEWS." Two robbers were crucified beside Him, one on His right and one on His left (15:21–28). The places at His right and left that James and John had begged for now go to two criminals.

Passersby shook their heads and hurled insults: aha! You who are going to destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days—save Yourself and come down from the cross! Chief priests and scribes mocked Him among themselves: He saved others, yet He cannot save Himself! Let the Christ, the King of Israel, come down from the cross right now so we may see and believe! Even those crucified with Him insulted Him (15:29–32). Their jeers spoke deeper truth than they knew: precisely to save others, He refuses to save Himself.

From the sixth hour until the ninth hour darkness fell over the whole land. At the ninth hour Jesus cried out in a loud voice, "Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?"—which means, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" Hearing this, some bystanders said: look, He is calling Elijah! Someone ran, soaked a sponge in sour wine, lifted it on a reed for Him to drink, and said: leave Him alone; let's see if Elijah comes to take Him down (15:33–36).

With a loud cry, Jesus breathed His last. And the curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom. Standing right in front of Him, the centurion saw how He breathed His last and said, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:37–39). At the start of Mark, heaven's voice called Him Son; at the end, the very first human being to speak that confession is a Roman soldier watching Him die.

Several women watched from a distance, among them Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James the younger and Joses, and Salome—women who had followed and cared for Him in Galilee, along with many other women who had come up with Him to Jerusalem (15:40–41). The male disciples fled; the women stayed.

Since it was Preparation Day—the day before the Sabbath—evening was approaching. Joseph of Arimathea, a respected member of the Council who was himself awaiting the kingdom of God, went boldly to Pilate and asked for Jesus' body. Surprised that He was already dead, Pilate summoned the centurion to verify whether He had died some time ago. Learning it from the centurion, he granted the body to Joseph (15:42–45).

Joseph bought a linen cloth, took Him down, wrapped Him in the linen, laid Him in a tomb cut out of rock, and rolled a stone against the entrance. Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses saw where He was laid (15:46–47).

## Key Takeaways

**The mocked King is the real King.** The phrase "King of the Jews" rings across the entire chapter—on Pilate's lips, in the soldiers' mockery, nailed above His head. Everyone uses it to sneer or accuse, yet Mark wants readers to see it as sheer truth. He truly is King, and the cross is His throne.

**The guilty walks free; the innocent dies in his place.** Barabbas is a violent rebel guilty of murder, whereas Pilate finds zero crime in Jesus (15:14). Yet Barabbas goes free and Jesus goes to the cross—acting out His own declaration that the Son of Man came "to give His life as a ransom for many" (10:45).

**He refuses to save Himself in order to save others.** The chief priests sneer, "He saved others, but He cannot save Himself!" (15:31). They accidentally hit half the truth: it is not that He *cannot* save Himself, but that He *will not*, because He is saving us.

**The cry of utter abandonment.** Right before dying, Jesus cries out, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (15:34). Mark never softens the horror of that moment. Christ enters the deepest distance from God a human soul can ever know.

**An outsider recognizes who He truly is.** Not the disciples, not the priests, but a Roman executioner watching how He dies confesses, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39). Throughout Mark, nobody truly understands who Jesus is until seeing Him on the cross.

## Deeper Meaning

**The psalm of the forsaken.** Jesus' cry opens Psalms 22: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken me?" (Psalms 22:1). That very psalm sketches Golgotha down to the details: enemies "hurl insults, shaking their heads" (Psalms 22:7) and "divide my garments among them" (Psalms 22:18)—yet ends in triumph as all nations turn back to the Lord (Psalms 22:27).

**Counted among transgressors.** Isaiah wrote that the Lord's Servant "was numbered with the transgressors, yet He bore the sin of many and made intercession for the transgressors" (Isaiah 53:12). Hanging between two criminals, Jesus takes the sinner's spot to carry sinners' guilt.

**Sour wine for the thirsty.** Psalms 69 laments, "they gave me vinegar to quench my thirst" (Psalms 69:21). The wine-soaked sponge in 15:36 echoes the cry of the tormented righteous sufferer.

**Darkness at noon.** Amos foretold a day of judgment: "I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight" (Amos 8:9). Three hours of midday darkness mark the cross as the place where God's judgment falls.

**Torn curtain, open road.** Hebrews explains that Jesus opened for us "a new and living way… through the curtain, that is, His body" (Hebrews 10:20). The temple veil splits "from top to bottom" (15:38)—opened by God's hand from heaven, never from below.

**No body left hanging overnight.** Mosaic law required that an executed body "must not remain on the tree overnight; you must be sure to bury him that same day" (Deuteronomy 21:23). Joseph of Arimathea hurrying before Sabbath sundown honors that law—a Council member doing the faithful service the absent disciples could not.

## Notes & Nuances

**The cry of abandonment is not unbelief.** Some read 15:34 and imagine Jesus collapsed into despair. Yet He prays Scripture and still clings to "*My* God." Since ancient Jews routinely referred to a whole psalm by its opening line, many hear Him invoking all of Psalms 22, including its hopeful finish. Even so, we must never water down the anguish: His experience of forsakenness was painfully real.

**The Passover amnesty custom.** Outside the Gospels, no surviving document describes Pilate releasing a prisoner annually at Passover. Some scholars question whether it was a formal standing rule; others view it as an informal local gesture too minor for Roman historians to log. What outside history firmly confirms is that Pilate was a brutal ruler willing to sacrifice justice whenever expediency suited him.

**Verse 15:28 is absent from the earliest manuscripts.** "And the Scripture was fulfilled that says, 'He was numbered with the transgressors'" appears in older translations yet is missing from Mark's earliest copies, likely added later from Luke—where the exact quotation stands directly on Jesus' lips.

**Alexander and Rufus.** Mark identifies Simon of Cyrene as the father of Alexander and Rufus (15:21), as if his first readers knew them personally—perhaps fellow members of the church receiving his Gospel. Paul greets a believer named Rufus in Rome (Romans 16:13), though we cannot be sure it is the same man.

**The crowd and historical guilt.** The crowd shouting "Crucify Him!" (15:13) was one agitated group in Pilate's courtyard whipped up by chief priests—never a stand-in for the Jewish people of that century or any century since. Weaponizing verses like these to persecute Jews is a grave betrayal of Scripture.

## What Commentators Say

**Tacitus**, the early second-century Roman historian and non-Christian, recounts Nero blaming Christians for the fire of Rome and casually explains their name: "Christus," he writes, suffered the extreme penalty during Emperor Tiberius's reign under the procurator Pontius Pilate—one of the earliest non-Christian historical witnesses to Jesus' crucifixion. *(Annals, book 15, chapter 44)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Tacitus's note on Christus executed under Pilate during Tiberius's reign appears in Annals book 15 chapter 44 -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, includes a famous paragraph about Jesus noting that Pilate, at the accusation of leading men among the Jews, condemned Him to the cross, yet those who loved Him did not cease doing so. Most scholars conclude Josephus wrote an authentic core notice that later Christian copyists embellished with explicit praise. *(Antiquities of the Jews, book 18, chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the Testimonium Flavianum in Antiquities book 18 chapter 3 and its probable Christian interpolations are widely discussed by scholars -->

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, rules that anyone executed by the court could not be buried in ancestral family tombs, requiring separate burial plots reserved by the court. Commentators often point to this rule to appreciate the courage of Joseph of Arimathea, a respected Council member, stepping forward to claim Jesus' body and lay Him in a new rock-cut tomb. *(Mishnah, Sanhedrin 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the rule of separate burial plots for executed criminals appears in Mishnah Sanhedrin chapter 6; applying it to Joseph of Arimathea is a common modern commentary insight -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa in the late fourth century, preached on Psalms 22 and the cry from the cross. Christ cried out on behalf of His body, the church—taking up the voice of our fallen humanity, Augustine explained. God never cast off His Son, yet the Son shouldered our cry of alienation from God in order to heal and transform us. *(Expositions of the Psalms, on Psalms 22)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine reads this cry as Christ speaking in the persona of His body the church; exact phrasing unverified and Latin Psalter numbers it Psalm 21 -->

**David Ulansey**, an American scholar, highlights that Mark uses the verb "torn" only twice: when the heavens tear open at Jesus' baptism (1:10) and when the temple curtain tears at His death. Since Josephus records that the outer temple veil bore an embroidered panorama of the starry heavens, bystanders literally saw an image of the sky ripped open—linking Mark's opening and climax, where both times heaven opens and Jesus is declared Son of God. *("The Heavenly Veil Torn," Journal of Biblical Literature, 1991)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: connecting 1:10 and 15:38 through Josephus's description of the star-embroidered veil is the core thesis of Ulansey's 1991 JBL article -->
