# Mark 1 — The Beginning of the Gospel

## Story

Mark includes no genealogy and no birth story. He opens with a single line: "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1). Immediately he cites prophecy about a messenger sent ahead, a voice crying in the wilderness: "Prepare the way for the Lord, make straight paths for Him" (1:2–3).

John appears in the wilderness, preaching a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins. All Judea and the people of Jerusalem stream out to him, confessing their sins and being baptized in the Jordan River. John wears camel's hair with a leather belt around his waist and eats locusts and wild honey (1:4–6). He preaches: after me comes One more powerful than I, the straps of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and untie. I baptize you with water, yet He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit (1:7–8).

Jesus comes from Nazareth in Galilee and is baptized by John in the Jordan. The instant He rises from the water, He sees the heavens torn open, the Spirit descending on Him like a dove, and a voice from heaven declaring, "You are My beloved Son; in You I am well pleased" (1:9–11).

"At once the Spirit drove Him into the wilderness." He stays there forty days being tempted by Satan, living among wild animals, while angels minister to Him (1:12–13). Mark compresses the entire temptation into two sentences. The word "immediately" will echo again and again through the book, propelling the narrative at a breathless pace.

After John is arrested, Jesus comes into Galilee proclaiming the gospel of God: the time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is near; "Repent and believe in the gospel!" (1:14–15).

Walking beside the Sea of Galilee, He spots Simon and Andrew casting nets. He calls: "Come, follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men." Immediately they leave their nets and follow Him. Going a little farther, He sees James and John, sons of Zebedee, mending nets in their boat. He calls them, and they leave their father in the boat with the hired men to follow Him (1:16–20).

Entering Capernaum on the Sabbath, He teaches in the synagogue. Listeners are amazed because He teaches as one who has authority, not like the scribes (1:21–22). Inside the synagogue a man possessed by an unclean spirit cries out: Jesus of Nazareth, have You come to destroy us? I know who You are—"the Holy One of God." Jesus rebukes it: "Be silent! Come out of him!" Shaking the man violently and shrieking loudly, the spirit leaves him. Everyone stares in astonishment: "What is this? A new teaching—and with authority!" He even commands unclean spirits and they obey Him. News about Him races across all Galilee (1:23–28).

Leaving the synagogue, He enters the home of Simon and Andrew. Simon's mother-in-law lies sick with a fever. Taking her by the hand, He lifts her up; the fever leaves her, and she begins serving them. That evening after sunset, people bring all the sick and demon-possessed; the whole town gathers at the door. He heals many and drives out many demons, refusing to let the demons speak because they know who He is (1:29–34).

Very early the next morning, while it is still dark, He gets up, goes out to a solitary place, and prays. Simon and his companions hunt Him down: everyone is looking for You! He replies: let us go on to the nearby villages so I can preach there too, for that is why I have come. He travels throughout Galilee, preaching in their synagogues and driving out demons (1:35–39).

A man with leprosy comes kneeling before Him: "If You are willing, You can make me clean." Moved with compassion, Jesus reaches out His hand and touches him: "I am willing. Be clean!" Immediately the leprosy vanishes. He strictly warns the man not to tell anyone, only to show himself to the priest and present the offering Moses commanded. Instead the man tells everyone he meets, so that Jesus can no longer enter a town openly and must stay outside in lonely places—yet people still keep coming to Him from everywhere (1:40–45).

## Key Takeaways

**The gospel is good news about a Person.** Mark titles his entire work "the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1). Good news is not a rulebook or a philosophy, but the story of Jesus—who He is and what He has done.

**The time is fulfilled; God's kingdom is here.** Jesus' opening sermon sums up His whole message: God's royal reign is breaking in, so turn around and trust this good news (1:15). Repenting means turning back; believing means placing your full trust in that message.

**His call invites an immediate answer.** Jesus offers no long pitch—only "Follow Me" (1:17). Simon and Andrew drop their nets; James and John leave their father sitting in the boat (1:18–20). Following Jesus sets Him ahead of everything else, including career and family.

**His word carries real power.** Crowds marvel that He teaches "with authority" (1:22). That authority does not stop at speech: demons flee, fevers break, leprosy vanishes (1:25–42). Jesus' word actually brings about what it speaks.

**Compassion reaches out and touches the outcast.** Nobody dared touch a leper. Jesus could have cured him from across the street, yet He stretches out His hand and touches him (1:41). Before healing his skin, He heals his isolation.

## Deeper Meaning

**The messenger preparing the way.** Mark combines two prophets. Malachi writes, "I will send My messenger, who will prepare the way before Me" (Malachi 3:1). Isaiah cries, "Prepare the way for the LORD in the wilderness" (Isaiah 40:3). In both prophecies, the road is cleared for the Lord Himself—and Mark applies both directly to Jesus.

**John dressed like Elijah.** John wears camel's hair and a leather belt (1:6), matching Scripture's exact portrait of Elijah: "He was a hairy man with a leather belt around his waist" (2 Kings 1:8). Malachi had promised God would send Elijah before His coming day.

**The heavens torn open.** Isaiah once pleaded, "Oh, that You would rend the heavens and come down!" (Isaiah 64:1). Mark's Greek verb in 1:10 literally means "torn open." He uses that exact verb only one other time in his Gospel—when the temple curtain is "torn in two from top to bottom" (Mark 15:38). The book opens and nearly closes with two cosmic tearings, each accompanied by the confession that Jesus is the Son of God (1:11; Mark 15:39).

**Son and Servant.** The heavenly voice blends two Old Testament scriptures. "You are My Son" speaks of God's royal king (Psalms 2:7). "In whom My soul delights" describes God's Servant (Isaiah 42:1). Jesus is a King—yet a King who comes to serve and suffer.

**Living among wild animals.** Only Mark mentions this vivid detail (1:13). Some sense the stark peril of the wilderness; others hear Isaiah's promise of restored peace when "the wolf will live with the lamb" (Isaiah 11:6)—where Adam failed, Jesus begins restoring creation's harmony.

**The leper living apart.** The Law required a leper to shout "Unclean! Unclean!" and "live alone outside the camp" (Leviticus 13:45–46). It also established a cleansing ritual for anyone healed (Leviticus 14:2), which Jesus instructs this healed man to carry out (1:44).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Mark say "written in Isaiah the prophet"?** Verse 1:2 attributes the opening quote to Isaiah, even though the first line actually comes from Malachi. Ancient writers commonly cited a composite quotation under the name of the major prophet. Later copyists noticed the quirk and altered several manuscripts to read "in the prophets."

**Why does Jesus keep commanding silence?** He silences demons (1:34) and orders the cleansed leper to tell no one (1:44)—a pattern running throughout Mark. Some think He wanted to avoid sparking a political Messiah uprising; others think nobody could grasp His true identity properly until the cross. Verse 1:45 also highlights a practical fallout: once the healed man ignores Him, Jesus can no longer walk openly into town.

**"Moved with compassion" or "indignant"?** Most translations read "moved with compassion" in 1:41, while a handful of ancient manuscripts read "moved with anger." If anger was original, Jesus would be angry at the disease and the cruelty of ostracism, not at the leper himself. Most translations retain "compassion."

**What is demon possession?** Mark overflows with exorcisms. Modern readers naturally ask whether these reflect mental illness or genuine evil spiritual powers. Mark explicitly distinguishes physical illness from demon possession (1:32–34) and shows demons recognizing Jesus' identity. Christian traditions evaluate these accounts in varying ways, yet Mark's main point is unmistakable: no dark power can stand before Jesus.

## What Commentators Say

**Papias**, early second-century bishop of Hierapolis, reported that Mark served as Peter's interpreter, carefully recording everything Peter remembered of the Lord's words and deeds, though not strictly in chronological order. **Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa in the late fourth century, dismissed Mark as Matthew's "attendant and abbreviator." Because of that verdict, Mark went largely neglected by commentators for centuries. Today most biblical scholars hold the reverse: Mark wrote first, and both Matthew and Luke drew upon his Gospel. *(Harmony of the Gospels, book 1, chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's description of Mark as Matthew's "pedisequus et breviator" in De consensu evangelistarum book 1 chapter 2 is famous -->

**The Talmud**, the collection of rabbinic commentary, records that once the last prophets died, the Holy Spirit departed from Israel, leaving only a faint "echo from heaven." Read against that silence, Mark 1:10–11—torn skies, descending Spirit, and God's own voice—breaks in as stunning news: God is speaking directly once again. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 11a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the statement on the Spirit departing after Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi and leaving only the bat kol appears in Sanhedrin 11a; linking it to Mark 1 is a common modern scholarly observation -->

**William Wrede**, a German New Testament scholar, published a landmark 1901 book analyzing Jesus' commands to stay silent in Mark, coining the famous phrase "the Messianic Secret." Wrede argued Mark invented the secrecy motif to explain why few recognized Jesus as Messiah during His lifetime. While many later scholars rejected Wrede's skeptical conclusion, his label remains standard to this day. *(The Messianic Secret, 1901)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this is the central thesis of Wrede's book -->

**Richard Bauckham**, a contemporary British New Testament scholar, notes that Simon is the very first disciple named in Mark (1:16) and the very last individual named by the angel at the close of the Gospel (Mark 16:7).
