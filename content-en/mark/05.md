# Mark 5 — Legion, the Bleeding Woman, and Jairus's Daughter

## Story

Jesus and His disciples crossed to the other side of the lake, to the region of the Gerasenes. As soon as He stepped out of the boat, a man with an unclean spirit came out of the tombs to meet Him (5:1–2).

The man lived among the tombs. Time after time people had bound his hands and feet with chains and shackles, yet he snapped the chains and broke the irons—no one was strong enough to subdue him. Night and day among the tombs and on the hills he cried out and cut himself with stones (5:3–5). Mark paints a human life utterly shattered, dwelling among the dead.

Seeing Jesus from a distance, the man ran, fell on his knees before Him, and shouted at the top of his voice: what do You want with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? In God's name, do not torture me! For Jesus had been commanding: come out of this man, you unclean spirit. Jesus asked him, "What is your name?" "My name is Legion," he replied, "for we are many." And he begged Jesus repeatedly not to send them out of the region (5:6–10).

A large herd of pigs was feeding on the hillside nearby. The demons begged Him to send them into the pigs, and He gave them permission. The unclean spirits came out and entered the pigs; the whole herd, about two thousand in number, rushed down the steep bank into the sea and drowned (5:11–13).

The herdsmen ran off and reported it in the town and countryside. People came out to see what had happened, and approaching Jesus they saw the man who had been possessed by the legion of demons sitting there, dressed and in his right mind—and they were terrified. Hearing eyewitness accounts of both the man and the pigs, they begged Jesus to leave their region (5:14–17). One human being was restored, yet two thousand pigs were lost; they chose the pigs.

As Jesus stepped back into the boat, the rescued man begged to go with Him. Jesus did not permit him, saying instead: go home to your own people and tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and how He has had mercy on you. So the man went away and began proclaiming throughout the Decapolis how much Jesus had done for him, and everyone marveled (5:18–20).

Crossing back to the other side, Jesus was immediately surrounded by a large crowd beside the sea. A synagogue ruler named Jairus arrived, fell at His feet, and pleaded earnestly: my little daughter is dying; please come and lay Your hands on her so she will be healed and live. Jesus went with him, and a massive crowd followed, pressing in around Him (5:21–24).

In that crowd was a woman who had suffered from bleeding for twelve years. She had endured much under many doctors and spent everything she owned without getting better—instead growing worse. Hearing about Jesus, she slipped through the crowd behind Him and touched His cloak, thinking: if I just touch His clothes, I will be healed. Instantly her bleeding stopped, and she felt in her body that she was freed from her affliction (5:25–29).

At once Jesus realized power had gone out from Him; turning around in the crowd, He asked, "Who touched My clothes?" His disciples said: You see the crowd pressing against You, and yet You ask who touched Me? Still He kept looking around to see who had done it. Trembling with fear, the woman came, fell down before Him, and told Him the whole truth. He said to her, "Daughter, your faith has healed you. Go in peace and be free of your affliction" (5:30–34). He refuses to let her slip away unseen; He wants her seen, honored, and called "Daughter."

While He was still speaking, messengers arrived from Jairus's house: your daughter is dead; why bother the Teacher anymore? Ignoring what they said, Jesus told the synagogue ruler, "Do not be afraid; just believe." He allowed no one to follow Him except Peter, James, and John (5:35–37).

Arriving at the house, He found a commotion, with people weeping and wailing loudly. Stepping inside, He asked: why all this commotion and wailing? The child is not dead, but asleep. They laughed at Him. Putting everyone outside, He took only the child's father and mother and the three disciples and went in where the child lay (5:38–40).

Taking the child by the hand, He said to her, "Talitha koum!"—which means, "Little girl, I say to you, get up!" Immediately the girl stood up and walked around; she was twelve years old. Everyone was completely astounded. He gave strict orders not to let anyone know about this, and told them to give her something to eat (5:41–43). Twelve years the woman bled; twelve years the girl had lived—and Jesus calls both of them "daughter."

## Key Takeaways

**Nobody is too far gone for Jesus to seek out.** He crosses the sea into Gentile territory, steps into a graveyard, and meets a man living among corpses whom nobody could tame (5:1–5). Human chains could never hold him, yet one word from Jesus sets him completely free.

**Jesus rules over uncleanness, sickness, and death.** This chapter presents three scenes: driving out an army of demons, healing a woman bleeding for twelve years, and raising a dead girl. Under ancient ritual law, all three involved defilement—tombs, blood, and a corpse. Yet when they touch Jesus, uncleanness does not spread to Him; His cleanness and life flow into them.

**People can fear God's grace more than demons.** Seeing the former demoniac sitting calm and clothed, the townsfolk panic and beg Jesus to leave (5:15–17). They had learned to live alongside a tormented madman, yet could not bear a power that disrupted their comfort and livelihood.

**Every rescued person becomes a witness.** The healed man begs to tag along, yet Jesus sends him home to tell "how much the Lord has done for you" (5:19). He becomes the very first evangelist in the Gentile Decapolis.

**"Do not be afraid; just believe."** Jairus receives news that his daughter is gone. Jesus offers no elaborate theory—simply "Do not be afraid; just believe" (5:36). Faith does not deny painful reality; it trusts that Jesus stands bigger than even death itself.

## Deeper Meaning

**Dwelling among tombs.** Isaiah described a rebellious people "who sit among the graves… and eat the flesh of pigs" (Isaiah 65:4). The Gerasene demoniac inhabits that exact landscape—tombs, pigs, total uncleanness—and Jesus steps straight into it to rescue him.

**Shattering chains.** Psalms 107 praises the Lord, who "brought them out of darkness and the shadow of death and broke away their chains" (Psalms 107:14). The man at Gerasa snapped iron fetters yet remained enslaved inside. Jesus breaks the chains he never could.

**Healing in His wings.** Malachi promised, "The sun of righteousness will rise with healing in its wings" (Malachi 4:2). In Hebrew, the word for "wings" also denotes the corners or hem of a garment where Jewish tassels hung. Many readers hear that echo as the bleeding woman reaches for Jesus' cloak.

**Elijah and Elisha.** Both great prophets raised a dead child. Elijah prayed until "the child's life returned to him" (1 Kings 17:22). Elisha shut the door alone with the boy and prayed (2 Kings 4:33). Jesus likewise clears the room, yet needs no long agonizing prayer—He simply speaks a single word on His own authority.

**Death as sleep.** Jesus says the girl is "asleep" (5:39), just as He later says of Lazarus, "Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I am going there to wake him up" (John 11:11). Paul likewise calls departed believers "those who have fallen asleep in Jesus" (1 Thessalonians 4:14). In Jesus' presence, death is no longer final.

## Notes & Nuances

**What about the herd of pigs?** Many readers feel uneasy about two thousand pigs drowning and their owners losing their livelihood. Mark offers no explanation for why Jesus granted the demons' request. Some see it as dramatic proof that the spirits truly left the man; others see the sheer destructive malice of demons, bent on ruining everything they touch. The scene also forces an uncomfortable question onto readers: isn't one restored human life worth more than a herd of pigs?

**"Legion" is Roman military language.** Mark uses the technical Latin loanword for a Roman legion—roughly six thousand troops. Some scholars read the story as an echo of Roman occupation, with "Legion" swept into the sea—noting too that a Roman legion stationed in the region even bore a wild boar as its emblem. That background enriches the story, though it never replaces its central truth: a real tormented man was delivered.

**Gerasenes, Gadarenes, or Gergesenes?** Ancient manuscripts and the Gospels vary on the region's name. Because the city of Gerasa lay far inland from the sea, many understand the term to name the wider district rather than the city itself. Matthew mentions two demoniacs (Matthew 8:28), while Mark and Luke focus on one—likely the more prominent spokesman.

**It was not a magic cloak that healed her.** The woman assumed simply touching His clothes would heal her, and she was indeed healed. Yet Jesus refuses to let her walk away thinking in terms of magic. Calling her forward to tell her story, He declares "your faith has healed you" (5:34). His power healed her body; calling her "Daughter" in public healed her shame.

Locals identified her as the healed bleeding woman and said the statue stood outside her former home.
