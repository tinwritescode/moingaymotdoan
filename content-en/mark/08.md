# Mark 8 — Bread for Four Thousand, the Blind Man at Bethsaida, and Peter's Confession

## Story

During those days another large crowd gathered around Jesus with nothing to eat. Calling His disciples, He said, "I have compassion for this crowd," for they have already stayed with Me three days and have nothing to eat; if I send them home hungry, they will faint along the way, since some have come from far off. His disciples replied: where in this remote place can anyone get enough bread to feed them? He asked: how many loaves do you have? Seven, they answered (8:1–5).

Telling the crowd to sit on the ground, He took the seven loaves, gave thanks, broke them, and gave them to His disciples to distribute to the people. They also had a few small fish; He gave thanks for those too and had them distributed. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and they picked up seven basketfuls of leftover pieces. About four thousand people were present. Dismissing the crowd, He immediately got into the boat with His disciples and went to the district of Dalmanutha (8:6–10).

Pharisees came out to argue with Him and test Him, demanding a sign from heaven. Sighing deeply in His spirit, He asked: why does this generation ask for a sign? Truly, no sign will be given to this generation. Leaving them behind, He stepped back into the boat and crossed to the other side (8:11–13).

The disciples had forgotten to bring bread, leaving only one loaf in the boat with them. Jesus warned them: watch out and beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and the leaven of Herod. They discussed it among themselves: it is because we have no bread. Aware of this, He asked: why are you talking about having no bread? Do you still not see or understand? Are your hearts hardened? "Do you have eyes but fail to see, and ears but fail to hear?" When I broke five loaves for the five thousand, how many baskets full of leftovers did you pick up? Twelve, they answered. And when I broke seven loaves for the four thousand, how many basketfuls? Seven. "Do you still not understand?" (8:14–21).

Arriving at Bethsaida, people brought a blind man and begged Jesus to touch him. Taking the blind man by the hand, He led him outside the village, spit on his eyes, laid His hands on him, and asked: do you see anything? Looking up, the man said: I see people, looking like trees walking around. Once more Jesus placed His hands on his eyes; he looked intently, his sight was restored, and he saw everything clearly. Jesus sent him home, instructing him not even to enter the village (8:22–26). This is the only miracle in the Gospels accomplished in two stages—and right before it, the disciples themselves have eyes that do not yet see clearly.

Jesus and His disciples headed to the villages around Caesarea Philippi. Along the way He asked them: who do people say I am? Some say John the Baptist, others Elijah, still others one of the prophets, they answered. Then He asked: but who do you say I am? Peter replied, "You are the Christ." And He warned them not to tell anyone about Him (8:27–30).

Then He began teaching them that the Son of Man must suffer many things, be rejected by the elders, chief priests, and scribes, be killed, and after three days rise again. He spoke this plainly. Peter took Him aside and began to rebuke Him. Turning and looking at His disciples, Jesus rebuked Peter: "Get behind Me, Satan!"—for you do not have in mind the things of God, but the things of men (8:31–33). Peter sees that Jesus is the Christ, yet cannot yet see a suffering Christ—just like the blind man who at first saw people like walking trees.

Calling the crowd together with His disciples, He declared: "If anyone wants to come after Me, he must deny himself and take up his cross and follow Me." Whoever wants to save his life will lose it, while whoever loses his life for Me and for the gospel will save it. What good is it for someone to gain the whole world yet forfeit their soul? What can anyone give in exchange for their soul? Whoever is ashamed of Me and My words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man will also be ashamed of them when He comes in His Father's glory with the holy angels (8:34–38).

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus' compassion cares about empty stomachs too.** He says, "I have compassion for this crowd," because they have stayed three days without food (8:2). God does not care solely about disembodied souls; He notices exhausted travelers who might collapse on the road home.

**Sign-demanders stay blind to signs.** Moments after four thousand people eat their fill, the Pharisees demand a sign from heaven (8:11). For a heart resolved not to trust, no miracle is ever big enough. Jesus simply sighs and walks away.

**Even disciples can have eyes that fail to see.** The very men who gathered twelve baskets and then seven baskets of leftovers panic over forgetting bread (8:16–21). Jesus asks, "Do you still not understand?" Even longtime followers can swiftly forget everything God has already done.

**Recognizing Christ comes in stages.** The blind man at Bethsaida sees in two steps—first blurry walking trees, then sharp clarity (8:24–25). Moments later Peter confesses Jesus as Messiah yet recoils from a suffering Messiah (8:29–32). He has only half-opened eyes.

**Following Jesus means picking up a cross.** Jesus lays out His own road plainly—suffering, rejection, death, and resurrection (8:31)—and invites disciples onto that exact road: "deny himself and take up his cross and follow Me" (8:34). Clinging to self-preservation is the surest way to lose real life.

## Deeper Meaning

**Do not put God to the test.** In the desert, Israel ate manna daily and still demanded God prove His presence. Moses warned, "Do not test the LORD your God as you did at Massah" (Deuteronomy 6:16). The Pharisees replay that exact mistake (8:11).

**Eyes that fail to see.** Jesus' piercing question to His disciples (8:18) quotes Jeremiah's indictment of Israel: "foolish and senseless people, who have eyes but do not see, who have ears but do not hear" (Jeremiah 5:21). Words earlier aimed at outsiders (4:12) now strike close to home among the Twelve.

**Despised and rejected.** Jesus announces that the Son of Man must be rejected (8:31). Isaiah had already painted God's Servant: "He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows" (Isaiah 53:3). Peter has not yet realized that the royal Messiah and the Suffering Servant are the same Person.

**The scandal of the cross.** Paul writes that Jews demand signs while he preaches "Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews" (1 Corinthians 1:22–23). Peter in 8:32 is the very first person to trip over that stumbling block.

**Crucified with Christ.** Paul later lived out "take up his cross": "I have been crucified with Christ, and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me" (Galatians 2:20). Surrendering your life for Christ is where real life begins.

**Counting everything as loss.** Jesus asks what good it does to gain the whole world and lose your soul (8:36). Paul answers with his own life: "I consider everything a loss compared to the surpassing greatness of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord" (Philippians 3:8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Two feedings or one?** Some critics suggest the feeding of the four thousand is merely a duplicate telling of the five thousand. Yet Mark records both, and Jesus Himself cites both with their distinct basket totals (8:19–20). Many readers note that the first feeding occurs in Jewish territory and the second in the largely Gentile Decapolis: Christ's bread feeds more than Israel alone.

**What is "leaven"?** A pinch of yeast silently works its way through a whole batch of dough; Jesus uses it for corrupting influence spreading unnoticed (8:15). The Pharisees' leaven likely points to unbelief demanding signs, while Herod's leaven points to ruthless hunger for power—both fundamentally opposed to the way of the cross.

**Why call Peter "Satan"?** Peter was not demon-possessed. Yet urging Jesus to skip suffering echoed Satan's exact wilderness temptation: claim Messiahship without a cross. Commanding Peter "get behind Me" literally puts him back in a disciple's rightful spot—walking behind the Master, not jumping in front to steer Him.

**"Taking up one's cross" does not mean tolerating any random annoyance.** People casually label an illness or difficult coworker "my cross to bear." In first-century Rome, however, someone carrying a cross was walking publicly toward execution and total disgrace. Jesus speaks of freely choosing allegiance to Him even at the price of reputation and life itself.

## What Commentators Say

**Ignatius**, bishop of Antioch in the early second century, wrote to the Roman church while being marched to Rome for execution, begging believers not to interfere with his martyrdom. All the kingdoms of this world would profit me nothing, he wrote; permit me to imitate the suffering of my God. **The Talmud**, the collection of rabbinic commentary, preserves a haunting tale about the Messiah. Asked where the Messiah waits, Elijah answers a rabbi: he sits at the gates of Rome among the poor and leprous, untying and rebinding his wounds one by one. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, sums up the entire Christian life as self-denial. We are not our own, he writes; therefore neither our reason nor our will should govern our thoughts and actions. We are God's; therefore let us live and die unto Him—grounding this vision in Jesus' call to "deny himself" in 8:34 and parallel texts. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, book 3, chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the famous opening passage "we are not our own" on self-denial appears at the beginning of Institutes book 3 chapter 7 -->

**Martin Hengel**, the twentieth-century German historian and New Testament scholar, wrote a concise study of Roman crucifixion. Romans regarded crucifixion as the ultimate humiliation, reserved for slaves and rebels, so polite society avoided even pronouncing the word "cross."
