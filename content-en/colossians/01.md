# Colossians 1 — Supreme Over All Creation

## Story

Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, together with Timothy our brother, sends grace and peace from God our Father to the holy and faithful brothers in Colossae. Every time they pray for them, they thank God for hearing of their faith in Christ Jesus and the love they show to all the saints. That faith and love spring from the hope stored up for them in heaven, which they heard about through the true message of the gospel. All over the world this gospel is bearing fruit and growing—just as it has been doing among them ever since Epaphras, Paul's dear fellow servant and faithful minister of Christ, taught them and brought back word of their love in the Spirit (1:1–8). Paul had never personally set foot in Colossae; Epaphras was the one who carried the gospel home to this small town in the Lycus Valley.

Ever since hearing that encouraging report, Paul and Timothy have not stopped praying for them, asking God to fill them with the knowledge of His will through all spiritual wisdom and understanding. The goal is never idle head-knowledge, but walking in a manner worthy of the Lord, pleasing Him in every way, bearing fruit in every good work, and growing deeper in the knowledge of God. Paul also prays that they will be strengthened with all power according to His glorious might so they may have great endurance and patience, joyfully giving thanks to the Father who qualified them to share in the inheritance of the saints in the light. For He has rescued them from the dominion of darkness and brought them into the kingdom of His beloved Son, in whom they have redemption, the forgiveness of sins (1:9–14).

Then Paul lifts into a soaring hymn to Christ, likely an early confession or hymn cherished by the first churches. First comes His supremacy over creation: "The Son is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn over all creation." Everything in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible—whether thrones or powers or rulers or authorities—was created in Him, through Him, and for Him. He is before all things, and "in Him all things hold together" (1:15–17).

Next comes His supremacy over the new creation: He is the head of the body, the church; He is the beginning and the firstborn from among the dead, so that in everything He might have supremacy. For God was pleased to have all His fullness dwell in Him, and through Him to reconcile to Himself all things—whether things on earth or things in heaven—by making peace through the blood of His cross (1:18–20).

From that cosmic panorama, Paul zooms straight in on the Colossians themselves. Once they were alienated from God and hostile in mind because of their evil deeds. Yet now God has reconciled them by Christ's physical body through death, to present them holy in His sight, without blemish and free from accusation—provided they continue in their faith, established and firm, not shifting from the hope held out in the gospel they heard, which has been proclaimed to every creature under heaven and of which Paul became a servant (1:21–23).

Paul rejoices in what he suffers for their sake, filling up in his flesh what is still lacking in regard to Christ's afflictions for the sake of His body, which is the church. God commissioned him to present the word of God in its fullness: the mystery kept hidden for ages and generations, now disclosed to the saints so they might see the glorious riches of this mystery among the Gentiles—"Christ in you, the hope of glory." Him they proclaim, admonishing and teaching everyone with all wisdom, so that they may present everyone mature in Christ. To this end Paul labors and struggles with all the energy Christ so powerfully works in him (1:24–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Christ is no minor intermediary; He is supreme over both creation and redemption.** The Colossians faced pressure to add extra wisdom, rituals, or angelic powers onto their faith. Paul answers right out of the gate by unveiling Christ's full stature: He creates and sustains the universe, leads His church as Head, and reconciles all things to God through His cross (1:15–20).

**Knowing God's will always shows up in a fruitful daily life.** In his opening prayer, Paul does not ask for esoteric secrets detached from ordinary living. Authentic spiritual understanding produces conduct worthy of the Lord, good deeds, joyful perseverance under trial, and gratitude to the Father (1:9–12).

**The heart of the gospel mystery is Christ Himself living inside believers.** To ancient Jewish ears, Gentiles becoming full heirs of God's promises once sounded unthinkable. Now Paul declares that the glory of that mystery is not a secret formula, but a living Person: Christ dwelling inside Gentile believers, guaranteeing their future glory (1:26–27).

**All teaching in the church aims at maturity for every single believer.** Three times in one brief verse Paul repeats "everyone"—admonishing everyone, teaching everyone, presenting everyone mature in Christ (1:28). True wisdom in the church never belongs to a secret elite, but is thrown wide open to all.

## Deeper Meaning

**Rescue from darkness recalls Israel's ancient Exodus.** Saying the Father rescued them from the dominion of darkness, transferred them into His Son's kingdom, and granted them a share in the saints' inheritance echoes God rescuing Israel from Egyptian bondage and leading them into the Promised Land to receive their inheritance (1:12–14; Exodus 6:6–8; Deuteronomy 33:3–4). The new Exodus crosses not the Red Sea, but moves from guilt and bondage into forgiveness and Christ's kingdom.

**The hymn of 1:15–20 connects straight back to Genesis and Old Testament Wisdom.** Proverbs personifies Wisdom standing beside God from the beginning as He fashioned heaven and earth (Proverbs 8:22–31), while Genesis says humanity was created in God's image (Genesis 1:26–27). Paul shows that Jesus Himself is the exact image of the invisible God—Agent of the first creation and Pioneer of the new creation through His resurrection (1:15–18; John 1:1–3; Hebrews 1:2–3).

**The cross does not merely rescue individual souls; it opens the healing of the whole cosmos.** Through the blood of His Son's cross, God reconciles all things on earth and in heaven to Himself (1:20). That cosmic hope links directly to Paul's portrait of creation groaning for liberation in Romans and the vision of a new heaven and new earth closing Scripture (Romans 8:19–22; Revelation 21:1–5).

## Notes & Nuances

**"The firstborn over all creation" (1:15) does not mean Christ is a created being.** Both in Greek usage and in the Old Testament, "firstborn" signifies supreme rank and right of inheritance above all else—just as God called King David His "firstborn" even though David was the youngest son in his family (Psalms 89:27). The very next verse (1:16) settles any doubt: Christ Himself created all things and existed before all things, placing Him over creation rather than inside it.

**"Filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions" (1:24) never suggests Jesus' atoning death was incomplete.** Only verses earlier, Paul affirmed that Christ's death achieved complete peace and reconciled believers blameless before God (1:20–22). What remains to be filled up is not atoning merit, but the missionary hardships followers of Christ endure as they spread the gospel: suffering persecution for the church, Paul shares the ongoing pain bound up with Christ's body on earth.

**"Reconciling all things... on earth and in heaven" (1:20) raises questions about universalism.** Some read this verse to claim that eventually every being—even the wicked and demons—will be saved. Yet right in 1:23 Paul explicitly requires steadfast faith, and in 2:15 he declares hostile powers disarmed and publicly shamed. Cosmic peace here means every rebellion is finally brought back under Christ's sovereign reign—either through believers' willing surrender or through the undeniable subjugation of hostile powers.

## What Commentators Say

**Athanasius**, fourth-century bishop of Alexandria, appealed to Colossians 1:15–17 to refute the Arian claim that the Son of God was the first created creature. He pointed out that "firstborn" denotes not sequence of creation, but the Son's sovereign rank and condescension in creating and renewing all things; since the next verse declares all things were created *through* Him, He Himself cannot be a created thing. *(Orations Against the Arians, Book 2, sections 62–64)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Athanasius's classic exposition of prototokos in Colossians 1:15 in Orations Against the Arians II.62–64 is widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, clarifies 1:24 sharply against any idea that saints' sufferings supplement Christ's atoning merit. Christ's redemptive suffering was finished once for all on the cross; the remaining afflictions Paul bears are the earthly trials Christ continues to share with His members as the church walks through suffering. *(Commentary on Colossians, on chapter 1 verse 24)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: distinguishing Christ's finished redemptive suffering from the church's participatory suffering is Calvin's classic note on Colossians 1:24 -->

**N. T. Wright**, British New Testament scholar, views the hymn of Colossians 1:15–20 as a tightly balanced two-stanza poem celebrating old creation and new creation. Paul places Jesus in the exact place Jewish tradition reserved for divine Wisdom and Torah, proclaiming Israel's monotheism freshly redefined around Christ. *(Tyndale New Testament Commentaries: Colossians and Philemon, 1986)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the old-creation/new-creation poetic structure and Jewish Wisdom background are central pillars of Wright's Colossians commentary -->
