# Colossians 3 — Put On the New Self

## Story

Since you have been raised with Christ, set your hearts on things above, where Christ is seated at the right hand of God. Set your minds on things above, not on earthly things, for you died to your old life, and your true life is now hidden with Christ in God. When Christ, who is your life, appears, then you also will appear with Him in glory (3:1–4).

Building on that reality, Paul calls them to put to death decisively everything belonging to their earthly nature: sexual immorality, impurity, lust, evil desires, and greed—stating bluntly that "greed is idolatry." Because of these things the wrath of God is coming on the disobedient; they too once walked in those very ways. Now they must strip it all off: anger, rage, malice, slander, and filthy language from their lips. Do not lie to one another, since they have taken off the old self with its practices and have put on the new self, which is being continually renewed in knowledge in the image of its Creator. In this new humanity there is no dividing wall between Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, or free—"but Christ is all and is in all" (3:5–11).

Therefore, as God's chosen people, holy and dearly loved, clothe yourselves with compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness, and patience. Bear with one another and forgive whatever grievances you may have against one another, forgiving just as the Lord forgave you. And over all these virtues put on love, "which binds them all together in perfect unity." Let the peace of Christ rule as umpire in your hearts—the peace to which you were called as members of one body—and always be thankful (3:12–15).

Let the word of Christ dwell richly among you as you teach and admonish one another with all wisdom through psalms, hymns, and songs from the Spirit, singing to God with gratitude in your hearts. And whatever you do, whether in word or deed, do it all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through Him (3:16–17).

At the close of the chapter, Paul carries that faith straight into the daily relationships of an ancient household. Wives, submit to your husbands as is fitting in the Lord; husbands, love your wives and never be harsh with them. Children, obey your parents in everything, for this pleases the Lord; fathers, do not provoke your children, or they will become discouraged. Slaves, obey your earthly masters in everything—not only when their eye is on you to win favor, but with sincerity of heart out of reverence for the Lord. "Whatever you do, work at it with your whole being, for the Lord and not for men," knowing that you will receive an inheritance from the Lord as your reward. You are serving the Lord Christ, and anyone who does wrong will be repaid for their wrong, for God shows no favoritism (3:18–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Christian ethics never begins with clenched-fist willpower, but with our new identity in Christ.** Right after rejecting empty ascetic rules at the close of chapter 2, Paul unveils the real engine of change: believers have already died and risen with Christ, their true life hidden securely in Him (3:1–3). Shedding old sins and putting on new virtues simply means living out who God is already remaking us to be (3:9–10).

**The church is the place where ethnic, cultural, and class walls collapse in Christ.** In Rome's sharply stratified world, Jews looked down on Gentiles, Greeks labeled outsiders "barbarians," Scythians stood as the stereotype of utter savagery, and a vast chasm separated masters from slaves. Paul declares every one of those labels powerless to define human worth anymore, because Christ is all and in all (3:11).

**Love, forgiveness, and gratitude form the steady heartbeat of church community.** Paul never paints a fairytale church free of conflict; he explicitly writes, "forgive whatever grievances you may have against one another" just as the Lord forgave you (3:13). Repeating thankfulness three times across three short verses shows Christian life is never grim fear of punishment, but overflowing gratitude (3:15–17).

**Authentic faith walks right down to the family dinner table and the daily workplace.** Paul addresses not only wives, children, and slaves, but places direct moral obligations on husbands and fathers—figures holding near-absolute legal power in Roman homes—commanding tender love, zero harshness, and refusing to crush children's spirits (3:18–21). Meanwhile, enslaved workers treated by society as property are addressed as dignified moral persons serving Christ Himself and promised an eternal inheritance (3:23–24).

## Deeper Meaning

**Renewal in the Creator's image reaches straight back to Genesis.** Humanity was originally fashioned in God's image (Genesis 1:26–27), which sin twisted and marred. Chapter 1 celebrated Christ as the flawless image of the invisible God (1:15); now chapter 3 shows God restoring that original image inside believers so they grow steadily into Christ's likeness (3:10; Romans 8:29).

**Christ seated at God's right hand echoes Psalm 110's royal Messiah.** Verse 3:1 directly recalls Christ enthroned at God's right hand—drawing on the single Old Testament verse most frequently quoted across the New Testament (Psalms 110:1). Because He reigns there right now, believers can lift their hearts upward without being swallowed by earthly anxiety or temptation (3:1–2; Ephesians 2:6).

**Naming greed as idolatry exposes the core of sin across all Scripture.** The first and tenth of the Ten Commandments form a closed circle: whenever human beings elevate money, possessions, or pleasure to supreme security, that craving steps onto God's throne as an idol (3:5; Exodus 20:3; Exodus 20:17; Ephesians 5:5).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verses 3:18–25 neither endorse abusive patriarchy nor defend slavery.** Under Roman law, the household patriarch held sweeping authority over wife, children, and slaves, and contemporary household codes lectured subordinates while laying minimal limits on superiors. Working within existing household structures to avoid needless social upheaval, Paul quietly transforms them from within: addressing subordinate members directly as accountable moral agents before Christ, and reminding authority figures they themselves answer to an impartial Lord in heaven (3:19; 3:21; 3:25; 4:1).

**"Let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts" (3:15) uses an athletic umpire metaphor.** The underlying Greek verb literally means *to act as referee or judge* in a contest. Paul is pointing not merely to a calm private feeling during quiet time, but to letting Christ's peace serve as the deciding referee whenever friction arises inside the church so everyone stays united as one body.

**Setting our minds on "things above" (3:1–2) never means despising real life or neglecting daily duty.** The second half of this very chapter proves the opposite: people whose minds rest on heaven are the very ones who speak truth, love their spouses tenderly, care for their children, forgive freely, and throw their whole heart into everyday work as for the Lord (3:9; 3:13; 3:18–24).

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century church father, lingers passionately over 3:16's charge to let Christ's word dwell richly among believers. He reminds his hearers that Paul addressed this command not to cloistered monks, but to married householders raising children and running businesses in Colossae—everyone needs Scripture deeply rooted in daily life. *(Homilies on Colossians, Homily 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's famous call for laypeople with families to read Scripture in Homily 9 on Colossians 3:16 is widely quoted -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, draws profound comfort from "your life is now hidden with Christ in God" in 3:3 when Christians look at their own frail outward state. A believer's real life resembles a seed buried underground during winter: invisible to human eyes for now, yet kept utterly safe in Christ's hand until the spring day He appears. *(Commentary on Colossians, on chapter 3 verses 3–4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the winter-seed analogy and hidden security in Christ are classic Calvin comments on Colossians 3:3 -->

**Douglas J. Moo**, American New Testament scholar, points out that it is no accident the household code of 3:18–4:1 devotes its longest section to slaves and masters. Because Colossians traveled in the very same mail pouch as Philemon—carried back to Colossae by the returning runaway slave Onesimus—how Christian slaves and masters treated one another was a burning real-life issue in that exact congregation. *(The Letters to the Colossians and to Philemon, PNTC, 2008)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking the expanded slave-master section of Colossians 3:22–25 to Onesimus's return is a notable insight of Moo's PNTC commentary -->

**N. T. Wright**, British New Testament scholar, highlights the stunning promise in 3:24 assuring enslaved believers they will receive "the inheritance as your reward." In Roman law, slaves could never inherit property; handing Israel's treasured covenant word *inheritance* directly to slaves quietly proclaims their full royal dignity inside God's household. *(Tyndale New Testament Commentaries: Colossians and Philemon, 1986)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Wright highlighting the contrast between slaves' legal inability to inherit and the covenant inheritance promise at 3:24; exact page not verified -->
