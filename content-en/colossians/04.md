# Colossians 4 — Prayer, Wisdom, and Fellow Workers

## Story

Paul completes his household instructions from the previous chapter with a direct word to masters: provide your slaves with what is right and fair, because you know that you also have a Master in heaven (4:1).

Then he turns to parting counsel for the entire congregation. Devote yourselves to prayer, being watchful and thankful. Pray also for Paul and his companions, that God may open a door for their message so they may proclaim the mystery of Christ—the very reason he is in chains—and make it clear, as he ought to speak (4:2–4). Remarkably, sitting inside a prison cell, Paul does not ask them to pray for his quick release, but for an open door for the gospel.

Toward outsiders around them, they must walk in wisdom, making the most of every opportunity. "Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt," so that they may know how to answer everyone they encounter (4:5–6).

The rest of the chapter offers warm personal notes introducing the network of coworkers around Paul. Tychicus—dear brother, faithful minister, and fellow servant in the Lord—will deliver this letter and bring full news of Paul's circumstances to encourage their hearts. Traveling alongside Tychicus is Onesimus, our faithful and dear brother, who is one of their own from Colossae; together they will tell the congregation everything happening where Paul is (4:7–9). Onesimus is the runaway slave featured in the Letter to Philemon, now returning not as a disgraced fugitive, but publicly commended by Paul as a faithful brother.

Next come greetings from six companions standing beside Paul. Three Jewish believers come first: Aristarchus, Paul's fellow prisoner; Mark, the cousin of Barnabas, concerning whom the church has already received instructions to welcome him if he visits; and Jesus who is called Justus. Paul remarks with deep feeling that these three are the only Jewish coworkers laboring beside him for the kingdom of God, and they have proved a genuine comfort to him. Three others follow: Epaphras, one of their own and a servant of Christ Jesus, who is constantly wrestling in prayer for the Colossians that they may stand firm in all the will of God, mature and fully assured—Paul testifies that Epaphras has worked hard not only for Colossae, but also for neighboring Laodicea and Hierapolis; Luke, "the beloved physician"; and Demas (4:10–14).

Finally, Paul sends greetings to the brothers in Laodicea, and to Nympha and the church that meets in her house. Once this letter has been read to them, they must see that it is also read in the church of the Laodiceans, and that they in turn read the letter coming from Laodicea. He adds a personal charge for Archippus: see to it that you fulfill the ministry you have received in the Lord. Taking the pen himself, Paul signs the closing line in his own hand: "Remember my chains. Grace be with you!" (4:15–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Christian masters no longer stand above accountability; they answer to a Master in heaven.** Placing 4:1 at the start of chapter 4 is simply a later chapter division; in Paul's letter it directly completes the slave-master exchange from the end of chapter 3. Reminding masters that they too answer to a heavenly Lord fundamentally transforms how they regard those serving under them (4:1).

**Watchful prayer is always woven together with gratitude and gospel mission.** Paul refuses to wall prayer off from everyday witness in the world. Even in chains, the prayer request he sends back to the church centers not on personal comfort, but on God opening doors so Christ's message rings out clearly (4:2–4).

**Engaging outsiders calls for wisdom, warmth, and thoughtful grace.** Salt both brings out flavor and preserves food from spoiling. Speech that is "always gracious, seasoned with salt" blends genuine kindness with lively clarity—steering clear of both abrasive harshness and bland clichés, listening closely so each answer fits the person in front of us (4:5–6).

**Gospel ministry is never a solo performance.** The rich roster of names closing this letter shows an astonishingly diverse community around Paul: Jews and Gentiles, an educated physician like Luke alongside a newly converted slave like Onesimus, a restored coworker like Mark who had once stumbled, a woman opening her home for worship like Nympha, and a quiet intercessor wrestling in prayer like Epaphras (4:7–17).

## Deeper Meaning

**Masters and servants standing equally before one Lord continues Old Testament justice.** The Law of Moses reminded Israel again and again never to oppress the vulnerable or hired worker, because Israel itself had been enslaved in Egypt and God judges impartially without favoritism (4:1; Deuteronomy 10:17–18; Deuteronomy 24:14–15; Ephesians 6:9).

**Salt imagery connects covenant loyalty and wholesome living throughout Scripture.** Under the Old Testament, every grain offering brought to God was seasoned with salt as a sign of an enduring covenant (Leviticus 2:13). In the New Testament, Jesus called His disciples the salt of the earth and urged them to have salt among themselves and be at peace with one another (4:6; Matthew 5:13; Mark 9:50). Salt-seasoned speech flows from a heart transformed by grace.

**The earliest churches already circulated and publicly read apostolic letters as authoritative teaching.** Paul's directive to swap letters between Colossae and Laodicea offers a front-row glimpse of how Pauline epistles were copied, shared from church to church, and eventually gathered into the New Testament canon we read today (4:16; 1 Thessalonians 5:27; 2 Peter 3:15–16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Scholars propose several theories regarding the missing "letter from Laodicea" (4:16).** Some think it was a dedicated letter Paul wrote specifically to Laodicea that was subsequently lost. Another widely held hypothesis identifies it with the Letter to the Ephesians—originally drafted as a circular letter routed through several Asia Minor churches, whose copy would pass through Laodicea on its way to Colossae. As for the apocryphal *Epistle to the Laodiceans* that surfaced in the fourth century, scholars universally recognize it as a later forgery stitched together from snippets of Paul's genuine letters.

**The names at the end of chapter 4 hint at deeply human stories unfolding behind the text.** Mark had abandoned Paul's first missionary journey, sparking a sharp break between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39); yet here Paul warmly commends him and asks the church to welcome him (4:10; 2 Timothy 4:11). By contrast, Demas stands faithfully beside Paul right here (4:14), yet years later deserts him out of love for this present world (2 Timothy 4:10).

**Archippus (4:17) and Nympha (4:15) reflect early house-church structure.** For the first two centuries, Christians owned no dedicated church buildings and gathered inside the homes of generous hosts—such as Nympha in Laodicea and Philemon in Colossae (Philemon 1:2). Since Archippus is also greeted right at the start of Philemon, many suppose he was Philemon's son or the leader temporarily overseeing the Colossian congregation while Epaphras was away.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, lingers over 4:3, marveling that Paul sits in chains yet asks the church to pray for an open door for preaching.
