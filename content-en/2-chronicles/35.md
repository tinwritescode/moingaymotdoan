# 2 Chronicles 35 — The Great Passover and Tragedy at Megiddo

## Story

Right after rediscovering the Book of the Law and renewing the covenant in chapter 34, King Josiah held a grand Passover in Jerusalem on the exact fourteenth day of the first month. He assigned the priests to their posts and encouraged them in the service of the house of the LORD (35:1–2). Turning to the Levites—the teachers of all Israel consecrated to the LORD—the king instructed them to set the sacred ark securely inside the temple Solomon had built; no longer did they have to carry it on their shoulders as in wilderness days, so they could now devote themselves to serving the LORD their God and His people Israel. He directed them to organize by family divisions according to the written instructions of David and Solomon, stand in the sanctuary matching the family groups of lay worshipers, slaughter the Passover lambs, and prepare everything for their brothers just as the LORD commanded through Moses (35:3–6).

So that no pilgrim would go without an offering, Josiah stepped forward with breathtaking generosity: from his own royal flocks and herds he donated thirty thousand lambs and young goats along with three thousand bulls for all the people present (35:7). His example spread instantly to his officials, who contributed willingly to the people, priests, and Levites: temple administrators Hilkiah, Zechariah, and Jehiel gave the priests two thousand six hundred lambs and goats plus three hundred bulls; Levitical chiefs Conaniah, Shemaiah, Nethanel, Hashabiah, Jeiel, and Jozabad provided their fellow Levites five thousand lambs and goats plus five hundred bulls (35:8–9).

Every part of the massive service ran with rare harmony and precision. Priests stood at their stations and Levites took their divisions according to royal command. Levites slaughtered and skinned the Passover lambs while priests received the blood from their hands and sprinkled it on the altar (35:10–11). Burnt offerings were set aside by family groups to be presented to the LORD as written in the Book of Moses—including the bulls. Following ordinance, they roasted the Passover lambs over fire, while boiling the other sacred offerings "in pots, kettles, and pans" and hurrying them out to all the people (35:12–13). Only after finishing the people's feast did the Levites prepare portions for themselves and for the Aaronic priests—who stayed busy from morning until nightfall offering burnt offerings and fat portions. Likewise, Asaphite singers following David's order and gatekeepers stationed at every temple gate never had to leave their posts, because their fellow Levites prepared their portions for them (35:14–15).

Within a single day the entire liturgy was carried out in full. All the Israelites present observed the Passover, followed immediately by seven days of the Feast of Unleavened Bread. The Chronicler crowns this festival in Josiah's eighteenth year with his highest praise: not since the days of the prophet Samuel had any Passover like it been kept in Israel, nor had any king of Israel ever celebrated such a Passover as Josiah held with the priests, Levites, all Judah and Israel present, and the residents of Jerusalem (35:16–19).

Then the narrative takes a sudden, heartbreaking turn. Years later, after Josiah had set the temple in order, Pharaoh Neco of Egypt marched north to fight at Carchemish along the Euphrates River. Josiah led Judah's army out to confront him (35:20). Eager to avoid a clash, Neco sent envoys with a remarkably plain message: "What is the issue between you and me, O king of Judah?" Neco explained he was not marching against Judah today, but against the house with which he was at war, and declared that God Himself had urged him to hurry: "Stop opposing God, who is with me, or He will destroy you" (35:21). Yet the very king whose heart had once melted before God's Word refused to turn aside—"he would not listen to what Neco had said at God's command." Instead, he "disguised himself" to go into battle and engaged the Egyptians on the plain of Megiddo (35:22).

During the clash, Egyptian archers shot King Josiah. Realizing the wound was mortal, he told his servants, "Take me away, for I am badly wounded" (35:23). Servants lifted him out of his chariot into a reserve chariot and rushed him back to Jerusalem. There Josiah died and was laid to rest in the tombs of his fathers, while all Judah and Jerusalem erupted in bitter mourning (35:24). The prophet Jeremiah composed laments for Josiah, and to this day male and female singers commemorate Josiah in song, preserved in "the Laments." The rest of Josiah's deeds and his faithful acts of devotion according to the Law of the LORD are written in the Book of the Kings of Israel and Judah (35:25–27).

## Key Takeaways

**True worship joins warm enthusiasm, open-handed generosity, and thoughtful order.** Josiah's Passover was neither chaotic noise nor stiff, lifeless ritual. From the king giving tens of thousands of animals from his own holdings, officials joyfully matching his lead, to priests, Levites, choirs, and gatekeepers holding every post faithfully—all worked together so the whole nation could worship God unhindered (35:7–16).

**Quiet behind-the-scenes service upholds the whole community.** One of the loveliest touches in this chapter is how the Levites served everyone else before serving themselves (35:14–15). Because they shouldered the sweaty labor of cooking and serving, crowds ate on time, priests kept uninterrupted watch at the altar, and singers and gatekeepers never had to abandon their stations. Inside God's house, the quietest tasks receiving the least applause often keep everything running.

**God can speak through the most unexpected mouthpieces.** Earlier Josiah heard God through Moses' scroll and the prophetess Huldah; this time Scripture explicitly affirms that God spoke through Pharaoh Neco of Egypt—a foreign pagan king (35:21–22). None of us gets to dictate that God may warn us only through familiar channels or people we approve of.

**A faithful track record never shields anyone from the cost of today's stubbornness.** Just one chapter earlier, God praised Josiah for a tender, humble heart (34:27). Thirteen years later, facing Neco's clear warning, he stubbornly refused to listen and disguised himself to jump into a battle that was not his (35:22). Scripture never airbrushes its heroes: even Judah's finest monarch can suffer a fatal stumble the moment he stops listening closely to God's voice.

## Deeper Meaning

**The pinnacle of Passover pointing forward to Christ.** Whereas Hezekiah's Passover in chapter 30 had to be postponed to the second month because people were unprepared (30:1–2; 30:26), Josiah's Passover took place right on the fourteenth day of the first month per Mosaic law (Exodus 12:6–9), unmatched in splendor since the days of Samuel (35:18). Yet tens of thousands of slain lambs and bulls that day still could not avert Judah's looming collapse. In the New Testament, Paul reveals that every Passover pointed toward one final, sufficient sacrifice: "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" for us (1 Corinthians 5:7).

**Ahab's eerie shadow falling across Josiah's death.** Anyone reading 2 Chronicles closely feels a chill at verses 35:22–24. Over two centuries earlier, wicked King Ahab brushed off God's prophet, "disguised himself" for battle, took an enemy arrow, and cried to his driver to carry him out of the line "for I am badly wounded" (18:29–34; cf. 1 Kings 22:30–35). Here in chapter 35, the Chronicler uses nearly identical strokes to recount righteous Josiah's fall: ignoring God's word, "disguising himself" for battle, struck by archers, and pleading to be taken away "for I am badly wounded." That heartbreaking parallel underscores that when God speaks, past pedigree never overrides present obedience.

**The resting ark and the enduring calling of the Levites.** Josiah reminding the Levites to leave the sacred ark inside the temple rather than lugging it on their shoulders—freeing them to serve God and teach His people (35:3)—echoes King David's historic transition as he planned the temple (1 Chronicles 23:25–26). Across changing eras, the forms of ministry shift—from carrying a tent across sand to teaching and leading worship in a fixed house—while the core mission stays timeless: pointing people's hearts to God.

**The weeping of Megiddo echoing to the end of Scripture.** Josiah's sudden death carved an unhealing scar into Israel's memory. Jeremiah—who later held Josiah up as the gold standard of a king defending the poor and doing justice (Jeremiah 22:15–16)—penned laments over him (35:25). Over a century later, foreseeing Israel weeping in repentance over the One they pierced, Zechariah still measures the deepest grief on earth by "the mourning for Hadad-rimmon in the plain of Megiddo" (Zechariah 12:11). Even the name of that battlefield—joined with Hebrew *har* ("mountain") as *Har-Megiddo*—passes straight into Revelation as Armageddon, site of history's climactic confrontation (Revelation 16:16).

**Even the best earthly king cannot save the nation from darkness.** Ancient Jewish tradition long heard grief for Josiah echoing in Lamentations' broken cry over "the LORD's anointed," in whose shadow they had hoped to live among the nations, now caught in their pit (Lamentations 4:20). With Josiah's passing, Judah's last bright lamp goes out, ushering in four faltering kings and national collapse in chapter 36. History pushes our eyes onward toward a King of David's line who never missteps and whom death cannot defeat.

## Notes & Nuances

**Roasting versus boiling the Passover meal.** Careful readers of the Pentateuch spot an apparent clash: Exodus 12:8–9 insists the Passover lamb must be roasted over fire and never boiled in water, whereas Deuteronomy 16:7 uses a Hebrew verb commonly meaning "boil" or "cook." Verse 35:13 shows how ancient Israel harmonized the two texts gracefully: the Passover lambs themselves were roasted over fire per ordinance, while the other "sacred offerings"—bulls and accompanying peace offerings—were "boiled in pots, kettles, and pans." Better still, the Hebrew verb rendered "roasted" in 35:13 literally fuses both words together as "cooked/boiled in fire," honoring Deuteronomy's verb while keeping Exodus's method intact.

**Why did Josiah tell the Levites to place the ark back in the temple?** Verse 35:3 reads "Put the sacred ark in the house," as though it was not currently resting in the Most Holy Place. Since Scripture leaves the backstory unstated, commentators weigh three possibilities: apostate kings like Manasseh or Amon may have displaced the ark when installing a carved idol inside the sanctuary (33:7); priests may have temporarily moved it to safekeeping while carpenters and masons renovated the temple in chapter 34; or Josiah was speaking generally about the Levites' settled post-wilderness vocation rather than implying the ark was wandering around.

**Differences regarding Megiddo between 2 Chronicles 35 and 2 Kings 23.** In 2 Kings 23:29–30, Josiah's death occupies just two cryptic verses: Pharaoh Neco marches up toward the Euphrates, Josiah intercepts him, and Neco kills him at Megiddo—with no warning envoy, no disguise, and no second chariot. Moreover, ancient Babylonian records (the Babylonian Chronicle for 609 BC) reveal that Neco was marching north to *aid* the remnants of Assyria against rising Babylon—not to fight Assyria as older renderings of 2 Kings 23:29 assumed (the Hebrew preposition can mean either "to" or "against"). Josiah likely tried to block Egypt lest Assyria revive and crush Judah again. Whatever the geopolitics, 2 Chronicles expands the scene sharply to answer readers' hardest theological question: why did Judah's most godly king die young on a battlefield? Chronicles answers bluntly: because he refused to heed God's word spoken through Neco (35:22).

**Is "the Laments" in 35:25 the biblical book of Lamentations?** Although ancient tradition linked the five-chapter book of Lamentations to Jeremiah, its poems clearly mourn Babylon's fiery destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC—more than twenty years after Josiah's death. Most scholars therefore view "the Laments" mentioned in 35:25 as a separate historical collection of national dirges no longer extant, even if echoes of grief for Josiah still color the mood of biblical Lamentations.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, classic collection of Jewish law and interpretation, reflects touchingly on Josiah's fatal final step. The sages explain that Josiah dared block Neco because he trusted Mosaic law's promise that no sword would pass through an obedient nation's land—yet his fatal mistake was marching out without consulting the prophet Jeremiah first. Tradition adds a poignant deathbed scene: leaning close over the dying king's lips in his chariot, Jeremiah feared Josiah might murmur complaints against heaven, only to hear the dying monarch whispering that the LORD is righteous because he himself had rebelled against God's command. *(Babylonian Talmud, Tractate Taanit 22b)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall the well-known rabbinic interpretation in Talmud Taanit 22b explaining Josiah blocking Neco without consulting Jeremiah and confessing God's righteousness while dying, exact wording not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, warmly praises the open-handedness of Josiah and his officials supplying animals out of their own herds—the higher one's station, the more eagerly one should clear the path for the poor to worship God. **Sara Japhet**, Israeli Jewish biblical scholar, highlights two masterstrokes of the Chronicler's craft in chapter 35. **Raymond B. Dillard**, American Old Testament scholar, brings ancient Near Eastern history to bear on Megiddo in 609 BC. Citing the Babylonian Chronicle, he explains that Pharaoh Neco was actually marching to prop up Assyria's last stand against Babylon, and Josiah miscalculated politically by trying to block an Egyptian-Assyrian linkup. Theologically, Dillard highlights verses 21 and 22 as displaying Chronicles' expansive vision of divine sovereignty: God can employ even a pagan ruler like Neco to speak His will, anticipating His use of Persian King Cyrus at the close of chapter 36. *(2 Chronicles, Word Biblical Commentary, 1987)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Dillard's 609 BC historical reconstruction from the Babylonian Chronicle and theological pairing of Neco with Cyrus in WBC chapter 35, exact page numbers not verified -->
