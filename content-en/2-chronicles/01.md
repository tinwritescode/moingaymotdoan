# 2 Chronicles 1 — Asking for Wisdom at Gibeon

## Story

Following King David's peaceful death at a ripe old age and smooth transfer of the crown (cf. 1 Chronicles 29:23–28), his son Solomon establishes himself firmly over his kingdom. The author states the source of that strength right in the opening sentence: "the LORD his God was with him and highly exalted him" (1:1).

The young king's very first public act is summoning all Israel—commanders of thousands and hundreds, judges, and every leader and family head—to go up together with him to the high place at Gibeon. The narrator pauses briefly to explain why the entire nation gathers there: at Gibeon still stood the Tent of Meeting that Moses, the servant of the LORD, had made in the wilderness, and in front of that tabernacle stood the bronze altar crafted long ago by Bezalel son of Uri, son of Hur. Meanwhile, David had already brought the ark of God up from Kiriath-jearim to a separate tent pitched in Jerusalem (1:2–5).

Before the Tent of Meeting, Solomon ascends to the bronze altar in the presence of the LORD and offers a thousand burnt offerings before the whole assembly (1:6).

That very night, God appears to Solomon and says, "Ask for whatever you want Me to give you." Solomon does not rush to ask for personal perks. First he recalls the great loving devotion God showed his father David, praying that God's covenant promise to David may now be confirmed, since God has made him king over a people "as numerous as the dust of the earth." Standing before so immense a responsibility, the young king asks: "Give me wisdom and knowledge, that I may lead this people, for who is able to govern this great people of Yours?" (1:7–10).

That heart pleases God deeply. God answers that because Solomon has not asked for wealth, riches, honor, the death of his enemies, or long life, but has asked solely for wisdom and knowledge to govern God's people, God grants him wisdom and knowledge. Going far beyond his request, God also promises him wealth, riches, and honor such as no king before him ever possessed and none after him will ever equal (1:11–12).

Leaving the high place at Gibeon, Solomon returns to Jerusalem and reigns over Israel. The chapter's closing verses immediately paint a vivid snapshot of the new kingdom's prosperity: Solomon amasses 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen, stationed in chariot cities and beside the king in Jerusalem. Silver and gold become "as common in Jerusalem as stones," and cedar as plentiful as sycamore-fig trees in the foothills. Royal merchants import horses from Egypt and Kue at fixed prices—600 shekels of silver for a chariot, 150 shekels for a horse—and export them across the region to all the Hittite and Aramean kings (1:13–17).

## Key Takeaways

**A ruler stands firm first of all because God is with him.** The book's opening verse says nothing about Solomon's military prowess or political savvy; it simply states, "the LORD his God was with him" (1:1). Across all of 2 Chronicles, the rise or fall of every reign turns on one single measure: whether the king walks with God.

**Worship lays the foundation for an entire reign.** Solomon's first major act in office is neither reviewing troops nor expanding borders, but leading the whole nation up to Gibeon to seek the LORD (1:2–6). Launching his kingship at the altar reminds the entire country that an even higher King rules over Israel's throne.

**God's open invitation reveals what lives deepest inside a heart.** God's offer—"Ask for whatever you want Me to give you" (1:7)—is both a dazzling privilege and a mirror exposing Solomon's true priorities. Handed a blank check by the Almighty, he asks neither for personal luxury nor revenge over rivals. What weighs most on his heart is governing wisely a vast nation he humbly calls "this great people of Yours" (1:10).

**When we put God's calling first, God takes care of everything else.** Solomon asks only for what he needs to fulfill the task God assigned him, never for private reward. Yet God is never stingy: He gives exactly what Solomon requests and overflows it with wealth, riches, and honor (1:11–12). The closing four verses follow immediately as visible proof that God keeps every word He promises (1:14–17).

## Deeper Meaning

**A thread running straight from Moses and Bezalel to Solomon.** The author intentionally notes Moses' wilderness Tent of Meeting and the bronze altar crafted by Bezalel (1:3; 1:5; cf. Exodus 31:2–6; 38:1–2). Centuries earlier, God filled Bezalel with "wisdom, understanding, and knowledge" to construct the wilderness sanctuary. Now Solomon stands right beside that altar praying for "wisdom and knowledge" (1:10). Readers immediately see that Solomon's reign and the coming temple are no royal sideline, but the direct continuation of the Exodus era.

**A young king stepping into ancient covenant promises.** Addressing God, Solomon calls Israel "as numerous as the dust of the earth" (1:9), echoing God's sworn promise to Abraham centuries earlier (Genesis 13:16), while asking God to confirm His covenant with David (1:9; cf. 1 Chronicles 17:11–14). Asking to know how to "lead this people"—literally "go out and come in before them" (1:10)—likewise echoes Moses' prayer for his successor Joshua, so the LORD's congregation would never be left like sheep without a shepherd (Numbers 27:17).

**Divided worship explains why the temple must be built.** The brief historical aside (1:4–5) highlights Israel's unfinished state at Solomon's accession: Moses' tabernacle and bronze altar sat at Gibeon, while the ark rested separately inside a tent in Jerusalem. Worship remained temporary and split across two sites. That very detail opens the door to the next eight chapters narrating the temple's construction, uniting ark and altar under one permanent roof.

**Pointing ahead to One greater than Solomon.** The kingdom principle illuminated at Gibeon is later distilled by Jesus for every disciple: seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things will be added to you as well (Matthew 6:33). James reminds believers that Solomon's invitation never closed after the Old Testament: if anyone lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault (James 1:5). Greater still, Jesus declares Himself "greater than Solomon" (Matthew 12:42), and Paul names Christ "the wisdom of God" given to all who believe (1 Corinthians 1:24; 1:30).

**A quiet warning beneath gleaming wealth.** The chapter's finale recounts Solomon buying thousands of chariots and horses from Egypt while piling up silver and gold in Jerusalem (1:14–17). Readers who know the Pentateuch instantly recall Moses' specific warning for Israel's kings: never accumulate large numbers of horses, never return toward Egypt to multiply horses, and never amass large amounts of silver and gold (Deuteronomy 17:16–17). Although 2 Chronicles places this scene here to showcase God's fulfilled promise of prosperity, the shadow of that old law quietly reminds readers that soaring wealth always walks beside sharp temptation.

## Notes & Nuances

**Second Chronicles tells Solomon's accession quite differently from 1 Kings.** In 1 Kings 1–2, Solomon reaches the throne amid tense palace plots, Adonijah's bid for power, and bloody political executions. Second Chronicles skips all of that to flow seamlessly from the close of 1 Chronicles, where the entire nation crowns Solomon in harmony (1 Chronicles 29:23–25). The author is neither ignorant nor covering up facts—his readers already knew Kings—but writing for a fragile post-exilic community, he selects historical threads highlighting national unity and God's faithfulness.

**Why did Solomon sacrifice at a "high place"?** Later in Kings and Chronicles, hilltop "high places" routinely became hubs of compromised or idolatrous worship condemned by prophets. At the opening of chapter 1, however, Jerusalem's temple did not yet exist, and Moses' authorized tabernacle and bronze altar legitimately stood at Gibeon. Explaining the altar's Mosaic origins (1:3–5) explicitly vindicates Solomon's worship there.

**Notable differences compared with 1 Kings.** In 1 Kings 3:5, the LORD appears to Solomon "in a dream," and the young king humbly calls himself "only a little child" (1 Kings 3:7). Second Chronicles skips mention of the dream as well as the celebrated courtroom case of the two women claiming one baby. Conversely, Chronicles lifts the summary of chariots, horses, and silver—located later in 1 Kings 10:26–29—straight up to the close of chapter 1 so readers witness immediate confirmation of God's promised riches.

**One thousand burnt offerings.** Sacrificing a thousand animals on the bronze altar sounds staggering if pictured in one single morning. Most likely this was a multi-day national festival assembling leaders from all twelve tribes (1:2; 1:6), displaying royal generosity and marking a solemn start to the new reign.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, observes that anyone stepping into heavy responsibility should begin by seeking God, just as Solomon opened his reign beside the altar at Gibeon. Solomon's request delighted God because it focused on serving the people faithfully rather than chasing private gain; those who put wisdom to obey God first discover He supplies whatever else life requires. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 2 Chronicles 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing opening a reign with worship and prioritizing duty over self-interest is classic Matthew Henry on 2 Chronicles 1; exact wording not checked -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, recounts Solomon's grand festival at Gibeon and stresses that the wisdom God granted him outshone every sage of his age. In Josephus's telling, silver as common as stones and massive chariot forces in Jerusalem stood as tangible evidence of God's favor resting on Solomon's early reverence. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus narrates Gibeon and Solomon's early prosperity in Book 8; exact chapter and section numbers not checked -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli biblical scholar, highlights the Chronicler's purposeful editing compared with 1 Kings: where 1 Kings depicts Solomon heading to Gibeon apparently alone, 2 Chronicles 1 has him summon commanders, judges, and clan heads so the sacrifice becomes a united act of "all Israel." She also notes that moving the chariot-and-silver summary from 1 Kings 10 up to the close of 2 Chronicles 1 creates a tight literary frame showing God's pledge in 1:12 fulfilled on the spot. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: highlighting the participation of all Israel at Gibeon and the structural shift of the wealth notice to 2 Chronicles 1 are prominent features of Japhet's OTL commentary -->

**Raymond B. Dillard**, the American Old Testament scholar, draws attention to Solomon's request for "wisdom and knowledge" here rather than "a discerning heart" for courtroom cases as in 1 Kings. Connecting that exact phrase straight back to Bezalel named just five verses earlier (1:5)—the wilderness artisan filled with wisdom and knowledge to construct the tabernacle—Dillard argues that Chronicles quietly aims Solomon's wisdom toward his defining calling: building God's temple. *(2 Chronicles, Word Biblical Commentary, 1987)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: linking Bezalel to Solomon's "wisdom and knowledge" is a signature observation of Dillard's WBC commentary on chapter 1; exact page not verified -->
