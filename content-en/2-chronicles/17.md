# 2 Chronicles 17 — King Jehoshaphat Teaches the Law and Builds Up Judah

## Story

Following Asa's death after his shadowed closing years, his son Jehoshaphat succeeds him as king over Judah. Right from the outset, the new monarch proactively bolsters Judah's defenses against the northern kingdom of Israel. He stations troops in all the fortified cities of Judah and sets garrisons throughout Judah as well as in the towns of Ephraim his father Asa had previously captured (17:1–2).

Yet Jehoshaphat's truest anchor rests neither in ramparts nor in garrisons. Scripture declares plainly that "the LORD was with Jehoshaphat" because he "walked in the earlier ways of his father David." While northern Israel plunged ever deeper into idolatry, Jehoshaphat did not consult the Baals; instead he sought the God of his father and walked in His commandments rather than imitating Israel's practices. Accordingly, the LORD established the kingdom firmly in his hand; all Judah gladly brought him tribute gifts, giving the king "great wealth and honor" (17:3–5).

Never letting prosperity soften his zeal, Jehoshaphat's heart took bold courage in the ways of the LORD. Pressing spiritual renewal onward, he "removed the high places and Asherah poles from Judah" (17:6).

Better still, Jehoshaphat goes far beyond clearing away false worship to actively build spiritual literacy across the nation. In the third year of his reign, he launches a traveling sixteen-member teaching commission: five royal officials (Ben-hail, Obadiah, Zechariah, Nethanel, and Micaiah), nine Levites (Shemaiah, Nethaniah, Zebadiah, Asahel, Shemiramoth, Jehonathan, Adonijah, Tobijah, and Tob-adonijah), and two priests (Elishama and Jehoram). Carrying "the Book of the Law of the LORD" with them, they tour every town throughout Judah teaching the people (17:7–9).

From that moment, an extraordinary calm settles over Judah's borders. The terror of the LORD falls on all the kingdoms surrounding Judah, so none dares wage war against Jehoshaphat. Quite the opposite: Philistines bring gifts and silver as tribute, while Arabs drive in immense flocks—7,700 rams and 7,700 goats (17:10–11).

Jehoshaphat grows greater and greater. He constructs fortresses and store cities throughout Judah, keeps ample supplies stocked in every town, and stations seasoned, valiant warriors right in the capital of Jerusalem (17:12–13).

The chapter closes with a roster of five standing army corps serving under the king, organized by ancestral houses across Judah and Benjamin. From Judah stand three commanders: Adnah leading 300,000 valiant men, Jehohanan leading 280,000, and Amasiah son of Zichri—singled out with a warm personal note as one "who volunteered himself for the service of the LORD"—leading 200,000. From Benjamin stand Eliada commanding 200,000 warriors armed with bows and shields, and Jehozabad commanding 180,000 troops equipped for battle. Altogether 1,160,000 soldiers stand ready in Jerusalem to serve the king, besides all the garrisons stationed in fortified cities across Judah (17:14–19).

## Key Takeaways

**Carry forward the strengths of the past without repeating its closing mistakes.** Jehoshaphat inherits the early reform zeal and territorial vigilance of his father Asa (17:2; 17:6), while steering clear of the bitter self-reliance and foreign bribery that marred Asa's final years. Reverence for God is never inherited on autopilot—every generation must choose faithfulness afresh.

**Tearing down falsehood is not enough; people must be taught what is true.** Jehoshaphat understood that knocking over high places and Asherah poles (17:6) could never hold hearts for long on its own. Leave minds empty, and old idols simply climb back under fresh disguises. Sending God's Book of the Law from town to town built understanding into everyday life (17:7–9). Lasting renewal always walks hand in hand with teaching Scripture.

**True national security rests in God's presence.** Chapter 17 lists fortresses, store cities, and vast troops in rich detail, yet anchors every ounce of strength in one root cause: "the LORD was with Jehoshaphat" (17:3). Before a single sword ever left its sheath, God set awe upon neighboring kingdoms so nobody dared attack (17:10). Careful preparation is our duty; keeping real peace belongs to God.

**Wholehearted devotion to God belongs right inside everyday vocations.** Right in the middle of a stark military tally of generals and regiments, the author pauses for one warm line honoring Amasiah son of Zichri—"who volunteered himself for the service of the LORD" (17:16). Serving God is never locked inside clerical collars or temple courts; a field officer can offer his career freely as wholehearted worship.

## Deeper Meaning

**A nationwide teaching mission linking Moses to the Great Commission.** Mosaic law originally entrusted Levi with teaching God's ordinances and statutes throughout Israel (Deuteronomy 33:10) while commanding every king to keep a personal copy of the law close by all his days to learn reverence for God (Deuteronomy 17:18–20). Partnering civil officials, Levites, and priests to carry Scripture from town to town (17:7–9) turns that vision into public reality. Centuries later, the same pattern resurfaces after exile when Levites read God's law clearly and explain its sense so the entire crowd understands (Nehemiah 8:7–8)—answering Hosea's lament that God's people perish for lack of knowledge (Hosea 4:6). In the New Testament, that teaching mission expands to all nations through Jesus' final commission: teaching disciples everywhere to obey everything He has commanded (Matthew 28:19–20).

**Echoes of David and Solomon's golden age.** The phrase "great wealth and honor" (17:5) is Chronicles' signature refrain previously reserved for Israel's twin golden-era monarchs, David (1 Chronicles 29:28) and Solomon (2 Chronicles 1:12). Philistines and Arabs bringing silver and livestock freely (17:11) likewise evokes foreign tributes streaming into unified Jerusalem. Divided though Israel had become, Chronicles insists God's covenant with David's house never expired: whenever king and people seek Him, original blessings still bloom anew.

**The terror of the LORD acting as an invisible shield.** Right after crossing the Red Sea, Israel sang confidently that terror and dread would fall on surrounding nations so God's people might pass safely (Exodus 15:16). Second Chronicles sounds that same note under Asa (14:14) and twice under Jehoshaphat—first here when he dispatches Bible teachers across the land (17:10), and again after God delivers Judah from an eastern coalition (20:29). Judah was not invincible in itself; the majesty of the God walking with them stayed enemy hands.

**A heart lifted high in God's ways versus lifted high in pride.** Verse 17:6 says Jehoshaphat's heart took courage in the LORD's ways—literally in Hebrew, his heart *was lifted high* in God's ways, expressing bold, eager zeal for righteousness. Tellingly, later in 2 Chronicles that exact same Hebrew verb flips sharply negative to explain the falls of King Uzziah (26:16) and King Hezekiah (32:25): once grown powerful, their hearts grew *lifted up* in pride. The very same inner energy, aimed toward God's ways, breeds fearless devotion; turned inward toward self-glory, it breeds downfall.

**Chapter 17's peak of success opens straight onto chapter 18's snare.** Verse 17:5 celebrates Jehoshaphat gaining "great wealth and honor" as fruit of faithfulness; yet the very opening sentence of chapter 18 repeats those exact words to introduce his gravest blunder—forging a marriage alliance with Ahab, the northern king championing Baal worship (18:1; 1 Kings 16:31–33). Blessing and wealth are not evil in themselves, yet standing atop success is precisely where watchfulness drops and compromise slips inside.

## Notes & Nuances

**Two major ways to read "the earlier ways of his father David" (17:3).** Readers often ask why the author adds the word *earlier* rather than simply saying "David's ways." In standard Hebrew texts, *earlier* distinguishes David's devoted early years from later grave sins like Bathsheba and the census. Intriguingly, however, several ancient Greek Septuagint manuscripts omit the name *David* here, reading simply "the earlier ways of his father"—referring directly to the faithful opening decades of Jehoshaphat's own father Asa prior to chapter 16's decline. Either reading leaves the lesson untouched: Jehoshaphat chose to imitate early fidelity rather than late-life failure.

**Reconciling removing the high places (17:6) with leaving them standing (20:33).** Verse 17:6 states Jehoshaphat removed high places and Asherah poles from Judah, yet his closing reign summary in both 2 Chronicles (20:33) and 1 Kings (1 Kings 22:43) states the high places were not removed—mirroring the identical tension under Asa (14:3; 15:17). Two complementary explanations solve it smoothly: first, Jehoshaphat tore down pagan idol shrines while local hillside altars where ordinary citizens still worshiped the LORD outside Jerusalem survived; second, royal decrees dismantled them temporarily, yet entrenched local habits kept bringing them back—as verse 20:33 itself explains: "the people had not yet set their hearts on the God of their fathers."

**Understanding 1,160,000 troops (17:14–18).** Summing the five Jerusalem commanders yields 1,160,000 soldiers—double Asa's 580,000 (14:8) and nearly triple Abijah's 400,000 (13:3), a vast number for tiny ninth-century BC Judah if taken as a permanent standing capital garrison. Readers weigh three standard approaches: taking the tally literally as a cumulative national reserve including northern migrants; reading Hebrew *eleph* ("thousand") as a smaller military squad or clan regiment; or recognizing conventional ancient epic numbers dramatizing the overflowing strength and divine blessing resting on Jehoshaphat's rule rather than a modern statistical roll.

**Nearly all of chapter 17 is unique to 2 Chronicles.** First Kings compresses Jehoshaphat's entire twenty-five-year reign into five brief summary verses (1 Kings 22:41–45), omitting the traveling Torah teachers, Philistine and Arab tribute, and the five military divisions. Writing for post-exilic Jews rebuilding life around Scripture and worship, Chronicles dedicates a full chapter to showcasing a monarch who planted God's word at the heart of public life.
