# 2 Chronicles 24 — Joash Repairs the Temple and Betrays His Benefactor

## Story

Joash took the throne at just seven years old following Athaliah's overthrow in the previous chapter. His mother was Zibiah of Beersheba. Joash reigned forty years in Jerusalem, and for as long as the priest Jehoiada lived, the king "did what was right in the eyes of the LORD." Jehoiada even chose two wives for him, and Joash had sons and daughters to rebuild David's nearly extinguished royal line (24:1–3).

Once settled on the throne, Joash set his heart on restoring the house of the LORD. He assembled the priests and Levites and told them to go out to all the towns of Judah each year to collect silver from all Israel for repairing the temple, urging them to act quickly. The Levites, however, dragged their feet. Summoning Jehoiada the chief priest, the king asked why he had not required the Levites to bring in the tax Moses had prescribed for the Tent of the Testimony. The need for urgent repairs was painfully clear: "that wicked woman Athaliah and her sons" had broken into the house of God and used all its sacred objects for the worship of the Baals (24:4–7).

Seeing the old method stalled, Joash ordered a chest made and placed right outside the gate of the house of the LORD, then issued a proclamation throughout Judah and Jerusalem calling everyone to bring the tax Moses had levied in the wilderness. The response was electric: all the officials and all the people rejoiced and brought their silver, dropping it into the chest until it was full. Whenever the Levites saw the chest heavy with silver, they brought it to the king's officers; the royal secretary and the high priest's deputy emptied it and returned it to its post. Day after day they did this, gathering silver in great abundance (24:8–11).

The king and Jehoiada handed the funds over to those supervising the temple restoration. They hired stonecutters and carpenters to repair the house of the LORD, along with skilled workers in iron and bronze. Because the workmen labored diligently, the temple of God was rebuilt to its original specifications and reinforced. Once restoration finished, they brought the leftover silver back before the king and Jehoiada, who used it to craft utensils, dishes, ladles, and other gold and silver articles for temple ministry. Throughout Jehoiada's entire lifetime, burnt offerings ascended continually in the house of the LORD (24:12–14).

At last Jehoiada grew old and full of days, dying at the remarkable age of one hundred and thirty. The nation granted him an extraordinary honor rare in Judah's history: they buried him in the City of David among the kings, because of all the good he had done in Israel for God and His temple (24:15–16).

Yet the moment that spiritual anchor disappeared, everything reversed. The officials of Judah came and paid homage to King Joash. Listening to their flattery, the king abandoned the house of the LORD, the God of his fathers, and turned to worship Asherah poles and idols. Because of that guilt, God's wrath came upon Judah and Jerusalem. Though the LORD sent prophets to bring them back to Himself and warn them earnestly, they refused to listen (24:17–19).

Finally, the Spirit of God clothed Zechariah, the very son of Jehoiada the priest. Standing before the crowd, he spoke God's word plainly: "Why do you transgress the commandments of the LORD? You will not prosper. Because you have forsaken the LORD, He has forsaken you." Stung by that blunt rebuke, they conspired against him—and at King Joash's own command, they stoned Zechariah to death right inside the courtyard of the house of the LORD. King Joash forgot all the steadfast kindness Jehoiada had shown him and murdered his benefactor's son. With his dying breath, Zechariah cried out, "May the LORD see this and call you to account!" (24:20–22).

That dying prayer did not wait long for an answer. At the turn of the year, an Aramean army marched against Joash, invaded Judah and Jerusalem, slaughtered all the leaders of the people, and shipped every ounce of plunder back to their king in Damascus. Chillingly, the Aramean force arrived with only a small band of men, yet the LORD handed Judah's massive army over to them because they had forsaken the God of their fathers. When the Arameans withdrew, they left Joash severely wounded. Right then his own servants—Zabad son of Shimeath the Ammonite and Jehozabad son of Shimrith the Moabite—conspired against him to avenge the blood of Jehoiada's son and assassinated him right on his bed. They buried Joash in the City of David, yet barred him from the tombs of the kings. The rest of his reign, his sons, the many prophecies against him, and the restoration of the temple are recorded in the Commentary on the Book of the Kings; his son Amaziah succeeded him (24:23–27).

## Key Takeaways

**Borrowed faith collapses the moment external props are removed.** Joash did what was right "all the days of Jehoiada the priest" (24:2)—a compliment carrying an ominous warning right inside it. As long as his steady mentor stood beside him, Joash championed temple repairs and faithful worship. The instant Jehoiada died, a little flattery from court officials swung the king straight back into idolatry (24:17–18). Devotion propped up solely by parents, mentors, or a Christian environment—without taking root in our own hearts—is perilously fragile.

**Transparency and willing hearts unlock God's work.** While collections lingered vaguely in Levitical hands, repairs sat stalled (24:5). The moment the king placed an open chest at the temple gate—counted jointly each day by representatives of crown and sanctuary—people joyfully poured in so much silver that the temple was thoroughly rebuilt with surplus left over for golden vessels (24:8–14). Shared ministry thrives when leadership stays clear, accountable, and able to awaken willing generosity.

**Ingratitude and refusal to accept correction harden people into cruelty.** Joash owed Jehoiada both his survival and his crown. Yet when his benefactor's own son confronted his sin publicly, Joash did not repent; he ordered Zechariah stoned to death in the very temple courtyard where Jehoiada's household had hidden Joash for six years to save his life (24:20–22). Once powerful people grow addicted to flattery, truth-tellers look like enemies to be silenced regardless of past loyalty.

**History honors faithfulness far above rank.** Two burials inside the same chapter paint an unforgettable contrast. The priest Jehoiada carried no royal blood, yet he was laid to rest among the kings because his whole life served God and His people (24:16). By contrast, Joash wore David's crown for forty years yet was shut out of the royal tombs because he ended in idolatry and innocent blood (24:25). Real honor before God and history depends not on the title we start with, but on how faithfully we finish.

**Superior numbers cannot rescue a people who abandon God.** The chapter spotlights a sobering detail: a tiny Aramean raiding force routed Judah's vast army (24:24). The real strength of God's people never rests in army size or thick walls, but in faithful fellowship with the God who shields them.

## Deeper Meaning

**The half-shekel tax and the meaning of ransom.** When rallying contributions for temple restoration, Joash pointed directly back to the tax Moses established for the Tent of the Testimony in the wilderness (24:6; 24:9). Exodus records that law: whenever Israel was numbered, every person paid half a shekel as a ransom for his life—the rich paying no more, the poor paying no less—to support the sanctuary service (Exodus 30:11–16). Giving toward God's house was therefore far more than a building fund; it was a tangible reminder that officials and commoners alike stood equal before God, living purely by His redeeming grace.

**Chronicles' recurring covenant principle.** Zechariah's warning in verse 20—that Judah would not prosper because they had forsaken the LORD, and therefore He had forsaken them (24:20)—echoes word for word King David's farewell charge to Solomon (1 Chronicles 28:9) and the prophet Azariah's earlier word to King Asa (2 Chronicles 15:2). Across Chronicles, God never walks out on His people first; rupture always begins when humans turn away from Him, and even then He patiently sends prophet after prophet pleading for them to return before judgment falls (24:19).

**Zechariah's blood on Jesus' lips centuries later.** In the Gospels, confronting the hardened religious leaders of His own generation, Jesus swept across all innocent blood shed "from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Berechiah, whom you murdered between the temple and the altar" (Matthew 23:35; Luke 11:50–51). In the traditional arrangement of the Hebrew Bible, Genesis stands as the very first book and Chronicles as the very last. Naming Abel near the start of Genesis and Zechariah near the end of 2 Chronicles means Jesus was summing up the entire Old Testament record of humanity rejecting and killing God's messengers.

**From Zechariah's cry for justice to Stephen's prayer of forgiveness.** As stones rained down on him, Zechariah cried, "May the LORD see this and call you to account!" (24:22). Centuries later, in that very same city of Jerusalem, the deacon Stephen fell beneath a shower of stones for proclaiming Jesus. Yet Stephen's dying breath turned the cry upside down: dropping to his knees, he pleaded, "Lord, do not hold this sin against them" (Acts 7:59–60). Setting those two deaths side by side displays the seismic turn of the gospel: under the Old Covenant, innocent blood cries out for righteous reckoning; on this side of the cross, the One who bore judgment Himself opens the door of forgiveness even for those throwing the stones.

## Notes & Nuances

**Differences between 2 Chronicles 24 and 2 Kings 12.** Reading the two accounts together reveals striking contrasts. 2 Kings covers the temple repairs (2 Kings 12:4–16), then jumps directly to King Hazael of Aram marching on Jerusalem—forcing Joash to strip temple and palace treasures as tribute—followed by Joash's assassination (2 Kings 12:17–21), with no mention of his apostasy or Zechariah's murder. Moreover, 2 Kings 12:13 notes that repair money was not used for gold and silver vessels, whereas 2 Chronicles 24:14 reports that leftover silver after repairs funded new temple utensils. Many interpreters see the two accounts as complementary rather than contradictory: initial funds went strictly to structural repairs, and only surplus funds afterward went toward liturgical vessels; meanwhile, the Chronicler supplies Joash's late-life apostasy to explain why a king who started so well suffered Aramean humiliation and assassination at home.

**A textual note on the victim's name in 24:22.** Verse 20 clearly identifies the martyred prophet as Zechariah son of Jehoiada, and the Hebrew text of verse 22—faithfully rendered in the Berean Standard Bible and standard English translations—continues to refer to Jehoiada's son. Notably, certain older non-English translations (such as the classic Vietnamese version) suffered a translational or typesetting slip in 24:22 printing "Ahaziah" instead of "Zechariah," likely triggered by King Ahaziah's prominence back in chapter 22. Checking the Hebrew text confirms unmistakably that the murdered son was Zechariah.

**Why does Matthew 23:35 call Zechariah "son of Berechiah"?** Readers frequently ask why 2 Chronicles 24:20 calls Zechariah the son of Jehoiada, while Matthew 23:35 calls him "son of Berechiah" and Luke 11:51 calls him simply "Zechariah" without naming a father. Scholars offer two main solutions. First, Berechiah may have been Zechariah's biological father and Jehoiada his famous grandfather—very plausible given that Jehoiada lived to 130, and Hebrew routinely calls grandsons "sons" of a prominent ancestor. Second, Matthew or an early scribe may have blended in the father's name of the later post-exilic prophet Zechariah (Zechariah 1:1). Either way, the unmistakable setting of his murder in the temple court convinces almost all scholars that Jesus was pointing straight to 2 Chronicles 24.

**Jehoiada choosing two wives for Joash and the closing acts of violence.** Verse 24:3 mentions without criticism that Jehoiada took two wives for Joash, even though Deuteronomy cautioned kings not to multiply wives (Deuteronomy 17:17). With David's line newly decimated by Athaliah down to a single boy, many commentators view Jehoiada's move as a practical safeguard to secure royal heirs quickly without building the massive harem typical of ancient kings. Regarding the two acts of bloodshed closing the chapter—Joash ordering Zechariah's stoning and servants murdering Joash in bed (24:21; 24:25)—Scripture records the assassination as historical retribution without endorsing private regicide; in the very next chapter, Joash's own son lawfully executes those two assassins.

**Does excluding Joash from the royal tombs contradict 2 Kings 12:21?** 2 Kings states summarily that Joash was buried "with his fathers in the City of David," whereas 2 Chronicles 24:25 clarifies that he was buried in the City of David "but not in the tombs of the kings." 2 Kings uses the standard royal burial formula for interment within the capital, while the Chronicler adds the sharper detail highlighting the people's repudiation of a king who turned apostate and murdered a prophet.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, reads the sharply divided halves of Joash's reign as a sobering warning to anyone living off someone else's spiritual warmth. Joash's youthful zeal for repairing the temple was real enough, Henry notes, yet resembled water that stays hot only while sitting over a fire: the instant Jehoiada passed away, Joash cooled off and slid into idolatry under court flattery. **Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, vividly pictures the temple repair chest with an opening cut into its lid so citizens could drop in freewill contributions according to their means. Recounting Jehoiada's death at one hundred and thirty, Josephus stresses his burial among the kings as a righteous man who had restored David's throne. **The Babylonian Talmud**, classic compendium of Jewish law and lore, preserves a haunting legend surrounding Zechariah's spilled blood. According to the ancient sages, Zechariah's blood kept seething and bubbling on the temple pavement for centuries right down to the day the Babylonian general Nebuzaradan marched in to destroy Jerusalem. Astonished to see blood still boiling, the pagan commander demanded an explanation, then slaughtered tens of thousands of priests and scholars hoping to appease the murdered prophet—yet the blood never stopped boiling until he cried out asking whether Zechariah wanted his entire nation wiped out, whereupon it finally grew still. Through that vivid parable, Jewish tradition expressed deep anguish over innocent blood shed inside God's house, treating it as a root cause of the Babylonian exile. *(Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 57b and Sanhedrin 96b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the aggadah of Zechariah's boiling blood on the temple floor before Nebuzaradan in Gittin 57b and Sanhedrin 96b is famous and widely cited -->

**Sara Japhet**, Israeli Jewish biblical scholar, analyzes the crisp two-part structure of chapter 24: a flourishing first half under Jehoiada's guidance and a steep plunge once he dies. She shows how the Chronicler deploys this familiar two-phase pattern to explain Joash's tragic closing years—already known from 2 Kings through the Aramean invasion and palace assassination—in terms of moral accountability within each monarch's reign.
