# 2 Chronicles 28 — King Ahaz: Sinking Deeper in Every Crisis

## Story

When upright King Jotham died, his son Ahaz took the throne at twenty years old and reigned sixteen years in Jerusalem. If ordinary Judeans had been quietly drifting into corruption under Jotham, under Ahaz the entire kingdom plunged headlong into brazen apostasy. Refusing to do what was right like his ancestor David, he walked straight down the wicked paths of Israel's northern kings. He cast metal idols for the Baals, burned incense in the Valley of Ben-hinnom, and even "burned his sons in the fire," copying the horrifying practices of pagan nations the LORD had driven out before Israel. Across every high place, hilltop, and spreading tree, he offered sacrifices and burned incense to false gods (28:1–4).

Disaster fell at once. The LORD his God handed Ahaz over to the king of Aram, whose forces defeated Judah and marched swarms of captives off to Damascus. Before Judah could catch its breath, God also handed him over to King Pekah son of Remaliah of Israel, inflicting a staggering catastrophe. In a single day Pekah's army cut down one hundred and twenty thousand valiant Judean fighting men because they had forsaken the LORD, the God of their fathers. An Ephraimite warrior named Zichri struck down Prince Maaseiah (the king's son), palace commander Azrikam, and Elkanah, second in command to the king. The northern troops seized two hundred thousand of their own kinsmen—women, sons, and daughters—and hauled off massive plunder bound for Samaria (28:5–8).

As the triumphant army marched its column of captives toward Samaria, a prophet of the LORD named Oded went out to confront them on the road. Bluntly he told them: the LORD had handed Judah into their grasp solely because He was angry with Judah, yet they had slaughtered them "with a rage that has reached heaven." And now they proposed to subjugate men and women of Judah and Jerusalem as "male and female slaves"? Oded drove the point straight into their consciences: "Do you not also have sins of your own against the LORD your God?" He urged them to send their captured brothers and sisters home immediately, because the fierce wrath of the LORD was already poised over them (28:9–11).

Four leaders of Ephraim—Azariah son of Jehohanan, Berechiah son of Meshillemoth, Jehizkiah son of Shallum, and Amasa son of Hadlai—stepped up to block the returning troops. Flatly they refused to let captives be brought into the city, warning that Israel's guilt was already towering and God's anger blazing without piling fresh cruelty on top. Moved by that stern stand, the soldiers released every captive and left the plunder right before the leaders and assembly. Those four leaders then stepped forward to tend the captives personally: using clothing from the plunder, they dressed every naked prisoner, put sandals on their feet, gave them plenty to eat and drink, anointed their wounds with oil, placed the weak onto donkeys, and personally escorted them all the way back to their kinsmen at Jericho—the City of Palms—before returning to Samaria (28:12–15).

Still reeling, Ahaz refused to turn back to God and instead sent envoys pleading for help from the king of Assyria. At that exact hour, neighbors piled in from every side to tear Judah apart: the Edomites returned to raid and carry off more captives, while the Philistines swept across the foothills and the Negev, seizing six towns with their surrounding villages—Beth-shemesh, Aijalon, Gederoth, Soco, Timnah, and Gimzo—and settling there permanently. Scripture states the reason plainly: the LORD humbled Judah because of Ahaz, who had "promoted wickedness in Judah" and acted with utter unfaithfulness toward Him. When King Tiglath-pileser of Assyria finally marched down, he "afflicted him instead of strengthening him." Ahaz stripped treasures from the house of the LORD, the royal palace, and the homes of his officials to pay off the Assyrian emperor, yet it brought him no help at all (28:16–21).

Rather than repenting, "in his time of distress King Ahaz became even more unfaithful to the LORD." Embracing a bleak, superstitious pragmatism, he reasoned that since the gods of Aram's kings had helped Damascus defeat him, sacrificing to those enemy gods might win their aid for himself. Instead, those very idols dragged both him and all Israel deeper into ruin. Finally Ahaz crossed the ultimate line: he gathered the furnishings of the house of God, cut them to pieces, slammed shut the doors of the house of the LORD, and erected pagan altars at every street corner in Jerusalem and high places in every town of Judah to burn incense to other gods, provoking the God of his fathers to anger (28:22–25).

The rest of Ahaz's reign and ways are recorded in the Book of the Kings of Judah and Israel. After sixteen years of exhausting his nation, Ahaz died. They buried him inside the city of Jerusalem, yet refused to lay him in the tombs of the kings of Israel. His son Hezekiah took the throne in his place, poised to launch a sweeping spiritual renewal in the next chapter (28:26–27).

## Key Takeaways

**Hardship never automatically softens a person; it simply reveals what is already inside the heart.** When waves of crisis strike, a God-fearing heart humbles itself in repentance, whereas a hardened heart fights back even more defiantly. Ahaz carries the chapter's bleakest epitaph: "In his time of distress King Ahaz became even more unfaithful to the LORD" (28:22). The deeper trouble grew, the tighter he bolted the temple doors and the harder he chased useless substitutes.

**Being used by God to discipline others never gives anyone a license for cruelty.** God allowed northern Israel to defeat Judah for its apostasy, yet immediately rebuked the victors when they slaughtered recklessly and tried to enslave their own kinsmen (28:9–10). Prophet Oded's piercing question unmasks an age-old human blind spot: staring eagerly at somebody else's sins easily makes us forget the mountain of our own guilt before God.

**Genuine mercy can shine brightest in the least expected places.** While Jerusalem languished in darkness under an idolatrous king of David's line, Samaria—notorious for chronic unfaithfulness—produced a fearless prophet and four God-fearing officials (28:9–15). Their compassion went far beyond polite sympathy: they freed captives, clothed the naked from the spoils, supplied sandals, food, drink, healing oil, and donkeys for the weak, and escorted them home safely.

**Leaning on earthly superpowers instead of God always brings a heavier yoke.** Ahaz imagined emptying the temple and palace treasuries would purchase Assyria's protection, only to watch his hired protector squeeze him harder (28:20–21). Worship of raw power even drove him to bow before the gods of the enemies who had just beaten him (28:23). Whenever we trust worldly might over the Creator, the very crutch we cling to becomes the chain enslaving us.

## Deeper Meaning

**The promise of Immanuel was spoken straight into Ahaz's panic.** Turning to Isaiah reveals that at the exact hour the Aramean-Ephraimite alliance left Ahaz and his people shaking "as the trees of the forest are shaken by the wind," Isaiah walked out to reassure the king that the enemy plot would fail—if only he stood firm in faith (Isaiah 7:1–9). God even invited Ahaz to ask for any sign he wished, yet the king politely declined behind pious words because his mind was already set on Assyria (Isaiah 7:10–12). Right in that moment of royal unbelief, God gave the landmark promise of a son called Immanuel—"God with us" (Isaiah 7:14). Human faithlessness can never derail God's saving promise.

**An unmistakable forerunner of the Good Samaritan.** The picture of Samaritans anointing wounded Judeans with oil, clothing them, lifting the weak onto donkeys, and carrying them down to Jericho (28:15) bears an uncanny resemblance to Jesus' celebrated parable centuries later (Luke 10:30–35). In that parable, a traveler heading from Jerusalem to Jericho falls among robbers who strip and beat him half to death; priest and Levite walk right past, until a lone Samaritan stops to pour on oil, lift the wounded man onto his own animal, and see to every need. Many scholars recognize this radiant episode in 2 Chronicles 28 as a direct literary backdrop for Jesus' parable—teaching that real neighbor-love crosses bitter regional and religious divides.

**The Valley of Ben-hinnom becomes the New Testament name for hell.** By burning incense and sacrificing his own children in the Valley of Ben-hinnom right outside Jerusalem (28:3), Ahaz trampled one of the fiercest prohibitions in Mosaic law (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 18:10). Later, Jeremiah condemned that monstrous rite and foretold the ravine would be renamed "the Valley of Slaughter" (Jeremiah 7:31–32). From the Hebrew *Ge-Hinnom* ("Valley of Hinnom") later Jewish speech coined the Greek word *Gehenna*, translated "hell" across Jesus' solemn warnings (Matthew 5:22; Mark 9:43–44). Ahaz's depravity burned a lasting warning deep into Israel's spiritual vocabulary.

**The self-inflicted trap of hiring Assyria.** Ahaz drained God's house of gold to summon Tiglath-pileser, only to be afflicted rather than helped (28:20–21). Isaiah vividly prophesied that the LORD would wield the king of Assyria like a "hired razor" shaving Judah bare, and pictured Assyrian armies like a raging river rising "up to the neck" (Isaiah 7:20; 8:7–8). Contemporary prophet Hosea likewise exposed the futility of running to Assyria, whose king "cannot heal you or cure your wound" (Hosea 5:13). Alliances purchased by abandoning faith inevitably turn hired rescuers into ruthless overlords.

**Hard-hearted Ahaz contrasted with humbled Manasseh.** Ahaz's tragic slide—growing more unfaithful in distress (28:22)—stands as the exact mirror opposite of his own grandson King Manasseh in chapter 33. Manasseh committed atrocities even darker than Ahaz's, yet when chained in affliction he "humbled himself greatly" and prayed, and God heard him and brought him home to Jerusalem (33:12–13). Ahaz's stubbornness instead anticipates the tragic picture in Revelation, where people struck by fierce judgments still refuse to "repent and give Him glory" (Revelation 16:9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Child sacrifice in fire (28:3)—a real ancient horror.** Reading that a king of David's dynasty "burned his sons in the fire" makes this one of Scripture's bleakest passages. Across the ancient Near East, burning children to Molech or Baal was an extreme ritual desperation move in wartime meant to coax angry deities into saving a city. Where 2 Kings 16:3 uses the slightly more indirect idiom "made his son pass through the fire," 2 Chronicles 28:3 states starkly "burned his sons in the fire"—in the plural—to unmask the full horror of the crime. Mosaic law outlawed child sacrifice from the beginning (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 18:10), and Jeremiah declared God had never commanded it nor had it ever entered His mind (Jeremiah 7:31). Scripture records it unflinchingly to reveal how far humanity can sink once it turns away from the true God—losing even basic parental instinct.

**The massive figures of 120,000 slain in one day and 200,000 captives (28:6; 28:8).** Modern readers are often startled by casualty and captive totals that appear enormous relative to small eighth-century BC Judah. Scholars generally note three interpretive approaches. Some take the numbers literally as the worst military calamity in Judah's history, perhaps summing losses across an entire campaign. Many Hebrew linguists note that the word *eleph* ("thousand") can also mean a *military unit* or *clan* containing far fewer actual soldiers. Still others point to ancient Near Eastern rhetorical conventions using vast symbolic numbers to convey catastrophic national scale. Regardless of how the numbers are parsed, the chapter's point stays unmistakable: apostasy plunged the nation into devastating bloodshed.

**Comparing perspectives in 2 Chronicles 28 and 2 Kings 16.** Reading the two accounts side by side highlights distinct emphases. 2 Kings 16:5 notes that the Aramean-Israelite coalition besieged Jerusalem "but could not overpower him," and that after Ahaz sent tribute, Assyria captured Damascus and killed Aram's king (2 Kings 16:7–9). Meanwhile, 2 Chronicles 28 stresses Judah's severe battlefield losses, devotes seven full verses to prophet Oded's rescue of the captives—completely absent from 2 Kings—and concludes that Assyria afflicted rather than helped Ahaz (28:20–21). Rather than contradicting each other, the two accounts interlock: northern armies failed to breach Jerusalem's walls yet devastated Judah's countryside forces, while Tiglath-pileser crushed Damascus to expand his own empire and promptly reduced Judah to a heavily taxed vassal state. Likewise, 2 Kings 16:10–18 details Ahaz building a Damascus-style altar to replace the bronze altar, while 2 Chronicles 28:24 carries his apostasy to its climax as he cuts up temple articles and bolts the doors shut.

**Ahaz's burial (28:27) compared with 2 Kings 16:20.** 2 Kings 16:20 states briefly that Ahaz was "buried with his fathers in the City of David," whereas 2 Chronicles 28:27 clarifies: "they buried him in the city of Jerusalem, but did not place him in the tombs of the kings of Israel." He was laid to rest inside the capital, yet excluded from the honored dynastic vaults of David's line—sharing the quiet rebuke dealt earlier to Jehoram, Joash, and Uzziah (21:20; 24:25; 26:23). History delivered a silent, firm verdict on the king who locked the doors of God's house.

**Echoes of the golden calf (28:19) and the ban on enslaving fellow Israelites (28:10).** The rare Hebrew verb saying Ahaz "promoted wickedness" or "let Judah run wild" (28:19) echoes the exact verb used when Aaron let Israel "run wild" around the golden calf at Sinai (Exodus 32:25)—quietly equating Ahaz's idolatry with Israel's founding disaster. Meanwhile, prophet Oded's warning against making Judeans "male and female slaves" (28:10) stands directly on covenant law: because every Israelite belonged to the LORD who redeemed them from Egypt, no Israelite was ever permitted to enslave a fellow citizen (Leviticus 25:39–43).

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, portrays Ahaz's reign as a steady downward slide from apostasy into national ruin. He explicitly notes Ahaz's gruesome Canaanite sacrifice of his own son, then vividly narrates prophet Oded intercepting the northern army marching Judean prisoners toward Samaria. **The Babylonian Talmud**, classic compilation of rabbinic law and debate, discusses Ahaz's sins extensively when weighing which wicked kings forfeited a share in the world to come. **Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, pauses deeply over verse 22 and Ahaz growing ever more unfaithful in distress. Affliction acts like a crucible revealing what already sits in the heart, Henry observes: without softening grace, suffering only drives stubborn souls into fiercer defiance, branding Ahaz's name with lasting infamy across Scripture. Turning to the chapter's first half, Henry applauds Oded's bravery in stepping in front of a blood-flushed victorious army, calling his piercing question in verse 10—asking whether they too have sins against God—a mirror for everyone eager to punish others' faults while ignoring their own. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 2 Chronicles chapter 28)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's characteristic reflections on Oded's probing question in verse 10, the four leaders' compassion, and hardening under affliction in verse 22, exact original text not verified -->

**Sara Japhet**, Israeli Jewish biblical scholar, observes that the Chronicler crafts Ahaz as the darkest monarch in Judah's entire dynasty—even grimmer than 2 Kings by depicting him smashing furnishings and slamming shut the temple doors. **Raymond B. Dillard**, American Old Testament scholar, highlights a dense web of literary allusions woven through chapter 28. He notes that the rare verb for Ahaz letting Judah run wild in verse 19 reaches straight back to Aaron and the golden calf in Exodus chapter 32, while Ahaz's hardening in distress at verse 22 forms a deliberate foil for Manasseh's humble repentance in chapter 33.
