# 2 Chronicles 32 — Jerusalem Under Assyrian Siege and Hezekiah's Test

## Story

Immediately after the sweeping revival and faithful care for God's house across chapters 29–31, a violent storm hit Judah. King Sennacherib of the Assyrian Empire invaded the country with a massive army and laid siege to its fortified cities, intending to conquer them all (32:1). Seeing Sennacherib advancing and setting his sights directly on Jerusalem, King Hezekiah did not sit passively waiting to die. Consulting his officials and military leaders, he moved to seal off every water spring outside the city walls. A huge crowd turned out to block all the springs and the stream running through the land, ensuring the invading Assyrians would find no ready water supply to sustain a long siege (32:2–4).

Simultaneously, Hezekiah took courage and threw himself into shoring up defenses. He repaired every broken stretch of city wall, raised towers along it, constructed a second outer wall, reinforced the Millo citadel inside the City of David, and forged weapons and shields in abundance (32:5). With defenses prepared, he appointed military commanders over the people and gathered everyone together in the open square at the city gate. Speaking straight to their hearts, he urged them, "Be strong and courageous! Do not be afraid or discouraged" before the king of Assyria and his vast horde, for a far greater Power stood with them than with the invader. Plainly he contrasted the two sides: with Sennacherib "is only an arm of flesh, but with us is the LORD our God to help us and to fight our battles." Encouraged to the core, the people took confidence from their king's words (32:6–8).

While his main army was still besieging Lachish, Sennacherib dispatched officers to Jerusalem to wage psychological warfare on the city's morale (32:9). Standing outside the walls, they mocked Judah's faith—demanding what gave them the nerve to stay cooped up under siege, and claiming Hezekiah was deceiving them only to let them die of hunger and thirst (32:10–11). Shrewdly they twisted Hezekiah's very worship reforms, arguing that he had torn down God's own high places and altars and forced everyone to worship before a single altar in Jerusalem (32:12). From there they boasted of Assyrian conquests: not a single god of any other nation had ever saved its people from Assyrian kings, so how could Judah's God possibly rescue them (32:13–15)?

Not stopping at spoken insults against God and His servant Hezekiah, Sennacherib also sent letters mocking the LORD, the God of Israel, reducing Him to the level of powerless pagan idols of conquered nations (32:16–17). Assyrian envoys deliberately shouted in Hebrew—the language of Judah—so ordinary defenders watching from the ramparts would understand every word, lose heart, and collapse in panic. They spoke about the God of Jerusalem exactly as they spoke about gods carved by human hands (32:18–19).

Faced with that storm of threats, King Hezekiah and the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz did not trade insults with the enemy. Together they prayed and cried out to heaven (32:20). The LORD answered decisively: He sent an angel who annihilated every mighty warrior, commander, and officer throughout the Assyrian camp. Humiliated, Sennacherib slunk back to his own homeland—where later, as he bowed inside the temple of his god, his own sons cut him down with the sword (32:21). Thus the LORD saved Hezekiah and Jerusalem from Sennacherib and every enemy on every side, granting them rest all around. Hearing what happened, many neighboring peoples brought offerings to Jerusalem for the LORD and precious gifts for Hezekiah, and from then on he stood exalted in the eyes of all nations (32:22–23).

Yet Hezekiah's story does not end on a flawless pedestal. Around that same season, he fell mortally ill. Praying to the LORD, he received God's answer along with a miraculous sign of healing (32:24). Ironically, this faithful king failed to respond with gratitude matching the grace shown him, "because his heart was proud." Consequently, wrath fell upon him and upon Judah and Jerusalem (32:25). Mercifully, Hezekiah caught himself in time. Both he and Jerusalem's residents humbled the pride of their hearts, so the wrath of the LORD did not fall on them during the remainder of Hezekiah's lifetime (32:26).

The narrator summarizes the extraordinary prosperity God showered on Hezekiah: he built treasuries for silver, gold, gemstones, spices, shields, and fine wares; storehouses for grain, wine, and oil; stalls for every kind of livestock and pens for huge flocks; and many new towns (32:27–29). Most famous of his civic works, he stopped the upper outlet of the Gihon spring and tunneled water underground straight to the west side of the City of David. Hezekiah succeeded in everything he undertook (32:30). Only one sobering footnote remains: when envoys arrived from Babylon's rulers to inquire about the miraculous sign that had occurred in the land, God stepped back "to test him and to know all that was in his heart" (32:31).

Chapter 32 closes the biography of one of Judah's noblest monarchs. His remaining deeds and faithful devotion were recorded in the Vision of the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz and in the Book of the Kings of Judah and Israel (32:32). When he died, they laid him to rest on the upper slope of the tombs of David's descendants, and all Judah and Jerusalem paid him deep honor at his passing. His son Manasseh succeeded him (32:33).

## Key Takeaways

**Faithfulness to God never guarantees a storm-free life.** The chapter opens with a line that jolts every reader: "After all that Hezekiah had so faithfully done, Sennacherib king of Assyria came and invaded Judah" (32:1). Right after pouring his whole heart into temple cleansing and national revival in chapters 29–31, what arrived next was not smooth sailing, but the deadliest superpower on earth. Crisis does not automatically signal disobedience; often it is the furnace where newly built faith gets tested.

**Trusting God never excuses neglecting wise preparation.** Hezekiah did not fold his hands waiting for a miracle while ignoring his royal duties. He blocked outside springs, tunneled water safely indoors, patched walls, raised outer ramparts, forged weapons, and organized commanders (32:3–6). Yet once every human precaution was finished, he never placed his trust in stone walls or iron blades, pointing his people squarely to the God fighting for them (32:7–8). Living faith works with full diligence while knowing human strength alone cannot save.

**The enemy always tries to crack our faith before attacking our walls.** Assyria's officers shouted right in Judah's native tongue to sow dread and doubt along the ramparts (32:18). They twisted truth—casting Hezekiah's faithful destruction of pagan shrines as an insult to God (32:12)—and paraded other nations' defeats to argue that God was helpless (32:13–15). In spiritual life too, the fiercest battles unfold in the mind whenever intimidating circumstances start sounding louder than God's promises.

**Pride is success's deadliest snare, yet humility keeps the door of mercy open.** Hezekiah stood rock-solid against Assyria's massive war machine, only to stumble once peace, riches, and healing arrived (32:24–25). Self-importance sneaks in most effortlessly right after big triumphs. What made Hezekiah truly great was not spotless perfection, but his readiness to humble himself immediately when confronted instead of doubling down defensively (32:26).

## Deeper Meaning

**"An arm of flesh" set against the God who dwells with His people.** Hezekiah's stirring gatehouse address (32:7–8) connects into one of Scripture's great courage motifs. Long before, God charged Joshua to be strong and courageous without fear because He was with him (Joshua 1:9), and assured Jehoshaphat surrounded by armies that the battle belonged to God (20:15–17; cf. Exodus 14:14). Isaiah—standing right beside Hezekiah during this crisis—reminded Judah that Egyptians and warhorses are mere flesh, not Spirit (Isaiah 31:3); later, Jeremiah contrasted the curse on anyone making flesh his strength with the blessing on whoever trusts the LORD (Jeremiah 17:5–7). In the New Testament, that same confident chorus rings out even brighter: "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Romans 8:31), and "He who is in you is greater than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:4).

**The biting irony separating the living God from hand-carved idols.** Assyrian envoys scoffed at Jerusalem's God as no different from "the gods of the peoples of the earth, the work of human hands" (32:19; cf. Psalms 115:3–8). Sennacherib's own end in verse 32:21 delivers history's sharpest reply: the emperor who boasted no god could rescue anyone from his grip was hacked down by his own sons right inside the temple of his own god, powerless to lift a finger for him (2 Kings 19:37). By contrast, little Jerusalem—with God dwelling in her midst—stood unshaken, echoing Psalm 46's celebration of the city gladdened by a stream and rescued by God at break of day (Psalms 46:4–7).

**Identical pride, opposite endings: Hezekiah versus Uzziah.** Readers tracing 2 Chronicles catch a clear parallel. Earlier, King Uzziah likewise enjoyed stunning success and divine help until power puffed up his heart into proud sin, leaving him leprous until death (26:16). Hezekiah tripped on the exact same root—healed, honored, and then "his heart was proud" (32:25). Unlike Uzziah, however, Hezekiah fulfilled the exact condition of God's temple covenant with Solomon: he humbled himself before God (7:14; 32:26). That humility preserved peace for an entire generation, illustrating Scripture's steady law that God opposes the proud yet gives grace to the humble (James 4:6).

**Tests designed to reveal what lies hidden inside the heart.** Verse 32:31 offers a deeply searching remark: when Babylonian envoys visited, God stepped back "to test him and to know all that was in his heart." That wording directly echoes God's forty-year wilderness journey with Israel, humbling and testing them to know what lay within their hearts (Deuteronomy 8:2). God does not test us only through enemies and sickness; sometimes He tests us through flattery, wealth, and prestigious guests from afar—bringing hidden pride out into daylight where we ourselves can finally see it.

## Notes & Nuances

**Comparing 2 Chronicles 32 with the longer account in 2 Kings 18–20.** 2 Kings (and Isaiah 36–39) spends three full chapters narrating events that 2 Chronicles condenses into one chapter. Notably, Chronicles omits Hezekiah's earlier moment of panic in 2 Kings 18:14–16—when he surrendered, paid three hundred talents of silver and thirty talents of gold, and even stripped gold plating from temple doors and pillars, only for Sennacherib to threaten Jerusalem anyway. Chronicles likewise condenses the angel's nighttime strike—where 2 Kings gives the figure of 185,000 soldiers (2 Kings 19:35)—along with Hezekiah's fifteen extra years, the shadow moving backward ten steps (2 Kings 20:8–11; Isaiah 38:1–8), and his boastful tour of the treasuries to Babylonian envoys triggering Isaiah's prophecy of Babylonian exile (2 Kings 20:12–19). Assuming readers already know Kings, the Chronicler streamlines the narrative to highlight its theological spine: trust brings rescue, pride invites wrath, humility turns wrath aside.

**Famous historical and archaeological discoveries linked to chapter 32.** Two landmarks from this chapter survive as physical evidence today. First is the underground aqueduct from the Gihon spring in 32:30 (cf. 2 Kings 20:20)—Hezekiah's Siloam Tunnel, more than five hundred meters carved through solid bedrock beneath the City of David, where archaeologists uncovered the ancient Hebrew Siloam Inscription vividly recording the instant two tunneling crews digging from opposite ends heard each other's pickaxes meet. Second is Sennacherib's Prism from Assyria: in his royal annals of 701 BC, Sennacherib boasts of conquering forty-six walled Judean towns and trapping Hezekiah inside Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage"—yet tellingly never claims to have captured Jerusalem itself. Naturally, Assyrian propaganda left out its disastrous army losses, while Babylonian and Assyrian records likewise confirm Sennacherib's assassination by his sons inside a temple years later (681 BC).

**The angel striking down the Assyrian camp.** Verse 32:21 summarizes briefly that the LORD sent an angel to annihilate Assyria's warriors and commanders, whereas 2 Kings records 185,000 dead in one night (2 Kings 19:35). Modern readers rightly feel the gravity of such massive sudden loss of life. Within Scripture, this stands as direct divine defense against a brutal empire that had openly blasphemed God and marched to wipe out Jerusalem. Historians and commentators have long noted that God may have acted through a sudden catastrophic plague ripping through the camp (echoed in Herodotus's legend of field mice gnawing Assyrian bowstrings and in Josephus's reference to pestilence), yet the biblical narrator cares less about physical mechanism than one unmistakable reality: Jerusalem survived solely by the intervention of God.

**What does "God left him to test him" (32:31) mean?** Readers sometimes worry whether verse 31 implies God tempts faithful servants into sin. James states flatly that God tempts no one to do evil. Rather, 32:31 describes God temporarily withholding special prompting or restraint so Hezekiah acted out what was genuinely sitting in his heart when Babylonian flattery arrived. Not that God lacked knowledge of his heart, but letting latent pride surface exposed Hezekiah's hidden weakness so both king and nation could see their need for humility.

**Chronological order in the second half of the chapter.** Reading straight from verse 23 to verse 24 makes it sound as though Hezekiah's sickness and Babylonian visitors occurred *after* Assyria's rout. Comparing 2 Kings 20:6 and Isaiah 38:6—where God promises Hezekiah fifteen more years *and* promises that He "will deliver" Jerusalem from Assyria—many scholars conclude that his illness and the Babylonian delegation (likely seeking anti-Assyrian allies) occurred shortly before or around the 701 BC crisis. The Chronicler arranges episodes thematically rather than strictly chronologically to close Hezekiah's life on the dual lesson of pride and humility.

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, recounts Sennacherib's invasion and quotes the ancient Babylonian historian Berossus to explain the calamity inside the Assyrian camp. According to that source, God sent a deadly pestilence upon the Assyrian army on the very first night of the siege, sweeping away one hundred and eighty-five thousand soldiers and officers and forcing Sennacherib to rush home to Nineveh. **Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, draws three enduring practical lessons from chapter 32. First, from the opening "After all that Hezekiah had so faithfully done," doing good never exempts us from trials, yet equips us to stand steady when trials hit. Second, watching Hezekiah stop springs, mend walls, and forge weapons while summoning Judah to rely on God, Henry remarks that prudent use of lawful means never clashes with faith so long as our heart leans on God rather than our tools.
