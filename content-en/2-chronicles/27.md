# 2 Chronicles 27 — Jotham: Growing Strong by Walking Steadily Before God

## Story

After years of administering royal affairs while his father Uzziah lived quarantined with leprosy, Jotham officially took the throne at twenty-five years old and reigned sixteen years in Jerusalem. His mother was Jerushah, daughter of Zadok—a name deeply rooted in Jerusalem's priestly line (27:1).

The opening assessment of Jotham both echoes his father's reign and draws one crucial contrast: he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD just as his father Uzziah had done, "unlike him, however, he did not enter the temple of the LORD. Yet the people continued their corrupt practices." In short, Jotham steered completely clear of his father's fatal intrusion at the incense altar, though spiritually the general population beneath him kept drifting downward (27:2).

Jotham carried forward his father's ambitious building legacy. He rebuilt the Upper Gate of the house of the LORD and carried out extensive construction along the wall of Ophel—the strategic ridge linking the City of David up toward the temple mount. He also constructed fortified towns across the hill country of Judah and erected forts and watchtowers throughout the wooded uplands to safeguard the realm (27:3–4).

On the battlefield, Jotham marched against the king of the Ammonites and won a decisive victory. That very year the Ammonites paid Judah an enormous tribute of one hundred talents of silver, ten thousand cors of wheat, and ten thousand cors of barley—and repeated the exact same payment in both the second and third years. Scripture distills the secret of this compact yet remarkably solid reign into one memorable sentence: "So Jotham grew powerful because he ordered his ways before the LORD his God" (27:5–6).

The rest of Jotham's achievements, wars, and policies are recorded in the Book of the Kings of Israel and Judah. The text notes once more that he was twenty-five when he began to reign and ruled sixteen years in Jerusalem. At the end, Jotham rested with his fathers and received an honorable burial right inside the City of David—spared the separate graves of his father and earlier apostate kings. His son Ahaz succeeded him on the throne (27:7–9).

## Key Takeaways

**Learning from the previous generation's stumble is rare wisdom.** Jotham grew up watching a gifted father end his days quarantined in isolation over a single proud trespass into the sanctuary. Taking power himself, Jotham kept everything noble from his father's reign—devotion, building vision, national defense—yet firmly refused to cross the sacred boundary his father violated (27:2–4). Refusing to repeat a predecessor's mistake while wielding the exact same power and success is never easy.

**True, lasting strength grows out of ordering our path steadily before God.** In chapter 26, growing strong proved the trap that toppled Uzziah. Here in chapter 27, Jotham likewise "grew powerful," yet Scripture names the bedrock underneath his strength: "because he ordered his ways before the LORD his God" (27:6). When a person steadily aligns their daily direction under God's gaze rather than chasing applause or pride, success does not ruin them.

**An upright leader cannot automatically transform the hearts of an entire nation.** Even while Jotham did right and built up the temple gate, Scripture slips in a somber line right at verse 2: "Yet the people continued their corrupt practices" (27:2). A good ruler's example can hold a nation steady for a season, yet faith cannot be legislated into human souls. Where ordinary people refuse genuine repentance, hidden decay simmers beneath the surface, ready to explode the moment the next ruler takes the stage.

**A quiet, steadfast life carries immense worth before God.** Spanning barely nine verses—the briefest royal biography in this stretch of Chronicles—chapter 27 boasts no dramatic miracles and no famous speeches. Yet Jotham stands out as the lone monarch in a four-king stretch (Joash, Amaziah, Uzziah, Jotham) who remained faithful from start to finish without a single late-life collapse, departing in peace and honor (27:9).

## Deeper Meaning

**A fuller picture emerges alongside the prophets of his day.** Three major Old Testament prophets—Isaiah, Hosea, and Micah—open their books by locating their ministries across the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah (Isaiah 1:1; Hosea 1:1; Micah 1:1). Reading Isaiah's and Micah's searing rebukes against bribery, exploiting the poor, and empty religious display immediately unlocks the quiet line "Yet the people continued their corrupt practices" (27:2). Outwardly, Jotham's Judah looked prosperous and secure, fortified with towers and overflowing with tribute grain; inwardly, society's soul was quietly rotting.

**Steadfastly setting one's heart on God—a golden thread through Chronicles.** The phrase "ordered his ways before the LORD" in verse 6 renders a core Hebrew verb meaning to *establish*, *fix firmly*, or *direct steadily* (27:6). It is the exact yardstick the Chronicler repeatedly applies to measure kings and people alike: Rehoboam did evil because he did not "set his heart to seek the LORD" (12:14); Jehoshaphat was commended because he "set his heart to seek God" (19:3); while the people in Jehoshaphat's day fell short because they had not "set their hearts on the God of their fathers" (20:33). Faithfulness to God is not an occasional emotional burst, but a steady resolve anchoring every step to His path.

**The wall of Ophel connects generations defending the holy city.** Jotham's work on the temple's Upper Gate and the wall of Ophel (27:3) belongs to a multi-century labor of love. Later, when King Manasseh repented after brief captivity and returned to God, one of his restoration works was raising the wall of Ophel to great height (33:14). Centuries later still, when Jewish exiles returned from Babylon to rebuild ruined Jerusalem under Nehemiah, temple servants lived along and repaired that exact same Ophel ridge (Nehemiah 3:26–27). Quiet investments made to strengthen God's house and city leave a legacy for generations yet to come.

**Storm clouds already gathering despite a peaceful close.** 2 Chronicles ends Jotham's reign in undisturbed calm, recording not a single military setback. 2 Kings, however, lets slip a revealing glimpse: during Jotham's closing years, the LORD had already begun sending King Rezin of Aram and King Pekah of Israel against Judah (2 Kings 15:37). The Chronicler holds that invasion crisis back for chapter 28 to sharpen the contrast between righteous father and apostate son Ahaz—while reminding us that the storm brewed by the people's hidden corruption was already dark on the horizon before Jotham died.

## Notes & Nuances

**Reading "he did not enter the temple of the LORD" (27:2) accurately.** Pulled out of context, that line could sound like Jotham grew cold and stopped attending worship. Placed immediately after chapter 26's story of Uzziah barging into the Holy Place to burn incense and being struck with leprosy (26:16–19), however, almost all interpreters recognize a clear compliment: Jotham followed his father's virtues while refusing to repeat his father's unlawful trespass into priestly duties. The very next verse showing him rebuilding the Upper Gate of the temple (27:3) confirms his genuine reverence for God's house. Even so, a few older commentators wondered whether the trauma of his father's leprosy may also have left Jotham unusually cautious around the temple precincts.

**What specifically were the people's "corrupt practices"?** Chapter 27 states only briefly that "the people continued their corrupt practices" without spelling them out (27:2). 2 Kings 15:35 states it plainly: as under prior kings, Jotham did not remove the high places, so ordinary citizens continued sacrificing and burning incense at local hilltop shrines rather than worshiping exclusively at Jerusalem (2 Kings 15:35). In the Chronicler's eyes, that long-tolerated compromise ate away at Judah's spiritual core, paving the way for the full-blown apostasy under Ahaz in the next chapter.

**Why does verse 8 repeat the exact age and reign summary from verse 1?** Attentive readers notice that verse 1 already notes Jotham began reigning at twenty-five and ruled sixteen years in Jerusalem, and verse 8 repeats the identical sentence almost word for word (27:1; 27:8). Scholars suggest this reflects the weaving together of older archival sources or a deliberate biographical frame. Moreover, because Jotham had already managed royal affairs during his father's leprous quarantine (26:21), many biblical chronologists conclude that his sixteen-year figure includes a period of co-regency alongside Uzziah.

**Why Ammonite tribute is recorded for only three years (27:5).** Annual tribute of one hundred talents of silver plus twenty thousand cors of wheat and barley represented immense wealth (a *cor* equaled roughly two hundred liters or several bushels of grain). That Scripture specifies exactly three years leads historians to suggest either that Ammon threw off tribute once Aramean-Israelite pressure mounted on Judah (2 Kings 15:37), or that the treaty explicitly fixed war reparations for a three-year term.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, weighs both angles of verse 2's note that Jotham "did not enter the temple of the LORD." First he applauds Jotham for learning from his father's presumption and refusing to usurp the incense altar; yet he also notes a subtle human quirk worth pondering—fearing a predecessor's overreach can sometimes swing people toward excessive hesitation. Observing the people's persistent corruption despite a good king, Henry remarks that a ruler can model virtue without being able to regenerate hearts, though perhaps Jotham lacked the bold resolve to dismantle the high places. **Sara Japhet**, Israeli Jewish biblical scholar, highlights chapter 27's distinctive place within 2 Chronicles.
