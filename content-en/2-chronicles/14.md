# 2 Chronicles 14 — Ten Years of Peace and King Asa's Prayer

## Story

Following his victory over the northern kingdom, King Abijah passes away and is buried in the City of David. His son Asa ascends the throne. The new reign opens onto a rare oasis of calm amid decades of border strife: for ten straight years, the land lies at peace (14:1).

Asa does what is good and right in the sight of the LORD his God. Refusing to tolerate deeply entrenched idolatry left behind from earlier reigns, he immediately cleans house: removing foreign altars and high places, smashing sacred pillars, and cutting down Asherah poles. Not content merely to tear down what is wrong, he commands Judah to seek the LORD, the God of their ancestors, and to obey His law and commandments. Removing the high places and incense altars from all the towns of Judah, he sees the kingdom rest quietly under his rule (14:2–5).

Asa does not squander ten peaceful years in complacency. Taking advantage of the calm because the LORD has given him rest from war, he builds fortified cities across Judah. Addressing his people, he states his reasoning plainly: let us build up these towns with walls, towers, gates, and bars while the land is still ours, "because we have sought the LORD our God; we sought Him, and He has given us rest on every side." Judah joins in building, and everything prospers (14:6–7).

Alongside sturdy fortresses, Asa builds a disciplined fighting force: 300,000 men of Judah armed with large shields and spears, plus 280,000 men of Benjamin bearing small shields and drawing bows—every one of them a valiant warrior (14:8).

Then a sudden storm sweeps up from the south. Zerah the Cushite invades Judah with a staggering host of 1,000,000 soldiers and 300 chariots, pushing all the way to Mareshah, a gateway fortress in the southwestern foothills. Refusing to cower behind city walls, Asa marches out to meet him, drawing up battle lines in the Valley of Zephathah near Mareshah (14:9–10).

Facing nearly double his troops backed by hundreds of iron chariots, Asa leans neither on fresh walls nor on his 580,000 soldiers. Instead, he cries out to the LORD his God in a brief, unforgettable prayer: "LORD, there is no one besides You to help the powerless against the mighty. Help us, O LORD our God, for we rely on You, and in Your name we have come against this multitude. O LORD, You are our God. Do not let a mere mortal prevail against You!" (14:11).

Instantly the LORD strikes down the Cushites before Asa and Judah. The invaders break and flee in disarray. Asa and his army pursue them all the way to Gerar on the southwestern frontier; so many Cushites fall that they cannot recover, "crushed before the LORD and His army." Because the terror of the LORD falls over the towns surrounding Gerar, Judah strikes those towns as well, gathering immense plunder, demolishing livestock enclosures, and driving back vast herds of sheep and camels in triumph to Jerusalem (14:12–15).

## Key Takeaways

**True reform takes two steps: uprooting wrong and planting right.** Asa did not stop at smashing sacred pillars, felling Asherah poles, and dismantling pagan altars; he immediately directed Judah to seek the LORD and obey His law (14:3–4). Pulling weeds without sowing healthy seed only leaves the soil wide open for weeds to return.

**Peacetime is God's gift to prepare for coming storms.** Instead of idling lazily through ten quiet years, Asa built walls, towers, gates, and trained soldiers (14:6–8). Better still, he never credited his masonry for their safety, telling the nation openly that peace on every side existed solely because they had sought and found the LORD (14:7).

**Careful preparation never replaces radical dependence on God.** Asa had fulfilled every duty of a prudent king, yet staring across the valley at a million troops and three hundred chariots made walls and soldiers suddenly look tiny (14:8–9). Faced with impossible odds, he never disguised his helplessness—he laid it squarely before God, trusting that no one else can help the powerless overcome the mighty (14:11).

**Deep prayer binds our cause to God's own honor.** Asa does not merely beg for Judah's skin. He pleads, "in Your name we have come against this multitude... Do not let a mere mortal prevail against You!" (14:11). When we walk into adversity standing for truth and in God's name, the battle is no longer ours to shoulder alone.

## Deeper Meaning

**Leading the nation straight back to Moses' original command.** Asa's sweeping purge—tearing down foreign altars, shattering pillars, cutting down Asherah poles (14:3)—follows almost word for word God's original charge to Israel before entering Canaan (Deuteronomy 7:5). In Chronicles' eyes, a great king does not invent flashy religious trends; he has the courage to bring God's people back to the foundational covenant.

**Rest echoing the golden promise to David and Solomon.** Describing how the LORD gave "rest" and "peace on every side" (14:6–7) connects straight back to God's pledge over Solomon's peaceful reign (1 Chronicles 22:9; 22:18). Chronicles assures readers that such blessing did not die with a bygone golden age: whenever a new generation humbles itself to seek God according to the temple promise (7:14), rest on every side still opens before them.

**Echoes of Jonathan, David, and the Psalms.** Asa's prayer (14:11) joins one proud chorus of Old Testament faith. Before him, Jonathan trusted that nothing can hinder the LORD from saving by many or by few (1 Samuel 14:6), and David faced Goliath without armor coming "in the name of the LORD of Hosts" (1 Samuel 17:45–47). The plea "Do not let a mere mortal prevail" echoes the Psalms' refusal to trust chariots and horses, trusting instead in God's name so mortal arrogance cannot triumph (Psalms 9:19–20; 20:7).

**A shining triumph turning into a haunting reminder later.** Soberingly, just two chapters ahead, facing threat from northern Israel, this exact same Asa forgets the miracle at Mareshah and raids the temple treasury to bribe Aram for rescue. Hanani the seer will summon this very Cushite-Libyan triumph to rebuke him (16:7–9). Yesterday's towering faith never carries tomorrow's battles automatically; reliance on God must be renewed all the way to the finish line.

**God's power perfected in human weakness.** Confessing that "there is no one besides You to help the powerless against the mighty" (14:11) expresses a timeless biblical law reaching straight into the New Testament. Centuries later, when Paul pleaded under a burden far past his strength, Christ answered that His grace was sufficient, for His power is perfected in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9–10). Right where human self-sufficiency runs dry, God's power shines brightest.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Asa truly remove all the high places?** Verses 14:3 and 14:5 report Asa removing high places across Judah's towns, yet the very next chapter (15:17) and 1 Kings (1 Kings 15:14) state the high places were not removed. Commentators commonly note three harmonizing explanations: first, chapter 14 describes purging pagan idolatrous shrines, while lingering local hills where citizens kept sacrificing to the LORD outside Jerusalem survived; second, verse 15:17 specifically notes high places were not removed from "Israel"—newly captured northern territory—whereas chapter 14 specifies Judah's towns; third, royal edicts cleared them out initially, yet deeply ingrained rural habits quietly rebuilt them in outlying villages. All three point to one lasting truth: signing reform decrees is always easier than uprooting old habits in people's hearts.

**Who was Zerah the Cushite, and how should we read "a million" troops?** *Cush* refers to Nubia south of Egypt (modern Sudan), though some researchers also note a related southern Arabian tribe. Many historians view Zerah as a Cushite general leading an Egyptian-backed expedition or mercenary army—echoing Shishak's force of Cushites and Libyans under Rehoboam (12:3; cf. 16:8)—since the spoils around Gerar included nomadic flocks and camels near the southwestern desert frontier (14:15; Genesis 20:1). As for Asa's 580,000 soldiers and Zerah's 1,000,000 (14:8–9), as in chapter 13, readers weigh three familiar options: literal numbers, understanding Hebrew *eleph* ("thousand") as a smaller military squad or unit, or ancient epic scale highlighting impossible odds overcome by God.

**Pursuit and plundering towns around Gerar.** The chapter's closing lines describing massive enemy losses, striking towns around Gerar, smashing livestock camps, and hauling back sheep and camels (14:13–15) feel stark to modern sensibilities. Historically, Gerar and neighboring southwestern frontier towns likely served as supply bases or allies supporting an invasion pushing up from Egypt toward Mareshah—one of Rehoboam's own southwest fortresses (11:8)—so Asa neutralized the staging ground for future raids. Scripture neither glosses over ancient wartime severity nor glorifies greed, stressing instead that victory came because "the terror of the LORD" fell upon those towns (14:14; cf. 17:10).

**Why is this dramatic battle absent from 1 Kings?** First Kings summarizes Asa's early reforms in just a few swift verses (1 Kings 15:9–15) without mentioning ten years of peace, fortress construction, or Zerah's invasion. Second Chronicles includes the full episode to frame two starkly contrasting halves of Asa's reign: seeking and relying on God in chapters 14–15 brings peace and miraculous deliverance; abandoning God for Aram in chapter 16 shatters that peace instantly.
