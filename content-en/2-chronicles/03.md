# 2 Chronicles 3 — Building the Temple on Mount Moriah

## Story

On the second day of the second month in the fourth year of Solomon's reign, construction of the house of the LORD formally begins in Jerusalem. The chosen site carries the deepest layers of Israel's history: on Mount Moriah, right at the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite—the very spot where the LORD had appeared to David and where David had prepared the ground for the temple (3:1–2).

Measured "by the old standard," the temple's foundation stretches sixty cubits long and twenty cubits wide. Across the front stands a portico twenty cubits wide—matching the temple's width—and rising 120 cubits high, overlaid inside with pure gold (3:3–4).

The main hall inside is paneled with cypress wood, overlaid with fine gold, and embossed with palm trees and ornamental chains. Solomon adorns the house with precious stones for beauty, using prized gold from Parvaim. Every beam, threshold, wall, and door gleams with gold overlay, while cherubim are carved across the walls (3:5–7).

Deeper inside lies the Most Holy Place, a perfect square twenty cubits long and twenty cubits wide, overlaid completely with 600 talents of fine gold. Even the gold nails weigh fifty shekels, and the upper rooms above are likewise overlaid with gold (3:8–9).

Inside the Most Holy Place, Solomon crafts two sculpted cherubim and overlays them with gold. Their four outstretched wings span the full twenty-cubit width of the room: each wing measures five cubits, with the two outer wings touching opposite walls and the two inner wings meeting wingtip to wingtip at the center. Both cherubim stand upright on their feet, facing outward toward the main hall. Separating the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place hangs a curtain woven of blue, purple, and crimson yarn and fine linen, embroidered with cherubim (3:10–14).

Out in front of the temple, Solomon erects two massive pillars thirty-five cubits high, each crowned with a five-cubit capital. He fashions ornamental chains like those in the inner sanctuary atop the capitals and sets a hundred pomegranates along the chains. Standing sentinel before the facade, the right pillar receives the name Jachin, and the left pillar receives the name Boaz (3:15–17).

## Key Takeaways

**The temple rises on ground consecrated by obedience and mercy.** The opening verse explicitly identifies this hill as Mount Moriah and Ornan's threshing floor (3:1). God's house rests not on a trophy field of military conquest or royal vanity, but right where an ancestor once obeyed God to the uttermost and where God once halted judgment to spare His people.

**Every proportion and material draws the eye toward holiness.** Stepping deeper inward, the sanctuary grows richer and more solemn: from the outer portico, through the main hall carved with palms and cherubim, into the square Most Holy Place gleaming with 600 talents of fine gold (3:4–9). Even hidden details like gold nails and upper chambers receive meticulous care, because this house is built first of all for the eyes of God.

**The veil and cherubim remind humanity of the boundary before the Holy One.** Two giant cherubim spread wings across the entire inner sanctuary, while a thick cherubim-embroidered curtain hangs across the entrance (3:10–14). Though God delights to dwell among His people, sinful humans cannot stride casually into His holy presence without the way God Himself opens.

**Two entrance pillars preach who truly holds the temple up.** Before anyone crosses the threshold, two towering bronze pillars greet them with speaking names: *Jachin* meaning *He establishes*, and *Boaz* meaning *in Him is strength* (3:17). Together they remind king and citizen alike that neither temple nor kingdom stands by thick walls or gleaming gold, but solely by the sustaining power of God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Abraham, David, and Christ converge on the exact same ridge.** This is the single place in all Scripture explicitly identifying Jerusalem's temple mount with Mount Moriah—where Abraham once led his beloved only son Isaac up for burnt offering and God Himself provided a substitute ram (Genesis 22:2). On this very ridge, at Ornan's threshing floor, David built an altar while plague swept Israel, fire fell from heaven accepting his sacrifice, and the angel sheathed his sword (1 Chronicles 21:18; 21:26–28; 22:1). Every sacrifice offered on this hill across the centuries pointed ahead to the complete sacrifice of Jesus, who was crucified right outside this same city of Jerusalem.

**A miniature Garden of Eden restored in the holy city.** Inside the temple, wooden panels bloom with carved palm trees, hanging pomegranates, and guardian cherubim (3:5; 3:7; 3:16). That imagery recalls Eden in the beginning, where God walked with humanity and stationed cherubim guarding the way back to the tree of life after humanity's fall (Genesis 3:24). The temple stands as a living sign that God has not abandoned humanity; He is reopening a meeting place where God and people dwell together again.

**The dividing curtain and the hour it tore in two.** The curtain of blue, purple, and crimson yarn and fine linen worked with cherubim (3:14) reproduces the exact veil screening the Most Holy Place in Moses' tabernacle (Exodus 26:31–33). At the very instant Jesus breathed His last on the cross, the temple curtain tore in two from top to bottom (Matthew 27:51). Hebrews explains that through His body given in death, Jesus opened a new and living way straight through the curtain so believers may enter the Most Holy Place with confidence (Hebrews 10:19–20).

**A perfect cube anticipating the New Jerusalem.** The Most Holy Place forms a spotless cube measuring twenty cubits along every edge (3:8; cf. 1 Kings 6:20). On the Bible's closing pages, the New Jerusalem descending out of heaven bears that exact cubic geometry—its length, width, and height all equal (Revelation 21:16). No small screened inner chamber is needed any longer, because the entire city where God dwells with His people has become one vast Most Holy Place.

**From bronze pillars to God's pledge to the faithful.** Twin pillars—Jachin and Boaz—stood immovable before Solomon's sanctuary (3:17). In Revelation, Jesus draws on that very emblem to promise every faithful overcomer: "I will make him a pillar in the temple of My God, and he will never again leave it" (Revelation 3:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 3 moves faster and with a distinct focus compared with 1 Kings 6–7.** First Kings describes outer side rooms, silent quarry dressing without hammer or chisel on site, and dates groundbreaking to "the 480th year after the Israelites had come out of the land of Egypt" (1 Kings 6:1). Second Chronicles 3 trims exterior masonry details to rivet attention on Mount Moriah, the pervasive gold overlay, the twin cherubim, and the curtain—a curtain never even mentioned in 1 Kings 6. Moreover, in Chronicles the whole architectural plan flows straight from the Spirit-given pattern David handed to his son (1 Chronicles 28:11–12).

**Two striking dimensional differences compared with 1 Kings.** Verse 3:4 records the portico rising 120 cubits high—roughly 180 feet (55 meters), four times the 30-cubit height of the main temple building (1 Kings 6:2). Verse 3:15 records the two pillars standing 35 cubits high, whereas 1 Kings 7:15 states each pillar measured 18 cubits. Regarding the portico height, scholars note that several ancient witnesses (including the Syriac and some Greek manuscripts) read 20 cubits, strongly suggesting an early scribal slip in numerals, though some readers still envision a towering entrance pylon common in ancient Near Eastern temples. As for the 35-cubit pillars, commentators often explain it as the combined length of both 18-cubit pillars cast together (minus the socket joint at the capitals), or another early numerical copying variant.

**What does "by the old standard" (3:3) mean?** The author specifies measuring the foundation "by the old standard"—the older royal cubit of roughly twenty inches (a forearm plus a handbreadth), longer than the standard cubit common in the post-exilic era. That small footnote shows the Chronicler addressing readers living centuries after Solomon, translating older measurements so they can grasp the first temple's true scale.

**Gold of Parvaim and 600 talents of gold.** The place name *Parvaim* (3:6) appears nowhere else in the Bible; researchers remain uncertain whether it lay in Arabia or elsewhere, knowing only that it denoted finest-grade ancient gold. Six hundred talents of gold overlaying the Most Holy Place—roughly twenty metric tons (3:8)—is an immense quantity reflecting years of David's stockpiling and Solomon's unstinting devotion to the nation's holiest building.
