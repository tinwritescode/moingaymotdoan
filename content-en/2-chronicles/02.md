# 2 Chronicles 2 — Temple Preparations and Letters with the King of Tyre

## Story

Firmly established on his throne and enjoying God-given prosperity, Solomon turns immediately to his life's defining mission: he resolves to build "a temple for the Name of the LORD and a royal palace for himself." Right away he conscripts 70,000 carriers, 80,000 stonecutters in the hills, and 3,600 foremen to oversee them (2:1–2).

Needing prized timber and master craftsmen, Solomon sends word to Hiram, king of Tyre—who had dealt warmly with David and supplied cedar for his palace—asking Hiram to partner with him the same way. In his letter, Solomon spells out the temple's purpose clearly: consecrated to the LORD for burning fragrant incense, setting out the perpetual showbread, and offering morning and evening burnt sacrifices along with Sabbaths, New Moons, and Israel's appointed feasts forever. The temple must be grand because "our God is greater than all other gods," yet in the very next breath Solomon confesses humbly: "But who is able to build a temple for Him, since the heavens, even the highest heavens, cannot contain Him? Who then am I, that I should build a temple for Him, except as a place to burn incense before Him?" (2:3–6).

Next, Solomon asks Tyre's king to send a master artisan skilled in gold, silver, bronze, iron, purple, crimson, and blue yarn, and fine engraving, to work alongside the skilled craftsmen in Judah and Jerusalem whom David had already gathered. He also requests cedar, cypress, and algum timber from the forests of Lebanon, promising his own servants will assist Tyre's loggers. In generous payment, Solomon pledges to supply the woodsmen 20,000 cors of ground wheat, 20,000 cors of barley, 20,000 baths of wine, and 20,000 baths of olive oil (2:7–10).

King Hiram replies in writing with remarkable warmth. Astonishingly, this foreign monarch lifts his voice to bless Israel's God: "Because the LORD loves His people, He has made you their king." He adds, "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who made heaven and earth!" thanking God for giving David a wise son endowed with insight and understanding to build a temple for the LORD and a palace for himself (2:11–12).

Hiram promptly dispatches a gifted master craftsman named Huram-abi, born to a mother from the tribe of Dan and a father from Tyre. Skilled across every medium—gold, silver, bronze, iron, stone, wood, purple, blue, and crimson yarn, fine linen, and intricate engraving—he comes ready to partner with David's craftsmen. Accepting Solomon's provisions of wheat, barley, oil, and wine, Hiram promises to cut all the timber needed from Lebanon, float it as rafts down the coast to Joppa, and let Solomon haul it up to Jerusalem (2:13–16).

Closing the chapter, the author explains the huge workforce noted at the beginning: Solomon conducts a census of all resident foreigners living in Israel following the registration his father David had carried out (cf. 1 Chronicles 22:2), numbering 153,600 men. Of these he assigns 70,000 as carriers, 80,000 as stonecutters in the hills, and 3,600 as foremen to supervise the labor (2:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Build our finest for God, yet never imagine we can box Him in.** Solomon spares no expense to construct a sanctuary worthy of the God above all gods (2:5). Yet in the very next sentence he grounds himself: even the highest heavens cannot contain Him (2:6). Authentic worship holds those two truths together—offering God our very best while humbly remembering He dwarfs every building human hands can raise.

**Solomon weaves witness to God right into a diplomatic letter.** A message to Tyre's king could easily have stayed purely transactional—timber prices and shipping dates. Instead, Solomon speaks plainly of the LORD's name, His greatness, and Israel's daily worship (2:4–6). That candid witness prompts a foreign king to bless the God of Israel as Maker of heaven and earth (2:11–12).

**God's work flourishes through wide collaboration and diverse gifts.** Israel brought rich harvests and covenant worship; Tyre brought Lebanon's forests, seafaring skill, and master metallurgy. Solomon gladly partnered with his neighbor and paid the loggers generously and fairly (2:8–10; 2:16).

**Great achievements grow out of the quiet groundwork of an earlier generation.** Twice in this chapter, both Solomon and Hiram refer back to the skilled artisans David had already prepared (2:7; 2:14), and the labor census builds directly on David's earlier survey (2:17; cf. 1 Chronicles 22:14–16). David never laid a temple wall himself, yet his years of faithful preparation live inside every stone his son sets in place.

## Deeper Meaning

**The temple is a sign of God's presence, never a cage for the Creator.** Solomon's humble confession (2:6) will ring out again on the day he dedicates the finished temple (6:18). Centuries later, Isaiah declares the same truth: heaven is God's throne and earth His footstool; what God looks upon is not stone masonry, but the humble and contrite person who trembles at His word (Isaiah 66:1–2). In the New Testament, Stephen cites those exact words before the Sanhedrin to shatter the habit of trapping God inside a house made by human hands (Acts 7:48–49).

**Huram-abi continues the legacy of Bezalel and Oholiab.** The catalog of Huram-abi's crafts—gold, silver, bronze, iron, stone, wood, purple, blue, crimson yarn, fine linen, and engraving (2:14)—mirrors astonishingly the gifts God poured upon Bezalel and Oholiab to build the wilderness tabernacle (Exodus 35:30–35). Huram-abi's mother even hails from Dan, the very tribe of Oholiab (Exodus 35:34). The Chronicler unmistakably binds Jerusalem's temple straight back to Moses' tabernacle.

**A preview of all nations helping build God's house.** Right from the planning stage, Israel's sanctuary bears an international stamp: a foreign monarch blesses the LORD, Lebanon's cedars float down the sea, and master builder Huram-abi carries mixed Israelite and Tyrian ancestry (2:12–16). That glimpse widens into the grand New Testament vision where Jews and Gentiles alike—no longer strangers—are built as "living stones" upon Christ the cornerstone into a dwelling where God lives by His Spirit (Ephesians 2:19–22; 1 Peter 2:5).

**Wise leadership is an expression of God's love for His people.** Hiram's remark deserves lingering reflection: "Because the LORD loves His people, He has made you their king" (2:11). A throne exists not for a ruler's private comfort, but as God's instrument for blessing His people. That truth reaches its summit in Jesus, the true King sent by God out of love for the whole world.

## Notes & Nuances

**Conscripting over 150,000 resident foreigners for forced labor (2:17–18) is hard to read.** These were surviving descendants of ancient Canaanite populations living inside Israel's borders (8:7–8). Placed beside Moses' repeated commands never to mistreat or oppress resident foreigners because Israel themselves had been foreigners in Egypt (Exodus 22:21; Leviticus 19:33–34), mobilizing tens of thousands of conscripted laborers creates genuine moral tension. Scripture reports candidly the real-world politics of an ancient Near Eastern monarchy without turning that practice into a moral standard for future generations.

**Workforce and supervisor counts compared with 1 Kings 5.** First Kings 5:13–14 adds that Solomon also conscripted 30,000 Israelites on rotating shifts in Lebanon, whereas 2 Chronicles 2 mentions only resident foreigners and later insists Israelites were not made forced laborers (8:9). As for foremen, 1 Kings 5:16 lists 3,300 while 2 Chronicles 2:18 lists 3,600. Yet add in the senior overseers—550 in 1 Kings 9:23 versus 250 in 2 Chronicles 8:10—and both books yield the exact same combined total of 3,850 supervisors, differing only in how middle and senior managers are grouped.

**Why is Huram-abi's mother from Dan here, yet from Naphtali in 1 Kings 7:14?** Dan and Naphtali bordered each other closely in Israel's far north, right next to Tyre. Common explanations note she may have been born in Dan, married a Naphtalite first (becoming a widow there), and later married a Tyrian craftsman, or lived in the shared borderland between both tribes. Scholars also observe that the Chronicler welcomed mentioning Dan because it evoked Moses' master craftsman Oholiab of Dan (Exodus 35:34).

**The meaning behind the name Huram-abi.** In Hebrew, the king of Tyre and the master artisan share practically the same name (*Hiram* / *Huram*). The suffix *-abi* literally means *my father*, yet in ancient Near Eastern court language it functioned as an honored title for a master craftsman or senior counselor—so verse 2:13 can be understood as Hiram sending "Huram, my master craftsman."

**How deep was King Hiram's personal faith?** Hiram's blessing of the LORD as Maker of heaven and earth (2:12) sounds deeply reverent. In ancient diplomacy, however, monarchs routinely spoke respectfully of a partner nation's deity to seal goodwill. Whether or not Hiram abandoned Tyre's own gods, the Chronicler gladly records his confession as proof that the LORD's name gained honor beyond Israel's borders.

## What Commentators Say

**Bede**, the eighth-century English monk and scholar, reads the partnership between Solomon and Tyre's king as a prophetic picture of the New Testament Church. Foreign loggers felling Lebanon's cedars and a master craftsman arriving from Tyre to help raise God's house foreshadow the day when Gentiles and Jews join together to build the spiritual temple of Christ. *(On the Temple — De Templo)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: interpreting Tyrian participation and foreign timber as a type of Gentiles joining Jews in building the spiritual temple of the Church runs throughout Bede's De Templo -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses over Solomon's humble confession that even the highest heavens cannot contain God (2:6). The temple's magnificence was never meant to add to God's already infinite glory, but to draw human hearts into reverent awe; even while presenting their grandest offering, mortals must remember how small they stand before the Creator. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 2 Chronicles 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing God's transcendence in 2:6 and humble temple service is classic Matthew Henry on 2 Chronicles 2; exact wording not checked -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, reproduces the correspondence between Solomon and Hiram and claims that copies of those very letters still survived in Tyre's public archives in his own day. He highlights both the warm alliance linking the two courts and the unmatched craftsmanship of the Tyrian master builder overseeing the temple's art. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus famously quotes the Solomon–Hiram letters in Book 8 and asserts the originals remained archived in Tyre during his lifetime -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli biblical scholar, compares Solomon's letter in 2 Chronicles 2 with 1 Kings 5 and spotlights its markedly richer theological depth: instead of a bare timber contract, Solomon explains Israel's daily liturgy and God's supremacy over all gods. She also points out that emphasizing an entirely foreign conscript workforce aligns with the Chronicler's consistent effort to portray Solomon's reign as sparing Israelite citizens from forced labor. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing the theological expansion of Solomon's letter and the emphasis on non-Israelite labor are signature points in Japhet's OTL commentary on chapter 2 -->
