# Ezra 3 — Rebuilding the Altar and Laying the Temple Foundation

## Story

As the seventh month arrives and the returning families settle into their towns, the entire people "assembled as one man in Jerusalem." Leading the way are Jeshua the high priest with his fellow priests and Zerubbabel with his kinsmen. Their very first project is neither personal houses nor defensive city walls, but rebuilding the altar of the God of Israel so they can offer burnt offerings strictly as written in the Law of Moses (3:1–2).

They carry out this work under real tension: "despite their fear of the peoples of the land," the hostile neighbors surrounding them. Right in the face of that fear, they set the altar firmly upon its ancient foundation and resume daily burnt offerings to the LORD, steadily morning and evening (3:3).

During that same seventh month, the people celebrate the Feast of Tabernacles just as the law prescribes, offering the appointed number of sacrifices for each day. From the first day of the seventh month onward, the full cycle of worship restarts unbroken: daily burnt offerings, New Moon sacrifices, every sacred appointed feast, and personal freewill offerings—all unfolding faithfully even though not a single foundation stone of the temple itself has yet been laid (3:4–6).

Alongside daily worship at the altar, the people begin gathering materials for the temple itself. They pay stonecutters and carpenters and send food, wine, and olive oil to Sidon and Tyre so cedar timber from Lebanon can be floated by sea to Joppa, exactly as authorized by King Cyrus (3:7).

In the second month of the second year after returning to Jerusalem, Zerubbabel, Jeshua, the priests, the Levites, and all the returning exiles officially launch temple reconstruction. They appoint Levites twenty years old and above to supervise the work, and the Levite clans step forward "together as one" to direct the laborers rebuilding God's house (3:8–9).

On the day the builders finish laying the temple foundation, a joyous service of thanksgiving breaks out. Priests in vestments hold trumpets and Levites of the family of Asaph raise cymbals, singing responsively just as King David ordained long ago: "For He is good; His loving devotion to Israel endures forever!" All the people erupt with a mighty shout praising the LORD (3:10–11). Yet right in that same moment, many older priests, Levites, and family heads—men who had seen the former temple with their own eyes—weep aloud as they look upon the new foundation. Many others shout for joy at the top of their lungs until nobody can tell the sound of joyful shouting from the sound of weeping—the mingled roar "was heard from afar" (3:12–13).

## Key Takeaways

**In the face of fear, God's people rebuild the altar first.** Standing inside an unwalled city ringed by suspicious neighbors, ordinary human instinct screams to build fortifications first. Instead, Judah sets the altar back upon its old base (3:3). They knew real community security rests never in thick walls and deep moats, but in restoring living fellowship with God.

**Unity and obedience to Scripture pave the way for renewal.** Twice in one chapter the text celebrates harmony: the people gather "as one man" (3:1), and the Levites unite "together as one" (3:9). Hand in hand with unity runs deep reverence for God's word: again and again they act "as it is written" in the Law of Moses and "as prescribed by David" (3:2; 3:4; 3:10). Rather than inventing fresh worship styles on whim, they return straight to God's revealed word.

**Never wait for the finished product to start thanking God.** When trumpets sound, cymbals ring, and crowds roar in praise (3:10–11), only the bare foundation stones are down—no walls, no roof, no gleaming gold interior. Yet they throw a festival celebrating God's steadfast love. Mature faith knows how to rejoice and give thanks even over modest first steps.

**Tears of grief and shouts of hope both have a home before God.** The chapter closes on an unforgettably human scene: younger generations cheer seeing the future open up, while older generations sob remembering everything lost (3:12–13). Scripture neither scolds the elders' tears nor dampens the young people's joy. Painful memories and fresh hope often sound together inside one heart and one community.

## Deeper Meaning

**Picking up the unbroken thread of worship from Moses, David, and Solomon.** Alert readers catch echoes of Israel's golden age in every verse of chapter 3. Morning-and-evening offerings and seventh-month Tabernacles tie straight back to Moses' law (Exodus 29:38–39; Leviticus 23:33–34). Appointing Levites from age twenty repeats King David's ordinance (1 Chronicles 23:24). Buying Lebanon cedars through Tyre and Sidon floated to Joppa and starting work in the second month mirror King Solomon launching the First Temple (2 Chronicles 2:16; 3:2). Returned exiles were declaring boldly: we still belong to that exact same God.

**Singing of God's steadfast love right amid the rubble.** The responsive anthem in verse 11 repeats the beloved refrain of the Psalms and Solomon's temple dedication: God is good; his steadfast love endures forever (Psalms 136:1; 2 Chronicles 5:13). Singing that song inside a gold-plated shrine filled with glory clouds is easy; singing those exact words standing amid ruins after seventy years of exile proves the steel of genuine faith.

**Later prophecy will directly comfort the tears of verse 12.** Over fifteen years later, when construction stalls and hearts cool, the prophet Haggai will ask these very elders who remembered the former temple whether this modest house looks like nothing in their eyes—and then announce God's staggering promise: "The latter glory of this house will be greater than the former" (Haggai 2:3; 2:9). Zechariah will likewise chime in: "Who despises the day of small things?" (Zechariah 4:10). God's eyes watch gladly over humble beginnings.

**A stone foundation pointing to the unshakable Foundation.** Every earthly stone temple eventually crumbles and tempts humans into tearful comparisons. The New Testament reveals one foundation that never shakes: "No one can lay a foundation other than the one already laid, which is Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 3:11). Every life and church stands firm only when built upon him.

## Notes & Nuances

**How should we understand "despite their fear of the peoples of the land" (3:3)?** In Hebrew, the preposition connecting fear with rebuilding the altar supports two rich readings. One reads it causally—*because they feared the surrounding peoples, they set up the altar*—meaning fear drove them straight to seek God's shelter through worship. Many modern translations read it concessively—*despite their fear of the local peoples, they rebuilt the altar*—spotlighting courage triumphing over intimidation. Either nuance delivers the same truth: fear drove them toward God rather than away from him.

**How could elders still remember Solomon's temple?** Babylon burned the First Temple in 586 BC, and this new foundation was laid around 536 BC—just fifty years later. Elders in their sixties and seventies could vividly recall Solomon's majestic sanctuary from childhood or youth.

**Reconciling chapter 3's foundation laying with Sheshbazzar in chapter 5.** Here Zerubbabel and Jeshua lay the foundation (3:8–10), while in chapter 5 Judah's elders tell Persian officials that Sheshbazzar laid it (5:16). Commentators commonly explain that Sheshbazzar, as first governor, presided over an initial ceremonial groundbreaking right upon arrival, or that both leaders collaborated in the early phase before Zerubbabel assumed full leadership.

**Notice the absence of a visible glory cloud.** When Solomon dedicated the First Temple to this exact chorus of praise, a dazzling cloud of God's glory instantly filled the sanctuary (2 Chronicles 5:13). Here in Ezra 3, no visible miracle flashes across the sky. God's people learn to walk forward in quiet faith and obedience rather than depending on dramatic spectacles.
