# Ezra 6 — Darius's Decree and the Dedication of the Temple

## Story

Receiving Governor Tattenai's report, King Darius immediately orders a search of the royal archives in Babylon where state treasures and records are stored. Nothing turns up in Babylon. Only up in the fortress of Ecbatana in the province of Media—the Persian kings' summer palace—do clerks locate a scroll preserving a memorandum from King Cyrus's first year (6:1–2).

The scroll records Cyrus's explicit directive regarding God's house in Jerusalem: let the temple be rebuilt as a place for offering sacrifices, with its foundations firmly laid. Its dimensions are listed as "sixty cubits high and sixty cubits wide," built with "three layers of large stones and one of timber," with all construction expenses funded straight from the royal treasury. Moreover, every gold and silver vessel Nebuchadnezzar had looted from Jerusalem's temple and hauled to Babylon must be returned to its rightful place inside the house of God (6:3–5).

Confronted with clear documentary proof, King Darius dispatches an authoritative decree back to Tattenai, Shethar-bozenai, and their colleagues west of the Euphrates. The king commands bluntly: "Stay away from there!"—do not interfere; leave the governor and elders of Judah alone to rebuild the house of God on its original site (6:6–7).

Going far beyond mere clearance, Darius transforms yesterday's inspectors into today's financial sponsors. He orders the full building costs paid promptly out of royal tax revenues collected west of the Euphrates so work never stalls. Whatever the priests in Jerusalem require day by day for burnt offerings to the God of heaven—young bulls, rams, lambs, wheat, salt, wine, and olive oil—must be supplied reliably without fail. In return, the king asks simply that the priests offer pleasing sacrifices to the God of heaven and "pray for the life of the king and his sons" (6:8–10).

To ensure total compliance, Darius appends a chilling Persian imperial penalty: if anyone alters this edict, pull a beam straight out of his own house, impale him on it, and reduce his house to a heap of rubble. The king even invokes a solemn curse praying that the God who caused his Name to dwell there will overthrow any king or people who lifts a hand to tamper with this decree or destroy God's temple in Jerusalem (6:11–12).

Upon receiving the royal mandate, Tattenai, Shethar-bozenai, and their associates "carried it out with all diligence." Backed by royal authority and buoyed by the preaching of Haggai and Zechariah, Judah's elders press ahead and prosper. The narrator sums up the whole arc in one glowing sentence: they finished building "by the command of the God of Israel and by the decrees of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes, kings of Persia." On the third day of the month of Adar in the sixth year of King Darius—roughly spring of 515 BC—the temple stands complete (6:13–15).

All Israel—priests, Levites, and the entire community of returned exiles—celebrate the temple's dedication with overflowing gladness. They sacrifice 100 bulls, 200 rams, 400 lambs, and notably twelve male goats as a sin offering for all Israel, matching the twelve ancient tribes. Next they organize the priests in their divisions and the Levites in their courses for Jerusalem temple service strictly as written in the Book of Moses (6:16–18).

Barely a month later, on the fourteenth day of the first month, the returned exiles celebrate Passover. Every priest and Levite has purified himself without exception, slaughtering the Passover lamb for all the exiles, for their fellow priests, and for themselves. Sharing in the Passover meal are not only returnees from Babylon, but also all who separated themselves from the uncleanness of neighboring peoples to join Israel in seeking the LORD. For seven joyful days they keep the Feast of Unleavened Bread, because the LORD has filled them with joy and turned the heart of "the king of Assyria" to strengthen their hands in building God's house (6:19–22).

## Key Takeaways

**When God intervenes, would-be obstacles turn into providers.** Tattenai's inspection looked ready to plunge Judah right back into another chapter 4 shutdown. Instead, the archive search unearthed Cyrus's charter, turning the very officials who sent the inquiry into daily tax-funded patrons of the temple (6:8–9).

**God's command outranks every imperial decree.** Summarizing how the temple reached completion, the author places "the command of the God of Israel" first, ahead of the decrees of Persia's three monarchs Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes (6:14). Likewise, the chapter closes praising not royal generosity first, but the LORD who turned the king's heart toward his people (6:22). Emperors wield royal signets, yet God steers history.

**Modest scale never dims genuine joy in worship.** Placed beside Solomon's breathtaking temple dedication with tens of thousands of cattle and sheep (1 Kings 8:63), a few hundred animals here look tiny (6:17). Yet joy bursts off every verse—rejoicing at the dedication (6:16) and celebrating seven straight days at Unleavened Bread (6:22). Real joy flows never from gilded splendor, but from God dwelling once more among his people.

**Unity and purity open the doors of worship.** Though most returnees hailed from Judah, Benjamin, and Levi, offering twelve goats for all twelve tribes declared their solidarity with all Israel (6:17). Before Passover, every priest and Levite purified himself without a single holdout (6:20). And the Passover table swung wide for anyone willing to forsake pagan uncleanness and seek the LORD (6:21).

## Deeper Meaning

**The king's heart rests in the LORD's hand.** Darius's stunning reversal brings Proverbs 21:1 vividly to life: the king's heart is a stream of water in the LORD's hand, turned wherever he wills. Even Darius's description of God—"who has caused His Name to dwell there" (6:12)—echoes the exact covenant phrasing Moses taught Israel centuries earlier (Deuteronomy 12:11).

**Praying for earthly rulers.** Darius underwrote daily sacrifices so priests would pray for the life of the king and his sons (6:10). This continues Jeremiah's counsel to exiles to pray for the peace of the foreign city where they lived (Jeremiah 29:7). In the New Testament, Paul lays the same duty upon the church: offer prayers for kings and all in authority—even pagan rulers—so believers may live peaceful, godly lives (1 Timothy 2:1–2).

**Seventy years complete and a fresh Exodus.** Babylon burned the First Temple in 586 BC. Exactly seventy years later, in 515 BC, the Second Temple stands dedicated—echoing Jeremiah's pledge that God would visit his people when seventy years closed (Jeremiah 29:10). Immediately afterward, Israel keeps Passover on the fourteenth of the first month and seven days of Unleavened Bread just as on Exodus night (Exodus 12:6; 12:15). As in King Josiah's revival (2 Chronicles 35:18), Passover heralds a fresh dawn for God's covenant people.

**A humble light pointing toward greater glory.** Elders who remembered Solomon's splendor might feel this house looked like nothing, just as Haggai candidly asked (Haggai 2:3). Yet Haggai immediately promised that the latter glory of this house would outshine the former, and in this place God would grant peace (Haggai 2:9). Over five hundred years later, Jesus himself walked into the courts of this very Second Temple—and Paul explained that every Passover and unleavened loaf pointed straight to him: Christ our Passover Lamb has been sacrificed, calling us to keep the festival in sincerity and truth (1 Corinthians 5:7–8).

**Faith's doorway open to all who seek God.** Verse 21 demonstrates that Jerusalem's worshiping community was no closed ethnic club defined solely by bloodline or exile papers (6:21). From the start, Moses' law welcomed foreigners joining God's people to share Passover (Exodus 12:48). Here, anyone separating from surrounding uncleanness to seek the LORD sits right at the Passover table—a vital truth to keep in mind before reading the painful marriage crisis in chapters 9 and 10.

## Notes & Nuances

**What is unusual about the temple dimensions in Cyrus's memo?** Verse 3 specifies a height of sixty cubits and a width of sixty cubits, omitting the length (6:3). Solomon's temple measured sixty cubits long, twenty wide, and thirty high (1 Kings 6:2). A temple twice as tall and triple as wide as Solomon's hardly fits Haggai's words about its modest look. Many scholars explain that Cyrus's memorandum set a maximum royal funding ceiling rather than architectural blueprints, or included outer courtyards and side chambers.

**Why does verse 14 name King Artaxerxes?** The temple finished in Darius's sixth year (515 BC), roughly half a century before Artaxerxes took the throne. Grouping "Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes" reflects that the book was compiled during or after Artaxerxes' reign, glancing back over all three Persian kings who aided God's house: Cyrus launched it, Darius funded its completion, and Artaxerxes later sent Ezra with treasury wealth to beautify worship in chapter 7 (6:14).

**The gruesome penalty in verse 11 reflects Persian imperial law, not biblical ethics.** Ripping a beam from a culprit's house to impale him and turning his home into rubble sounds horrific (6:11). Ancient Persian inscriptions confirm impalement was Darius's infamous imperial penalty for rebels and edict-breakers. Ezra quotes the Persian royal decree verbatim to show how fiercely the crown protected the temple, never endorsing such cruelty for God's people.

**Language shift midway through the chapter.** From Ezra 4:8 through 6:18, the text runs in Aramaic—the Persian Empire's international administrative tongue—covering official royal correspondence. Right at verse 19, as the narrative returns to Israel's internal Passover feast, the original text switches smoothly back into Hebrew (6:19).

**Why does verse 22 call Persia's monarch "the king of Assyria" (6:22)?** By then Assyria had fallen nearly a century earlier, its entire territory absorbed by Persia. Because Persian kings inherited the ancient Mesopotamian imperial mantle, writers occasionally used older titles like *king of Assyria* or *king of Babylon*. Spiritually too, the title hints that even the imperial power that once swallowed Israel's ten northern tribes now bends under God's hand to serve his purpose.
