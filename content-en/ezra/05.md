# Ezra 5 — Two Prophets Speak and the Governor's Inspection

## Story

After many long years with the temple site lying idle, the breakthrough comes not from a royal palace decree, but from the word of God. The prophets Haggai and Zechariah son of Iddo rise up and prophesy to the Jews in Judah and Jerusalem in the name of the God of Israel. Stirred awake by God's word, Zerubbabel and Jeshua immediately stand up and resume rebuilding the house of God, with both prophets right beside them supporting the work (5:1–2).

No sooner does construction pick up steam than Tattenai, governor of the province west of the Euphrates, together with Shethar-bozenai and their associates arrive on-site to inspect. They question the Jews on who authorized them to rebuild this temple and finish this structure, and request the names of the men directing the work. Judah's leaders answer candidly (5:3–4).

Right then something remarkable happens: "the eye of their God was on the elders of the Jews," so the inspection team does not halt construction. Instead, they let building proceed uninterrupted while sending a formal inquiry to King Darius so the crown can adjudicate (5:5).

The report from Tattenai, Shethar-bozenai, and their fellow officials opens with a formal greeting of peace and relays with admirable honesty everything they observed in the province of Judah. They report that the Jews are building "the house of the great God" out of massive dressed stones with timber set into the walls, carried out diligently and progressing rapidly in their hands. Asked who authorized and led the project, the Jewish elders gave a straightforward answer (5:6–10).

The elders' reply is quoted right inside the governor's letter. They introduce themselves simply as "the servants of the God of heaven and earth," rebuilding a house originally built generations ago by a great king of Israel. They make zero attempt to cover up why it fell: because their ancestors provoked the God of heaven to anger, he handed them over to King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon, who destroyed the temple and exiled the people. Yet in his first year as king, Cyrus issued a decree to rebuild God's house, returning the gold and silver vessels from Babylon's temple treasury to Governor Sheshbazzar to restore to Jerusalem. Sheshbazzar laid the foundation, and ever since then the work has been under way though not yet finished (5:11–16).

Closing the letter, Tattenai respectfully requests that King Darius search the royal archives in Babylon to verify whether King Cyrus truly issued a decree to rebuild God's house in Jerusalem, and then send back the king's ruling on how to proceed (5:17).

## Key Takeaways

**God's word awakens hands that have dropped to their sides.** For roughly sixteen years the temple sat exposed to wind and rain amid rubble. Judah neither drifted spontaneously back into zeal nor waited around for fresh Persian paperwork. The ringing preaching of Haggai and Zechariah rekindled faith, emboldening leaders and citizens alike to pick up tools even while old legal decrees remained unverified (5:1–2).

**God's protection often works through very ordinary channels.** Verse 5 describes no angel blocking the Persian governor's road—just one quiet line: "the eye of their God was on the elders of the Jews" (5:5). The fruit of God's watchful eye was simply a fair-minded imperial official following proper procedure, refusing to abuse power by shutting down the site and sending an unbiased report to the king.

**Answer earthly authority with honesty and humility.** Standing before an imperial governor, Judah's elders neither hid their names, dodged questions, nor spoke defiantly. They gave God his loftiest title—"the God of heaven and earth"—while claiming for themselves the humblest title: "servants" (5:11). They even openly confessed their ancestors' sin that led to Babylon's conquest (5:12). Transparent candor lent their testimony undeniable credibility.

**God's work deserves excellence and diligence.** Even an outside Persian inspector felt moved to report that Judah was building with massive hewn stones and sturdy timbers, "being carried on with diligence and making rapid progress in their hands" (5:8). Working for God never excuses sloppy half-measures.

## Deeper Meaning

**Two prophetic books lift the curtain on verses 1 and 2.** Ezra 5 spends barely two sentences mentioning Haggai and Zechariah's preaching, yet opening their two biblical books lets us hear their exact sermons from that season. In the sixth month of Darius's second year, Haggai challenged the people straight up to consider their ways, climb the hills for timber, and rebuild God's house for his glory—and God stirred Zerubbabel, Jeshua, and all the remnant with the assurance, "I am with you" (Haggai 1:1; 1:7–8; 1:12–14). Two months later, Zechariah reinforced them with his famous word to Zerubbabel: this work succeeds not by might nor by power, "but by My Spirit," and the same hands that laid the foundation will set the capstone in place (Zechariah 1:1; 4:6–9).

**"The eye of God" shines across all Scripture.** God watching over Judah's elders (5:5) links to a beloved thread running from Old Testament to New. Second Chronicles declares that the eyes of the LORD roam throughout the earth to strengthen those whose hearts are fully committed to him (2 Chronicles 16:9). The Psalms sing that the LORD's eye rests on those who fear him (Psalms 33:18). And in the New Testament, Peter quotes that exact truth to steady believers questioned and suspected inside a pagan world (1 Peter 3:12).

**How to testify before civil authorities.** The elders' poised, respectful, unafraid testimony before Governor Tattenai recalls Jesus preparing his disciples to stand before governors and kings as witnesses (Matthew 10:18–19), along with Peter's charge to stay ready to give a reason for our hope "with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15).

**An honest confession refusing to rewrite history.** Unlike ancient nations that buried defeats or blamed weaker local gods, Judah's elders declared plainly in verse 12 that Babylon conquered Jerusalem solely because the God of heaven handed them over for their own ancestors' sins (5:12). Even through exile, Israel's faith never shrank the LORD into a beaten local deity—he remained Lord of heaven and earth.

## Notes & Nuances

**Tattenai is utterly different from Rehum in chapter 4.** Reading straight from chapter 4 into chapter 5, readers easily lump Tattenai in with Jerusalem's enemies. Actually, Tattenai governed an entire imperial province, and inspecting a major stone building going up without his paperwork on file was standard administrative duty. He filed no slander about rebellion, offered no bribes, made no threats, and never halted work—simply asking the king to verify the royal archives and rule lawfully.

**Why does verse 4 shift pronouns (5:4)?** The original Aramaic manuscript reads literally "then *we* told them the names of the men building this structure." Many modern translations follow the ancient Greek and Syriac versions to read "*They* also asked us for the names of the men constructing this building," harmonizing smoothly with verse 10 inside Tattenai's letter (5:10). Either way, the storyline stands crystal clear: the Persian delegation asked for the leaders' names to include in their report.

**Was Zechariah the "son" or "grandson" of Iddo?** Ezra 5:1 calls Zechariah "son of Iddo," whereas Zechariah 1:1 gives the fuller line "son of Berekiah, the son of Iddo." In Hebrew and Aramaic, *son* routinely serves broadly for *grandson* or *descendant*, especially when a grandfather like Iddo was a widely known priestly family head.

**Does verse 16 ("from that time until now it has been under construction") contradict chapter 4 verse 24?** Commentators point out that the elders were briefly explaining to Persian officials that the building represented lawful continuation of Cyrus's authorized project under Sheshbazzar rather than an illegal new start—and minor site upkeep may well have lingered even while primary construction stood suspended.
