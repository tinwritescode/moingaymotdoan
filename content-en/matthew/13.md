# Matthew 13 — Parables of the Kingdom of Heaven

## Story

That same day Jesus went out and sat beside the sea. Such a large crowd gathered around Him that He got into a boat and sat down while all the people stood on the shore. He taught them many things in parables (13:1–3).

The first parable: "A farmer went out to sow his seed." Some fell along the path, and birds came and ate it up. Some fell on rocky ground, sprang up quickly, yet was scorched by the sun and withered because it had no root. Some fell among thorns, which grew up and choked it. Still other seed fell on good soil and produced a crop—a hundred, sixty, or thirty times what was sown. "He who has ears, let him hear" (13:3–9).

The disciples asked: why do You speak to them in parables? He answered: the knowledge of the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven has been given to you, but not to them. Seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not understand, just as Isaiah foretold, because this people's heart has grown calloused. "But blessed are your eyes because they see, and your ears because they hear." Many prophets and righteous people longed to see what you see and did not see it (13:10–17).

Then He explains the parable: seed along the path is the one who hears the word of the kingdom without understanding, and the evil one snatches it away. Rocky soil is the one who hears and welcomes the word with joy right away, yet has no root; when trouble or persecution comes because of the word, they quickly fall away. Thorny ground is the one who hears, yet the worries of this life and the deceitfulness of wealth choke the word. Good soil is the one who hears, understands, and yields a harvest (13:18–23).

A second parable: the kingdom of heaven is like a man who sowed good seed in his field. While everyone slept, his enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat and slipped away. When the wheat sprouted, the weeds appeared too. The servants asked if he wanted them to pull the weeds. No, the owner replied, lest in pulling the weeds you uproot the wheat with them. Let both grow together until the harvest; then I will tell the harvesters to bundle the weeds to be burned and gather the wheat into my barn (13:24–30).

He tells two shorter parables. The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, yet when it grows it becomes a tree so that birds of the air perch in its branches. The kingdom of heaven is also like leaven that a woman worked into three measures of flour until all of it was leavened (13:31–33). He spoke everything to the crowds in parables, fulfilling the prophet's words: I will open My mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden since the foundation of the world (13:34–35).

Inside the house, the disciples asked Him to explain the parable of the weeds. The sower of good seed is the Son of Man; the field is the world; the good seed are the sons of the kingdom; the weeds are the sons of the evil one; the enemy is the devil; the harvest is the end of the age; the harvesters are angels. On that day the angels will weed out everything that causes sin and all evildoers, and "the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (13:36–43).

Three more parables follow. The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field; when a man found it, he hid it again, and in his joy went and sold all he had and bought that field. The kingdom of heaven is like a merchant seeking fine pearls; finding one of great value, he sold everything he owned and bought it. The kingdom of heaven is also like a dragnet cast into the sea catching fish of every kind; hauled ashore, workers sort the good fish into baskets and throw the bad away. So it will be at the end of the age when angels separate the wicked from the righteous (13:44–50).

He asks: have you understood all these things? Yes, they reply. He tells them: therefore every scribe who has been trained for the kingdom of heaven is like a homeowner who brings out of his storeroom both new treasures and old (13:51–52).

Finishing these parables, He returned to His hometown and taught in their synagogue. People were astounded: where did this man get such wisdom and miraculous power? "Isn't this the carpenter's son?" Isn't His mother Mary, and aren't His brothers James, Joseph, Simon, and Judas, and aren't all His sisters right here among us? And they took offense at Him (13:53–57). He said: a prophet is not without honor except in his own town and in his own home. And He did not do many miracles there because of their unbelief (13:57–58).

## Key Takeaways

**One seed, four kinds of soil.** The sower does not change, and the seed does not change. What differs is the soil (13:3–8). The parable of the sower confronts every listener: what kind of soil is my heart? Is God's word snatched away, scorched, choked—or received to bear fruit?

**God's kingdom starts small and finishes vast.** A tiny mustard seed becomes a tree sheltering birds; a pinch of yeast transforms a huge batch of dough (13:31–33). When Jesus spoke, the kingdom looked like an unimpressive handful of disciples. Never judge God's kingdom by its small beginnings.

**Good and evil grow side by side until harvest.** The owner forbids uprooting the weeds early lest wheat be torn up with them (13:29–30). Final sorting belongs to God, in God's own timing. We are called to patience, not to frantic crusades trying to scrub the world clean ourselves.

**The kingdom is worth more than everything else.** Both the man who stumbles on hidden treasure and the merchant hunting fine pearls sell everything they have to possess it (13:44–46). Notice that the first man sells "in his joy." Giving things up for God's kingdom is not grim self-deprivation; it is trading lesser things for the supreme treasure.

**Familiarity breeds contempt.** The townsfolk of Nazareth watched Jesus grow up, knew His mother, and knew His carpentry bench (13:55). That very familiarity blinded them to who He was. Their unbelief cost them the wonders He might have worked among them.

## Deeper Meaning

**Isaiah's word on hardened hearts.** At his commissioning, Isaiah was warned that the people would keep hearing without understanding and seeing without perceiving (Isaiah 6:9–10). Jesus quotes this prophecy to explain why crowds miss the point of His parables (13:14–15). Parables simultaneously open doors to sincere seekers and veil truth from closed hearts.

**God's word never returns empty.** Isaiah compares God's word to rain and snow watering the earth so it sprouts, promising it "will not return to Me empty" (Isaiah 55:10–11). Even though three portions of seed fail, the seed in good soil yields thirty, sixty, and a hundredfold (13:8). The harvest is still abundant.

**A great tree where birds nest.** In Daniel and Ezekiel, a towering tree with birds nesting in its boughs symbolizes a kingdom sheltering the nations (Daniel 4:12; Ezekiel 17:23). By saying the mustard seed grows into a tree where birds perch, Jesus invokes that vision: His kingdom will make room for every nation.

**The righteous shining like the sun.** Jesus' promise in verse 43 echoes Daniel, where the wise "will shine like the brightness of the heavens" (Daniel 12:3). In the end, everything currently tangled together will be brought into clear light.

**Counting everything as loss.** Paul lived out the parables of the treasure and the pearl: he considered every former gain a loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ (Philippians 3:8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Speaking in parables so people fail to understand sounds as if God intentionally hides truth.** Verses 13:13–15 are challenging. Matthew writes "because seeing they do not see," indicating parables respond to an existing refusal to see. Bright sunlight helps healthy eyes see more clearly, yet makes eyes squeezed shut pinch even tighter. Parables are an invitation: whoever truly wants to understand must draw closer and ask, just as the disciples did (13:10; 13:36).

**The field is the world, not the church.** Jesus states plainly that "the field is the world" (13:38). Yet since Augustine's day, many have applied the parable to the church, where genuine and false believers live side by side. Both applications have value, provided nobody uses this parable to oppose all church discipline, since Jesus explicitly teaches church discipline in chapter 18 (18:15–17).

**Is a mustard seed really the smallest seed?** Botanically, smaller seeds exist, such as orchid seeds. Yet for Galilean farmers, mustard seed was the tiniest seed sown in a garden, and "small as a mustard seed" was an everyday proverb. Jesus speaks in the language of His listeners, not as a botany textbook.

**Is leaven always a symbol of evil?** Elsewhere in Scripture leaven represents spreading corruption (16:6; 1 Corinthians 5:6). Here, however, leaven pictures the kingdom of heaven (13:33). The point of comparison is quiet, pervasive influence, not whether something is good or bad.

**In Nazareth, Jesus "did not do many miracles."** Matthew gives the reason: because of their unbelief (13:58). Mark puts it even more starkly: He could not do any mighty work there, except healing a few sick people (Mark 6:5). His power was not somehow drained; rather, He refuses to stage miracles for closed hearts, because His miracles are always invitations to trust.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, drew heavily on the parable of the wheat and weeds in his controversy with the Donatists. The Donatists demanded a spotless church made only of the pure and separated themselves from anyone who had faltered under persecution. Augustine replied that the church on earth is a mixed field; only at the harvest will God sort it out. Trying to yank up weeds prematurely risks tearing out the wheat. *(Anti-Donatist Writings)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine uses this parable against the Donatists; uncertain of the exact treatise and chapter -->

**C. H. Dodd**, a British New Testament scholar, offered the most widely quoted definition of a parable. At its simplest, he wrote, a parable is a metaphor or simile drawn from nature or common life, arresting the hearer by its vividness or strangeness, and leaving the mind in enough doubt about its precise application to tease it into active thought. A parable is not a riddle with one neat answer, but a summons demanding a response. *(The Parables of the Kingdom, 1935)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Dodd's definition of a parable in the opening chapter is very widely cited -->

**Joachim Jeremias**, a German New Testament scholar, called the mustard seed and the leaven "parables of contrast." The focus is not gradual growth, he argued, but the stark contrast between an insignificant beginning and a staggering outcome. Jesus is answering skeptics who ask: can this unimpressive band of followers possibly be the kingdom of God? Yes—just like a mustard seed. *(The Parables of Jesus, 1947)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: contrast between small start and vast finish is Jeremias's signature reading of these two parables -->

**Amy-Jill Levine**, an American Jewish scholar, wrote a book restoring Jesus' parables to their Jewish setting. She pushes back against the claim that mustard was considered a noxious weed whose planting rendered a Jewish garden impure. First-century listeners knew mustard as a useful cultivated plant. She cautions preachers against inventing a caricatured "rigid Judaism" merely as a foil to make Jesus look better. *(Short Stories by Jesus, 2014, chapter on the mustard seed)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Levine refutes the "impure weed" reading of the mustard seed; uncertain of the exact details of her argument -->
