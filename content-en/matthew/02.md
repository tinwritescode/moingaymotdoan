# Matthew 2 — The Magi and King Herod

## Story

Jesus was born in Bethlehem in Judea, during the reign of King Herod. Magi from the east arrived in Jerusalem and asked, "Where is the One who has been born King of the Jews?" They had seen His star in the east and had come to worship Him (2:1–2).

When King Herod heard this, he was disturbed, and all Jerusalem with him. He assembled all the chief priests and scribes of the people and asked them where the Christ was to be born. They answered: in Bethlehem in Judea, for the prophet wrote that out of Bethlehem would come a ruler "who will be the shepherd of My people Israel" (2:3–6). The people who knew Scripture best pointed to the exact spot, yet not one of them set out on the road.

Herod secretly summoned the Magi and found out from them the exact time the star had appeared. Then he sent them to Bethlehem with instructions: go and search carefully for the child, and as soon as you find Him, report back to me so that I too may go and worship Him (2:7–8). Those words were a trap.

The Magi set out, and the star they had seen in the east went ahead of them until it stopped over the place where the child was. When they saw the star, they rejoiced with great delight (2:9–10).

Entering the house, they saw the child with His mother Mary. They fell down and worshiped Him, opened their treasures, and presented Him with gifts: "gold and frankincense and myrrh" (2:11). Warned in a dream not to return to Herod, they went back to their own country by another route (2:12).

After they had gone, an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream: get up, take the child and His mother and flee to Egypt, and stay there until I tell you, because Herod is going to search for the child to kill Him. That very night Joseph took mother and child and left. They stayed in Egypt until Herod died. Matthew sees a prophecy fulfilled here: "Out of Egypt I called My Son" (2:13–15).

Realizing he had been outwitted by the Magi, Herod flew into a rage. He ordered the killing of all the boys in Bethlehem and its vicinity who were two years old and under, in keeping with the time he had learned from the Magi (2:16). Matthew recalls Jeremiah's words: "Rachel weeping for her children, refusing to be comforted, because they are no more" (2:17–18). Here is a new Pharaoh, and a new Moses escaping the king's hand.

Herod died. An angel of the Lord appeared again to Joseph in Egypt: take the child and His mother and return to the land of Israel, for those who were seeking the child's life are dead. Joseph brought mother and child home (2:19–21).

But when he heard that Archelaus, Herod's son, was reigning in Judea in place of his father, Joseph was afraid to go there. Warned in a dream, he withdrew to the district of Galilee and settled in a town called Nazareth. Matthew says this fulfilled what was spoken through the prophets: "He will be called a Nazarene" (2:22–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Those far away come seeking, while those nearby stand still.** The Magi are Gentiles who travel a long road following a star. The chief priests and scribes know Scripture inside out and point straight to Bethlehem, yet none of them goes (2:4–6). Knowing the way without walking it is no better than not knowing.

**There are two ways to meet a king.** The Magi bow down in worship and offer their finest treasures. Herod claims he wants to worship, while his heart plots murder (2:8; 2:16). Jesus still places everyone before those same two choices: bow to Him, or cling to your own throne.

**God protects the child through ordinary means.** There are no flashy miracles here. God uses dreams, an obedient father, and a nighttime flight (2:13–14). He guards His Son through the faithfulness of small, unassuming people.

**God's word goes ahead of every event.** Born in Bethlehem, fleeing to Egypt, weeping in Ramah, settling in Nazareth—Matthew sees Scripture anticipating every step. Even while rulers scheme, God's plan stays right on course.

## Deeper Meaning

**A star from Jacob.** Long ago, Balaam, a foreign diviner, prophesied, "A star will come forth from Jacob" (Numbers 24:17). How striking that the very first people to follow a star to Christ are likewise Gentiles arriving from the east.

**The nations coming to the light.** Isaiah foresaw a day when the nations would stream to the Lord's light, bringing "gold and frankincense" as tribute (Isaiah 60:3; 60:6). The Psalms likewise speak of kings bowing before God's king (Psalms 72:10–11). The Magi are the vanguard of that procession.

**A new Moses.** Pharaoh ordered the slaughter of Hebrew baby boys, and Moses escaped death (Exodus 1:22). Later, God told Moses to return because all the men who sought his life were dead (Exodus 4:19). The angel's words to Joseph nearly echo that sentence verbatim (2:20). Matthew is declaring that the new Deliverer has arrived.

**A Son called out of Egypt.** Hosea spoke of Israel as a nation: God loved Israel as a son and called His son out of Egypt (Hosea 11:1). Matthew applies those words to Jesus (2:15). His point is that Jesus relives the story of His people and fulfills what they failed to accomplish.

**From little Bethlehem.** Micah noted how small Bethlehem was among the clans of Judah, yet out of it would come a ruler (Micah 5:2). God loves to choose small places and small people. Nazareth is the same: people asked, "Can anything good come from Nazareth?" (John 1:46).

## Notes & Nuances

**Three kings, or how many visitors?** Scripture never calls the Magi kings and never says there were three. The number three grew out of the three gifts. The word translated "Magi" or "wise men" does not mean physicians, but scholars of the stars, typically associated with priestly circles in Persia or Babylon.

**They did not arrive at the stable.** Luke tells of shepherds visiting the manger on the night Jesus was born. Matthew says the Magi entered a "house," and Herod killed boys aged two and under (2:11; 2:16). They likely arrived some time later, perhaps more than a year after the birth.

**What was the star?** Some suggest a comet; others propose a planetary conjunction, as the astronomer Kepler calculated. Yet a star that "went ahead of them" and "stopped" over a specific house is hard to explain by ordinary astronomy (2:9). Many readers take it as a special sign given directly by God.

**No other historian records the massacre.** The Jewish historian Josephus catalogs many of Herod's atrocities, including executing his wife and three of his sons, yet never mentions a slaughter of children in Bethlehem. Some question the event on this basis. Others reply that Bethlehem was a tiny village, likely numbering only a few dozen boys at most, and for a tyrant like Herod such an episode was too minor for a historian to notice.

**"Nazarene" does not appear verbatim in the Old Testament.** Matthew writes "the prophets" in the plural, as if summarizing a theme rather than quoting a single line (2:23). Many scholars think he is playing on the Hebrew word for "branch" or "shoot" (*netzer*) growing from Jesse's roots (Isaiah 11:1), or signaling that the Messiah would be despised just as Galileans from Nazareth were.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, argues that this was no ordinary star. It traveled from north to south, shone in broad daylight, appeared and disappeared, and descended low enough to mark a single house. In his view, it was an invisible power taking the appearance of a star. *(Homilies on Matthew, Homily 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the argument that the star was not an ordinary star appears in Homily 6 and is frequently cited -->

**Raymond Brown**, an American Catholic priest and biblical scholar, points out that chapter 2 is woven on the pattern of the Moses story. Jewish tradition of the period even added that Pharaoh had been forewarned of Moses' birth and ordered the slaughter of infants in response. According to Brown, a Jewish audience would recognize this pattern immediately. *(The Birth of the Messiah, 1977)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Brown thoroughly analyzes the Moses pattern and Jewish traditions about Pharaoh in his section on Matthew 2 -->

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, preached many sermons for Epiphany, the feast celebrating the Magi. He said the shepherds represent the Jews who were near, while the Magi represent the Gentiles who were far off. Both come together in Christ, like two walls meeting at the cornerstone. *(Sermons on the Epiphany)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine uses the cornerstone uniting two walls imagery in his Epiphany sermons; uncertain of exact sermon number -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, focuses on the chief priests and scribes. They answered the king's question accurately, yet not one of them went along with the Magi to see for himself. Calvin takes this as a warning to everyone who holds Scripture in their hands: knowing God's word without being moved by it only deepens one's guilt. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Matthew 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin rebukes the priests for knowing without going; uncertain of exact phrasing -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, did not write commentary on Matthew, yet his histories show what sort of man Herod was. He recounts that the king executed his wife Mariamne, killed three of his own sons, and before dying ordered the execution of prominent citizens so the whole nation would mourn. That portrait matches the king of chapter 2. *(Antiquities of the Jews, books 15–17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: these events are recorded by Josephus in books 15 to 17 and are widely cited -->
