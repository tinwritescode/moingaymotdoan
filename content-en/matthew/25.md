# Matthew 25 — The Ten Virgins, the Talents, and the Sheep and Goats

## Story

Jesus continues: the kingdom of heaven will be like ten virgins who took their lamps and went out to meet the bridegroom. Five of them were foolish and five were wise. The foolish took their lamps without bringing extra oil; the wise brought oil in flasks along with their lamps. Since the bridegroom was delayed, all ten grew drowsy and fell asleep (25:1–5).

At midnight the cry rang out, "Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him!" The virgins woke up and trimmed their lamps. The foolish asked the wise to share their oil because their lamps were going out. The wise answered: there may not be enough for both us and you; go to those who sell and buy some for yourselves. While they were away buying oil, the bridegroom arrived; those who were ready went in with him to the wedding banquet, and the door was shut (25:6–10).

Later the other virgins came knocking: Lord, Lord, open the door for us! He replied: truly I tell you, I do not know you. Jesus concludes, "Therefore keep watch, because you do not know the day or the hour" (25:11–13).

A second parable: a man going on a journey summoned his servants and entrusted his property to them. To one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one, each according to his ability, and then he left. The servant who received five talents went at once, traded with them, and gained five more. The one with two gained two more. Yet the one who received one talent dug a hole in the ground and hid his master's money (25:14–18).

After a long time the master returned to settle accounts. The servant who received five talents brought five more; his master praised him: "Well done, good and faithful servant!" You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things; enter into the joy of your master. The servant with two talents received the exact same praise (25:19–23).

Then the man who received one talent came forward: Master, I knew you are a hard man, harvesting where you have not sown and gathering where you have not scattered seed; so I was afraid and hid your talent in the ground; here, take back what is yours. His master answered, "You wicked, lazy servant!" If you knew that, you should at least have deposited my money with the bankers so that on my return I would have received it back with interest. Take the talent from him and give it to the one who has ten. Whoever has will be given more, while whoever does not have will have even what he has taken away. Throw that worthless servant outside into the darkness (25:24–30).

Then He speaks of the day of judgment: when the Son of Man comes in His glory with all the angels, He will sit on His glorious throne. All the nations will be gathered before Him, and He will separate them one from another as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats, placing the sheep on His right and the goats on His left (25:31–33).

The King will say to those on His right: come, you who are blessed by My Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. For I was hungry and you gave Me something to eat; I was thirsty and you gave Me something to drink; I was a stranger and you welcomed Me; I was naked and you clothed Me; I was sick and you looked after Me; I was in prison and you visited Me (25:34–36).

The righteous will ask in surprise: Lord, when did we see You hungry and feed You, or thirsty and give You drink, or a stranger and welcome You, or naked and clothe You, or sick or in prison and visit You? The King will answer: whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of Mine, you did for Me (25:37–40).

Then He will say to those on His left: depart from Me, you who are cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels. For I was hungry, thirsty, a stranger, naked, sick, and in prison, and you did not help Me. They too will ask: when did we see You like that and fail to help You? He will reply: whatever you did not do for one of the least of these, you did not do for Me. They will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life (25:41–46).

## Key Takeaways

**Readiness cannot be borrowed.** All ten virgins carry lamps, walk out to meet the bridegroom, and fall asleep. The lone difference is having oil or not (25:3–4). When the bridegroom arrives, nobody can lean on someone else's preparation.

**The bridegroom is delayed.** Every parable in this chapter involves someone who stays away a long time before returning (25:5; 25:19). Jesus prepares His followers for a long wait, not just a few days.

**What God entrusts is meant to bear fruit.** Each servant receives a different amount according to ability (25:15). The master never demands that the two-talent servant earn as much as the five-talent servant, and both hear identical praise. The condemned servant is not punished for losing money on a venture, but for letting fear paralyze him into doing nothing.

**Christ identifies Himself with the suffering.** In the judgment scene, the King says: I was hungry, I was thirsty, I was a stranger, I was in prison (25:35–36). Serving someone small and vulnerable counts as serving Him directly (25:40).

**The righteous do not realize they are righteous.** Both groups ask in genuine surprise, "When did we see You?" (25:37; 25:44). The praised did not show kindness to rack up points for a reward. They helped simply because compassion had become second nature to them.

## Deeper Meaning

**Helping the poor lends to the Lord.** Proverbs says, "Kindness to the poor is a loan to the LORD" (Proverbs 19:17). Jesus goes even further: it is not only lending to the Lord, it is meeting the Lord face-to-face.

**The fast God chooses.** Isaiah declares that God desires His people "to share your bread with the hungry" and "when you see the naked, to cover him" (Isaiah 58:7). The list of merciful deeds in 25:35–36 directly echoes that prophetic call.

**Sheep and goats.** Ezekiel records God promising to judge "between one sheep and another, and between rams and goats" (Ezekiel 34:17). In Ezekiel, the condemned animals are the strong ones trampling the weak. That exact concern runs through this judgment scene.

**"Why do you persecute Me?"** On the road to Damascus, Jesus did not ask Saul why he was persecuting His followers, but "why do you persecute Me?" (Acts 9:4). Christ unites Himself with His people just as He does in 25:40.

**Welcoming strangers.** Hebrews reminds believers, "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for by so doing some people have entertained angels without knowing it" (Hebrews 13:2). In Matthew 25, those who welcome strangers have welcomed Christ Himself without realizing it.

**Faith without deeds.** James asks what good it does if a brother or sister lacks clothing and food and we merely wish them "keep warm and well fed" (James 2:15–16). This judgment scene illustrates the very same reality: living faith shows itself in tangible care.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who are "the least of these brothers of Mine"?** Many readers take this to mean all poor and suffering people everywhere. Yet throughout Matthew, Jesus' "brothers" specifically refer to His disciples (12:50), and "little ones" refer to vulnerable messengers sent out in His name (10:42). Some commentators therefore read this as the nations being judged by how they treated Christ's impoverished, persecuted followers. Both readings have strong defenders, and the second never lessens our calling to care for all who suffer.

**Were the five wise virgins selfish?** Refusing to share oil sounds ungenerous at first glance (25:9). Yet the parable is not teaching a lesson about sharing physical supplies. Oil represents personal readiness and faith—something nobody can transfer to another person at the last second.

**A talent is not a "skill."** A talent was an enormous sum of money, roughly fifteen to twenty years of a laborer's wages. The English word "talent" meaning natural gift actually grew out of this very parable. Within the story, a talent stands for whatever precious trust God places in our hands—His grace, His word, or any responsibility He assigns.

**Is this teaching salvation by works?** Read in isolation, the sheep-and-goats scene might appear to say people earn heaven by charity. Yet notice the righteous never knew they were serving the King; they were not trying to buy a ticket to heaven. Most commentators understand these merciful deeds as the natural fruit revealing a heart that already belongs to Christ, not a payment purchasing salvation.

**Eternal fire prepared for the devil.** Verse 25:41 is one of the sternest warnings of judgment in Jesus' teaching. Crucially, the kingdom was prepared specifically for the righteous (25:34), whereas the eternal fire was prepared for the devil and his angels—never originally intended for human beings.

## What Commentators Say

**Sulpicius Severus**, late fourth-century biographer of Saint Martin of Tours, recounts how Martin, while still a soldier, encountered a freezing beggar outside the gates of Amiens in dead winter. Martin sliced his military cloak in two with his sword and gave half to the beggar. That night Martin dreamed of Jesus wearing the half-cloak and telling the angels that Martin had clothed Him. **John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, preached that Jesus does not demand heroic feats. He does not say, "I was sick and you cured Me," or "I was in prison and you freed Me"—only "I was sick and you visited Me; I was in prison and you came to Me." Chrysostom uses this to strip away every human excuse. *(Homilies on Matthew, on Matthew 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Chrysostom highlights how modest Christ's demands are in the judgment scene; uncertain of exact homily number and wording -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa in the late fourth century, preached on the ten virgins. Asking what the oil represents, he identifies it as love. The foolish virgins carry lamps—outward good works—yet lack oil because they act for human praise rather than love. And love is the one thing you cannot borrow from someone else. *(Sermons)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine reads the oil as love in his preaching on the ten virgins; uncertain of the exact sermon number -->

**The Talmud**, the collection of rabbinic commentary, preserves a legal rule on safeguarding entrusted money: money is securely guarded only when buried in the ground. A custodian who buries it bears no liability if it goes missing. By that standard, the one-talent servant took the safest, most cautious course available. That makes the master's rebuke all the more jarring: the Master does not want timid preservation, but courageous faithfulness. *(Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 42a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the rule that money is safe only when buried appears in Bava Metzia 42a and is frequently cited by modern commentators linking it to this parable -->

**R. T. France**, a late twentieth-century British New Testament scholar, holds that "the least of these brothers of Mine" in 25:40 refers to Jesus' disciples. Throughout Matthew, "brothers" consistently denotes disciples, meaning the nations are judged by how they receive Christ's vulnerable messengers. France adds, however, that this interpretation never excuses believers from caring for the poor everywhere. *(The Gospel of Matthew, 2007)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident France takes "brothers" as disciples; the added remark about the poor generally is recalled in substance rather than verbatim -->
