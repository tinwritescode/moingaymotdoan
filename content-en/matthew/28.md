# Matthew 28 — He Has Risen, and the Great Commission

## Story

After the Sabbath, at dawn on the first day of the week, Mary Magdalene and the other Mary went to look at the tomb (28:1). They were the last to leave the grave, and the first to come back.

Suddenly there was a violent earthquake, for an angel of the Lord descended from heaven, rolled back the stone, and sat on it. His appearance was like lightning, and his clothes were white as snow. The guards trembled with fear and collapsed like dead men (28:2–4). The stone was rolled away not to let Jesus out, but to let witnesses look in.

The angel spoke to the women: do not be afraid, for I know you are looking for Jesus, who was crucified. "He is not here; He has risen, just as He said!" Come see the place where He lay, then go quickly and tell His disciples that He has risen from the dead and is going ahead of you into Galilee; there you will see Him (28:5–7).

The women hurried away from the tomb, "with fear and great joy," running to tell His disciples. Suddenly Jesus met them and said, "Greetings!" They came up, clasped His feet, and worshiped Him. He told them: do not be afraid; go tell My brothers to head to Galilee, and there they will see Me (28:8–10). He calls the very disciples who deserted Him "My brothers."

While the women were on their way, some of the guards went into the city and reported everything to the chief priests. After meeting with the elders, the priests gave the soldiers a large sum of money and instructed them: say that His disciples came during the night and stole Him away while we were asleep. If this reaches the governor's ears, we will satisfy him and keep you out of trouble. The soldiers took the money and did as they were told, and that story has circulated among the Jews to this day (28:11–15).

The eleven disciples traveled to Galilee, to the mountain Jesus had designated. When they saw Him, they worshiped Him—yet some doubted (28:16–17).

Jesus approached and spoke to them: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me." Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything I have commanded you. "And surely I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (28:18–20). The book opened with Immanuel—God with us—and closes with that very promise.

## Key Takeaways

**He rose, just as He said.** The angel does not merely announce an empty grave; he points back to Christ's own word: "He has risen, just as He said!" (28:6). Three times on the road to Jerusalem Jesus foretold His death and resurrection (16:21; 17:23; 20:19). The Resurrection is a promise kept.

**Women stand as the first witnesses.** They watched from afar as He died, sat opposite His tomb as He was buried, and reached the grave first at dawn (27:56; 27:61; 28:1). The risen Jesus appears to them first and commissions them to carry the news to the male apostles (28:10).

**The risen Lord calls runaways His brothers.** The disciples abandoned Him; Peter disowned Him. Yet His first greeting sent their way is "go tell My brothers" (28:10). Resurrection life opens with forgiveness.

**Even doubters get sent on mission.** On the mountain, the disciples worship Him, "but some doubted" (28:17). Jesus dismisses nobody. He steps closer and entrusts the Great Commission to all of them. Faith is not the absence of every question; it is following the One we worship.

**A command framed by power and presence.** Jesus sends His followers to disciple all nations, baptizing and teaching obedience (28:19–20). That mission rests between two unshakeable bookends: first, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me," and last, "I am with you always, to the very end of the age." Disciples go under His authority and in His company.

## Deeper Meaning

**The Son of Man receives dominion.** In Daniel's vision, the Son of Man "was given dominion, glory, and kingship, that the people of every nation and language should serve Him" (Daniel 7:14). Jesus' declaration—"All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me"—is the fulfillment of that vision.

**The promise to Abraham fulfilled.** God promised Abraham, "All the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (Genesis 12:3). Matthew opened with "the son of Abraham" (1:1) and closes by sending disciples out to every nation. That ancient promise now breaks open worldwide.

**From Israel's lost sheep to all nations.** On their first missionary trip, Jesus told the Twelve not to go to Gentiles, only to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 10:5–6). Now He sends them to every nation. Isaiah had foretold that the Lord's Servant would not only restore Jacob's tribes, but become "a light for the nations" (Isaiah 49:6).

**Immanuel from cover to cover.** Matthew opens with the name Immanuel, "God with us" (Matthew 1:23), and finishes with "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (28:20). The entire Gospel lives inside that embrace.

**If Christ has not been raised.** Paul declares plainly, "If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile" (1 Corinthians 15:17). He notes that the risen Christ appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once (1 Corinthians 15:6). Christian faith stands or falls on the event in this chapter.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Teach" or "make disciples"?** Older versions render 28:19 as "go and teach all nations." In the Greek original, the single main verb is "make disciples," with "going," "baptizing," and "teaching" describing how it happens. The commission is not merely passing along classroom information, but drawing people into a living walk with Christ.

**The Trinitarian baptismal formula.** Verse 28:19 names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit inside one singular "name." Some scholars have wondered whether the early church added this wording later, since Acts repeatedly mentions baptizing "in the name of Jesus." Yet every surviving ancient manuscript of Matthew contains this verse, and the *Didache*, a remarkably early church manual, uses the exact same formula.

**The rumor of the stolen body.** Matthew notes this explanation was still circulating when he wrote (28:15). Strikingly, the rumor itself concedes that the tomb was empty. The first-century debate was never whether the grave was empty, but how it became empty.

**Differences among the Gospel accounts.** Matthew describes one angel, Luke two. Matthew highlights appearances in Galilee, Luke in Jerusalem. Critics call these contradictions; many readers view them instead as the hallmark of independent eyewitness accounts—never artificially scrubbed to match down to every detail. All four agree on the essentials: an empty tomb on Sunday morning, women arriving first, and the risen Jesus appearing alive to His followers.

## What Commentators Say

**Justin Martyr**, the mid-second-century Christian apologist in Rome, wrote a dialogue with a Jewish scholar named Trypho. He reported that leaders in Jerusalem dispatched emissaries everywhere claiming Jesus' disciples stole His body at night and then announced He had risen. Justin's testimony confirms that the counter-story in 28:15 was still spreading nearly a century later. *(Dialogue with Trypho, chapter 108)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Justin's report of emissaries spreading the stolen-body claim appears in Dialogue with Trypho chapter 108 and is routinely cited -->

**The Didache**, a very early Christian manual likely composed in the late first century, gives practical instructions for baptism. It directs believers to baptize in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in running water—or if none is available, in other water, or by pouring water three times over the head. This offers some of the earliest evidence of the church using the exact formula of 28:19. *(Didache, chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the Trinitarian formula and water instructions appear in Didache chapter 7 -->

**William Carey**, an English shoemaker and Baptist missionary, published a slim tract in 1792 when many Christians assumed 28:19 applied only to the original apostles. Carey reasoned that if the promise "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" still holds today, the command attached to it must still hold too. That pamphlet helped ignite the modern missionary movement, and Carey sailed for India the next year. *(An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians, 1792)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the argument linking the ongoing validity of the commission to its attached promise forms the opening argument of the Enquiry -->

**Pinchas Lapide**, a twentieth-century German Jewish scholar, examined the resurrection narratives rigorously. He concluded that Jesus' resurrection was a real historical event, because nothing else can account for how terrified, crushed disciples were transformed overnight into fearless witnesses willing to die for their testimony. Even so, Lapide still did not accept Jesus as Israel's Messiah. *(The Resurrection of Jesus: A Jewish Perspective)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this conclusion is the central thesis of Lapide's book, published in English in 1983 -->

**N. T. Wright**, the contemporary Anglican bishop and New Testament scholar, argues that the empty tomb and the resurrection appearances belong inseparably together. An empty tomb alone would suggest grave robbers; appearances alone would suggest visions or ghosts. Only both together explain early Christian faith. He also underscores that no ancient fabricator inventing a story would have chosen women as the primary eyewitnesses. *(The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: both the combined empty-tomb-plus-appearances argument and the criterion of female witnesses appear in the closing part of the book -->
