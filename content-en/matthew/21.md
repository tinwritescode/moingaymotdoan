# Matthew 21 — The King Enters the City, the Temple, and the Vineyard

## Story

Approaching Jerusalem at Bethphage on the Mount of Olives, Jesus sent two disciples ahead: go into the village opposite you, and immediately you will find a donkey tied there with her colt beside her; untie them and bring them to Me. If anyone questions you, say the Lord needs them, and they will send them right away (21:1–3). Matthew sees Zechariah's prophecy fulfilled: "Behold, your King comes to you, gentle and riding on a donkey" (21:4–5).

The two disciples obeyed, brought the donkey and colt, laid their cloaks on them, and He sat on them. A very large crowd spread their cloaks on the road, while others cut branches from the trees and scattered them along the way. The crowds ahead of Him and behind Him shouted, "Hosanna to the Son of David! Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!" (21:6–9).

When He entered Jerusalem, the whole city was stirred and asked, "Who is this?" The crowds replied: this is Jesus, the prophet from Nazareth in Galilee (21:10–11).

Jesus entered the temple courts, drove out all who were buying and selling there, and overturned the tables of the money changers and the benches of those selling doves. He declared: it is written, My house will be called a house of prayer, "but you are making it 'a den of robbers'" (21:12–13).

The blind and the lame came to Him inside the temple, and He healed them. When the chief priests and scribes saw the wonderful things He did and heard children shouting in the temple, "Hosanna to the Son of David!" they were indignant and asked Him: do You hear what these children are saying? Yes, He replied; have you never read: out of the mouths of children and infants You have ordained praise? Then He left the city and went to Bethany to spend the night (21:14–17).

Early the next morning, returning to the city, He was hungry. Seeing a fig tree by the road, He went up to it and found nothing on it except leaves. He said to it: may you never bear fruit again! Immediately the tree withered. The disciples marveled. He told them: if you have faith and do not doubt, you will not only do what was done to the fig tree, but even say to this mountain, "Go, throw yourself into the sea," and it will happen. Whatever you ask in prayer, believing, you will receive (21:18–22). A leafy fig tree bearing no fruit mirrors a bustling temple devoid of true prayer.

While He was teaching in the temple, the chief priests and elders came up and asked: by what authority are You doing these things, and who gave You this authority? He answered with a question: John's baptism—where did it come from, heaven or men? They conferred among themselves: if we say from heaven, He will ask why we didn't believe him; if we say from men, we fear the crowd, for everyone holds John to be a prophet. We do not know, they replied. Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things, Jesus answered (21:23–27).

Then He told two parables. First: a father told his first son to go work in the vineyard. I will, sir, the son replied, yet never went. The father told the second son; he said I will not, yet later changed his mind and went. Which of the two did his father's will? The second, they answered. Jesus declared: tax collectors and prostitutes are entering the kingdom of God ahead of you, because John came to show you the way of righteousness and you did not believe him, while they did; and even after seeing that, you did not repent (21:28–32).

Second parable: a landowner planted a vineyard, put a wall around it, dug a winepress, built a watchtower, rented it to tenant farmers, and went away. At harvest time he sent servants to collect his fruit; the tenants beat one, killed another, and stoned a third. He sent more servants than before, and they treated them the same way. Finally he sent his own son, thinking they would respect him. Yet seeing the son, the tenants told one another: this is the heir; let's kill him and seize his inheritance. They grabbed him, threw him out of the vineyard, and killed him (21:33–39).

Jesus asked: when the owner of the vineyard comes, what will he do to those tenants? They replied: he will bring those wretches to a miserable end and lease the vineyard to others. Jesus quoted the Psalms: "The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone." Therefore, the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people who will produce its fruit (21:40–44).

Hearing His parables, the chief priests and Pharisees realized He was talking about them. They looked for a way to arrest Him, yet feared the crowds because the people held Him to be a prophet (21:45–46).

## Key Takeaways

**A king riding a donkey.** Conquering monarchs rode into cities astride warhorses. Jesus chooses a donkey—the mount of ordinary folk and of peace (21:5). He truly is King, just not the kind of king the crowd expects.

**Crowds turn quickly.** The very shouts of "Hosanna!" today will shift within days to "Crucify Him!" (21:9; 27:22). Cheering Jesus when He matches our expectations is easy; staying faithful when He chooses a different path is the real test.

**Jesus burns with anger when His Father's house is corrupted.** The temple was meant to be a sanctuary of prayer for every nation, yet had been turned into a marketplace and profit center (21:13). Right after overturning the tables, He heals the blind and lame inside those same courts (21:14). He clears the clutter so God's house once again welcomes the vulnerable.

**Leaves without fruit.** The fig tree wore lush green foliage yet yielded zero fruit (21:19). Religious show without a transformed life is no different. Placed right between His two visits to the temple, the withered fig tree acts out a living warning.

**Saying "yes" is not enough; you have to go.** One son promises obedience and stays put; the other refuses at first, repents, and shows up (21:28–30). God looks at our actual follow-through, not our polite opening promises. Repentant tax collectors and prostitutes walk into the kingdom ahead of religious leaders who talk without obeying (21:31).

## Deeper Meaning

**Zechariah's King.** Zechariah foresaw a King arriving in Jerusalem "gentle and riding on a donkey," followed immediately by the promise that He will banish chariots and speak peace to the nations (Zechariah 9:9–10). Jesus fulfills the scene down to the detail, making an unmistakable royal proclamation without saying a word.

**"Hosanna" is a cry for rescue.** The word comes from Psalms 118 and means "save us, we pray!" (Psalms 118:25). The very next verse sings, "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD" (Psalms 118:26). That same psalm contains the line about the rejected stone becoming the cornerstone, which Jesus quotes at the end of the chapter (Psalms 118:22; Matthew 21:42).

**A house of prayer for all nations.** Jesus fuses two prophets. Isaiah proclaimed God's temple would be called a house of prayer for all peoples (Isaiah 56:7). Jeremiah stood at the temple gate accusing Israel of turning God's house into "a den of robbers" and warning it would be destroyed (Jeremiah 7:11; 7:14). Quoting Jeremiah quietly pronounces doom on the temple itself.

**God's vineyard.** The parable of the tenants echoes Isaiah's vineyard song nearly word for word: wall, winepress, watchtower (Isaiah 5:2). In Isaiah, the vineyard is Israel, where God looked for justice and found bloodshed instead (Isaiah 5:7). Jesus' listeners knew instantly whom He was addressing.

**The rejected stone.** After Pentecost, Peter stood face-to-face with these exact chief priests and declared: Jesus is the stone you builders rejected, now made the cornerstone (Acts 4:11). Jesus' warning here came true through the Resurrection.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Jesus ride two donkeys at once?** Matthew mentions both mother donkey and colt, saying the disciples draped cloaks on them and He sat on them (21:7). Critics sometimes accuse Matthew of misreading Hebrew poetic parallelism in Zechariah, which describes one animal twice. Many readers point out, however, that an unbroken colt would naturally be led alongside its mother, and "on them" refers to the cloaks piled over the animal He rode.

**What did the fig tree do wrong?** Mark adds "for it was not the season for figs" (Mark 11:13), making the scene even more perplexing. Critics such as Bertrand Russell dismissed it as an irrational outburst. Most commentators recognize a prophetic symbolic act, familiar from Old Testament prophets: the fig tree represents God's fruitless people (Jeremiah 8:13). Notably, this is Jesus' sole miracle of judgment—and He directs it at a tree, never at a human being.

**Did He cleanse the temple at the beginning or end of His ministry?** Matthew, Mark, and Luke place the temple scene in the final week. John places a temple cleansing at the start of Jesus' ministry (John 2:13–17). Some conclude Jesus performed the sign twice; others think John arranged the episode thematically rather than chronologically.

**To whom is "the kingdom… will be taken away from you" addressed?** Verse 21:43 has tragically been misused to argue God rejected the Jewish people altogether. Yet verse 21:45 explicitly identifies the audience as the chief priests and Pharisees—the corrupt leadership. The Jewish crowds themselves still honored Jesus as a prophet (21:46), and the apostles who first received the vineyard's stewardship were Jews as well.

## What Commentators Say

**E. P. Sanders**, an American New Testament scholar, argued that overturning tables in the temple was not a minor cleanup to make worship tidier. Money changers and dove sellers were essential to temple sacrifice; halting them disrupted the sacrificial system itself. In Sanders's view, Jesus performed a symbolic prophecy of the temple's impending destruction—the exact act that triggered His arrest and execution. *(Jesus and Judaism, 1985)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the temple action is the starting point of the book and the destruction-symbol thesis is widely cited -->

**N. T. Wright**, the British Anglican bishop and New Testament scholar, reads the fig tree and the temple cleansing together. Jesus enters Jerusalem as the true King returning to Zion as the prophets promised. Finding His house fruitless, He reenacts Jeremiah's warning of judgment. Wright argues that Jesus was presenting Himself as the new living meeting place between God and humanity, replacing the stone temple. *(Jesus and the Victory of God, 1996)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Wright reads the temple and fig tree as judgment and sees Jesus replacing the temple; uncertain where specifically he pairs the fig tree with Jeremiah -->

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, asks why Jesus—who performed every other miracle to heal and bless—withered a fig tree. He answers that Jesus wanted His disciples to know He possessed power to judge as well, so they would not panic when seeing Him arrested. And in His mercy, He chose to demonstrate that power on a tree rather than on people. *(Homilies on Matthew, on Matthew 21)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Chrysostom's explanation that Christ showed judgment power on a tree rather than humans; uncertain of the exact homily number -->

**Justin Martyr**, a second-century Christian apologist in Rome, highlights an often-overlooked prophecy. In Jacob's blessing of Judah, Jacob foretold that the ruler would "tie his donkey to a vine, his colt to the choicest branch" (Genesis 49:11). Justin read the tied colt outside the village, which Jesus sent His disciples to untie, as a fulfillment of that ancient blessing. *(First Apology)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Justin connects Genesis 49:11 to the tied colt; uncertain of the exact chapter number -->
