# Matthew 5 — The Sermon on the Mount: Blessings, Salt and Light, and the Law

## Story

Seeing the large crowds, Jesus went up on a mountainside and sat down; His disciples came to Him, and He began to teach them (5:1–2). Moses had received the Law on a mountain; now Jesus sits on a mountain to teach the law of the kingdom of heaven.

He opens with blessings for people the world rarely counts fortunate: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." Blessed are those who mourn, the meek, those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers, and those persecuted because of righteousness (5:3–10).

He speaks directly to His disciples: blessed are you when people insult you, persecute you, and falsely say all kinds of evil against you because of Me. Rejoice and be glad, because great is your reward in heaven; in the same way they persecuted the prophets who came before you (5:11–12).

Then He tells the disciples who they are: "You are the salt of the earth." If salt loses its saltiness, it is good for nothing except to be thrown out and trampled underfoot. "You are the light of the world." Nobody lights a lamp and puts it under a basket; they set it on a stand so it gives light to everyone in the house. Let your light shine before others, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father in heaven (5:13–16).

He addresses the Law: "I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill them." Until heaven and earth pass away, not the smallest letter or stroke of a pen will disappear from the Law. And unless your righteousness surpasses that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven (5:17–20).

Six times after this He says, "You have heard… but I tell you." The Law forbids murder; Jesus says anyone angry with a brother or insulting him is already subject to judgment. If you are offering a gift at the altar and remember your brother has something against you, leave your gift there and go be reconciled first. Settle quickly with your adversary while you are still on the way to court (5:21–26).

The Law forbids adultery; Jesus says anyone who looks at a woman with lust has already committed adultery with her in his heart. If your right eye or right hand causes you to sin, cut it off and throw it away; better to lose one part of your body than for your whole body to go into hell. On divorce, He says that anyone who divorces his wife except for sexual immorality makes her the victim of adultery (5:27–32).

On oaths, He teaches them not to swear at all—neither by heaven, nor by earth, nor by Jerusalem, nor by their own head. "Simply let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No'" (5:33–37).

The Law taught "Eye for eye and tooth for tooth." Jesus says: do not resist an evil person. If someone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also. If someone sues you for your tunic, let him have your cloak as well. If someone forces you to go one mile, go with him two. Give to the one who asks you (5:38–42).

Finally, people had heard that they should love their neighbor and hate their enemy. Jesus says, "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you," that you may be sons of your Father in heaven, who makes His sun rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the righteous and the unrighteous. Loving only those who love you is nothing remarkable—even tax collectors do that. "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (5:43–48).

## Key Takeaways

**The kingdom of heaven turns the world's values upside down.** The poor, the grieving, the meek, the persecuted—the world writes them off as losers. Jesus calls them blessed (5:3–10). The Beatitudes are not entrance requirements to earn God's kingdom; they are good news announcing that the kingdom opens its doors to these very people.

**Disciples live in the world, not in retreat from it.** Salt has to touch food to season it. A lamp has to sit on a stand to give light (5:13–15). Followers of Jesus are called to make a difference right where they live.

**God's law reaches all the way down to the heart.** Jesus does not discard the old Law; He digs down to its root. Refraining from murder is not enough; anger and contempt are already the seeds of murder. Refraining from adultery is not enough; a lustful gaze is already adultery in the heart (5:21–22; 5:27–28).

**Words should be trustworthy without needing an oath.** People swear oaths because their ordinary word cannot be trusted. A disciple of Jesus lets "yes" mean yes and "no" mean no (5:37).

**Loving enemies mirrors our heavenly Father.** God causes the sun to shine and the rain to fall on both good and evil alike (5:45). Anyone can love people who love them back. Loving those who hate us is the family likeness of God's children.

## Deeper Meaning

**A new mountain, a new law, a new Moses.** Moses climbed Mount Sinai to receive the Law and brought it down to the people (Exodus 19:20; 20:1–17). Jesus sits on a mountain and teaches on His own authority—not saying "Thus says the LORD," but "but I tell you" (5:22). Matthew lets the reader see for themselves: this One is greater than Moses.

**The Beatitudes echo Isaiah and the Psalms.** Isaiah foresaw an Anointed One sent to bring good news to the poor and comfort all who mourn (Isaiah 61:1–2). The Psalms promised that the meek would inherit the land (Psalms 37:11). Jesus is saying: those promises come true today.

**"Eye for eye" was originally a law to limit revenge.** The Law of Moses established it so retaliation would never exceed the original injury, and to place punishment in the hands of judges rather than private vendettas (Exodus 21:24). Jesus goes further: lay down revenge altogether. Paul catches the same spirit when he writes to overcome evil with good (Romans 12:20–21).

**Loving one's neighbor already stood in Leviticus.** "Love your neighbor as yourself" was a command of Moses (Leviticus 19:18). Jesus stretches the word "neighbor" all the way out to include enemies. On the cross, He Himself asked the Father to forgive the very men nailing Him there (Luke 23:34).

**"Be perfect" echoes "Be holy."** Leviticus commands, "Be holy, because I… am holy" (Leviticus 19:2). Jesus says: be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect (5:48). Children growing to resemble their Father is the goal of every commandment.

## Notes & Nuances

**No Old Testament law ever commanded "hate your enemy."** Scripture commanded loving one's neighbor, yet never told anyone to hate enemies (5:43). Jesus is likely echoing a popular deduction: if we must love our neighbor, then we may hate our enemy. The Dead Sea Scrolls show that at least one Jewish group of that period explicitly taught this.

**Gouging out an eye or cutting off a hand is vivid hyperbole.** Jesus uses shocking imagery to show how deadly sin truly is (5:29–30). The early church understood that He was not commanding self-mutilation—a left eye can still lust just as easily. The point is to cut away decisively whatever pulls us into sin.

**Does "turning the other cheek" forbid self-defense or state force?** This has been fiercely debated. Anabaptists, Quakers, and the novelist Leo Tolstoy read the command literally for every sphere of life. Luther and Calvin held that Jesus addresses the personal life of the disciple, while civil authorities still bear a duty to protect the vulnerable. Nor does Jesus command leaving the innocent undefended against cruelty.

**"Perfect" does not mean never making a mistake.** The Greek word in verse 48 means whole, complete, mature, undivided. In context, it describes a love that leaves no one out—just like the Father's love (5:45–48).

**On divorce and oaths**, chapters 19 and 23 explore these questions more deeply (19:3–9; 23:16–22). Here it is enough to see that Jesus stands up for wives in a society where men could dismiss them on almost any pretext.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, wrote an entire treatise on the Sermon on the Mount while still a young priest. He views the Beatitudes as ascending steps of the spiritual life, starting with humility and rising toward peace. He even links them in reverse order to the gifts of the Spirit in Isaiah 11, rising from the fear of the Lord to wisdom. *(Our Lord's Sermon on the Mount, book 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking the Beatitudes to the seven gifts of the Spirit in Isaiah 11 appears early in book 1 and is widely cited -->

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, preached a series of sermons on Matthew 5–7. He distinguishes two roles of a Christian: as a private individual, a believer must endure wrong and turn the other cheek; as a parent, judge, or soldier, that same person has a duty to defend others. In Luther's view, Jesus is not laying down rules for governing a state, but shaping the heart of a disciple. *(The Sermon on the Mount, 1532)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: distinguishing personal life from public office is Luther's central argument on 5:38–42 -->

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, the German pastor executed by the Nazis in 1945, reads the question "what are you doing more than others?" in verse 47 as a key to the passage. What sets a disciple apart, he says, is "the extraordinary"—a love that goes beyond ordinary expectation and reaches enemies. Writing while the German church was accommodating the regime, he called a faith that demands nothing "cheap grace." *(The Cost of Discipleship, section on the Sermon on the Mount)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: "the extraordinary" on verse 47 and "cheap grace" are two famous themes of the book -->

**Walter Wink**, an American theologian, argues that the three examples in verses 39–41 do not teach passive submission. A slap on the right cheek meant a backhanded strike—how a superior humiliated an inferior. Offering the left cheek forced the striker to hit as an equal. Handing over one's cloak in court or carrying a Roman soldier's pack past the legal one-mile limit were similarly acts of nonviolent resistance that exposed injustice. *(Engaging the Powers, 1992)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the "third way" reading of these three examples is Wink's best-known argument -->

**Martin Luther King Jr.**, pastor and leader of the American civil rights movement, preached a sermon titled "Loving Your Enemies" on verse 44. Only love can transform an enemy into a friend, he said, while hatred only multiplies hatred. He distinguishes loving from liking: God does not ask us to like an oppressor, but to desire their genuine good. *(Strength to Love, "Loving Your Enemies," 1963)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this sermon appears in Strength to Love and these points are its core -->

**Jacob Neusner**, an American Jewish scholar, wrote a book imagining himself standing in the crowd listening to the Sermon on the Mount. He respects the teachings and sees that most of them already appear in the Torah. Yet he halts at "but I tell you": this teacher speaks on His own authority, placing Himself alongside God's Law. As a Jew, Neusner therefore respectfully chooses to stay with Moses. *(A Rabbi Talks with Jesus, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident the book centers on an imagined dialogue and his reason for staying with Torah; uncertain which chapter discusses "but I tell you" -->
