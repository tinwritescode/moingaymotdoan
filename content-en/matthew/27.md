# Matthew 27 — Pilate, the Cross, and the Tomb

## Story

Early in the morning, all the chief priests and elders reached a decision to put Jesus to death. They bound Him, led Him away, and handed Him over to Pilate the governor (27:1–2).

Seeing that Jesus had been condemned, Judas was seized with remorse and returned the thirty pieces of silver to the chief priests and elders: "I have sinned by betraying innocent blood." What is that to us? they replied; that is your responsibility. Throwing the silver into the temple, Judas went away and hanged himself (27:3–5).

The chief priests picked up the coins and said: it is unlawful to put this into the treasury, since it is blood money. They decided to use it to buy the potter's field as a burial place for foreigners; that is why it is called the "Field of Blood" to this day. Matthew sees here the prophecy of thirty silver coins, the price set on Him (27:6–10). Men who just hunted false witnesses to kill an innocent man stay scrupulously careful about treasury rules.

Jesus stood before the governor. Pilate asked Him, "Are You the King of the Jews?" Jesus answered, "You have said so." When accused by the chief priests and elders, He gave no answer. Pilate asked: don't You hear how many charges they are bringing against You? Yet Jesus did not reply to a single charge, to the governor's great amazement (27:11–14).

At every feast it was the governor's custom to release one prisoner chosen by the crowd. At that time they held a notorious prisoner named Barabbas. Pilate asked the crowd: which one do you want me to release—Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ? For he knew they had handed Him over out of envy. While he sat on the judgment seat, his wife sent word: have nothing to do with that righteous man, for I have suffered greatly in a dream today because of Him (27:15–19).

Yet the chief priests and elders persuaded the crowd to ask for Barabbas and have Jesus executed. Pilate asked: which of the two do you want me to release to you? Barabbas! they answered. What then shall I do with Jesus who is called Christ? They all shouted: crucify Him! Why—what evil has He done? Pilate asked. They shouted all the louder, "Crucify Him!" (27:20–23).

Seeing he was getting nowhere and a riot was starting, Pilate took water and washed his hands in front of the crowd: I am innocent of this man's blood; see to it yourselves. All the people answered, "His blood be on us and on our children!" Pilate released Barabbas, had Jesus flogged, and handed Him over to be crucified (27:24–26).

The governor's soldiers took Jesus into the Praetorium and gathered the whole company around Him. They stripped Him, draped a scarlet robe over Him, twisted together a crown of thorns to press on His head, put a staff in His right hand, and knelt mocking Him: hail, King of the Jews! They spit on Him and struck His head repeatedly with the staff. After mocking Him, they stripped off the robe, put His own clothes back on, and led Him out to crucify Him (27:27–31).

Along the way they found a man from Cyrene named Simon and forced him to carry His cross. Coming to a place called Golgotha, meaning Place of the Skull, they offered Him wine mixed with gall; tasting it, He refused to drink. They crucified Him, divided His clothes by casting lots, and sat down to keep watch over Him. Above His head they placed the written charge: "THIS IS JESUS, THE KING OF THE JEWS." Two robbers were crucified beside Him, one on His right and one on His left (27:32–38).

Passersby shook their heads and hurled insults: you who are going to destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, save Yourself! If You are the Son of God, come down from the cross! Chief priests, scribes, and elders joined the mockery: "He saved others, but He cannot save Himself!" If He is the King of Israel, let Him come down now and we will believe Him. He trusts in God; let God rescue Him now if He delights in Him. Even the two robbers crucified with Him heaped insults on Him (27:39–44).

From the sixth hour until the ninth hour darkness covered the whole land. About the ninth hour Jesus cried out in a loud voice, "Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?"—meaning, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" Hearing this, some standing nearby said: he is calling Elijah. One man ran, soaked a sponge in sour wine, lifted it on a reed, and gave it to Him to drink. Others said: leave Him alone; let's see if Elijah comes to save Him. Crying out again in a loud voice, Jesus yielded up His spirit (27:45–50).

At that exact moment, the curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom; the earth shook, rocks split, tombs broke open, and the bodies of many saints who had fallen asleep were raised; coming out of the tombs after His resurrection, they entered the holy city and appeared to many (27:51–53). Seeing the earthquake and everything that happened, the centurion and those guarding Jesus were terrified and exclaimed, "Truly this was the Son of God!" (27:54).

Many women were watching from a distance—women who had followed Jesus from Galilee to care for Him, among them Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joseph, and the mother of Zebedee's sons (27:55–56). The male disciples had fled; the women stayed.

When evening came, a rich man from Arimathea named Joseph, who had himself become a disciple of Jesus, went to Pilate and asked for Jesus' body, and Pilate ordered it given to him. Joseph wrapped the body in a clean linen cloth, laid it in his own new tomb cut out of rock, rolled a large stone against the entrance, and went away. Mary Magdalene and the other Mary sat opposite the tomb (27:57–61).

The next day, the chief priests and Pharisees went to Pilate: sir, we remember that while that impostor was still alive He claimed after three days He would rise again. Order the tomb secured until the third day, lest His disciples steal the body and tell the people He has risen—the last deception would be worse than the first. You have a guard, Pilate replied; make it as secure as you know how. They went, sealed the stone, and posted the guard (27:62–66).

## Key Takeaways

**The innocent is condemned; the guilty walks free.** Barabbas, a notorious criminal, goes home; Jesus, in whom even Pilate finds no crime, hangs on a cross (27:21–26). Here stands the clearest living picture of the gospel: the Righteous One dying in place of the guilty.

**Washing your hands cannot wash off guilt.** Pilate knows Jesus is innocent, receives a warning from his own wife, and still hands Him over to crucifixion (27:18–26). The chief priests refuse to drop "blood money" into the sacred treasury while feeling no qualms about shedding innocent blood (27:6). Everyone works hard to keep their hands looking clean while their hearts go crooked.

**He saves others by refusing to save Himself.** The jeer "He saved others, but He cannot save Himself" (27:42) accidentally tells the deepest truth. Precisely because He refuses to climb down off the cross, others are saved.

**Jesus enters the very bottom of human abandonment.** The cry "why have You forsaken Me?" (27:46) reveals that He did not die like a calm philosophical hero. He drank down the full isolation from God that sin brings upon humanity.

**Outsiders see what insiders miss.** Religious leaders jeer and disciples run away, while a Roman centurion confesses, "Truly this was the Son of God!" (27:54). Faithful women stay to the end, and a wealthy counselor steps forward to ask for His body (27:55–58).

## Deeper Meaning

**Psalms 22 enacted at the cross.** Jesus' cry is the opening line of Psalms 22: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken me?" (Psalms 22:1). The whole psalm unfolds around the cross: mockers wag their heads (Psalms 22:7–8), divide garments, and cast lots (Psalms 22:18). Yet Psalms 22 does not end in despair—by its close, all the families of the nations bow down before the Lord (Psalms 22:27).

**Gall and sour wine.** Psalms 69 laments, "They poisoned my food with gall and gave me vinegar to quench my thirst" (Psalms 69:21). Matthew notes both details (27:34; 27:48).

**Silent before His shearers.** Jesus stands silent before Pilate (27:14), just like Isaiah's Suffering Servant who "did not open His mouth" (Isaiah 53:7). Isaiah also foretold He was assigned a grave "with a rich man in His death" (Isaiah 53:9)—fulfilled in the new tomb of wealthy Joseph.

**Washing hands of blood.** The Law of Moses prescribed a handwashing ritual when a murder victim was discovered with no known killer: elders washed their hands declaring, "Our hands did not shed this blood" (Deuteronomy 21:6–7). Pilate, a Gentile, mimics the exact gesture—while knowing full well whose hand is authorizing the execution.

**The torn curtain.** The temple curtain sealed off the Most Holy Place, entered only once a year by the high priest. Hebrews explains that by Jesus' blood we now enter boldly "through the curtain, that is, His body" (Hebrews 10:19–20). Torn from top to bottom, the opening comes from God's hand, never human effort.

**Opened graves.** Ezekiel heard the Lord promise, "I will open your graves and bring you up from them" (Ezekiel 37:12). The risen saints of 27:52 announce that Jesus' death has already begun breaking death's grip.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 27:25 has been horribly abused across history.** The crowd's shout—"His blood be on us and on our children!"—was twisted for centuries to blame and persecute Jewish people everywhere. That violently misreads the scene. It was the cry of one stirred-up crowd on one morning, never a verdict on an entire people across all generations. Many commentators also catch a deep irony: in Matthew, Jesus' blood is shed "for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28).

**Which prophet spoke of the thirty silver coins?** Matthew attributes the prophecy to Jeremiah (27:9), yet the closest wording comes from Zechariah, where thirty silver pieces are thrown to the potter in the Lord's house (Zechariah 11:13). Jeremiah supplies the purchase of a field (Jeremiah 32:9) and a potter's jar (Jeremiah 19:1–2). Matthew likely blends two prophetic passages and names the major prophet.

**How did Judas die?** Matthew reports that Judas hanged himself (27:5), while Acts says he "fell headlong, burst open in the middle, and all his intestines spilled out" (Acts 1:18). Readers since early times have reconciled the two by imagining the rope or branch breaking after hanging. Both accounts agree the field was bought with betrayal money and named the Field of Blood.

**Saints emerging from tombs.** Only Matthew records dead saints rising and entering Jerusalem (27:52–53), with no outside historical mention. Some read it as a literal miraculous event; others read it as Matthew employing prophetic imagery to declare that Jesus' death unlocks resurrection. Notice also the unusual sequencing: they are raised at His death, yet emerge from their tombs only after His resurrection.

**The cry of abandonment is not a loss of faith.** Jesus still addresses God as "*My* God." He prays using the Scriptures of Israel, just as suffering Jews had done for centuries. Theologians still wrestle with how the Son experienced forsakenness by the Father, yet no Christian tradition reads this cry as unbelief.

## What Commentators Say

**Philo**, the first-century Jewish philosopher of Alexandria and contemporary of Jesus, preserves a letter describing Pilate's character. It depicts him as inflexible, cruel, given to bribery, insults, and executions without trial. **Melito**, second-century bishop of Sardis, wrote a celebrated Passover homily. He declared: He who hung the earth in space is Himself hung; He who fixed the heavens is fixed to wood. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, reflected deeply on the cry in 27:46. Christ did not merely suffer bodily death, Calvin wrote, but endured in His soul the full terror of a condemned, God-forsaken person under divine wrath. Calvin interpreted "He descended into hell" in the Apostles' Creed in exactly this sense: Christ's inner agony on the cross. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, book 2, chapter 16)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's interpretation of the descent into hell as Christ's spiritual torment on the cross in Institutes book 2 chapter 16 is well known -->

**Jürgen Moltmann**, the twentieth-century German Protestant theologian, placed the cry "why have You forsaken Me?" at the heart of his theology. At the cross, he argued, the Father loses the Son and the Son loses the Father, as God enters the absolute depths of human abandonment. Therefore no sufferer anywhere ever stands outside God's reach. *(The Crucified God, 1972)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: mutual abandonment of Father and Son on the cross is the central thesis of The Crucified God -->
