# Matthew 15 — What Defiles a Person, and the Canaanite Woman

## Story

Pharisees and scribes came from Jerusalem to ask Jesus: why do Your disciples break the tradition of the elders by not washing their hands before they eat? (15:1–2). They were not talking about hygiene, but about a ceremonial cleansing handed down by earlier teachers.

He answered with a question of His own: and why do you break the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition? God commanded: honor your father and mother. Yet you say that if someone tells their parents, "Whatever support you might have received from me is given to God," they no longer have to honor their father or mother. Thus, for the sake of your tradition, you nullify the word of God (15:3–6).

"You hypocrites!" Isaiah prophesied accurately about you: "These people honor Me with their lips, but their hearts are far from Me." They worship Me in vain, teaching human rules as doctrine (15:7–9).

Summoning the crowd, He said: listen and understand. It is not what enters the mouth that defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth that defiles them (15:10–11). The disciples warned Him that the Pharisees were offended when they heard this. He answered: every plant that My heavenly Father has not planted will be pulled up by the roots. Leave them alone; they are blind guides, and if the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit (15:12–14).

Peter asked Him to explain the parable. Jesus replied: are you still so dull? Whatever enters the mouth goes into the stomach and passes out of the body. Yet what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart, and that is what defiles a person—for out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false testimony, and slander. Eating with unwashed hands does not defile anyone (15:15–20).

Jesus left that area and withdrew to the region of Tyre and Sidon, Gentile territory. A Canaanite woman from that vicinity came crying out: Lord, Son of David, have mercy on me! My daughter is severely oppressed by a demon. Yet He did not answer her a word. His disciples urged Him to send her away because she kept shouting behind them (15:21–23).

He said: I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. She came and knelt before Him: "Lord, help me!" He replied, "It is not right to take the children's bread and toss it to the dogs." She answered: yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table (15:24–27).

Jesus exclaimed, "O woman, your faith is great! Let it be done for you as you desire." And her daughter was healed from that very hour (15:28). A woman with no claim of privilege at all becomes the one praised for great faith.

Returning along the Sea of Galilee, He went up on a mountainside and sat down. Huge crowds came to Him, bringing the lame, the blind, the mute, the crippled, and many others, laying them at His feet, and He healed them. Watching the mute speak, the crippled restored, the lame walk, and the blind see, the crowds marveled and glorified the God of Israel (15:29–31).

He called His disciples to Him: I have compassion for this crowd because they have already been with Me three days and have nothing to eat; I do not want to send them away hungry, or they may collapse on the way. The disciples asked: where in this remote place can we get enough bread for so many people? He asked: how many loaves do you have? Seven, they answered, and a few small fish (15:32–34).

Telling the crowd to sit on the ground, He took the seven loaves and the fish, gave thanks, broke them, and gave them to the disciples, who distributed them to the people. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and they gathered seven basketfuls of leftover pieces. Four thousand men ate, besides women and children. Then He dismissed the crowd, boarded the boat, and went to the region of Magadan (15:35–39).

## Key Takeaways

**Human rules can bury God's commands.** Traditions around handwashing or vowed offerings may begin with a sincere desire to please God. Yet the moment a rule lets someone neglect aging parents, it has turned against God's own command (15:5–6). Every religious custom must be tested against God's word.

**Defilement begins in the heart.** Food passes through the body and is gone. Evil thoughts, lies, adultery, and theft rise out of the heart (15:17–19). Clean hands paired with a corrupt heart leave a person just as unclean. Jesus moves the question "How do I become clean?" from the dining table to the human heart.

**Persistent faith refuses to let go.** The Canaanite woman meets three closed doors: silence, Jesus saying He was sent only to Israel, and the remark about bread and dogs (15:23–26). She neither argues nor storms off in resentment; she takes hold of His very words—even little dogs get crumbs (15:27). Jesus honors her for "great faith."

**Bread for the Gentiles too.** Immediately after that exchange about "crumbs," Jesus feeds another vast crowd, this time in largely Gentile territory (15:29–38). The woman asked for scraps, and abundant bread is already spread across the table for everyone.

## Deeper Meaning

**Lips and heart.** Jesus quotes Isaiah: this people honors God with their lips while their hearts stay far away (Isaiah 29:13). Jeremiah speaks even more bluntly: the heart is deceitful above all things (Jeremiah 17:9). What humanity truly needs is not extra rituals, but a brand-new heart.

**Honoring parents carries real weight.** The Law of Moses not only commanded honoring father and mother, but treated cursing parents as a grave offense (Exodus 21:17). Mark supplies the name of the vow practice Jesus criticizes: *Corban*, meaning dedicated to God (Mark 7:11).

**From the dinner table to Peter's vision.** Mark adds a comment that by these words Jesus "declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). Yet Peter himself still needed a heavenly vision years later to grasp it, when God told him not to call anything impure that God had made clean (Acts 10:15). Immediately afterward, Peter stepped into a Gentile home.

**Another outsider in Sidon.** Centuries earlier, God sent Elijah to a widow in Zarephath, "in the region of Sidon" (1 Kings 17:9). She was a Gentile, yet God sustained her and restored her son. The Canaanite woman walks right in that widow's footsteps.

**First the Jew, then the Gentile.** Saying "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (15:24) does not slam the door on Gentiles; it states an order of mission. Paul follows the same pattern when he says the gospel comes "first for the Jew, then for the Greek" (Romans 1:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Jesus call her a "dog"?** Verse 15:26 sounds harsh to modern ears. Jews of that era sometimes used "dogs" as a slur for Gentiles. The word Jesus uses means "little dogs" or household puppies, yet it remains blunt. Some think He was testing her faith, like a teacher posing a tough challenge to draw out a student's deepest conviction. Others think He voiced the conventional Jewish assumption only to overturn it. However one explains His tone, the ending is unmistakable: she is praised and granted her request.

**Handwashing was not about germs.** The Law of Moses required priests to wash before serving in the temple. The Pharisees wanted everyday Jews to share priestly holiness, so they extended handwashing to ordinary meals. It began with noble intent, yet hardened into a burden that could overshadow God's own commandments.

**Matthew omits the remark "declaring all foods clean."** Mark includes that editorial note (Mark 7:19), whereas Matthew ends with the simpler statement about unwashed hands (15:20). Because Matthew's audience consisted largely of Jewish believers who continued observing kosher practices, he avoids pressing that particular point.

**Are the two feedings really one event told twice?** Some scholars suggest so. Yet Jesus Himself refers back to both feedings separately with their distinct numbers in the very next chapter (16:9–10). The first took place in Jewish territory with twelve baskets left over; the second likely occurred in Gentile territory with seven baskets remaining.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, treasured the story of the Canaanite woman and preached on it repeatedly. He said she "caught God in His own words": He called her a dog, and she accepted the title and claimed a dog's share. **John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, argued that Jesus did not stay silent or push back because He wanted to send the woman away. He knew beforehand how she would respond and wanted her remarkable faith displayed openly before His disciples. **The Mishnah**, the collection of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, reveals that later rabbis worried about vows harming parents as well. It rules that a dedicatory vow may be annulled once a person realizes it brings dishonor to father or mother.
