# Matthew 14 — The Death of John, Five Loaves, and Walking on Water

## Story

Herod the tetrarch heard reports about Jesus and told his attendants: this is John the Baptist, risen from the dead, which is why miraculous powers are at work in him (14:1–2). The king's conscience was not at peace.

Matthew steps back to tell what had happened. Herod had arrested John, bound him, and thrown him into prison because of Herodias, the wife of his brother Philip. John had told the king straight out, "It is not lawful for you to have her." Herod wanted to execute John, yet feared the crowd because they regarded John as a prophet (14:3–5).

On Herod's birthday, the daughter of Herodias danced before the guests and pleased Herod so much that he swore with an oath to give her whatever she asked. Prompted by her mother, she said, "Give me here on a platter the head of John the Baptist." The king was distressed, yet because of his oaths and his dinner guests, he ordered it done. John was beheaded in prison; his head was brought on a platter and given to the girl, who carried it to her mother. John's disciples came, buried his body, and went to tell Jesus (14:6–12).

Hearing the news, Jesus withdrew by boat privately to a solitary place. The crowds heard of it and followed Him on foot from the towns. Stepping ashore and seeing a vast crowd, He felt deep compassion for them and healed their sick (14:13–14).

As evening approached, the disciples came to Him: this is a remote place and the hour is late; send the crowds away to the villages to buy themselves food. Jesus answered, "They do not need to go away. You give them something to eat." They replied: all we have here are five loaves and two fish. He said, "Bring them here to Me" (14:15–18).

He directed the crowd to sit down on the grass, took the five loaves and two fish, looked up to heaven and gave thanks, broke the loaves and handed them to the disciples, and the disciples distributed them to the people. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and the disciples picked up twelve basketfuls of leftover pieces. Those who ate numbered about five thousand men, besides women and children (14:19–21).

Immediately afterward, He made the disciples get into the boat and cross ahead of Him while He dismissed the crowd. Then He went up on a mountainside by Himself to pray. By nightfall the boat was already far from land, battered by waves because the wind was against them (14:22–24).

During the fourth watch of the night, Jesus came toward them walking on the sea. Seeing Him, the disciples were terrified—"It's a ghost!"—and cried out in fear. Right away He spoke to them: "Take courage! It is I. Do not be afraid" (14:25–27).

Peter answered: Lord, if it is You, command me to come to You on the water. He said, "Come." Peter climbed out of the boat and walked on the water toward Jesus. Yet when he saw the strength of the wind, he grew frightened, began to sink, and cried out, "Lord, save me!" Immediately Jesus reached out His hand and caught him: "You of little faith, why did you doubt?" (14:28–31).

When they climbed into the boat, the wind died down. Those in the boat worshiped Him, saying, "Truly You are the Son of God!" (14:32–33).

Crossing over, they landed at Gennesaret. The local people recognized Him, sent word through the surrounding country, and brought all their sick to Him, begging simply to touch the fringe of His cloak—and all who touched it were completely healed (14:34–36).

## Key Takeaways

**Speaking truth can cost a life.** John spoke a single sentence to a ruler: it is not lawful for you to take your brother's wife (14:4). That sentence put him in chains and led to the executioner's blade. Followers of God are not promised safety; they are called to faithfulness.

**The most powerful man in the room is actually the weakest.** Herod fears the crowd, fears losing face in front of dinner guests, and lets a girl and her mother steer him around (14:5; 14:9). He lacks the courage to break a foolish oath, yet has no trouble killing an innocent man. Afterward his own conscience haunts him (14:2).

**Even in grief, Jesus chooses compassion.** Hearing of John's murder, He longs to be alone. Yet seeing the crowds, He feels compassion and heals their sick (14:13–14). Personal sorrow never shuts His heart to other people's pain.

**God works through the little we offer.** Five loaves and two fish cannot feed a crowd. Yet Jesus says, "Bring them here to Me" (14:18). He does not conjure food out of thin air; He takes the small lunch the disciples place in His hands, blesses it, and hands it back to them to distribute.

**Eyes on Christ stand firm; eyes on the waves sink.** Peter stepping out of the boat is an act of genuine faith. He sinks only when his eyes shift from Jesus to the wind (14:30). Yet even while sinking, he knows the exact person to call—and Jesus' hand is right there.

## Deeper Meaning

**Elijah and Ahab, John and Herod.** Elijah confronted King Ahab and Queen Jezebel, a queen who sought the prophet's life (1 Kings 21:20). John, the Elijah of the new era, confronts Herod and Herodias. This time the vengeful queen wins, and the prophet dies.

**A banquet in the wilderness.** God fed Israel with manna in the desert. Elisha fed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves, and "they had some left over" (2 Kings 4:43–44). Jesus feeds more than five thousand with five loaves and gathers twelve baskets left over. Someone greater than the prophets is here.

**Bread blessed, broken, and shared.** The exact sequence of Jesus' actions—taking bread, giving thanks, breaking it, and giving it to the disciples (14:19)—will return at the Last Supper, when He says "this is My body" (26:26). The meal on the hillside foreshadows the meal in the upper room.

**Only God treads on the waves.** Job says of God, "He alone… treads on the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8). The Psalms say God's pathway runs through the sea (Psalms 77:19). Walking on water, Jesus does what Scripture attributes to God alone.

**"It is I."** In Greek, Jesus' words in 14:27 literally read "I am," echoing the divine name God revealed to Moses at the burning bush: "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14). The disciples catch the meaning, which is why they bow in worship and confess Him as the Son of God (14:33).

## Notes & Nuances

**This Herod is not the Herod of chapter 2.** This is Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, ruling only Galilee and a territory across the Jordan. Matthew gives him his exact title, "tetrarch"—ruler of a quarter-province (14:1). The Law of Moses forbade marrying a brother's wife while the brother lived (Leviticus 18:16).

**The girl's name never appears in Scripture.** The name Salome comes from the historian Josephus. Later art and drama added lavish detail to her dance, whereas Matthew keeps the account sparse and never mentions her age.

**Some attempt a natural explanation of the loaves and fish.** They imagine the crowd had hidden lunches and, inspired by shared generosity, pulled them out to share with one another. That makes a pleasant moral tale, yet it is not what Matthew tells. He recounts an unmistakable miracle—the only miracle besides the resurrection recorded in all four Gospels.

**Was Jesus scolding Peter?** "You of little faith" sounds like a rebuke (14:31). Yet Jesus speaks those words while already holding Peter safely by the hand. The other eleven never sank—because they never climbed out of the boat.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, also records Herod Antipas's execution of John, though giving a political motive. According to Josephus, Herod feared John's massive sway over the public might spark a rebellion, so he imprisoned him at the fortress of Machaerus and had him killed. Josephus likewise confirms that Herodias left her husband to marry Herod. The two accounts complement one another: one highlights political fear, the other a family scandal. *(Antiquities of the Jews, book 18)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus's account of John's death in book 18 is widely cited -->

**C. S. Lewis**, the British writer and scholar, observes that multiplying bread is not foreign to how God ordinarily acts. Every single year God turns a handful of grain into abundant bread through soil, rain, and harvest. On the hillside, Jesus does the very same thing—only swiftly and in a small space, so people can recognize the hand already working quietly throughout nature. *(Miracles, 1947, chapter on miracles of the old creation)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the point that miracles accelerate what God normally does slowly in nature is a famous argument by Lewis, citing both bread and wine -->

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, preached on Peter walking on the water. He reads Peter as a picture of the whole church: sometimes striding forward boldly, sometimes faltering and sinking. He encourages his listeners: whenever you feel yourself going under in life's storms, cry out like Peter, "Lord, save me." *(Sermons)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine treats Peter as an image of the church in preaching on 14:24–33; uncertain of the exact sermon number -->

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, preached on the people touching the fringe of Jesus' cloak at the end of this chapter. He asked: do you wish to honor the body of Christ? Do not leave Him naked outside the church door while adorning the sanctuary inside with silk.
