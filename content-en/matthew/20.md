# Matthew 20 — The Vineyard Workers and the Son of Man Who Serves

## Story

Jesus tells a parable: the kingdom of heaven is like a landowner who went out early in the morning to hire workers for his vineyard. He agreed to pay them a denarius for the day and sent them into his vineyard (20:1–2).

About the third hour he went out again, saw others standing idle in the marketplace, and told them to go into his vineyard too, promising to pay whatever was right. He did the same at the sixth hour and the ninth hour. At the eleventh hour, with only one hour of daylight left, he found still others standing there and asked: why have you been standing here all day doing nothing? Because no one has hired us, they answered. He told them: you go into my vineyard as well (20:3–7).

When evening came, the owner instructed his foreman to summon the workers and pay their wages, beginning with the last hired and ending with the first. Those hired at the eleventh hour each received a full denarius. Seeing this, those hired first expected to receive more, yet each of them also received a single denarius (20:8–10).

They began grumbling against the landowner: these latecomers worked only one hour, and you made them equal to us who bore the burden and scorching heat of the whole day. He answered one of them: friend, I am doing you no wrong; didn't you agree to work for a denarius? Take your pay and go. I choose to give the last man the same as I gave you. Don't I have the right to do what I want with my own money? Or are you envious because I am generous? Jesus concludes, "So the last will be first, and the first will be last" (20:11–16).

On the road up to Jerusalem, He took the twelve disciples aside and foretold His passion a third time: the Son of Man will be handed over to the chief priests and scribes; they will condemn Him to death and deliver Him to the Gentiles to be mocked, flogged, and crucified; and on the third day He will be raised to life (20:17–19).

Right then, the mother of Zebedee's sons came kneeling before Him with her two sons and asked: grant that one of these two sons of mine may sit at Your right and the other at Your left in Your kingdom. He replied: you do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup I am going to drink? We are able, they answered. He told them: you will indeed drink My cup, yet sitting at My right or left is not Mine to grant—it belongs to those for whom My Father has prepared it (20:20–23).

Hearing about this, the other ten were indignant with the two brothers. Jesus called them together: you know that the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their high officials exercise authority over them. It shall not be so among you. Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wants to be first must be your slave—"just as the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many" (20:24–28).

As they were leaving Jericho, a large crowd followed Him. Two blind men sitting by the roadside heard Jesus was passing by and shouted, "Lord, Son of David, have mercy on us!" The crowd rebuked them to keep quiet, yet they shouted all the louder (20:29–31).

Jesus stopped, called them, and asked, "What do you want Me to do for you?" Lord, they replied, we want our sight. Moved with compassion, He touched their eyes; immediately they received their sight and followed Him (20:32–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Grace is not calculated by the time clock.** Both the one-hour worker and the twelve-hour worker receive the exact same denarius (20:9–10). The landowner cheats nobody; he keeps his promise to the letter. He simply overflows with generosity toward those arriving late. God's kingdom runs on God's openhandedness, never on human scorecards.

**Envy twists our vision.** The full-day laborers lost nothing at all. They resented seeing someone else receive more than they thought fair (20:12). The owner's piercing question—"are you envious because I am generous?" (20:15)—exposes the quiet sting longtime believers sometimes feel when newcomers are warmly welcomed.

**The road to the throne runs straight through the cross.** For the third time Jesus announces His death, this time in sharpest detail: handed over to Gentiles, mocked, flogged, crucified (20:18–19). In the very next breath, two disciples lobby for premier seats. They hear the word "kingdom," and tune out the word "cross."

**True greatness looks like service.** Jesus flips the world's pyramid of power upside down: authority is not a pedestal for being waited on, but a summons to serve (20:26–27). He Himself is the pattern, coming to serve and give His life as a ransom (20:28).

**"What do you want Me to do for you?"** Jesus asks the two blind men the exact question He had just asked Zebedee's family (20:21; 20:32). Two disciples ask for prestige. Two blind men ask to see. The blind men turn out to be the ones who truly see what they need.

## Deeper Meaning

**Paying wages before sunset.** The Law of Moses commanded paying hired laborers the very same day, "before the sun sets, because he is poor" (Deuteronomy 24:15). A single denarius covered a family's daily food. The landowner in the parable does not merely satisfy the Law; he makes sure nobody walks home empty-handed.

**Grace, not wages.** Paul writes that to the one who works, wages are not credited as a gift but as an obligation, whereas to the one who trusts God, righteousness is credited freely (Romans 4:4–5). The vineyard parable paints that truth in living color.

**The Lord's cup.** In the Old Testament, "the cup" often signifies God's cup of wrath poured out against sin (Jeremiah 25:15). In Gethsemane, Jesus will pray for that cup to pass, and then drink it anyway (26:39). James, one of the two brothers seeking thrones, eventually did drink his Master's cup, becoming the first apostle martyred for the faith (Acts 12:2).

**On His right and on His left.** When Jesus is finally lifted up under the title "King of the Jews," the two figures stationed at His right and left are two criminals crucified beside Him (27:38). The two brothers truly had no idea what they were asking.

**A ransom for many.** Verse 20:28 echoes Isaiah 53, where the Servant "poured out His life unto death" and "bore the sin of many" (Isaiah 53:12). Paul likewise writes that Christ "took the form of a servant" and became obedient to death on a cross (Philippians 2:7–8).

## Notes & Nuances

**This parable is not an economic manifesto on wages.** Some invoke it to debate modern pay structures. Jesus states plainly that it illustrates "the kingdom of heaven" (20:1). Even so, it reveals an employer who cares that working families have enough to eat, not merely about hours logged.

**Nor does it encourage putting off repentance until your deathbed.** The eleventh-hour workers were not lazy; they stood waiting all day because "no one has hired us" (20:7). The instant they were called, they stepped into the vineyard. The parable celebrates God's generosity, never a scheme to delay obedience.

**Who asked for the seats—the mother or the sons?** Matthew reports the mother making the request (20:20). Mark reports James and John asking directly (Mark 10:35). Notice that in Matthew, Jesus directs His reply plural ("you") straight to the two sons, suggesting the mother spoke on their behalf.

**One blind man or two?** Mark and Luke mention one blind man, whom Mark names Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46). Matthew mentions two, matching his recurring habit of recording pairs (cf. 8:28; 9:27). Mark likely singles out the one who later became known by name in the early church.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, preached an Easter sermon that Eastern Orthodox churches still read aloud every year. Drawing on this parable, he welcomes everyone to the feast: whoever labored from the first hour, receive your just reward; whoever arrived at the eleventh hour, fear not, for the Master welcomes the last even as the first. **Gregory the Great**, pope in Rome in the late sixth century, interprets the hours of the day in two ways. Historically, they represent the ages of salvation from Adam, Noah, Abraham, and Moses down to Christ. Personally, they represent childhood, youth, adulthood, older age, and frail old age. God calls people across every era and at every stage of life. *(Forty Gospel Homilies)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Gregory interprets the hours as historical eras and stages of human life; uncertain of the exact homily number -->

**Joachim Jeremias**, a German New Testament scholar, compares this parable with a story in the Jerusalem Talmud. Mourning a young rabbi who died early, speakers told of a king who paid full wages to a man working just two hours because he accomplished more than others did all day. Jeremias pinpoints the sharp contrast: in the Talmudic story, the latecomer earns full pay through superior output; in Jesus' parable, they receive full pay purely through the owner's goodness. *(The Parables of Jesus, section on the vineyard workers)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the comparison with Jerusalem Talmud Berakhot 2 is a celebrated highlight of Jeremias's book -->

**Amy-Jill Levine**, an American Jewish scholar, pushes back against reading the grumbling early workers as resentful Jews and the latecomers as favored Gentiles. She insists the parable deals with tangible reality: poor day laborers hoping for survival and a landowner ensuring every worker goes home with enough for dinner. In her view, the parable confronts listeners directly: what are you doing with the wealth in your hands? *(Short Stories by Jesus, 2014, chapter on the laborers in the vineyard)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Levine explicitly critiques anti-Jewish allegorical readings and highlights the economic dimension in this chapter -->
