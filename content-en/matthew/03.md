# Matthew 3 — John the Baptist and the Baptism of Jesus

## Story

Thirty years pass in silence. Then John the Baptist appears, preaching in the wilderness of Judea: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near" (3:1–2). Matthew says John is the very one Isaiah foretold: "A voice of one calling in the wilderness, 'Prepare the way for the Lord'" (3:3).

John wore clothing of camel's hair with a leather belt around his waist, and his food was locusts and wild honey (3:4). That appearance reminded people of the prophet Elijah. Crowds poured out to him from Jerusalem, all Judea, and the whole region around the Jordan. Confessing their sins, they were baptized by him in the Jordan River (3:5–6).

Seeing many Pharisees and Sadducees coming as well, John speaks bluntly: "You brood of vipers, who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?" Produce fruit worthy of repentance. Do not presume to tell yourselves that Abraham is your father; God can raise up children for Abraham out of these very stones (3:7–9).

He warns them: "The axe lies ready at the root of the trees"; every tree that does not produce good fruit will be cut down and thrown into the fire (3:10). Ancestry saves no one; what God looks for is a changed life.

John speaks of himself: I baptize you with water for repentance. But the One coming after me is more powerful than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire. His winnowing fork is in His hand, and He will clear His threshing floor, gathering His wheat into the barn and burning up the chaff with unquenchable fire (3:11–12).

Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan to be baptized by John. John tried to stop Him: "I need to be baptized by You, and do You come to me?" (3:13–14).

Jesus answered: let it be so now, "for in this way it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness." Then John consented (3:15). The sinless One stood shoulder to shoulder with people coming to confess their sins.

As soon as Jesus came up out of the water, the heavens were opened, and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and resting on Him. And a voice from heaven said, "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased!" (3:16–17). Father, Son, and Spirit are present together in a single moment.

## Key Takeaways

**Repentance is turning around, not merely feeling sorry.** John does not ask people simply to feel bad about their sins and move on. He demands that they "produce fruit worthy of repentance" (3:8). True repentance changes the direction of a life.

**No one is saved by family pedigree.** The Pharisees and Sadducees assumed that being Abraham's descendants was enough. John tells them God can turn stones into Abraham's children (3:9). What God looks at is the heart, not a birth certificate.

**John is only the forerunner.** He was famous and drew huge crowds, yet he always pointed to the One coming after him, insisting he was unworthy even to carry His sandals (3:11). The best servant of God is the one who helps others see God rather than seeing the servant.

**Jesus steps into line with sinners.** He had no sin to confess, yet He walked down into the same waters alongside people confessing theirs (3:13–15). From His very first public act, He chose to stand on the side of the people He came to save.

**Father, Son, and Spirit appear together.** The Son stands in the water, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father speaks from heaven (3:16–17). Before Jesus performs a single miracle or preaches a single sermon, the Father calls Him His beloved Son. Love comes before work.

## Deeper Meaning

**Elijah returns.** Malachi, the final book of the Old Testament, promised that God would send Elijah before the great day of the Lord (Malachi 4:5). John's hairy garment and leather belt mirror Elijah's exact dress (2 Kings 1:8). Later, Jesus states plainly that John "is the Elijah who was to come" (11:14).

**The wilderness and a new way.** Isaiah spoke of a highway opened in the wilderness for the Lord to lead His people back from exile (Isaiah 40:3). John stands in that very wilderness beside the Jordan, the river Israel once crossed to enter the Promised Land. A new exodus is beginning.

**The voice from heaven blends three Scriptures.** "My Son" recalls the psalm of God's anointed king (Psalms 2:7). "In whom I am well pleased" recalls the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah, upon whom God's Spirit rests (Isaiah 42:1). "Beloved Son" echoes Isaac on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:2). This King is also the Suffering Servant and the only Son who will be offered up.

**The Spirit over the waters.** On the opening page of Scripture, the Spirit of God hovered over the waters when God created the world (Genesis 1:2). At the Jordan, the Spirit again descends over the water. Many readers see this as the mark of a new creation.

**Numbered with the transgressors.** Isaiah foretold that the Servant would be counted among the wicked and bear the sins of many (Isaiah 53:12). Jesus' baptism is the opening step along that road—the road Paul sums up by saying that God made Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf (2 Corinthians 5:21).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why would a sinless man receive a baptism of repentance?** Even John found it startling (3:14). Jesus simply replied, "it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness" (3:15). Commentators understand this in several complementary ways: He obeyed every command of God, including John's call; He identified completely with a sinful people; or He inaugurated His work of bringing the Father's saving plan to completion.

**What is "the kingdom of heaven"?** Matthew regularly writes "kingdom of heaven," whereas Mark and Luke write "kingdom of God." Jewish speakers avoided pronouncing God's name directly and often said "heaven" in its place. It does not mean a distant place up in the clouds, but God's royal reign breaking in upon the earth.

**"Baptism with fire."** Fire here may mean purifying fire, like the tongues of fire at Pentecost (Acts 2:3). Yet the next verse speaks of fire burning up chaff (3:12), pointing toward judgment. Many interpreters believe John means both: the same fire purifies the repentant and consumes the hardened.

**The dove does not mean the Spirit is a bird.** Matthew says the Spirit descended "like" a dove (3:16). It is a visible sign of an invisible reality.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, writes about John the Baptist independently of the Gospels. He calls John a good man who urged Jews to practice righteousness toward one another and piety toward God. According to Josephus, John's baptism was not for forgiving sins, but for cleansing the body once the soul had already been purified by upright living. *(Antiquities of the Jews, book 18)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus's passage on John in book 18 is widely cited -->

**The Community Rule**, discovered at Qumran near the Dead Sea, belonged to a Jewish group living in John's time. They also quoted Isaiah 40:3—"prepare the way"—to explain why they withdrew into the wilderness to study the Law. John uses the same verse, yet instead of retreating from the nation, he calls all the people to repent and welcome the One coming after him. *(Community Rule, column 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Qumran's use of Isaiah 40:3 in column 8 of the Community Rule is widely noted by scholars -->

**Tertullian**, a theologian in Carthage, North Africa, around AD 200, wrote the earliest surviving treatise on baptism. He compares the dove at the Jordan to the dove Noah sent out after the flood, returning with an olive branch to announce that wrath had passed. In the same way, the Holy Spirit descends like a dove to announce God's peace to the world. *(On Baptism, chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: chapter 8 compares the Jordan dove with Noah's dove -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explains "fulfill all righteousness" in terms of obedience. Having taken the role of a servant, Jesus kept everything the Father commanded, including baptism. Calvin adds that He was baptized to sanctify baptism itself and to assure us that we are united with Him. *(Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Matthew 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin interprets verse 15 along lines of obedience and union; uncertain which emphasis is primary -->

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, asks why the sinless One needed a baptism of repentance. He answers that Jesus needed no forgiveness of sins, yet He observed what God commanded through the prophet John, just as He observed the rest of the Law. The voice from heaven and the dove were given so the crowd would realize He was no ordinary sinner confessing alongside them. *(Homilies on Matthew, on Matthew 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Chrysostom addresses this question in his homilies on chapter 3; uncertain of the exact homily number and phrasing -->
