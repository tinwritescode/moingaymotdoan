# Matthew 26 — The Last Supper, Gethsemane, and Three Denials

## Story

When Jesus finished saying all these things, He told His disciples: two days from now is the Passover, and the Son of Man will be handed over to be crucified. At that very time, the chief priests and elders assembled at the palace of Caiaphas the high priest, plotting to arrest Jesus stealthily and kill Him. Yet they said: not during the feast, or there may be a riot among the people (26:1–5).

In Bethany, at the house of Simon the leper, a woman came with an alabaster jar of very expensive perfume and poured it on His head as He reclined at the table. Indignant, the disciples said, "Why this waste?" That perfume could have been sold at a high price and the money given to the poor. Jesus replied: why are you bothering this woman? She has done a beautiful thing to Me. The poor you will always have with you, yet you will not always have Me. Pouring this perfume on My body, she prepared Me for burial. Wherever this gospel is preached throughout the whole world, what she has done will also be told in memory of her (26:6–13).

Then Judas Iscariot, one of the Twelve, went to the chief priests and asked: what are you willing to give me if I hand Him over to you? They counted out for him "thirty pieces of silver." From then on he looked for an opportunity to betray Him (26:14–16).

On the first day of Unleavened Bread, the disciples asked Him where He wanted to eat the Passover. He sent them into the city to a certain man's house with the message: the Teacher says My appointed time is near; I will celebrate the Passover at your house with My disciples. They did as He instructed and prepared the meal (26:17–19).

When evening came, He reclined at table with the Twelve. While they were eating, He said: truly one of you will betray Me. Deeply saddened, they began asking Him one after another, "Surely not I, Lord?" He replied: the one who has dipped his hand into the bowl with Me will betray Me. The Son of Man goes just as it is written of Him, yet woe to that man who betrays the Son of Man; it would be better for him if he had never been born. Judas asked: surely not I, Rabbi? Jesus replied, "You have said it yourself" (26:20–25).

While they were eating, Jesus took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and gave it to His disciples: "Take and eat; this is My body." Then He took the cup, gave thanks, and offered it to them: drink from it, all of you, "for this is My blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins." I tell you, I will not drink of this fruit of the vine from now on until that day when I drink it new with you in My Father's kingdom. After singing a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives (26:26–30).

He told them: tonight you will all fall away on account of Me, for it is written: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered. Yet after I have risen, I will go ahead of you into Galilee. Peter replied: even if everyone else falls away because of You, I never will. Jesus answered: this very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny Me three times. Peter insisted: even if I have to die with You, I will never deny You. All the other disciples said the same (26:31–35).

He arrived at a place called Gethsemane, told the disciples to sit there, and took Peter and the two sons of Zebedee along. He began to grow sorrowful and deeply distressed: "My soul is consumed with sorrow to the point of death. Stay here and keep watch with Me." Going a little farther, He fell facedown and prayed: My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me; yet not as I will, but as You will (26:36–39).

Returning, He found the disciples asleep and said to Peter: couldn't you keep watch with Me for one hour? Watch and pray so that you will not enter into temptation; "the spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak." He went away a second time and prayed: My Father, if this cup cannot pass unless I drink it, may Your will be done. Coming back, He found them sleeping again because their eyes were heavy. He left them and prayed a third time, saying the same words. Returning, He told them: the hour has come, and the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners. Rise, let us go; here comes My betrayer (26:40–46).

While He was still speaking, Judas arrived with a large crowd armed with swords and clubs, sent by the chief priests and elders. He had arranged a signal: the one I kiss is the man; arrest him. Stepping up to Jesus, Judas said, "Greetings, Rabbi!" and kissed Him. Jesus said: friend, do what you came for. Then the men stepped forward and seized Him (26:47–50).

One of Jesus' companions drew his sword and struck off the ear of the high priest's servant. Jesus told him: put your sword back in its place, "for all who draw the sword will die by the sword." Do you think I cannot call on My Father, and He will at once put at My disposal more than twelve legions of angels? Yet how then would the Scriptures be fulfilled? He addressed the crowd: have you come out with swords and clubs to capture Me like a rebel, when day after day I sat teaching in the temple courts and you did not arrest Me? At that moment all the disciples deserted Him and fled (26:51–56).

They led Him away to the house of Caiaphas, where the scribes and elders had gathered. Peter followed at a distance and sat in the courtyard with the guards to see the outcome (26:57–58). The Sanhedrin sought false testimony to put Him to death, yet though many false witnesses came forward, they found none. Finally two men stepped up: this man said he can destroy the temple of God and rebuild it in three days. The high priest asked why He did not answer, yet Jesus remained silent (26:59–63).

The high priest said: I put you under oath by the living God—tell us whether you are the Christ, the Son of God! Jesus answered, "You have said it yourself." And from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven. The high priest tore his robes: he has spoken blasphemy! Why do we need any more witnesses? They answered, "He deserves to die!" Then they spit in His face, struck Him with their fists, slapped Him, and mocked: prophesy to us, Christ—who hit You? (26:63–68).

Peter was still sitting outside in the courtyard. A servant girl approached him: you also were with Jesus of Galilee. He denied it before everyone: "I do not know what you are talking about." Moving out to the gateway, another servant girl saw him and told those standing there: this man was with Jesus of Nazareth. Again he denied it with an oath: "I do not know the man!" After a little while, bystanders came up: surely you are one of them too; your accent gives you away. Then he began to call down curses and swore, "I do not know the man!" Immediately a rooster crowed (26:69–74).

Peter remembered Jesus' word: "Before the rooster crows, you will deny Me three times." He went outside and "wept bitterly" (26:75).

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus walks into death willingly.** He foretells the exact day He will be handed over (26:2), names His betrayer (26:21), and predicts Peter's denial (26:34). He could summon twelve legions of angels and chooses not to (26:53). Nobody wrenches His life away; He lays it down freely.

**Love does not calculate cost.** The disciples see the perfume jar as sheer waste (26:8). Jesus sees a beautiful act honoring His coming burial. Sometimes loving Christ means offering something bystanders consider extravagant.

**The bread and cup seal a new covenant.** Inside the Passover meal, Jesus calls the bread His body and the cup "My blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28). His death is no tragic mishap, but a covenant-making sacrifice.

**Gethsemane proves Jesus is truly human.** Overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death, He pleads for the cup to pass before surrendering: "yet not as I will, but as You will" (26:39). Obedience never means feeling no pain; it means choosing the Father's will right inside the pain.

**Anyone can fall—even the fiercest loyalist.** Peter swears he will never fold, then caves three times before a servant girl (26:69–74). "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak" (26:41). Yet Peter's bitter tears lead down a different road from Judas: one weeps and returns; the other does not.

## Deeper Meaning

**Blood of the covenant.** At Mount Sinai, Moses sprinkled blood over Israel saying, "This is the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you" (Exodus 24:8). Jeremiah later promised a day when God would "make a new covenant" (Jeremiah 31:31). Jesus' words over the cup unite both promises.

**The Passover lamb.** On the night Israel left Egypt, lamb's blood painted on doorframes marked homes for rescue (Exodus 12:13). Jesus chooses that exact festival to unveil the meaning of His death. Paul teaches that every time believers eat the bread and drink the cup, they "proclaim the Lord's death until He comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26).

**Bearing the sins of many.** The words "for many" echo Isaiah's Suffering Servant who "bore the sin of many and made intercession for the transgressors" (Isaiah 53:12).

**The friend sharing bread.** The psalmist lamented, "Even my close friend… he who shared my bread, has lifted up his heel against me" (Psalms 41:9). Judas dips into the very same dish before betraying Him. Thirty pieces of silver likewise recalls Zechariah, where the shepherd's wages are weighed out cheaply (Zechariah 11:12).

**Strike the shepherd, scatter the sheep.** Jesus quotes Zechariah: "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (Zechariah 13:7). The disciples scatter into the night on cue (26:56).

**Prayers offered with tears.** Hebrews notes that during His earthly days, Christ "offered up prayers and petitions with loud cries and tears" (Hebrews 5:7)—a vivid portrait of Gethsemane.

**Three denials, three questions.** Peter denies Him three times beside a fire. After rising from the dead, Jesus asks him three times, "Do you love Me?" and commissions him to feed His sheep (John 21:17). Peter's story does not end at the rooster's crow.

## Notes & Nuances

**Was the Last Supper a Passover meal?** Matthew, Mark, and Luke explicitly call it the Passover meal (26:17–19). Yet John reports that the next morning religious leaders avoided entering Pilate's headquarters so they could still "eat the Passover" (John 18:28), placing Jesus' death at the exact hour Passover lambs were slaughtered. Proposed solutions include two different Jewish calendars in use or Jesus holding an early Passover meal a day ahead. No single explanation satisfies everyone.

**Was the trial legal?** Later Jewish legal codes forbade holding capital trials at night or on the eve of a festival, making the midnight proceeding at Caiaphas's house look blatantly illegal. Some conclude the trial broke Jewish law outright; others view the nighttime session as an informal preliminary hearing before the formal morning council (27:1). Still others note those codes were compiled nearly two centuries later and may not reflect first-century practice.

**What does "this is My body" mean?** Christian traditions interpret this differently. Some believe the bread literally becomes Christ's body; others believe Christ is truly present in, with, and under the bread; still others view the bread as a sign of His body while Christ is spiritually present by the Spirit as believers eat in faith. This question became a major fault line during the Reformation.

**"All who draw the sword will die by the sword."** Some read this as an absolute prohibition against all armed force. Others understand Jesus specifically to forbid defending Him or advancing God's kingdom through violence. Either way, Christ's kingdom is never built by the sword.

**Why did Judas betray Him?** Matthew simply reports him asking for payment (26:15). Thirty pieces of silver was the statutory compensation for a slave gored by an ox (Exodus 21:32)—hardly a fortune. Readers have speculated about political disillusionment, greed, or satanic influence as Luke and John note. Scripture leaves the full darkness of his motives unplumbed.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the Jewish oral law code compiled around AD 200, stipulates that capital cases must be tried during daylight and that a guilty verdict cannot be pronounced on the same day—ruling out trials on the eve of a Sabbath or festival. It also requires two witnesses whose testimonies agree completely. **Tertullian**, a theologian in Carthage, North Africa, around AD 200, wrote against Marcion, who denied that Christ possessed a real fleshly body. Tertullian reasoned that when Jesus said "this is My body," He meant "this is a figure of My body"—and there cannot be a figure unless there is a real physical body to represent. *(Against Marcion, book 4, chapter 40)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the phrase "figura corporis mei" in Adversus Marcionem book 4 chapter 40 is frequently cited in Eucharistic debates -->

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, met Ulrich Zwingli of Zurich at Marburg in 1529 to debate the Lord's Supper. Zwingli read "this is" as "this signifies." Luther chalked the Latin words "This is My body" onto the table and refused to budge from the word "is." The two agreed on almost every other point of Christian faith, yet parted ways over this single verb. *(Marburg Colloquy, 1529)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: contemporary accounts record Luther writing "Hoc est corpus meum" on the table at Marburg -->

**Maximus the Confessor**, a seventh-century Greek monk and theologian, appealed to Gethsemane to defend the belief that Christ possessed both a divine will and a genuine human will. Saying "not as I will" reveals a true human will that shrinks from death yet freely surrenders to the Father. Had Christ lacked a human will, Maximus argued, He could never have healed and saved ours. *(Disputation with Pyrrhus)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Maximus uses Gethsemane to defend Christ's two wills; recalled in Disputation with Pyrrhus and related opuscula, uncertain of exact location -->

**Blaise Pascal**, the seventeenth-century French mathematician and thinker, wrote a haunting meditation on Gethsemane. Jesus sought comfort from His three closest friends, and they slept.
