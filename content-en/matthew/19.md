# Matthew 19 — Marriage, Children, and the Rich Young Man

## Story

When Jesus finished these sayings, He left Galilee and went into the region of Judea beyond the Jordan. Large crowds followed Him, and He healed their sick there (19:1–2).

Pharisees came to test Him: is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife for any and every reason? He answered from Genesis: from the beginning the Creator made them male and female, and said that a man shall leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh. So they are no longer two, but one flesh. "Therefore what God has joined together, let man not separate" (19:3–6).

They asked: why then did Moses command a man to give a certificate of divorce and send her away? He answered: Moses permitted you to divorce your wives because your hearts were hard, "but it was not this way from the beginning." Anyone who divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another woman commits adultery (19:7–9).

The disciples remarked: if that is the situation between husband and wife, it is better not to marry. He replied: not everyone can accept this word, only those to whom it has been given. Some are eunuchs from birth, some were made eunuchs by men, and others have chosen celibacy for the sake of the kingdom of heaven. The one who can accept this should accept it (19:10–12).

People brought little children to Him so He might lay His hands on them and pray, yet the disciples rebuked them. Jesus said, "Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them! For the kingdom of heaven belongs to such as these." After laying His hands on them, He went on from there (19:13–15).

A man approached Him and asked: Teacher, what good thing must I do to inherit eternal life? He replied: why do you ask Me about what is good? There is only One who is good. If you want to enter life, keep the commandments. Which ones? the man asked. Jesus listed them: do not murder, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not give false testimony, honor your father and mother, and "love your neighbor as yourself" (19:16–19).

The young man answered: all these I have kept; what do I still lack? Jesus said: if you want to be complete, go, sell your possessions and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow Me. Hearing this, the young man walked away sorrowful, because he had great wealth (19:20–22).

Jesus told His disciples: truly it is hard for a rich person to enter the kingdom of heaven. "It is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God." Astonished, the disciples asked: who then can be saved? Looking at them, He replied: with man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible (19:23–26).

Peter spoke up: look, we have left everything to follow You; what then will there be for us? He answered: at the renewal of all things, when the Son of Man sits on His glorious throne, you who have followed Me will also sit on twelve thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel. Everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or children or fields for My name's sake will receive many times as much and will inherit eternal life. "But many who are first will be last, and the last will be first" (19:27–30).

## Key Takeaways

**Marriage is joined by God, not an easily broken contract.** The Pharisees ask: what grounds allow us to dismiss a wife? Jesus refuses to hand them a list of loopholes and takes them back to page one of Scripture: God made male and female, and the two become one flesh (19:4–6). The right question is not "when am I allowed to quit?" but "what did God intend from the start?"

**Singleness is a valid calling too.** When the disciples say it is better not to marry, Jesus does not mock them. He affirms that some are gifted to remain unmarried for the sake of the kingdom of heaven (19:12). Both marriage and singleness can be faithful ways of serving God.

**Children need no credentials to approach Jesus.** The disciples try to shoo them away, assuming the Teacher has more important business (19:13). Jesus says the kingdom of heaven belongs to people just like them (19:14). Coming to God means coming empty-handed.

**Wealth can wrap tightly around the heart.** The rich young man keeps the commandments earnestly and genuinely wants eternal life. Yet the instant Jesus touches his possessions, he turns away sad (19:22). What he lacked was not one more good deed, but willingness to place Christ above everything he owned.

**What is impossible for humans is possible for God.** A camel threading a needle's eye is flatly impossible (19:24). Jesus does not water down the standard; instead He points to the sole source of hope: salvation is God's miracle, never a human achievement (19:26).

## Deeper Meaning

**Returning to the beginning.** Jesus links two Genesis texts together: God created humanity male and female, and a man leaves father and mother and clings to his wife (Genesis 1:27; 2:24). He reads the Law of Moses in the light of original creation. Malachi similarly declares God's hatred of treacherous divorce (Malachi 2:16).

**Moses' divorce law was meant to shield wives.** The Law permitted a husband to write "a certificate of divorce" if he found "something indecent" in his wife (Deuteronomy 24:1). That document legally freed a dismissed woman to remarry instead of being left stranded. Jesus explains it was a concession to hard hearts, never God's original ideal (19:8).

**The childless still have a place in God's house.** Under older regulations, eunuchs were barred from the assembly. Yet Isaiah promised them a memorial inside God's house and "a name better than that of sons and daughters" (Isaiah 56:4–5). Jesus' affirmation of those who remain unmarried for the kingdom stands squarely on that promise.

**Paul's calling to singleness.** Paul wished everyone could be as free to serve as he was, yet acknowledged that "each has his own gift from God" (1 Corinthians 7:7). That mirrors verse 19:11: "only those to whom it has been given."

**The renewal of all things.** Jesus speaks of a coming renewal when the Son of Man sits enthroned (19:28). Revelation brings that promise to its climax as the One seated on the throne proclaims, "Behold, I make all things new" (Revelation 21:5). Everything disciples surrender will be returned richly in that world.

## Notes & Nuances

**The exception "except for sexual immorality" appears only in Matthew.** Mark and Luke record Jesus' teaching without that clause (Mark 10:11–12). Christian traditions interpret this differently: Catholics forbid remarriage while a spouse still lives; most Protestants and Eastern Orthodox allow remarriage for an innocent spouse in cases of adultery. Some scholars argue the Greek word here refers specifically to unlawful marriages between close relatives.

**This passage does not trap abuse victims in dangerous marriages.** Jesus was confronting men who dismissed wives effortlessly, not addressing spouses enduring violence. Most pastors today agree that protecting the safety of an abused spouse comes first. Paul likewise recognizes situations not covered in this brief exchange (1 Corinthians 7:15).

**There was no "Needle's Eye Gate" in Jerusalem.** A popular sermon legend claims Jerusalem had a low pedestrian gate called the Eye of the Needle that camels had to crawl through on their knees. No ancient evidence for any such gate exists. Jewish proverbs similarly spoke of an elephant passing through a needle's eye to picture sheer impossibility. Jesus deliberately chose an impossible image so the disciples would ask, "Who then can be saved?" (19:25).

**"Why do you ask Me about what is good?" is not a denial of His divinity.** Jesus prompts the young man to think carefully about what "good" really means (19:17). Only God is truly good. Once the young man perceives who Jesus actually is, Jesus' closing command—"come, follow Me"—takes on its full weight.

**Does this require every Christian to sell everything?** Jesus spoke this specific command to one man held captive by his fortune. Others, like Zacchaeus, gave away half (Luke 19:8). Still, anyone rushing to say "this verse doesn't apply to me" would do well to stop and examine their own heart.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the collection of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, records the exact debate the Pharisees threw at Jesus. The school of Shammai allowed divorce only for marital unfaithfulness. The school of Hillel permitted divorce even if a wife burned dinner. Rabbi Akiva went further, allowing divorce if a husband simply found someone prettier. The Pharisees' question—"for any and every reason" in 19:3—sits squarely inside that controversy. *(Mishnah, Gittin 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the closing section of Gittin detailing Shammai, Hillel, and Akiva is widely cited on 19:3 -->

**David Instone-Brewer**, a British scholar specializing in Jewish law, argues that the phrase "for any reason" in 19:3 was the technical label for an "any-cause" divorce invented by the school of Hillel out of Deuteronomy's phrase "something indecent." On his view, Jesus repudiated that arbitrary "any-cause" divorce without revoking other Mosaic grounds such as neglect or abandonment. Many scholars remain cautious about how broadly he extends that conclusion. *(Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible, 2002)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the "any-cause" divorce thesis is the centerpiece of this book -->

**Eusebius**, the fourth-century church historian, recounts that Origen, the brilliant Alexandrian theologian, took 19:12 literally in his youth and castrated himself. Eusebius describes it as an act of immature zeal, noting that Origen himself later interpreted the verse spiritually. The episode is often retold as a cautionary tale about literalistic Bible reading. *(Church History, book 6, chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Eusebius's account of Origen in book 6 chapter 8 is widely cited -->

**Athanasius**, fourth-century bishop of Alexandria, wrote the biography of Antony, father of Christian monasticism. He recounts how Antony, a wealthy young man, walked into church just as the reader proclaimed, "If you want to be perfect, go, sell your possessions." Taking the verse as spoken directly to him, Antony sold his lands, gave the proceeds to the poor, and headed into the desert. Verse 19:21 thus sparked the entire monastic movement. *(Life of Antony, chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the scene of Antony hearing 19:21 in chapter 2 is widely cited -->
