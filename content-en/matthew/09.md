# Matthew 9 — Authority to Forgive, the Tax Collector, and Crowds Like Sheep Without a Shepherd

## Story

Jesus stepped into a boat and crossed back to His own town. Some men brought to Him a paralyzed man lying on a mat. Seeing their faith, Jesus said to the paralytic: take courage, son; your sins are forgiven (9:1–2).

At this, some of the scribes said to themselves, "This man is blaspheming." Knowing their thoughts, Jesus asked: which is easier to say, "Your sins are forgiven," or "Get up and walk"? So that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, He told the paralytic: get up, pick up your mat, and go home. The man got up and went home. The crowds were filled with awe and glorified God, who had given such authority to men (9:3–8).

Leaving that place, He saw a man named Matthew sitting at the tax booth, and said to him, "Follow Me." Matthew got up and followed Him (9:9). Tax collectors worked for Rome and were despised by their own people as traitors.

While Jesus was dining at the house, many tax collectors and sinners came and ate with Him and His disciples. The Pharisees asked His disciples: why does your teacher eat with such people? Hearing this, Jesus replied: it is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick. Go and learn what this means: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners (9:10–13).

John's disciples came and asked: why do we and the Pharisees fast, while Your disciples do not? He answered: how can the wedding guests mourn while the bridegroom is still with them? The days will come when the bridegroom will be taken from them; then they will fast. No one sews a patch of unshrunk cloth on an old garment, nor pours new wine into old wineskins; new wine must be poured into new wineskins (9:14–17).

While He was still speaking, a synagogue ruler came and knelt before Him: my daughter has just died; come and lay Your hand on her, and she will live. Jesus got up and went with him, and His disciples followed (9:18–19).

Along the way, a woman who had suffered from bleeding for twelve years came up behind Him and touched the fringe of His cloak, saying to herself: if only I touch His cloak, I will be healed. Jesus turned, saw her, and said: take courage, daughter; your faith has healed you. And the woman was healed from that very hour (9:20–22).

Arriving at the synagogue ruler's house, He saw the flute players and a noisy crowd. He told them: go away, the girl is not dead, she is asleep. They laughed at Him. Once the crowd was put outside, He went in and took her by the hand, and the girl got up. News of this spread throughout that whole region (9:23–26).

Two blind men followed Him, crying out, "Have mercy on us, Son of David!" He asked them: do you believe that I am able to do this? They answered: yes, Lord. He touched their eyes: "According to your faith will it be done to you." Their eyes were opened. He sternly warned them to tell no one, yet they went out and spread His fame through the entire region (9:27–31).

Next, people brought Him a mute man who was demon-possessed. When the demon was driven out, the mute man spoke. The crowds marveled: nothing like this has ever been seen in Israel. Yet the Pharisees said, "It is by the prince of demons that He drives out demons" (9:32–34).

Jesus went through all the towns and villages, teaching in their synagogues, proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and sickness. Seeing the crowds, He had compassion on them, because they were harassed and helpless, "like sheep without a shepherd." He said to His disciples, "The harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few." Ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers into His harvest field (9:35–38).

## Key Takeaways

**The deepest sickness is sin.** Friends carried the paralyzed man to Jesus so he could walk again. Yet Jesus' first words are: your sins are forgiven (9:2). He does not dismiss the man's paralyzed legs, yet He sees an even deeper wound that needs healing first. Then He heals the legs to prove His forgiveness is real (9:6).

**Jesus comes for sinners.** He calls a tax collector as a disciple and then shares a meal with his friends (9:9–10). To the Pharisees, that ruined a good reputation. To Jesus, it was simply a doctor's job—going where the sick are (9:12).

**Where Jesus is, a wedding feast is underway.** The question about fasting reveals something startling: Jesus compares Himself to a bridegroom (9:15). Following Him is first of all joy, not a dreary burden. What He brings is new wine that cannot be crammed into old containers (9:17).

**No one is too lowly to make Him stop.** A prominent synagogue official and an unnamed woman shunned for twelve years intersect inside a single story (9:18–22). Jesus makes time for both. He calls the nameless woman "daughter."

**Compassion gives birth to mission.** Seeing the crowds like sheep without a shepherd, Jesus is moved with compassion and tells His disciples to pray for more workers (9:36–38). That prayer leads straight into chapter 10, where those very disciples are the ones sent out.

## Deeper Meaning

**Only God has authority to forgive sins.** Through Isaiah, the Lord declares: I, even I, am He who blots out your transgressions (Isaiah 43:25). That is why the scribes charge Jesus with "blaspheming" (9:3). They caught the exact claim He was making about Himself. The title "Son of Man" also recalls Daniel, where one "like a son of man" is given everlasting dominion (Daniel 7:13–14).

**Mercy above sacrifice.** Jesus quotes Hosea: God desires steadfast love and mercy rather than sacrifice (Hosea 6:6). He will quote this same verse again in chapter 12 (12:7). Rituals are not wrong in themselves, yet whenever they keep people from showing mercy, they have lost their soul.

**The bridegroom is God Himself.** In the Old Testament, God pictures Himself as a bridegroom rejoicing over His bride, Israel (Isaiah 62:5). When Jesus calls Himself the bridegroom, He steps into the place the prophets reserved for the Lord.

**Signs of the Messianic age.** Isaiah promised a day when "the eyes of the blind will be opened" and "the tongue of the mute will shout for joy" (Isaiah 35:5–6). Right in this chapter, the blind see and the mute speak (9:27–33). In chapter 11, Jesus will point to these very deeds to answer John the Baptist (11:4–5).

**Sheep without a shepherd.** Moses begged God to appoint a leader for Israel so the congregation would not be "like sheep without a shepherd" (Numbers 27:17). Ezekiel rebuked false shepherds who let the flock scatter (Ezekiel 34:5) and promised that God Himself would shepherd His sheep. Jesus is that Shepherd.

## Notes & Nuances

**Matthew or Levi?** Mark and Luke tell this same story but name the tax collector Levi son of Alphaeus (Mark 2:14). Most readers understand him to be one person with two names, just as Simon is also called Peter. Early church tradition held that this Matthew authored the Gospel bearing his name, though the book itself never names its writer.

**Was the bleeding woman considered ritually unclean?** Under the Law, a woman with a chronic flow of blood was ceremonially unclean (Leviticus 15:25). Many sermons claim she was completely ostracized by society and that Jesus overturned the purity laws. Jewish scholar Amy-Jill Levine reminds readers that Matthew never mentions uncleanness here at all. The story centers on faith and compassion, not on abolishing Torah.

**The "fringe" of His cloak was likely a tassel.** The Law instructed Israelites to attach tassels to the corners of their garments to remember God's commandments (Numbers 15:38). That is what she touched. Yet Jesus makes clear that "your faith" healed her, not a piece of cloth (9:22).

**"Just died" or "at the point of death"?** Here the father says his daughter has just died (9:18). Mark tells it in two stages: the father arrives while she is dying, and household messengers later report her death (Mark 5:23; 5:35). Matthew routinely tells stories briskly, compressing two steps into one.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, notices how Matthew tells his own story. The other Gospels call him by the less familiar name Levi, while Matthew names himself directly and even labels himself "the tax collector" in the list of apostles (10:3). **Bede**, an eighth-century English monk and historian, preached a homily on 9:9. He wrote that Jesus looked at the tax collector with mercy and chose him. Bede's Latin phrase—"by having mercy and by choosing"—was centuries later adopted by Pope Francis as his personal motto. *(Homilies on the Gospels, book 1, Homily 21)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the phrase "miserando atque eligendo" from this homily of Bede is widely cited -->

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, the German pastor executed by the Nazis in 1945, opens his chapter on the call to discipleship with the calling of the tax collector. He observes that the Gospel reports none of the man's inner deliberations before following. There is only the call, and then obedience. Bonhoeffer writes: only he who believes is obedient, and only he who is obedient believes. *(The Cost of Discipleship, chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the chapter on the call begins with Levi in Mark 2:14, and the saying "only he who believes is obedient" is famous -->

**Claude Montefiore**, an early twentieth-century British Jewish scholar, wrote a commentary on the first three Gospels. He held that most of Jesus' teachings already existed within Jewish tradition, yet pointed to one genuinely new element: Jesus actively went seeking sinners instead of waiting for them to repent first.
