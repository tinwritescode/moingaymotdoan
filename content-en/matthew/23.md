# Matthew 23 — Seven Woes

## Story

Jesus spoke to the crowds and to His disciples: the scribes and Pharisees sit in Moses' seat. So practice and observe everything they tell you, yet do not imitate what they do, "for they preach, but do not practice" (23:1–3).

They tie up heavy, unbearable loads and pile them on people's shoulders, while refusing to lift a finger themselves to move them. Everything they do is performed to be seen by others: they widen their phylacteries, lengthen the tassels on their robes, love the seats of honor at banquets and the front seats in synagogues, and relish being greeted in marketplaces and called "Rabbi" (23:4–7).

But you are not to be called "Rabbi," for you have only one Teacher, and you are all brothers. Call no one on earth "father," for you have one Father, who is in heaven. Nor are you to be called "master," for you have one Master, the Christ. The greatest among you shall be your servant. "For whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted" (23:8–12).

Then He pronounces a series of "Woe to you" indictments. Woe to you, because you shut the door of the kingdom of heaven in people's faces—you do not enter yourselves, nor let those trying to enter go in. Woe to you, because you devour widows' houses and for a show make lengthy prayers. Woe to you, because you travel over land and sea to win a single convert, and when you succeed you make them twice as much a child of hell as yourselves (23:13–15).

Woe to you, blind guides. You say swearing by the temple means nothing, yet swearing by the temple's gold binds you; swearing by the altar means nothing, yet swearing by the gift on the altar binds you. Blind fools! Which is greater: the gold, or the temple that makes the gold sacred? The gift, or the altar that makes the gift sacred? Anyone who swears by the temple swears by it and by the One who dwells in it; anyone who swears by heaven swears by God's throne and by the One seated upon it (23:16–22).

Woe to you, because you tithe mint, dill, and cumin, yet have neglected the weightier matters of the Law: justice, mercy, and faithfulness. You should have practiced the latter without neglecting the former. "You blind guides! You strain out a gnat but swallow a camel" (23:23–24).

Woe to you, because you scrub the outside of cup and dish while inside they are full of greed and self-indulgence. Clean the inside first, and the outside will become clean too. Woe to you, because you are like whitewashed tombs, handsome on the outside yet filled inside with dead bones and every uncleanness. Outwardly you appear righteous to people, yet inwardly you are full of hypocrisy and wickedness (23:25–28).

Woe to you, because you build tombs for the prophets and decorate the graves of the righteous, saying: had we lived in our ancestors' days, we never would have shared in shedding the prophets' blood. By saying that, you testify against yourselves that you are descendants of those who murdered the prophets. Fill up, then, the measure of your ancestors! "You snakes! You brood of vipers!" How will you escape being condemned to hell? (23:29–33).

Therefore I am sending you prophets, wise men, and scribes; some of them you will kill and crucify, others you will flog in your synagogues and pursue from town to town. Thus all the righteous blood shed on earth, from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah, will come upon this generation (23:34–36).

Then fierce indictment melts into weeping: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem," you who kill the prophets and stone those sent to you! How often I have longed to gather your children together as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, yet you were not willing. Look, your house is left to you desolate. You will not see Me again until you say, "Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord" (23:37–39).

## Key Takeaways

**Keep true teaching even when teachers fail.** Jesus never tells the crowds to toss out God's Law because religious teachers are hypocrites. He tells them to follow what is right without copying hypocritical behavior (23:3). Truth does not lose its worth because its spokesmen fail to live up to it.

**Religion staged for applause is hollow.** Phylacteries, tassels, seats of honor, marketplace greetings—none of these things is evil in itself, yet each turns rotten the moment it becomes a tool for self-display (23:5–7). Inside Christ's community there is only one Teacher, and everyone else stands as brothers and sisters (23:8).

**Do not strain out a gnat and swallow a camel.** The Pharisees meticulously measured every tenth leaf of mint while ignoring what matters most: justice, mercy, and faithfulness (23:23–24). Their failure was not being careful, but putting minor rules above central realities.

**Outer polish cannot hide inner rot.** Cups washed on the outside yet packed with greed inside, tombs freshly whitewashed over decaying bones (23:25–28). God looks into the heart, so cleansing must start from within.

**Indictment ends in tears.** After seven scorching woes, Jesus does not slam the door—He laments like a mother hen longing to shelter her chicks under her wings (23:37). His anger is the wounded anger of love, never cold hostility.

## Deeper Meaning

**What God truly requires.** The three weightier matters Jesus highlights echo Micah's famous summary: "to act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). The prophets had been saying this for centuries.

**Tassels meant to remind, not to boast.** The Law instructed Israel to bind God's words on hand and forehead (Deuteronomy 6:8) and sew tassels onto garment hems so seeing them would call God's commands to mind (Numbers 15:38–39). Reminders designed to point toward God had been repurposed to draw attention to oneself.

**From Abel to Zechariah.** Abel was the first righteous person murdered in Scripture (Genesis 4:8). Zechariah was stoned in the temple courtyard (2 Chronicles 24:21). In the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles stands as the very last book, so these two deaths span the Old Testament from cover to cover. Dying, Zechariah cried out for God to avenge him (2 Chronicles 24:22). The story will turn completely when another Man's blood is shed for forgiveness instead.

**Beneath God's wings.** Sheltering wings are an Old Testament image for God Himself. Boaz blessed Ruth for taking "refuge under the wings of the LORD" (Ruth 2:12), and the psalmist sang, "under His wings you will find refuge" (Psalms 91:4). Jesus speaks of Himself using imagery reserved for the Lord.

**A desolate house, then a future welcome.** Jeremiah warned that if kings refused to listen, "this house will become a ruin" (Jeremiah 22:5). Yet Jesus does not end in despair. He quotes Psalms 118, the very greeting crowds had just sung at His entry (Psalms 118:26), hinting at a day when Jerusalem will finally welcome Him.

**Weeping over the city.** Luke records that as Jesus approached Jerusalem, He looked at the city and wept over it (Luke 19:41). The lament in 23:37 beats with that exact same heartbroken love.

## Notes & Nuances

**This is not a condemnation of the Jewish people.** Jesus was a Jew speaking to Jews about specific religious leaders within His own nation, echoing the fierce self-critique of Israel's own prophets. Tragically, this chapter has been weaponized across history to fuel antisemitism. That utterly misreads the text: its target is religious hypocrisy, a sickness thriving in every religion—the Christian church included.

**Forbidding the title "father" does not ban calling your own dad "father."** Jesus rejects honorific titles used to elevate oneself above fellow believers. Paul still called himself a spiritual father to the Corinthians (1 Corinthians 4:15). What Jesus forbids is the hunger to dominate others, not an isolated vocabulary word.

**Which Zechariah?** Matthew calls him "son of Berechiah." Yet the Zechariah stoned in the temple court was the son of Jehoiada the priest (2 Chronicles 24:20), whereas Zechariah son of Berechiah was the prophet who wrote the book of Zechariah (Zechariah 1:1). Commentators variously suggest a scribal conflation, that Jehoiada was his grandfather, or that another Zechariah is in view.

**Verse 23:14 is absent from many ancient manuscripts.** The woe about devouring widows' houses appears in traditional Bibles, yet is missing from the oldest Greek manuscripts of Matthew. Many modern translations place it in a footnote because it closely matches lines in Mark and Luke and was likely inserted later by scribes.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, stresses that Jesus begins by telling the crowds to obey true teaching before criticizing the teachers. He wanted to cut off in advance the excuse of people who abandon God's Law simply because its teachers behave hypocritically. **Jerome**, the late fourth-century translator of the Bible into Latin, examines the puzzle of Zechariah "son of Berechiah." After surveying several explanations, he notes that in the Gospel used by the Nazarenes this verse read "son of Jehoiada." *(Commentary on Matthew, book 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jerome's remark that the Gospel of the Nazarenes read "son of Jehoiada" here is very widely cited -->

**The Talmud**, the collection of rabbinic commentary, itself lists seven types of Pharisees—most of them satirical caricatures. One parades good deeds on his shoulder to be admired; another walks eyes downcast so dramatically he bangs into walls. Only the final type—those who serve out of pure love for God—wins praise. Thus Jewish sages themselves skewered hypocrisy inside their own ranks. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 22b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the catalog of seven types of Pharisees in Sotah 22b is well known and also appears in Jerusalem Talmud Berakhot 9 -->

**Anselm**, the eleventh-century Archbishop of Canterbury, composed a tender prayer built around 23:37. "And you, Jesus, are you not also a mother," he asks, "like a hen gathering her chicks beneath her wings?" He begs to shelter beneath those wings to be warmed and healed. *(Prayer to Saint Paul)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Anselm's imagery of Jesus as mother hen in his tenth prayer, addressed to Saint Paul, is famous -->

**Amy-Jill Levine**, an American Jewish New Testament scholar, warns that preachers routinely turn the Pharisees of this chapter into a caricature standing for everything bad about "Judaism."
