# Matthew 6 — The Sermon on the Mount: Living Before the Father

## Story

Jesus continues teaching: be careful not to practice your righteousness in front of others to be seen by them; if you do, you will have no reward from your Father in heaven (6:1).

When you give to the needy, do not blow a trumpet ahead of you as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and on the streets to be praised by others; they have received their reward in full. When you give, do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your giving may be in secret, and your Father, "who sees what is done in secret," will reward you (6:2–4).

The same goes for prayer. Hypocrites love to stand and pray in the synagogues and on street corners to be seen by everyone. Instead, go into your inner room, shut the door, and pray to your Father who is unseen. Do not babble on with empty repetitions like the Gentiles, who think they will be heard because of their many words, for your Father knows what you need before you ask Him (6:5–8).

Then He teaches them how to pray: "Our Father in heaven, hallowed be Your name. Your kingdom come, Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven." Give us today our daily bread; forgive us our debts as we also have forgiven our debtors; and lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one (6:9–13). He adds: if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you; if you do not forgive, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses (6:14–15).

When you fast, do not put on a gloomy face like the hypocrites so everyone knows you are fasting. Anoint your head and wash your face, so that only your Father, who is unseen, sees your fast (6:16–18).

Next He speaks about wealth: do not store up treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and thieves break in and steal. Store up treasures in heaven. "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (6:19–21). The eye is the lamp of the body: if your eye is healthy, your whole body is full of light; if your eye is bad, your whole body is full of darkness (6:22–23). No one can serve two masters: "You cannot serve both God and money" (6:24).

Therefore, do not worry about your life, what you will eat or drink, or about your body, what you will wear. Look at the birds of the air: they do not sow or reap or store away in barns, yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not much more valuable than they? Can any one of you by worrying add a single hour to your life? (6:25–27).

Consider the lilies of the field: they do not labor or spin, yet not even Solomon in all his splendor was dressed like one of these. If God so clothes the grass of the field, which is here today and thrown into the fire tomorrow, how much more will He clothe you, "you of little faith" (6:28–30).

So do not worry, asking: what shall we eat, what shall we drink, what shall we wear? The Gentiles run after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them. "But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things will be added unto you." Do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself; "Each day has enough trouble of its own" (6:31–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Who are you performing for?** Giving, praying, fasting—all three are good practices, yet all three can turn into stage performances (6:1–18). Jesus does not ask only what we do, but for whom we do it. If we act for applause, that applause is the entire reward.

**Prayer is speaking to a Father.** There is no need for long speeches or ornate phrasing, because the Father already knows our needs before we ask (6:8). The model prayer opens with God's name, God's kingdom, and God's will, before moving on to our bread, forgiveness, and protection (6:9–13).

**Those who receive forgiveness must forgive.** This is the only petition in the prayer that Jesus immediately explains further (6:14–15). A heart clenched tightly around resentment shuts the door to grace as well.

**The heart follows its treasure.** Wherever we place our treasure, our heart goes with it (6:21). Mammon—that is, money and possessions—is not merely a neutral thing; it can become a rival master demanding our service (6:24).

**Worry adds nothing.** Birds and wildflowers do not fret, yet the Father feeds and dresses them (6:26–30). The cure for anxiety is not telling ourselves "stop thinking about it," but seeking God's kingdom first and trusting that our Father knows what we need (6:32–33).

## Deeper Meaning

**God looks at the heart.** When choosing David, the Lord told Samuel that people look at outward appearance, while the Lord looks at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). The phrase "your Father, who sees what is done in secret," repeated three times in this chapter, says the very same thing (6:4; 6:6; 6:18).

**Daily bread recalls the manna.** In the wilderness, God gave Israel manna each morning and instructed them to gather only enough for that day (Exodus 16:4). Proverbs likewise prays, "feed me with the bread that is my portion"—neither so rich as to forget God nor so poor as to steal (Proverbs 30:8–9). Asking for daily bread trains us to depend on God one day at a time.

**Hallowing God's name.** Through Ezekiel, God promised to restore the holiness of His great name, which had been profaned among the nations (Ezekiel 36:23). Praying "hallowed be Your name" is asking God to fulfill His own promise—to make known to everyone who He truly is.

**"Your will be done" in Gethsemane.** The prayer Jesus taught His disciples, He Himself prayed on His darkest night: "not as I will, but as You will" (26:39). He never taught anything He did not live out Himself.

**Casting anxiety on God.** Paul and Peter echo Jesus closely: do not be anxious about anything, but bring every request to God in prayer and thanksgiving (Philippians 4:6), because He cares for you (1 Peter 5:7).

## Notes & Nuances

**The closing doxology is absent from the earliest manuscripts.** Traditional translations include the line "For Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever" (6:13). Most modern translations place it in a footnote because it does not appear in the oldest Greek manuscripts. The early church probably added it when reciting the prayer together in worship, following the Jewish custom of ending prayers with praise.

**"Lead us not into temptation" does not mean God tempts us to sin.** James states clearly that God tempts no one (James 1:13). This petition asks God to spare us from trials beyond our strength and to keep us from falling when testing arrives.

**Verses 14–15 do not mean we "earn" forgiveness.** God does not forgive our sins as payment for forgiving someone else. Rather, anyone who truly realizes how much they have been forgiven cannot cling stubbornly to debts owed to them. The parable of the unmerciful servant in chapter 18 spells this out clearly (18:23–35).

**A "bad eye" is an idiom.** In Jewish speech of that era, a "bad" or "evil eye" meant stinginess, while a "good" or "healthy eye" meant generosity (6:22–23). Placed right between two teachings on money, the saying likely means that greed leaves a person's whole life in darkness.

**Do not worry does not mean do not work.** Birds do not sow or reap, yet they still fly out to forage. Scripture repeatedly praises diligence. What Jesus forbids is the anxious fretting that forgets we have a Father.

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, a theologian in Carthage, North Africa, around AD 200, wrote the earliest surviving commentary on the Lord's Prayer. He calls it a summary of the whole gospel: in just a few brief lines it contains everything Jesus taught about the Father, God's kingdom, sin, and forgiveness. *(On Prayer, chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: calling the Lord's Prayer a summary of the whole gospel appears in chapter 1 and is widely cited -->

**Cyprian**, bishop of Carthage in the third century, draws attention to the word "our." Jesus does not teach us to say "My Father" or "give me," but "Our Father" and "give us." In Cyprian's view, a believer never prays in isolation, but always on behalf of the whole people of God, because all are one. *(On the Lord's Prayer, chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the communal rather than private character of the prayer is a famous section of the treatise -->

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, wrote a catechism for children and ordinary laypeople. He explains "daily bread" as everything the body needs: food, drink, clothing, house, devout spouse and children, good government, favorable weather, peace, and faithful friends. He adds that God gives daily bread even to the wicked; we pray so that we may recognize it and receive it with gratitude. *(Small Catechism, on the fourth petition)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this catalog of what belongs to "daily bread" is a well-known answer in the Small Catechism -->

**Søren Kierkegaard**, the nineteenth-century Danish philosopher, wrote three meditations on the lilies of the field and the birds of the air. He calls them teachers who instruct us in three lessons: silence, obedience, and joy. Human beings grow anxious because they try to live in tomorrow; flowers and birds live wholly in today, before God. *(The Lily of the Field and the Bird of the Air, 1849)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the three meditations explicitly take silence, obedience, and joy as their themes -->

**Joachim Jeremias**, a German New Testament scholar, argued that the word "Father" Jesus used was the Aramaic *Abba*, the intimate word a child used for a father at home. He claimed Jews of that time did not address God that way in prayer, making Jesus' practice strikingly new. Later scholars argued he overstated the case: *Abba* was also used by adult children, and Jews did occasionally address God as Father, though rarely. *(The Prayers of Jesus, 1967)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jeremias's thesis on Abba and the subsequent scholarly responses are widely discussed -->
