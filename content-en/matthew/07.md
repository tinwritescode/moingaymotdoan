# Matthew 7 — The Sermon on the Mount: Two Roads, Two Foundations

## Story

"Do not judge, or you will be judged." In the same way you judge others, you will be judged (7:1–2). Why do you look at the speck in your brother's eye and pay no attention to the plank in your own eye? You hypocrite, first take the plank out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye (7:3–5). Yet He also warns: do not give dogs what is sacred, and do not throw your pearls to pigs (7:6).

"Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened to you." Which father among you, if his son asks for bread, will give him a stone, or if he asks for a fish, will give him a snake? If you, though you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him? (7:7–11).

He sums up the entire Law and the Prophets in a single sentence: in everything, do to others what you would have them do to you (7:12).

"Enter through the narrow gate." Wide is the gate and broad is the road that leads to destruction, and many enter through it. Small is the gate and narrow the road that leads to life, and only a few find it (7:13–14).

Watch out for false prophets, who come in sheep's clothing yet inwardly are ferocious wolves. By their fruit you will recognize them. Nobody picks grapes from thornbushes or figs from thistles. Every good tree bears good fruit, and a bad tree bears bad fruit; every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire (7:15–20).

Not everyone who says to Him, "Lord, Lord," will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of the Father in heaven. On that day many will say: we prophesied in Your name, drove out demons, and performed many miracles. He will tell them plainly, "I never knew you; depart from Me" (7:21–23).

He closes with the image of two houses. Everyone who hears His words and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock: rain poured down, floods rose, winds blew and beat against that house, yet it did not fall. Everyone who hears His words and does not put them into practice is like a foolish man who built his house on sand: rain, floods, and winds came, and the house fell with a great crash (7:24–27).

When Jesus finished saying these things, the crowds were astonished at His teaching, because He taught as one who had authority, and not as their scribes (7:28–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Look at yourself before looking at others.** Jesus does not forbid helping another person mend a fault, yet He tells us to remove the plank from our own eye first (7:5). Someone who is honest about their own sin corrects others gently, not from a judge's bench.

**The heavenly Father wants us to ask.** Ask, seek, knock—three invitations, each more persistent than the last (7:7). Beneath that invitation stands the Father's goodness. An earthly father does not hand a stone to a hungry child; how much more can we trust our Father in heaven (7:9–11).

**One sentence sums up the whole Law.** Do for others what you want them to do for you (7:12). Easy to memorize, hard to live, because it forces us to measure another person's good with the care we instinctively give ourselves.

**There are two roads, and we must choose.** Narrow gate and wide gate, good tree and bad tree, house on rock and house on sand—at the end of His sermon, Jesus sets clear choices before His listeners (7:13–27). Hearing without doing is as good as never having heard.

**Words cannot replace obedience.** Calling out "Lord, Lord," and even working miracles in His name, is still not enough without doing the Father's will (7:21–22). What matters most is whether Christ "knows" us—whether there is a real, living relationship with Him (7:23).

## Deeper Meaning

**Judging others condemns ourselves.** Paul writes nearly the same thought: whenever you pass judgment on another, you condemn yourself, because you practice the very same things (Romans 2:1). Only God is a righteous judge.

**The greatest good the Father gives.** In the parallel passage, Luke writes that the heavenly Father will "give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him" (Luke 11:13). The greatest gift is not whatever item we ask for, but God's own presence.

**Two paths from Deuteronomy to Jeremiah.** Before Israel entered the Promised Land, Moses declared: I have set before you life and death; now choose life (Deuteronomy 30:19). Jeremiah echoed him: the Lord sets before the people "the way of life and the way of death" (Jeremiah 21:8). Psalms 1 opens the entire Psalter with two ways. Jesus concludes His sermon on that same foundation.

**Knowing a tree by its fruit.** Paul lists the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness (Galatians 5:22). John likewise urges believers to test the spirits, because many false prophets have gone out (1 John 4:1). True and false prophets are measured not by eloquence or miracles, but by their lives.

**Hearing and doing.** James, likely the brother of Jesus, writes, "Be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves" (James 1:22). That is the house on the rock stated in plain prose.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Do not judge" does not mean refusing to tell right from wrong.** This verse is often quoted as if nobody may ever call anything wrong. Yet immediately afterward, Jesus warns against giving sacred things to dogs and tells us to watch out for false prophets (7:6; 7:15). Doing that requires discernment. What He forbids is a fault-finding, condemning spirit that looks down on others.

**Who are the dogs and pigs?** Verse 7:6 is notoriously brief. In that culture, dogs and pigs were unclean animals. Many take this to refer to people who repeatedly scorn holy things, much like Proverbs advising not to rebuke a mocker (Proverbs 9:8). The point is not to label people as animals, but to discern when to speak and when to stay silent.

**"Ask, and it will be given to you" is not a blank check.** This verse does not promise that God will hand us whatever we wish for. The picture of a father makes the point clear: a good father gives "good gifts," which is sometimes not the exact thing a child asks for (7:11).

**"Only a few find it" is not a statistical headcount.** Verse 7:14 is not meant to let us calculate how many people are saved. It is a wake-up call not to assume safety in the crowd. When someone asked directly, "Lord, will only a few people be saved?" Jesus answered: strive to enter through the narrow door (Luke 13:23–24).

## What Commentators Say

**Hillel**, a famous Jewish teacher of the Law living just before Jesus' time, appears in a story in the Talmud. A Gentile challenged him to teach the entire Torah while standing on one foot. Hillel replied, "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor. That is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary. Go and learn." Jesus puts the same principle positively: actively do for others what you wish them to do for you. *(Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the story of Hillel standing on one foot is a famous Talmudic passage with well-established location -->

**The Didache**, an early Christian teaching manual likely written in the late first century, opens with "two ways: one of life and one of death," and states the Golden Rule in negative form like Hillel. When discussing the Lord's Supper, it quotes "Do not give dogs what is sacred" to instruct that only the baptized may partake—one of the earliest interpretations of 7:6. *(Didache, chapters 1 and 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the two ways in chapter 1 and the application of 7:6 to the Eucharist in chapter 9 are widely cited -->

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, interprets "do not judge" with attention to motives. Actions that are plainly sinful can still be named as sinful. Yet actions whose inner intent is unclear—things that could spring from either good or bad motives—should never be judged in the worst light. *(Our Lord's Sermon on the Mount, book 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine distinguishes manifestly sinful deeds from deeds with unclear motives on 7:1; uncertain of exact wording -->

**John Stott**, an Anglican pastor in London, titled his book on the Sermon on the Mount *Christian Counter-Culture*. On 7:1–6 he observes that Jesus does not ask disciples to shut their eyes to sin, but forbids the censorious habit of hunting faults to feel superior. The saying about dogs and pigs confirms that believers must still exercise discernment. He sums it up: do not be a judge, yet do not be blind either. *(The Message of the Sermon on the Mount, on chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident in Stott's main thrust on 7:1–6; uncertain of exact phrasing -->
