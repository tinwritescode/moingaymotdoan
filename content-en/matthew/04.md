# Matthew 4 — Temptation in the Wilderness and the First Disciples

## Story

Right after His baptism, the Holy Spirit led Jesus into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. He fasted forty days and forty nights, and afterward He was hungry (4:1–2). Israel had been tested for forty years in the wilderness; now the Son of God steps into that same testing.

The tempter approached Him: "If You are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread." Jesus answered with words from Deuteronomy: "Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God" (4:3–4).

Then the devil took Him to the holy city and set Him on the pinnacle of the temple. This time the devil quotes Scripture too: if You are the Son of God, throw Yourself down, for it is written that His angels will lift You up. Jesus replied, "Do not put the Lord your God to the test" (4:5–7).

Again, the devil took Him to a very high mountain and showed Him all the kingdoms of the world and their splendor: all this I will give You if You will fall down and worship me. Jesus said, "Away from Me, Satan!" For it is written: worship the Lord your God, and serve Him only. Then the devil left Him, and angels came and ministered to Him (4:8–11).

When Jesus heard that John had been imprisoned, He withdrew to Galilee. Leaving Nazareth, He went and lived in Capernaum by the sea, in the region of Zebulun and Naphtali (4:12–13). Matthew recalls Isaiah's prophecy concerning Galilee of the Gentiles: "the people living in darkness have seen a great light" (4:14–16).

From that time on Jesus began to preach, with the same message John had proclaimed: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near" (4:17).

Walking beside the Sea of Galilee, He saw two brothers, Simon called Peter and Andrew, casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen. He said to them, "Come, follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men." At once they left their nets and followed Him (4:18–20).

Going on a little farther, He saw two other brothers, James and John, sons of Zebedee, in a boat with their father mending their nets. He called them. Immediately they left the boat and their father and followed Him (4:21–22).

Jesus went throughout Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and sickness among the people (4:23). News about Him spread all over Syria. People brought to Him all who were ill, the demon-possessed, those having seizures, and the paralyzed, and He healed them all. Large crowds followed Him from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and the region beyond the Jordan (4:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Temptation often arrives right after blessing.** Moments after hearing the Father call Him "My beloved Son," Jesus is led into the wilderness (3:17; 4:1). Twice the devil begins with "If You are the Son of God," trying to plant doubt about the very declaration the Father just spoke (4:3; 4:6).

**Three temptations, three shortcuts.** Turning stones to bread means using divine power for His own stomach. Throwing Himself from the temple roof means forcing God to perform a miracle as proof. Worshiping the devil to gain the world means taking a shortcut to the throne that bypasses the cross. Every temptation promises something good, yet only by abandoning obedience.

**Jesus fights back with Scripture.** Three times He answers "It is written," and all three quotations come from Deuteronomy (4:4; 4:7; 4:10). He does not win by reaching for special private powers, but by using the weapon available to everyone: God's word, rightly understood and obeyed.

**Light comes to the darkest place.** Jesus does not begin in prestigious Jerusalem, but in Galilee, a backwater looked down upon and mixed with Gentiles (4:15–16). The gospel starts on the margins.

**The call demands leaving something behind.** Peter and Andrew leave their nets. James and John leave their boat and even their father (4:20; 4:22). Following Jesus is not adding one more hobby to life; it is turning the whole direction of life around.

## Deeper Meaning

**Israel failed; Jesus stands firm.** Moses reminded the people that God led them forty years in the wilderness "to humble you and test you" (Deuteronomy 8:2). Hungry, they grumbled; thirsty, they tested God at Massah (Exodus 17:7); and then they turned to worship a golden calf. Jesus' forty days relive those forty years—this time without stumbling.

**A new Adam.** In the Garden of Eden, Adam and Eve were tempted with fruit in the middle of abundance and fell (Genesis 3:6). In the wilderness, Jesus is tempted with bread in the middle of starvation and triumphs. He restarts the human story from the very point where it broke.

**A High Priest who understands our weakness.** The letter to the Hebrews says Jesus "has been tempted in every way, just as we are, yet was without sin" (Hebrews 4:15). Anyone facing temptation can therefore approach Him without fear of being despised.

**What the devil offers, the Father will give.** Satan promised Jesus all the kingdoms of the world in exchange for bowing down to him (4:9). At the close of the book, after the cross and resurrection, Jesus declares that all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Him (28:18). He receives everything, but along the Father's path, not the shortcut.

**Light for the land of the Gentiles.** Isaiah promised that the regions of Zebulun and Naphtali, the first territories trampled by Assyria, would be the first to see a great light (Isaiah 9:1–2). The child promised in that prophecy is called Wonderful Counselor and Mighty God (Isaiah 9:6).

## Notes & Nuances

**The devil knows how to quote Scripture too.** In the second temptation, he quotes Psalms 91 accurately (Psalms 91:11–12). Knowing a verse is not enough; it must be read within the whole counsel of God. Jesus does not argue that the psalm is wrong; He sets another verse beside it: "Do not put the Lord your God to the test" (4:7).

**Were the wilderness and temple pinnacle literal trips?** Some think Jesus was physically transported from place to place. Others read these moments as visions, especially since no mountain on earth is high enough to display "all the kingdoms of the world" (4:8). Either way, the struggle was utterly real.

**Could Jesus have sinned?** Theologians have long debated this question. If He could not sin, was the temptation real? A common answer is that the temptation was real and intense because He was truly human and genuinely starving. Precisely because He never gave in, He felt the full weight of temptation more acutely than anyone else ever has.

**The disciples followed "at once," yet likely not at first sight.** The Gospel of John records that Andrew and Peter had already met Jesus earlier while following John the Baptist (John 1:35–42). The call by the lake may mark the decisive moment when they left their trade permanently to travel with Him.

## What Commentators Say

**Fyodor Dostoevsky**, the nineteenth-century Russian novelist, wrote a famous parable called "The Grand Inquisitor" in *The Brothers Karamazov*. The Inquisitor scolds Jesus for rejecting the three temptations. Humans, he claims, do not want freedom; they want bread, miracles, and authority to obey, and his church has embraced all three in Christ's name. The story reveals how these three temptations remain perennial temptations for every religious institution. *(The Brothers Karamazov, book 5, chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: "The Grand Inquisitor" is a famous passage built directly on the three temptations of Matthew 4 -->

**Henri Nouwen**, a Dutch Catholic priest, wrote a slim book for church leaders. He reads the three temptations as the three core temptations of Christian ministry: to be relevant, to be spectacular, and to be powerful. In their place he commends prayer, confession, and letting others lead us. *(In the Name of Jesus, 1989)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the three temptations form the explicit framework of the entire book -->

**Joseph Ratzinger**, later Pope Benedict XVI, observes that in the second temptation the devil speaks like a biblical exegete. He recalls a tale by the Russian philosopher Soloviev in which the Antichrist even holds an honorary doctorate in theology. His point is that Scripture itself can be weaponized against God whenever people demand that God prove Himself on human terms. *(Jesus of Nazareth, volume 1, chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: volume 1 chapter 2 discusses the temptations and cites Soloviev's Antichrist story -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714 and wrote a commentary on the entire Bible, points out that when quoting Psalms 91 the devil omitted the words "in all your ways."
