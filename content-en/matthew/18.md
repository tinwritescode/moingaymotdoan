# Matthew 18 — Who Is Greatest, the Lost Sheep, and Seventy Times Seven

## Story

The disciples came to Jesus and asked, "Who then is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?" Calling a little child to Him, He placed the child among them and said: unless you change and become like little children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven. Whoever humbles himself like this child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven. And whoever welcomes one such child in My name welcomes Me (18:1–5).

Yet if anyone causes one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble, it would be better for him to have a heavy millstone hung around his neck and be drowned in the depths of the sea. Woe to the world because of things that cause people to stumble; such things must come, yet woe to the one through whom they come. If your hand, foot, or eye causes you to sin, cut it off and throw it away; better to enter life maimed or with one eye than to have two and be thrown into eternal fire (18:6–9).

See that you do not despise one of these little ones, for their angels in heaven always see the face of My Father, and "the Son of Man has come to save that which was lost" (18:10–11).

He tells a parable: if a man owns a hundred sheep and one wanders off, will he not leave the ninety-nine on the hills and go look for the one that wandered? And when he finds it, he rejoices over that one more than over the ninety-nine that never strayed. In the same way, your Father in heaven is not willing that any of these little ones should perish (18:12–14).

If your brother sins against you, go and show him his fault in private; if he listens to you, you have won your brother over. If he will not listen, take one or two witnesses along. If he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the church; and if he refuses to listen even to the church, treat him as you would a Gentile or a tax collector (18:15–17).

He adds: whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven. If two of you on earth agree about anything they ask for, it will be done for them by My Father in heaven, "For where two or three gather together in My name, there am I with them" (18:18–20).

Peter came up and asked: Lord, how many times must I forgive my brother when he sins against me—up to seven times? Jesus answered: not seven times, "but seventy times seven" (18:21–22).

Then He told a parable: the kingdom of heaven is like a king who wanted to settle accounts with his servants. One man owed him ten thousand talents—an impossible sum no lifetime could repay. The king ordered him, his wife, his children, and all he owned to be sold to pay the debt. The servant fell to his knees begging: be patient with me, and I will pay back everything. Moved with compassion, the master released him and canceled the entire debt (18:23–27).

Walking out, that same servant found a fellow servant who owed him a hundred denarii, grabbed him by the throat, and demanded: pay back what you owe! His fellow servant fell to his knees pleading the exact words he had just spoken to the king, yet he refused and had the man thrown into prison until he should pay the debt (18:28–30).

Distressed by what they saw, the other servants went and reported everything to their master. Summoning the man, the master said, "You wicked servant," I canceled all that debt of yours because you begged me; shouldn't you have had mercy on your fellow servant just as I had on you? In anger his master handed him over to the jailers until he should pay back all he owed. Jesus concludes: this is how My heavenly Father will treat each of you unless you forgive your brother from your heart (18:31–35).

## Key Takeaways

**In God's kingdom, greatness means knowing you are small.** The disciples ask who is greatest. Jesus sets a child in their midst (18:2). Children in that culture held zero status; they could only receive and depend. Entering the kingdom means stepping out of the status race and becoming a receiver of grace.

**God treasures the little ones.** Causing a fragile believer to stumble is so serious that drowning at sea would be preferable (18:6). The shepherd leaves ninety-nine safe sheep to hunt for one lost lamb (18:12). In God's eyes, no person is ever a statistic.

**Correction aims to win a brother back.** Going privately to someone who wronged you is never about winning an argument, but about "winning your brother" (18:15). The escalating steps—adding witnesses, then telling the church—move carefully from private to public, and only as needed.

**Christ is present wherever believers gather in His name.** Two or three united in Him are enough to have Christ right in their midst (18:20). The church is not measured by crowd size or architecture, but by His presence.

**The lavishly forgiven must forgive.** Ten thousand talents is an unpayable debt. A hundred denarii is a real debt, yet tiny by comparison (18:24; 18:28). Whoever truly grasps the mountain of debt God canceled for them will not strangle someone else over small change.

## Deeper Meaning

**Seventy times seven reverses Lamech's boast.** Early in Genesis, Lamech boasted: if Cain is avenged sevenfold, then Lamech "seventy-sevenfold" (Genesis 4:24). Jesus takes that very same phrasing and applies it to forgiveness (18:22). The boundless vengeance of fallen humanity is overturned by the boundless forgiveness of God's kingdom.

**God is the Shepherd who seeks the lost.** Through Ezekiel, God promised, "I will search for the lost," bring back the strays, and bind up the injured (Ezekiel 34:16). The parable of the lost sheep shows Jesus carrying out what God Himself promised to do.

**Two or three witnesses.** The Law of Moses required two or three witnesses to establish any accusation (Deuteronomy 19:15). Jesus adopts that safeguard to protect the accused as well, ensuring nobody is condemned on a single person's word.

**Restoring with gentleness.** Paul teaches that if someone is caught in a trespass, believers should restore them in a spirit of gentleness, keeping watch on themselves lest they too be tempted (Galatians 6:1). That reflects the heart of a shepherd seeking a stray, not a harsh judge.

**Forgiving as God forgave.** Paul sums up the parable of the unforgiving servant in a single line: forgive one another "just as in Christ God forgave you" (Ephesians 4:32). The mercy we extend flows directly from the mercy we have received.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 18:11 is absent from many early manuscripts.** Traditional Bibles include "For the Son of Man has come to save that which was lost." Many modern translations move it to a footnote because it was likely copied across from Luke (Luke 19:10). The truth of the sentence is completely sound; only its original place here is debated.

**Do "their angels" mean personal guardian angels?** Verse 18:10 underpins the traditional belief that each person has a guardian angel. Scripture states that angels are sent to serve those who will inherit salvation (Hebrews 1:14), though it never explicitly assigns one individual angel per person. The core message here is that the vulnerable have immediate representation before the Father's throne.

**Treating someone "as a Gentile or a tax collector" does not mean hating them.** Verse 18:17 describes the final step of discipline: someone refusing every plea is recognized as standing outside the community's fellowship. Yet Jesus Himself dined with tax collectors and sought out Gentiles. The ultimate goal remains awakening the person to return.

**Forgiving does not mean erasing every consequence.** Forgiveness does not mean pretending abuse never happened or staying exposed to ongoing harm. Forgiveness releases our claim to personal vengeance and hands judgment over to God. Someone suffering abuse can forgive in their heart while still setting firm boundaries to stay safe.

**Does the final warning make God's forgiveness conditional?** Verse 18:35 sounds as though God might revoke forgiveness. Many commentators understand the parable as a stark test of reality: someone who flatly refuses to forgive anyone demonstrates they have never truly understood or received God's forgiveness in the first place.

## What Commentators Say

**The Talmud**, the vast collection of rabbinic commentary, records the teaching of Rabbi Jose ben Judah: when someone wrongs you once, twice, or three times, forgive them; the fourth time, do not forgive. Peter, proposing seven times, thought he was doubling standard generosity. **The Mishnah**, the collection of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, preserves a striking parallel to 18:20. Rabbi Hananiah taught that when two people sit together and words of Torah pass between them, the Divine Presence rests between them. In Jesus' words, His own name stands in the place of Torah, and He Himself stands in the place of the Divine Presence. *(Mishnah, Avot 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the saying on two studying Torah and the Shekhinah in Avot 3 is widely compared with 18:20 -->

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, preached on 18:15. He taught that sins committed in private should be corrected in private, while public sins require public correction. Dragging a brother's private failing into the open air is not seeking his restoration, but slandering him. *(Sermons)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Augustine distinguishes private and public rebuke on this verse; uncertain of the exact sermon number -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, built Geneva's church discipline on 18:15–18. Discipline, he wrote, acts like sinews holding the body's limbs together. It serves three goals: protecting God's name from disgrace, keeping the rest of the flock from catching the contagion of sin, and moving the offender through healthy shame to repentance. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, book 4, chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the three aims of church discipline and the sinews metaphor in book 4 chapter 12 are widely quoted -->
