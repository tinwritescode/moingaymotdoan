# Matthew 16 — Peter's Confession

## Story

The Pharisees and Sadducees came to test Jesus, asking Him to show them a sign from heaven. He replied: at evening you say it will be fair weather because the sky is red, and in the morning you say there will be a storm today because the sky is red and overcast. You know how to read the appearance of the sky, yet you cannot read the signs of the times. A wicked and adulterous generation demands a sign, yet none will be given it except the sign of Jonah. Then He left them and went away (16:1–4).

Crossing to the other side, the disciples forgot to bring bread. Jesus warned them, "Watch out and beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees." They discussed it among themselves: it must be because we didn't bring any bread (16:5–7).

Aware of this, He said: you of little faith, why are you debating about having no bread? Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand and the seven loaves for the four thousand, and how many basketfuls you gathered? I was not talking about bread. Then they understood He was warning them against the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees, not yeast used in baking (16:8–12).

Arriving in the region of Caesarea Philippi, He asked His disciples: who do people say the Son of Man is? They replied: some say John the Baptist, others Elijah, and still others Jeremiah or one of the prophets (16:13–14).

He asked them, "But what about you? Who do you say I am?" Simon Peter answered, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (16:15–16).

Jesus responded: blessed are you, Simon son of Jonah, because flesh and blood did not reveal this to you, but My Father in heaven. "And I tell you that you are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it." I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven; whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven. Then He warned the disciples not to tell anyone that He was the Christ (16:17–20).

From that time on, Jesus began to show His disciples clearly that He must go to Jerusalem, suffer many things at the hands of the elders, chief priests, and scribes, be killed, and on the third day be raised to life (16:21).

Peter took Him aside and began to rebuke Him: "Far be it from You, Lord! This shall never happen to You!" Turning to Peter, He said: get behind Me, Satan! You are a stumbling block to Me, because you do not have in mind the things of God, but the things of men (16:22–23). The man just named a foundation rock becomes a stumbling stone in the very next breath.

Then He spoke to His disciples: "If anyone wants to come after Me, he must deny himself and take up his cross and follow Me." Whoever wants to save his life will lose it, while whoever loses his life for Me will find it. What good will it be for someone to gain the whole world yet forfeit their soul? Or what can anyone give in exchange for their soul? (16:24–26).

The Son of Man is going to come in His Father's glory with His angels, and then He will repay each person according to what they have done. He adds: truly I tell you, some who are standing here will not taste death before they see the Son of Man coming in His kingdom (16:27–28).

## Key Takeaways

**The defining question: "Who do you say I am?"** People hold plenty of opinions about Jesus, mostly complimentary—a prophet, Elijah, John come back (16:14). Yet Jesus does not settle for polling public opinion. He turns the question straight onto His disciples (16:15). That same question faces every reader.

**Knowing Christ is a gift from the Father.** Peter gets the answer right, yet Jesus says that insight did not come from flesh and blood (16:17). Recognizing who Jesus really is does not spring from clever deduction, but from the Father opening our eyes.

**The Christ must suffer.** The moment He is confessed as Messiah, Jesus spells out that He must suffer, die, and rise again (16:21). Peter wants a Messiah without a cross. Jesus calls that thought satanic, because it is the very shortcut the devil pitched in the wilderness (4:8–10).

**Disciples walk the same road as their Master.** If the Master carries a cross, the disciple must carry one too (16:24). Gaining the whole world at the cost of one's soul is the worst bargain imaginable (16:26).

**The same person can be a foundation stone or a stumbling stone.** Peter is blessed in verse 17 and rebuked in verse 23. When echoing what the Father reveals, he is bedrock. When thinking in merely human terms, he becomes a tripping hazard. That remains true for every follower of Christ.

## Deeper Meaning

**A foundation stone.** Isaiah promised that the Lord would lay in Zion "a precious cornerstone as a sure foundation" (Isaiah 28:16). Paul insists no one can lay any foundation other than Jesus Christ (1 Corinthians 3:11), yet also writes that the church is built "on the foundation of the apostles" (Ephesians 2:20). Peter himself later calls all believers "living stones" built into a spiritual house (1 Peter 2:5).

**Keys of the house of David.** Isaiah tells how God placed "the key of the house of David" on a new steward's shoulder: what he opens no one can shut (Isaiah 22:22). A steward is not the king, but a trusted servant entrusted with managing the royal household. That image illuminates the keys given to Peter.

**Binding and loosing belong to the whole church too.** In chapter 18, Jesus repeats this exact authority to the disciples collectively in the plural (18:18). After His resurrection, He entrusts them with declaring sins forgiven or retained (John 20:23). Peter receives the charge first, yet not alone.

**A new name.** The Gospel of John records that on their very first meeting Jesus named Simon *Cephas*, Aramaic for "rock" (John 1:42). At Caesarea Philippi, He unpacks the meaning of that name.

**Nothing can ransom a soul.** The Psalms declare that no human can pay God a ransom for a life so it might live on forever (Psalms 49:7–8). Jesus' question—"what can anyone give in exchange for their soul?"—finds its answer in chapter 20: the Son of Man Himself comes to give His life as a ransom (20:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**What is "this rock"?** This is one of the most heavily debated verses in church history. Four main readings exist: the rock is Peter himself; the rock is Peter's confession of faith; the rock is Jesus; or the rock is Peter as representative of the apostles. Greek plays on the words *Petros* (Peter) and *petra* (rock); in Aramaic, both would simply be *Kepha*.

**Does this verse establish the papacy?** Catholics understand Peter's authority as passing down to the bishops of Rome after him. Eastern Orthodox and Protestants disagree. Even many modern Protestant scholars agree the rock refers to Peter personally, while noting the passage mentions nothing about successors.

**Calling Peter "Satan" does not mean he was demon-possessed.** The word *Satan* means "adversary." Peter was voicing the devil's exact temptation: claim kingship without the cross. Jesus shuts down that idea just as sharply as He dismissed Satan in the wilderness (4:10).

**What does "some… will not taste death" refer to?** Verse 16:28 has puzzled many readers. Numerous Church Fathers tied it to the Transfiguration, occurring six days later, when three disciples beheld His glory (17:1–2). Others connect it to the resurrection, Pentecost, or the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70.

## What Commentators Say

**Cyprian**, bishop of Carthage in the third century, drew on 16:18 to discuss church unity. He wrote that Christ built His church upon one man, Peter, even though the other apostles received equal authority. According to Cyprian, Christ did this to signify that the church must remain one. **Origen**, the third-century theologian of Alexandria, taught that Jesus' promise to Peter was not exclusive to him. Anyone who confesses as Peter did that Jesus is the Christ, by revelation from the Father, becomes a rock—a "Peter"—against whom the gates of Hades cannot prevail. *(Commentary on Matthew, on Matthew 16)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Origen applies the promise to every believer who shares Peter's confession; uncertain of the exact book number -->

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, shifted his own mind on this verse. Reviewing his writings late in life, he noted that he had earlier identified the "rock" as Peter, yet more often later identified the "rock" as Christ whom Peter confessed. He refused to force a single verdict, leaving the reader to choose whichever reading seemed more convincing. *(Retractations, book 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's remark leaving readers free to choose between the two interpretations is widely quoted -->

**Oscar Cullmann**, a Franco-German Protestant theologian, wrote a landmark study of Peter respected by Catholics and Protestants alike. He concluded that the "rock" truly means Peter the person rather than his confession, since Aramaic makes no distinction between the two words. Yet he argued that Peter's role was unique to the founding era of the church, and the text speaks nowhere of successors. *(Peter: Disciple, Apostle, Martyr, 1952)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this twofold conclusion is the central thesis of the book -->

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, the German pastor executed by the Nazis in 1945, reflected on taking up one's cross. His most famous sentence comes from this passage: when Christ calls a man, He bids him come and die. For Bonhoeffer, the cross is not random misfortune, but the suffering that naturally arrives when a believer remains faithful to Christ. *(The Cost of Discipleship, "Discipleship and the Cross")* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the famous sentence "when Christ calls a man, He bids him come and die" appears in the chapter on 16:24 and is widely quoted -->
