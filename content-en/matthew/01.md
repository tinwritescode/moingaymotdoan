# Matthew 1 — Genealogy and Birth

## Story

The book opens with an introductory line: "This is the record of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham" (1:1). Those two names carry the two great promises of the Old Testament: the promise of blessing to all nations through Abraham, and the promise of an eternal king from the line of David.

The genealogy traces from Abraham through Isaac, Jacob, and Judah down to King David (1:2–6). In the middle of an all-male list, Matthew includes four women: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and "the wife of Uriah" (1:3; 1:5–6). Each carries an unusual story—some are Gentiles, and some are bound up with the sins of others.

From Solomon, the royal line runs through the kings of Judah down to Josiah and Jeconiah, at the time of the exile to Babylon (1:7–11). After the exile, the line continues through little-known names down to Jacob, the father of Joseph, "the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus, who is called Christ" (1:12–16). The final line shifts its wording: no longer "was the father of" as in every previous generation, but saying that Jesus was born of Mary.

Matthew divides history into three stages of fourteen generations each: from Abraham to David, from David to the exile, and from the exile to the Christ (1:17). The history of God's people is presented as ordered and moving toward its destination.

Then comes the story of the birth. Mary was pledged in marriage to Joseph, but before they came together, she was found to be with child through the Holy Spirit. Joseph, being a righteous man and unwilling to disgrace her publicly, decided to divorce her quietly (1:18–19).

While he was considering this, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream: "Joseph, son of David," do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, because the child conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit. She will give birth to a son, and you are to give Him the name Jesus, "for He will save His people from their sins" (1:20–21).

Matthew says all this took place to fulfill what the prophet had spoken: "Behold, the virgin will be with child and will give birth to a son," and they will call Him Immanuel, which means "God with us" (1:22–23).

Joseph woke up and did exactly as the angel of the Lord had commanded him: he took Mary home as his wife. Yet he had no union with her until she gave birth to a son, and he gave Him the name Jesus (1:24–25). By naming the child, Joseph adopted Him into his own line of David.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus is the destination of the entire history of God's people.** The genealogy is not a dry list. It says that every prior generation—from Abraham through David, and even through the years of exile—was heading toward one person. Three stages of fourteen generations show that history has someone at the helm (1:17).

**God works through imperfect people.** In the genealogy there are good kings and wicked kings, foreigners, and people bearing deep stains. God does not wait for a spotless family tree before going to work. He works right in the middle of tangled human stories.

**The child comes from God, not from human will.** Matthew makes this clear: Mary conceived by the Holy Spirit before coming together with Joseph (1:18). The Savior is not an achievement of David's house, but a gift from heaven placed into that house.

**The child's name tells us what He will do.** Jesus means "the Lord saves," and the angel explains right away: this son will save His people from their sins (1:21). Immanuel tells us who He is: God with us (1:23).

**Joseph is righteous in a merciful way.** He had every right to make a public case of it, yet he chose quietness. And when God spoke, he obeyed at once, even at the cost of public gossip (1:19; 1:24). His righteousness is not hard like bare law, but soft with mercy.

## Deeper Meaning

**The promise to Abraham.** God promised that all the peoples of the earth would be blessed through Abraham (Genesis 12:3). Matthew opens his book with Abraham's name and closes it with the command to make disciples of all nations (28:19). From beginning to end, that blessing is spreading outward.

**The promise to David.** Through the prophet Nathan, God promised to establish the throne of David's kingdom forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16). After the exile, that royal line looked like a felled tree left with only a stump. Matthew says: from that stump, a shoot has sprung up (Isaiah 11:1).

**Four women, four stories of grace.** Tamar posed as a prostitute to bear a child by her father-in-law (Genesis 38). Rahab was a Canaanite prostitute (Joshua 2:1). Ruth was a Moabite (Ruth 1:4). Bathsheba was taken by David after he had her husband killed (2 Samuel 11). All four are named in the lineage of Christ, foreshadowing that He comes for outsiders and for sinners.

**Immanuel from start to finish.** Matthew opens with the promise "God with us" (1:23) and closes with the risen Jesus promising to be with His disciples always, to the very end of the age (28:20). The whole book rests between those two promises.

**The prophecy of Isaiah.** The word about a virgin conceiving was first spoken to King Ahaz in a moment of national crisis (Isaiah 7:14). Matthew reads that word as a sign whose meaning reaches far beyond Ahaz's day, brought to full realization in Jesus.

## Notes & Nuances

**Matthew's genealogy differs from Luke's.** Luke also includes a genealogy, but it works backward from Jesus to Adam, and after David it runs through Nathan rather than Solomon (Luke 3:23–38). Many explanations have been offered since ancient times: Matthew records the legal royal line while Luke records the biological line, or Luke records Mary's line. No single explanation has won universal agreement.

**"Fourteen generations" is a deliberate structure, not an exhaustive headcount.** Compared with the books of Kings, Matthew skips several kings between Joram and Uzziah (1:8). Ancient writers routinely streamlined genealogies to make them memorable. Some also note that the Hebrew letters of David's name add up to the numerical value fourteen.

**"Virgin" or "young woman"?** The Hebrew word in Isaiah 7:14 means a young woman of marriageable age. The ancient Greek translation made by Jewish scholars rendered it as "virgin," and Matthew follows that translation. Jews and Christians have long debated this verse.

**"Pledged in marriage" carried more weight than modern engagement.** In that world, betrothal was already a legally binding marriage contract, though the couple did not yet live together. Ending it required a formal divorce, which is why Joseph is already called Mary's "husband" (1:19).

**"Until" she gave birth.** Verse 25 states that Joseph had no marital relations with Mary until she gave birth to a son. Protestants generally understand that they lived as husband and wife normally afterward. Catholics and Eastern Orthodox believe Mary remained a virgin throughout her life, holding that the word "until" does not imply any change afterward.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, was so renowned for his preaching that he was called "Golden-Mouthed." He asks why Matthew mentions Tamar and women with tarnished reputations. In his view, Christ came not to avoid human sin, but to take it away. **Jerome**, the fourth-century translator of the Bible into Latin, wrote a short treatise to refute a man named Helvidius. Helvidius argued that the word "until" in verse 25 proves Mary later had children with Joseph. Jerome replied that Scripture often uses "until" without implying that things changed afterward. *(Against Helvidius, 383)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the entire treatise addresses verse 25 and the word "until" and is widely cited -->

**Rashi**, the most famous Jewish commentator of the Middle Ages, writing in eleventh-century France, reads Isaiah 7:14 within the historical context of King Ahaz. He holds that the young woman was the prophet's own wife and that the child was a sign for that era. This reflects the standard Jewish reading, distinct from how Matthew uses the verse. *(Commentary on Isaiah, on chapter 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Rashi explicitly identifies the young woman as the prophet's wife and is widely cited in discussions of this verse -->

**Raymond Brown**, an American Catholic priest and biblical scholar, wrote a landmark volume on the infancy narratives. He argues that the four women in the genealogy share two traits: their unions were in some way irregular, and through them God still carried out His plan. In this way they prepare the reader for Mary, who conceives uniquely by the Holy Spirit. *(The Birth of the Messiah, 1977)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the two shared traits of the four women are Brown's well-known thesis on the genealogy -->

**Tim Keller**, a New York pastor who died in 2023, wrote that Matthew's genealogy is a bulletin of grace. In his view, ancient royal lineages normally hid shameful names, whereas this genealogy puts them on display.
