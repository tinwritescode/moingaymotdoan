# 1 Corinthians 1 — The Message of the Cross

## Story

Paul, called to be an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, together with Sosthenes our brother, writes to the church of God in Corinth. He addresses them as those sanctified in Christ Jesus and called to be saints, together with all those everywhere who call on the name of the Lord, and greets them with grace and peace (1:1–3). Paul had planted this church during an eighteen-month stay in Corinth, a wealthy port city famous for its moral looseness; Sosthenes may well be the synagogue ruler who was beaten in front of the proconsul's tribunal there (Acts 18:1–17). Paul writes this letter from Ephesus a few years after leaving them.

He gives thanks to God for the grace given to them in Christ Jesus. They have been enriched in every way—in all speech and all knowledge—and do not lack any spiritual gift as they eagerly wait for the Lord's return. God will keep them firm to the end, so that they will be blameless on the day of our Lord Jesus Christ, for God is faithful, who called them into fellowship with His Son (1:4–9). This thanksgiving is striking: speech, knowledge, and spiritual gifts are the very areas this church is misusing, as later chapters reveal. Yet Paul begins with what God has done for them, not with what they have done wrong.

Then he gets straight to the point. He appeals to them to agree with one another, leaving no divisions among them, united in mind and conviction. People from Chloe's household have informed him that there are quarrels among them: one says, "I follow Paul," another, "I follow Apollos," another, "I follow Cephas," and still another, "I follow Christ." "Is Christ divided?" Was Paul crucified for them? Were they baptized in the name of Paul (1:10–13)? Apollos was an eloquent preacher from Alexandria who had arrived in Corinth after Paul (Acts 18:24–28); Cephas is Peter's Aramaic name. None of the three leaders had organized a faction; the believers themselves were splitting around their favorite teachers.

Paul is thankful that he baptized very few of them: Crispus, Gaius, and the household of Stephanas—beyond that, he cannot recall baptizing anyone else. That way no one can claim they were baptized in Paul's name. For Christ did not send him to baptize, but to preach the gospel, and not with words of eloquent wisdom, lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power (1:14–17). Crispus was the synagogue ruler who believed in the Lord along with his entire household (Acts 18:8). Paul's candid aside—"beyond that, I do not remember if I baptized anyone else"—reminds us this is a real letter dictated from memory, with the author correcting himself mid-thought.

From here Paul digs down to the root of their divisions: the Corinthians are enamored with worldly wisdom and polished rhetoric. He writes, "For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God." Scripture declares that God will destroy the wisdom of the wise (Isaiah 29:14). Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe? Where is the debater of this age? Since in the wisdom of God the world through its wisdom did not come to know Him, God was pleased through the foolishness of what was preached to save those who believe (1:18–21).

Jews demand miraculous signs and Greeks look for wisdom, but Paul preaches Christ crucified. To Jews, that is a stumbling block: a Messiah hung on a tree was unthinkable. To Gentiles, it is sheer folly: what kind of god dies like a condemned criminal? Yet to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God. For the foolishness of God is wiser than human wisdom, and the weakness of God is stronger than human strength (1:22–25).

Paul tells them to look at themselves. When they were called, not many of them were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth. God chose the foolish things of the world to shame the wise, the weak things to shame the strong, the lowly and despised things—and the things that are "not"—to nullify the things that "are," so that no one may boast before Him. It is because of Him that they are in Christ Jesus, who has become for us wisdom from God—that is, our righteousness, holiness, and redemption. Therefore, as it is written: "Let him who boasts boast in the Lord" (1:26–31; Jeremiah 9:23–24). Paul says "not many," not "none": the Corinthian church did include a few well-to-do members, such as Gaius, whose house was large enough to host the whole congregation (Romans 16:23).

## Key Takeaways

**The church still belongs to God even when it is deeply flawed.** The Corinthians are quarrelsome and proud, yet Paul still addresses them as saints and thanks God for the grace poured out on them (1:2; 1:4–9). Every rebuke in the letter rests on this foundation: they are God's own people, which is precisely why they must live in a manner worthy of that calling.

**Forming factions around preachers turns Christ into just one brand among many.** When one circle claims Paul and another claims Apollos or Cephas, they are treating the apostles like rival philosophical masters in Greek society (1:10–12). Christ alone was crucified for them, and in His name alone they were baptized (1:13).

**The cross turns the world's measuring stick upside down.** Jews seek dramatic signs to prove power and Greeks seek sharp arguments to satisfy the intellect, yet God saves the world through a Messiah executed like a criminal (1:22–24). What looks from the outside like weakness and absurdity turns out to be God's power and wisdom (1:25).

**God chooses people who have nothing to brag about so that no one boasts except in the Lord.** The social makeup of the Corinthian church itself—mostly people without rank or clout—offers living proof of how God works (1:26–29). Every priceless gift believers possess is found in Christ, leaving no ground for self-congratulation (1:30–31).

## Deeper Meaning

**A crucified Messiah stumbled Jewish expectations because of the ancient law.** Paul notes that Christ crucified is "a stumbling block to Jews" (1:23). The Law of Moses declared that "anyone who is hung on a tree is under God's curse" (Deuteronomy 21:23), making the idea of a Messiah dying on wood seem completely contrary to their hope. In another letter, Paul explains that by becoming a curse for us in that very way, Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law (Galatians 3:13).

**Choosing the small and overlooked has been God's pattern ever since Israel's beginnings.** When Paul reminds the Corinthians that few among them were noble or influential, he echoes a familiar refrain running through all of Scripture (1:26–28). Long ago God told Israel He set His affection on them not because they were more numerous than other peoples—for they were the fewest of all—but because He loved them and kept His oath (Deuteronomy 7:7–8).

**The call to boast only in the Lord ties directly back to the prophet Jeremiah.** The closing line of chapter 1—"Let him who boasts boast in the Lord"—distills the word of the LORD through Jeremiah (1:31). There God commands the wise not to boast in wisdom, the strong not to boast in strength, and the rich not to boast in riches, but to boast in knowing Him as the LORD who exercises loving devotion, justice, and righteousness (Jeremiah 9:23–24).

**Jesus Himself thanked the Father for revealing truth to the humble.** When Paul says God made the world's wisdom foolish and used plain preaching to save believers, he walks in step with Jesus' own prayer (1:20–21). Jesus praised the Father because He had "hidden these things from the wise and learned, and revealed them to little children" (Matthew 11:25).

**Church unity connects directly to Jesus' prayer on the night before His death.** Paul's plea that the Corinthians be "perfectly united in mind and thought" aims at far more than polite harmony (1:10). On the eve of the cross, Jesus prayed that all who believe in Him would be one, so that the world might believe that the Father sent Him (John 17:20–21).

## Notes & Nuances

**Calling the Corinthians "saints" does not mean they were morally spotless.** In everyday speech, "saint" suggests someone of flawless virtue. In Scripture, however, being "sanctified" means first and foremost being set apart by God as His own (1:2). That is why Paul can call a messy, troubled congregation "saints," and then summon them to live up to who they already are in Christ.

**Paul is not minimizing the importance of baptism.** When he thanks God that he baptized only a handful of people and says Christ sent him to preach rather than to baptize, he is not dismissing the ordinance Jesus Himself commanded (1:14–17; Matthew 28:19). He simply refuses to let the Corinthians turn the person who performs baptism into a faction leader, as though baptism marked entry into Paul's personal fan club.

**Criticizing "the wisdom of the world" is not an attack on learning or careful thought.** Paul himself was deeply educated and argued with fierce rigor. What he rejects is the proud self-reliance that imagines human intellect can measure and discover God on its own, or the flashy rhetoric that hides the stark reality of the cross (1:17; 1:20–21).

**The group claiming "I follow Christ" in 1:12 is not necessarily the one group getting it right.** Readers skimming the verse sometimes assume this fourth slogan belonged to the faithful remnant amid three rival cliques. Yet Paul lists all four slogans side by side and immediately asks, "Is Christ divided?" (1:12–13). Using Christ's name as a party badge to look down on other believers is just as divisive as the other three slogans.

## What Commentators Say

**Clement of Rome**, leader of the Roman church around AD 96, wrote to this very Corinthian congregation decades later when factionalism flared up again. **John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, pays close attention to Paul opening with thanksgiving before offering rebuke. He notes that Paul does not immediately point out their faults, but first affirms the gifts they have received—like a skilled physician soothing the patient before operating, so that the correction afterward will be better received. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom frequently notes Paul's pastoral strategy in opening thanksgivings; exact homily number needs verification -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, built his entire "theology of the cross" upon this passage. He contrasts those who seek God in glory and triumph with those who recognize Him in suffering and the cross: the world gazes on the shameful wood and sees defeat, while faith looks there and beholds the saving power of God. *(Heidelberg Disputation, 1518, Theses 19–21)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Heidelberg Disputation Theses 19–21 establish the theology of the cross directly on 1 Corinthians 1:18–25 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, reads the four descriptions of Christ in 1:30 as a complete summary of salvation. When Paul says Christ has become our wisdom, righteousness, holiness, and redemption, Calvin observes that every door to human boasting is shut tight, since not a single piece of salvation remains for us to claim as our own merit. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 1 verse 30)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin expounds 1 Corinthians 1:30 thoroughly against human merit; exact wording not recalled -->

**Gordon Fee**, twentieth-century American New Testament scholar, argues that Corinth's problem was not merely personality clashes, but importing the city's obsession with rhetoric and social status into the church. They were rating preachers the way Greek audiences judged traveling sophists, forcing Paul to pull them back to the cross to shatter that very standard. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 1 verses 10–31)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this is a central thesis throughout Fee's NICNT commentary on 1 Corinthians 1–4 -->
