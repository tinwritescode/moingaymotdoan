# 1 Corinthians 13 — Love

## Story

Paul has just been talking about spiritual gifts; now he turns to the one thing that gives every gift its worth. "If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am only a ringing gong or a clanging cymbal." Even if he has the gift of prophecy and can fathom all mysteries and all knowledge, and even if he has faith that can move mountains, without love he is nothing. Even if he gives away everything he owns to feed the poor and surrenders his body to be burned, without love he gains nothing (13:1–3). Corinth was famous across the Mediterranean for its bronze foundries; readers there knew firsthand how loud and hollow a brass gong sounded. In verse 3, some early manuscripts read "so that I may boast" while others read a nearly identical Greek word meaning "to be burned."

Paul then describes love—not with an abstract definition, but in action. "Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. It is not rude, it is not self-seeking, it is not easily angered, it keeps no account of wrongs. Love takes no pleasure in evil, but rejoices in the truth." It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things (13:4–7). Almost every phrase here strikes directly at a problem already exposed in this letter: envy and rivalry in chapter 3, arrogance in chapters 4 and 8, rude conduct in chapter 11, self-seeking in chapter 10. This portrait of love also serves as a mirror showing the Corinthians themselves.

Love never fails. Prophecies will pass away, tongues will cease, and knowledge will pass away. For we know in part and we prophesy in part, yet when the perfect comes, the partial will pass away (13:8–10). Interpreters understand "the perfect" in different ways: most take it to mean the day Christ returns, while some think it refers to the completion of the New Testament canon, concluding from that reading that certain miraculous gifts have ceased.

Paul draws a comparison from growing up: when he was a child, he talked, thought, and reasoned like a child; when he became a man, he put childish ways behind him. "Now we see but a dim reflection as in a mirror; then we shall see face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I am fully known" (13:11–12). Ancient mirrors were made of polished bronze—a prized Corinthian craft—producing dim, distorted reflections. The Corinthians imagined they already possessed full knowledge, yet their own apostle admits that for now he sees only dimly.

"And now these three remain: faith, hope, and love; but the greatest of these is love" (13:13). Faith will one day turn into sight, and hope will turn into possession; love alone endures forever, because God is love (1 John 4:8).

## Key Takeaways

**Even the most dazzling spiritual gifts amount to nothing apart from love.** Speaking in every tongue, grasping every mystery, wielding mountain-moving faith, or giving away all one's wealth leaves a person empty and profited nothing if love is absent (13:1–3). Love is not one gift lined up beside the others; it is the breath that gives every gift life.

**Biblical love is not a vague emotion, but concrete daily conduct.** Rather than offering abstract theory, Paul lists fifteen down-to-earth actions: patience, kindness, refusing envy, boasting, self-seeking, or resentment, turning from injustice and delighting in truth (13:4–7). Love shows its real face in how we treat difficult people right next to us.

**All present gifts and knowledge are temporary and incomplete; love alone lasts forever.** Prophecy will end, tongues will fall silent, and all human knowledge right now is like peering into a hazy bronze mirror (13:8–12). Because we currently know only in part, nobody has grounds to boast over their gifts or insight.

**Among the three pillars of Christian life, love sits on the highest throne.** Faith, hope, and love uphold believers throughout this present age, yet love stands supreme because it walks right with us into eternity (13:13).

## Deeper Meaning

**Love—not tongues or miracles—is the authentic mark of Jesus' disciples.** By placing love above every dramatic gift (13:1–2), Paul echoes Jesus' parting words on the night before the cross: "By this everyone will know that you are My disciples, if you love one another" (John 13:35).

**Love endures forever because God's very essence is love.** Paul declares that "love never fails" (13:8). The apostle John supplies the deepest reason why: "because God is love" (1 John 4:8). Gifts such as tongues and prophecy are temporary tools for this present age, whereas love belongs to the eternal nature of God Himself.

**The promise that "we shall see face to face" fulfills an Old Testament longing stretching back to Moses.** Long ago, Moses alone enjoyed the rare privilege of God declaring, "With him I speak face to face, clearly and not in riddles" (Numbers 12:8). Paul promises that on the final day every believer will step into that same clear, face-to-face encounter (13:12)—the very hope John describes when he writes, "when Christ appears, we will be like Him, for we will see Him as He is" (1 John 3:2).

**The triad of faith, hope, and love runs as a familiar refrain throughout Paul's letters.** Closing chapter 13 with "faith, hope, and love" (13:13), Paul returns to the same three anchors for which he thanked God in the church at Thessalonica: "your work of faith, your labor of love, and your enduring hope" (1 Thessalonians 1:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 13 was written not as a wedding poem, but as a sharp correction to a fractured church.** Reading it at weddings remains fitting and beautiful, yet hearing it only in romantic settings dulls its original punch. Paul wedged chapter 13 squarely between chapters 12 and 14 to remind gift-boasting, rivalrous believers that noisy religion stripped of love is nothing more than a clanging gong.

**"Bears all things, believes all things" in 13:7 does not call for gullibility or covering up wrongdoing.** The preceding verse (13:6) sets an unmistakable boundary: love "takes no pleasure in evil, but rejoices in the truth." Believing all things means refusing a cynical, suspicious posture and clinging to hope for good and for God's transforming power—never closing one's eyes to deceit or injustice.

**"The perfect" in 13:10 has two primary interpretations tied to debates over spiritual gifts.** Most commentators understand "the perfect" as Christ's return, because 13:12 immediately contrasts our present partial knowledge with seeing "face to face" and knowing fully—something plainly still ahead of us. Some scholars who hold that miraculous sign gifts have ceased understand "the perfect" as the completion of the New Testament Scriptures.

**In the original Greek, every single trait of love in 13:4–7 is expressed as a verb.** Paul does not paint love as a static quality to admire from afar, but as concrete actions we choose to do or refrain from doing every day. That allows every reader to examine their own life immediately rather than merely admiring love in theory.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, observes that Paul puts love at the root of every virtue by opening with patience and kindness. He emphasizes that 13:3 proves even giving away one's fortune or surrendering one's body to flames can spring from vanity when love is missing, for love alone gives sacrifice worth before God. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians, Homilies 32 and 33)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's Homilies 32–33 on 1 Corinthians 13 are famous, stressing that even martyrdom without love profits nothing -->

**Thomas Aquinas**, thirteenth-century Catholic theologian, draws on 1 Corinthians 13:13 in expounding the three theological virtues of faith, hope, and charity. Charity is the living form that animates every other virtue, surpassing faith and hope because those two belong to our earthly pilgrimage whereas love unites us to God forever in eternity. *(Summa Theologiae, Part II-II, Question 23)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Aquinas's doctrine of the three theological virtues and charity as the form of the virtues rooted in 1 Corinthians 13:13 is classic in Summa Theologiae II-II Q. 23 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, maintains that "when the perfect comes" in 13:10 clearly refers to the day Christ returns, when we no longer gaze dimly into a mirror but see face to face (13:12). From this he urges all theologians and believers to walk humbly, since even the wisest person in this life still knows only in part. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 13 verses 9–12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin clearly reads 13:10–12 as referring to the last day and draws a lesson in intellectual humility -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, insists that chapter 13 is no lyrical digression dropped into the middle of the letter, but Paul's sharpest arrow aimed straight at the Corinthian congregation. Every trait in 13:4–7 flips one of their exact faults—boasting, arrogance, self-seeking, and envy—while 13:1 strikes directly at their obsession with speaking in tongues. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 13 verses 1–13)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee powerfully argues for the central, corrective function of chapter 13 in addressing Corinthian abuses -->

**C. S. Lewis**, twentieth-century British writer and apologist, distinguishes Christian charity from passing romantic warmth. Love, he writes, is not a feeling but a steady posture of the will: we cannot force ourselves to feel instant affection for a difficult neighbor, yet we can choose to treat them kindly, fairly, and with genuine desire for their good, just as Christ did. *(Mere Christianity, Book 3, chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Lewis's chapter on Charity in Mere Christianity Book 3 chapter 9 is famous for distinguishing willpower from feelings; he discusses Christian love generally rather than expounding 1 Corinthians 13 verse by verse -->
