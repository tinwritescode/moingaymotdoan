# 1 Corinthians 3 — I Planted, Apollos Watered

## Story

Paul now says plainly what the previous chapter left hanging. He could not address them as spiritual people, but as people of the flesh—mere infants in Christ. He fed them milk, not solid food, because they were not ready for it, and even now they are still not ready. The proof is the jealousy and quarreling among them: when one says, "I follow Paul," and another says, "I follow Apollos," they are behaving just like ordinary worldly people (3:1–4). A congregation proud of its wisdom and spiritual gifts is told they are still babies, and the mark of their immaturity is not lack of information, but factionalism.

Who, then, is Apollos, and who is Paul? Merely servants through whom the Corinthians came to believe, each carrying out the task the Lord assigned. "I planted the seed and Apollos watered it, but God made it grow." Neither the one who plants nor the one who waters is anything; only God, who makes things grow, counts. The planter and the waterer share a single purpose, and each will be rewarded according to his own labor. They are God's fellow workers; the church is God's field and God's building (3:5–9).

Shifting from field to building, Paul continues. By the grace God gave him, he laid a foundation like a skilled master builder, and someone else is building on it. Yet each person must be careful how he builds, for no one can lay any foundation other than the one already laid, which is Jesus Christ (3:10–11).

Some build on that foundation with gold, silver, and costly stones; others build with wood, hay, and straw. Each builder's work will be exposed for what it is, because "the Day" will bring it to light: it will be revealed with fire, and the fire will test the quality of each person's work. If what someone has built survives, he will receive a reward. If it is burned up, he will suffer loss; he himself will be saved, "yet only as through fire" (3:12–15). Paul is speaking here of those who labor in the church, teaching and shaping it: a worker may personally be saved while a lifetime's shoddy work goes up in ashes.

Then he asks: do they not know that they are God's temple, and that God's Spirit dwells in them? If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him, for God's temple is sacred, and they together are that temple (3:16–17). The "you" here is plural: the entire church together forms God's temple. Tearing the church apart through division is, in Paul's eyes, vandalizing the temple of God.

Let no one deceive himself. If anyone among them thinks he is wise by the standards of this age, he should become a fool so that he may become truly wise, for the wisdom of this world is foolishness in God's sight. Scripture says He catches the wise in their craftiness, and again, the Lord knows that the thoughts of the wise are futile (3:18–20; Job 5:13; Psalms 94:11).

So then, no more boasting about human leaders, for all things belong to them: whether Paul or Apollos or Cephas, or the world or life or death, or the present or the future. All are theirs, they belong to Christ, and Christ belongs to God (3:21–23). Paul flips the factions' slogans completely inside out: they were saying, "I belong to Paul," while Paul tells them, "Paul belongs to you."

## Key Takeaways

**Spiritual maturity is measured by how believers treat one another, not by knowledge or eloquence.** The Corinthians considered themselves profound, yet their envy and rivalries exposed them as still "infants in Christ" (3:1–4). No matter how impressively a church talks, if it splits into factions, it is still living by the world's playbook.

**Ministry leaders are only servants; God alone gives growth.** Paul puts both himself and Apollos in their rightful place: one plants, another waters, both are fellow laborers, and neither stands above the other (3:5–9). Idolizing the planter or the waterer forgets the only One who gives life to the seed.

**Every way of building the church will be tested by fire on the Day of the Lord.** The sole foundation—Jesus Christ—is already laid, yet everyone who teaches and builds upon it must choose enduring materials rather than wood, hay, and straw (3:10–13). A worker may escape with his own salvation intact while flawed ministry burns to ashes (3:14–15).

**The whole church together is God's temple, and no one may damage it.** God takes the unity of His church so seriously that He warns He will destroy anyone who destroys His temple (3:16–17). Remembering that they belong to Christ, and Christ belongs to God, frees believers from boasting in human figures (3:21–23).

## Deeper Meaning

**The image of milk and solid food diagnoses the danger of staying stuck in spiritual infancy.** Paul laments that he still has to feed the Corinthians milk because they cannot handle solid food (3:1–2). The Book of Hebrews uses the exact same metaphor to rebuke believers who ought to be teachers by now yet still need milk instead of solid food, remaining unacquainted with the teaching about righteousness (Hebrews 5:12–13).

**God's new temple is no longer a building of stone, but the living community of believers.** In 3:16, Paul calls the entire congregation "God's temple," the place where His Spirit dwells. This vision echoes across the New Testament: the church is built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets with Christ Jesus as the cornerstone, growing into "a dwelling place for God in His Spirit" (Ephesians 2:20–22), while believers like "living stones, are being built into a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5).

**The testing fire connects to the Old Testament portrait of the Day of the Lord.** Paul declares that each builder's work will be revealed with fire on that Day (3:13). Centuries earlier, the prophet Malachi foretold that the Lord's coming would be like "a refiner's fire," purifying His servants like gold and silver (Malachi 3:2–3). Later Paul likewise reminds the Corinthians that all must appear before the judgment seat of Christ to receive what is due for what they have done (2 Corinthians 5:10).

**Overturning proud human wisdom forms a continuous thread from the Old Testament into the New.** To pull the Corinthians down from self-satisfaction, Paul cites ancient Scripture directly: God traps the wise in their own craftiness and knows their reasonings are empty (3:19–20; Job 5:13; Psalms 94:11). Only by letting go of self-assured cleverness can a person enter God's true wisdom.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 3:15 is not teaching a post-mortem purgatory.** Historically, some Catholic theologians cited the phrase "saved, yet only as through fire" in discussions of purgatory. However, the Reformers and many modern scholars—including many Catholic exegetes—point out that the passage is not describing the cleansing of souls between death and final judgment, but the Last Day itself, when church-building labor is tested and a negligent builder escapes like someone fleeing a burning house with nothing left in hand.

**"Temple" in 3:16 refers to the entire church, differing from its use in chapter 6.** In 3:16–17, the pronouns are plural: the whole community of believers together constitutes one temple. Only later, in 6:19, does Paul apply the temple metaphor to the individual believer's body. Catching the plural here makes clear why fracturing the church is treated as destroying God's temple.

**The warning in 3:17 is far more severe than 3:15.** In 3:15, a worker builds with inferior materials yet still builds on Christ's foundation, losing his reward while remaining saved. In 3:17, Paul turns to a very different figure: someone who actively destroys God's temple through divisive factions or false teaching, and whose end is destruction by God Himself.

**Calling them "people of the flesh" in 3:1–3 does not mean they are unbelievers.** Back in 2:14, Paul spoke of natural people who do not have the Spirit. Here in 3:1–3, he still calls them "brothers" and "infants in Christ": they genuinely possess the Spirit, yet they are acting like people who do not, letting envy and strife steer their lives.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, reflected on the testing fire in 3:15 and did not rule out the possibility of a temporary purifying fire for believers who clung too tightly to earthly possessions. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, rejected reading 3:15 as support for purgatory. In his view, Paul is clearly speaking in metaphor: gold and silver stand for sound gospel teaching, straw stands for vain human inventions, and the testing fire represents the scrutiny of God's Word and the Day of Judgment—not an afterlife prison for souls. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 3 verses 12–15)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin directly argues against using 1 Corinthians 3:15 for purgatory in this section of his commentary -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, highlights the line "I planted the seed and Apollos watered it, but God made it grow" in 3:6. He urges both preachers and listeners never to exalt servants in place of the Master, since all the effort of planting and watering accomplishes nothing unless God supplies life. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's exposition of 3:6 stresses that all growth belongs to God; exact homily number needs verification -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, cleanly distinguishes three groups of builders in 3:10–17: faithful builders using gold and silver, poor builders using wood and straw who lose their reward yet are saved, and temple destroyers in 3:17 whom God Himself will destroy. He argues that 3:16–17 targets those tearing the Corinthian church apart with worldly wisdom. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 3 verses 10–17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee clearly lays out the three categories of builders and differentiates 3:15 from 3:17 in NICNT -->

**Anthony Thiselton**, British New Testament scholar, reads 3:21–23 as a bold declaration of Christian freedom.
