# 1 Corinthians 15 — The Resurrection

## Story

Paul reminds the Corinthians of the gospel he preached to them, which they received, on which they stand, and by which they are being saved—provided they hold firmly to the word he preached to them; otherwise they have believed in vain. What he received and passed on to them as of first importance is this: Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures; He was buried; He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures; and He appeared to Cephas, and then to the Twelve (15:1–5). The verbs "received" and "passed on" are standard Jewish terms for faithfully handing down memorized teaching. This is almost certainly an early summary of the faith Paul learned just a few years after Jesus' death, very likely from Peter and James themselves in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:18–19).

After that, Christ appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once, most of whom are still living, though some have fallen asleep. Then He appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, He appeared to Paul as well, as to one abnormally born. Paul calls himself the least of the apostles, unworthy even to be called an apostle, because he persecuted the church of God. Yet by the grace of God he is what he is, and God's grace to him was not in vain: he worked harder than all of them—yet not he, but the grace of God that was with him. Whether it was Paul or the other apostles, this is what they preach, and this is what the Corinthians believed (15:6–11). Noting that "most of whom are still living" carries a clear challenge: anyone who doubts can go ask them. The James named here is Jesus' own brother, who had once not believed in Him (John 7:5).

Now if Christ is preached as raised from the dead, how can some among them say there is no resurrection of the dead? If there is no resurrection of the dead, then not even Christ has been raised. And if Christ has not been raised, our preaching is useless and so is your faith, and the apostles turn out to be false witnesses about God, having testified that He raised Christ from the dead. If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile; you are still in your sins, and those who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished. If our hope in Christ is for this life alone, we are to be pitied more than all people (15:12–19). Most Greeks did not believe physical bodies would rise again; to them, freeing the soul from the body was the ideal. Some in Corinth probably believed Jesus rose, while denying that they themselves would ever rise bodily.

In reality, however, Christ has indeed been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. Since death came through one man, the resurrection of the dead also comes through one man. Just as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive—each in his own turn: Christ the firstfruits, then when He comes, those who belong to Him. Then the end will come, when He hands over the kingdom to God the Father after destroying every ruler, authority, and power. For He must reign until He has put all His enemies under His feet, and the last enemy to be destroyed is death. God has placed everything under His feet—obviously excluding the One who put everything under Him. When everything has been subjected to the Son, then the Son Himself will be subjected to the One who subjected all things to Him, so that God may be all in all (15:20–28; Psalms 110:1; Psalms 8:6). The firstfruits were the opening sheaf of harvest presented to God, guaranteeing that the whole harvest would follow (Leviticus 23:10–11). Jesus' resurrection is never an isolated miracle; it is the first installment of the resurrection of everyone who belongs to Him.

If there is no resurrection, what will those do who are baptized for the dead? If the dead are not raised at all, why are people baptized for them? And why do the apostles face danger every hour? Paul dies every day—he swears it by the pride he has in them in Christ Jesus our Lord. If he fought wild beasts in Ephesus for merely human reasons, what did he gain? If the dead are not raised, "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die." Do not be deceived: bad company corrupts good character. Come back to your senses as you ought, and stop sinning, for some have no knowledge of God—he says this to their shame (15:29–34; Isaiah 22:13). Being "baptized for the dead" remains one of the most mysterious lines in the Bible, with dozens of proposed explanations. Paul simply mentions a local Corinthian practice—neither endorsing nor condemning it—to point out that their own behavior assumes the dead have a future. The "wild beasts" in Ephesus probably refer figuratively to ferocious opponents, since a Roman citizen would rarely have been thrown into an arena.

Someone will ask: how are the dead raised, and with what kind of body will they come? Foolish question! What you sow does not come to life unless it dies. What you sow is not the plant that will grow, just a bare seed—perhaps of wheat or something else—and God gives it a body just as He determined, giving each kind of seed its own body. Not all flesh is the same: human flesh is one kind, animals another, birds another, and fish another. There are heavenly bodies and earthly bodies, each with its own splendor. The sun has one kind of splendor, the moon another, and the stars another—and star differs from star in splendor (15:35–41). A seed and the plant that springs from it are simultaneously continuous and radically transformed: the same life, clothed in a brand-new form.

So will it be with the resurrection of the dead. What is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable. Sown in dishonor, raised in glory; sown in weakness, raised in power; sown a natural body, raised a spiritual body. If there is a natural body, there is also a spiritual body. So it is written: "The first man Adam became a living being"; the last Adam became a life-giving spirit. The spiritual did not come first, but the natural, and after that the spiritual. The first man was of the dust of the earth; the second man is from heaven. As was the earthly man, so are those who are of the earth; and as is the heavenly man, so also are those who are of heaven. Just as we have borne the likeness of the earthly man, so shall we bear the likeness of the heavenly man (15:42–49; Genesis 2:7). A "spiritual body" does not mean a disembodied ghost made of spirit rather than flesh; it means a real body completely animated and governed by the Holy Spirit. In verse 49, many ancient manuscripts read "let us also bear" instead of "so shall we bear."

Paul states plainly: flesh and blood—human beings in our present frail, mortal condition—cannot inherit the kingdom of God, nor does the perishable inherit the imperishable. Then he reveals a mystery: we will not all sleep, yet we will all be changed—in an instant, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. For the trumpet will sound, the dead will be raised imperishable, and we will be changed. This perishable body must clothe itself with the imperishable, and this mortal body with immortality. Then the saying that is written will come true: "Death has been swallowed up in victory. Where, O Death, is your victory? Where, O Death, is your sting?" (15:50–55; Isaiah 25:8; Hosea 13:14). Believers still alive when Christ returns will not die, but will be transformed instantly (1 Thessalonians 4:15–17).

The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law. Yet thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ! Therefore, beloved brothers, stand firm, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that your labor in the Lord is not in vain (15:56–58). After an entire monumental chapter on resurrection, Paul lands on something wonderfully down to earth: because resurrection is real, everything you do for Christ today matters forever.

## Key Takeaways

**The gospel is news about real historical events.** The core summary in 15:3–5 does not begin with moral advice, but with four concrete facts: Christ died, was buried, was raised, and appeared. His burial confirms He truly died; His appearances confirm He truly rose, with eyewitnesses still alive to corroborate it (15:6). Christian faith stakes everything on events in history and invites scrutiny.

**Remove the resurrection and everything collapses.** Paul refuses to treat the resurrection as an optional decorative bonus. If Christ did not rise, apostolic preaching is empty, faith is futile, believers remain trapped in their sins, and the apostles are liars (15:14–17). He willingly lets the entire faith stand or fall on a single historical question.

**Grace transforms a persecutor into an apostle.** Paul calls himself "the least of the apostles" because he persecuted the church (15:9). Yet he refuses to stay frozen in shame: "by the grace of God I am what I am" (15:10). The risen Lord appeared not only to His friends, but also to His enemy—and remade his entire life.

**Death is an enemy, never a friend.** People sometimes soothe themselves by calling death a natural part of life. Paul calls it "the last enemy to be destroyed" (15:26). Believers still weep when loved ones die because death is an intruder in God's good creation—yet they grieve with hope, knowing that enemy has been conquered.

**Future hope transforms present living.** Without resurrection, the motto "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" makes perfect sense (15:32). Because resurrection is real, Paul can risk his life every hour (15:30), and believers have every reason to sober up and turn away from sin (15:34). What we believe about tomorrow governs how we live today.

## Deeper Meaning

**Which Scriptures say He died and rose "according to the Scriptures"?** Paul repeats twice that Christ died and rose "according to the Scriptures" (15:3–4) without naming a single verse. Readers naturally think of Isaiah 53, where the servant was "pierced for our transgressions" (Isaiah 53:5), and Hosea's promise, "on the third day He will raise us up" (Hosea 6:2). Quite likely Paul has in mind not just isolated proof-texts, but the entire Old Testament storyline in which God leads His people through death into new life.

**Two Adams.** The line "in Adam all die" (15:22) unfolds more fully in Romans: sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin (Romans 5:12). Paul then sets Christ directly opposite him: "through one act of righteousness there is justification that brings life for all men" (Romans 5:18). Christ inaugurates a brand-new humanity just as Adam stood at the head of the old.

**Those who sleep in the dust will awake.** Paul calls deceased believers those who "have fallen asleep" (15:20). That tender language reaches back to Daniel, the clearest resurrection promise in the Old Testament: "Multitudes who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2). Sleep has an ending. Calling Christian death "sleep" never minimizes its pain; it proclaims that morning is coming.

**A grain of wheat.** Paul's image of a seed dying to sprout new life in 15:36–38 echoes Jesus' own words about Himself: "unless a kernel of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains only a seed; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). What happened to Him in the tomb will happen to the bodies of all who belong to Him.

**A body like His glorious body.** Paul tells the Philippians that Christ "will transform our lowly bodies to be like His glorious body" (Philippians 3:21). That is the exact hope expressed in 15:49 about bearing "the likeness of the heavenly man." Jesus' resurrected body—capable of eating and being touched, yet stepping into a locked room—is the prototype of the body believers will receive (Luke 24:39–43; John 20:19).

**From a mountain banquet to a new heaven and new earth.** "Death has been swallowed up in victory" comes from Isaiah 25, where God spreads a rich feast for all peoples on His mountain, "will swallow up death forever," and "will wipe away the tears from every face" (Isaiah 25:8). Revelation picks up that promise for the final renewal: "there will be no more death" (Revelation 21:4). As for Hosea's line quoted alongside it, in the prophet's original book God was likely summoning death's plagues upon a rebellious nation (Hosea 13:14)—and Paul flips it into a joyful taunt over defeated death.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Baptized for the dead" has three main lines of interpretation.** First, some Corinthians may actually have practiced proxy baptism on behalf of relatives or converts who died before being baptized. Second, people may have been baptized out of longing to be reunited with deceased believing loved ones. Third, "the dead" may refer to believers' own mortal bodies, making baptism a public confession of belief in bodily resurrection. Notice that Paul speaks of "those" who do this rather than "we," and nowhere else does the New Testament instruct such a rite. Consequently, mainstream churches do not practice proxy baptism for the dead, though a few modern groups appeal to this verse to do so (15:29).

**Does the Son being "subjected" to the Father make the Son inferior?** In the fourth century, Arians cited 15:28 to argue that the Son was lower than God the Father. Mainstream interpreters reply that the verse describes the Son's mediatorial mission rather than His divine nature. The One who exists "in the form of God" (Philippians 2:6) accepted a mission to conquer every rival power. Once that task is complete, He presents the conquered kingdom to the Father like a victorious commander laying trophies before the king. Subjection here is not inferiority, but the loving harmony between Father and Son.

**Who were the people saying "there is no resurrection of the dead"?** Besides Greek disdain for physical matter, another plausible background exists: some Corinthians may have believed they had already experienced a purely "spiritual" resurrection upon receiving the Spirit, leaving no future bodily resurrection to await. Second Timothy mentions teachers claiming "the resurrection has already occurred" (2 Timothy 2:18)—an attitude fitting Corinthians who already imagined themselves rich, full, and reigning (4:8).

**The eyewitness list leaves out the women.** All four Gospels report that women were the very first witnesses of the risen Jesus (Matthew 28:9; John 20:18), yet Paul's summary in 15:5–8 omits them. The usual explanation is that this creed served as an official public testimony, and first-century courts often discounted women's testimony. By the same token, the fact that the Gospels nonetheless insist women saw Jesus first—despite its apologetic inconvenience—is widely viewed as strong evidence that the Gospel accounts were not fabricated (Luke 24:11).

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, early Christian writer in Carthage around AD 200, wrote an entire treatise defending the resurrection of this very physical flesh. He reads 15:50—"flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God"—not as excluding bodily flesh, but as excluding sinful works of the flesh and our present mortal decay. In his view, this exact body will "clothe itself with the imperishable" as 15:53 declares, rather than being swapped out for a wholly unrelated body. *(On the Resurrection of the Flesh)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Tertullian discusses 15:50 at length in terms of sinful works and mortal corruption; exact chapter number needs verification -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century Greek preacher, reports that Marcionite groups in his day practiced proxy baptism by hiding a living person under a dead man's bed to answer "yes" when the corpse was asked whether it wished to be baptized—a ritual he mocks as theatrical absurdity. On his reading, "for the dead" means for one's own mortal body: candidates confessed belief in "the resurrection of the dead" and were baptized upon that confession. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians, Homily 40)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's story of the Marcionites hiding someone under a bed in Homily 40 is widely cited in discussions of 15:29 -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo around AD 400, explains that a "spiritual body" is not a body dissolved into pure spirit, but genuine flesh completely submitted to the Spirit—free from decay and inner rebellion. Just as our present "natural body" is not made out of soul-stuff but is a physical body animated by the soul, a "spiritual body" is physical flesh wholly energized by the Spirit. *(The City of God, Books 13 and 22)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine clearly explains "spiritual body" as flesh wholly subject to the Spirit in City of God Books 13 and 22; exact chapter numbers omitted as uncertain -->

**The Mishnah**, Jewish oral law codified around AD 200, opens its discussion of the afterlife by declaring that all Israel has a share in the world to come, except a short list of excluded groups—and the very first group listed is anyone who denies the resurrection of the dead. That shows how deeply the Pharisaic tradition to which Paul belonged (Acts 23:6) cherished bodily resurrection, over against the Sadducees who rejected it (Acts 23:8). Paul writes chapter 15 straight out of that conviction. *(Mishnah, Sanhedrin, chapter 10, section 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the opening mishnah of Sanhedrin chapter 10 on denying the resurrection is famous; the connection to Paul is the editor's -->

**Karl Barth**, twentieth-century Swiss theologian, devoted an entire early book to 1 Corinthians 15. He argues that chapter 15 is no end-times appendix tacked onto the back of the letter, but the interpretive key to all of 1 Corinthians. Every dysfunction in Corinth—from factions to boasting over gifts—flowed from acting as though they themselves were the center; Paul sets a radically different center before them: God raising the dead. *(The Resurrection of the Dead, 1924)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: treating chapter 15 as the key to the entire letter is the central thesis of Barth's book -->

**N. T. Wright**, Anglican bishop and British New Testament scholar, demonstrates that "resurrection" in first-century Jewish and Greek usage always meant returning to bodily life after death, never merely a disembodied soul surviving. He reads "spiritual body" as a physical body powered by God's Spirit rather than composed of airy spirit-substance, famously summarizing Christian hope as "life after life after death": being with Christ upon death, followed later by bodily resurrection in a renewed creation. *(The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the lexical definition of resurrection, the reading of "spiritual body," and "life after life after death" are core arguments of Wright's volume -->
