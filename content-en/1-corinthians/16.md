# 1 Corinthians 16 — Do Everything in Love

## Story

The final question concerns the financial collection for the saints. Paul instructs them to follow the same plan he gave the churches in Galatia: on the first day of every week, each person should set aside a sum at home in keeping with their income, so that no collections need to be rushed when he arrives. Once he gets there, he will send whichever representatives they approve, carrying his letters of introduction, to deliver their gift to Jerusalem; and if it seems advisable for him to go as well, they will travel together (16:1–4). The believers in Jerusalem were enduring severe poverty, and Paul viewed Gentile generosity toward them as visible proof that Jews and Gentiles are one in Christ (Romans 15:25–27). "The first day of every week" is Sunday, the day Jesus rose—one of our earliest glimpses of Christians already marking that day as special.

Paul plans to come to them after passing through Macedonia. He hopes to stay with them a while—perhaps spending the entire winter—so they can send him on his way wherever he heads next. This time he does not want just a passing visit; if the Lord permits, he wants unhurried time together. First, however, he will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost, because a wide door for effective work has opened to him there, even though many oppose him (16:5–9). Later those travel plans had to change, prompting critics in Corinth to accuse him of being fickle (2 Corinthians 1:15–17). His stay in Ephesus is chronicled in Acts 19, including the silversmiths' riot.

If Timothy arrives, they should put him at ease while he is with them, for he is doing the Lord's work just as Paul is. No one should look down on him; send him on his way in peace so he can return to Paul, who is waiting for him along with the brothers. As for their brother Apollos, Paul strongly urged him to visit them with the other brothers, yet Apollos was completely unwilling to go right now; he will come when he has the opportunity (16:10–12). Timothy was relatively young and somewhat timid (1 Timothy 4:12). The note about Apollos proves the two men were not rivals as the Corinthian factions imagined; Apollos himself probably stayed away for the moment to avoid fueling fresh party spirit.

Next Paul fires off five crisp imperatives: be on the alert, stand firm in the faith, be men of courage, be strong—"Do everything in love" (16:13–14). That final command sums up the entire letter: factions, lawsuits, idol food, the Lord's Supper, and spiritual gifts all find their answer in the love of chapter 13.

He draws their attention to the household of Stephanas—the first converts in Achaia—who have devoted themselves to serving the saints. He urges them to submit to such people and to everyone who joins in the work and labors at it. Paul rejoices over the arrival of Stephanas, Fortunatus, and Achaicus, because they supplied what was lacking from the Corinthians' absence, refreshing both Paul's spirit and theirs. Such people deserve recognition (16:15–18). These three men likely carried the Corinthian church's letter of questions to Paul (7:1) and probably carried 1 Corinthians back home.

The churches in the province of Asia send their greetings. Aquila and Priscilla greet them warmly in the Lord, together with the church that meets in their house. All the brothers there send greetings. Greet one another with a holy kiss. Paul then takes the pen himself to write his personal greeting. If anyone does not love the Lord, let him be under a curse—*anathema*. "Maranatha!" He wishes the grace of the Lord Jesus upon them, and sends his love to all of them in Christ Jesus (16:19–24). Aquila and Priscilla were the couple who had worked beside Paul making tents in Corinth and were now based in Ephesus (Acts 18:18–19). Paul normally dictated letters to a scribe before signing off in his own handwriting for authentication. "Maranatha" is Aramaic meaning either "Come, O Lord!" or "Our Lord has come"; Greek-speaking Corinthians preserved this cherished prayer directly from the earliest believers. Warning and blessing sit side by side, yet the letter's very last word is Paul's love for all of them.

## Key Takeaways

**Resurrection leads straight into practical generosity.** Chapter 15 closes by promising that labor in the Lord "is not in vain" (15:58). The very next sentence begins, "Now about the collection for the saints" (16:1). Paul sees no gap between the two. People who trust that their bodies will rise again know that caring for hungry neighbors today carries eternal weight.

**Give steadily, proportionately, and thoughtfully.** Paul does not whip up an emotional one-time fundraiser. He instructs every believer, on the first day of every week, to set aside a sum "in keeping with his income" (16:2). Even people of modest means have a share, because everyone gives in proportion to what they have. Generosity becomes a steady habit rather than a passing impulse.

**God's money requires transparent accountability.** Paul refuses to carry the cash by himself. He lets the church select trusted couriers and then writes letters confirming them (16:3). The higher a leader's reputation, the more carefully they should let others see how shared funds are handled.

**Open doors and fierce opposition often arrive together.** In Ephesus, "a great door for effective work" stands wide open alongside "many who oppose me" (16:9). Paul never mistakes resistance for a closed door from God; sometimes opposition crowds in precisely because God has flung a door open.

**Servants earn honor by stooping to serve.** Stephanas's household is commended not for fancy titles or smooth rhetoric, but because they "have devoted themselves to the service of the saints" (16:15). Paul urges the church to "submit to such people" (16:16). In a city obsessed with ranking who looked most impressive, he points to a different measure: who bent down to serve.

## Deeper Meaning

**Remembering the poor.** When the Jerusalem apostles recognized Paul's mission to the Gentiles, they asked only one thing of him: "that we should continue to remember the poor" (Galatians 2:10). The collection of 16:1–4 fulfills that pledge. In his follow-up letter, Paul deepens the teaching on giving: never under compulsion, "for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7).

**The first day of the week.** Acts records that in Troas, on "the first day of the week," believers assembled to break bread (Acts 20:7). John refers to a distinct day as "the Lord's Day" (Revelation 1:10). Gradually, the day of Christ's resurrection became the church's rhythm for worship, prayer, and—as here—setting aside resources for those in need.

**"If the Lord permits."** Paul lays careful travel plans: passing through Macedonia, perhaps wintering in Corinth, remaining in Ephesus until Pentecost. Yet he anchors every plan with four humble words: "if the Lord permits" (16:7). James teaches the same humility: "Instead, you ought to say, 'If the Lord is willing, we will live and do this or that'" (James 4:15). Planning is wise; forgetting that tomorrow belongs to the Lord is folly.

**Doors the Lord opens.** Paul frequently pictures gospel opportunity as a door opened by the Lord rather than forced open by human effort (2 Corinthians 2:12). Christ speaks the same assurance to the church in Philadelphia in Revelation: "See, I have placed before you an open door that no one can shut" (Revelation 3:8). Opponents may be numerous, yet they do not hold the keys.

**Scripture's closing prayer.** *Maranatha* is a cry longing for Christ's return. The entire Bible closes with that very prayer spoken in Greek: "Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20). First Corinthians opened with a church eagerly awaiting the Lord's appearing and ends with the cry begging Him to come (1:7; 16:22).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does the curse "anathema" mean?** The word denotes being placed under God's solemn judgment and cut off from Him. Paul uses the same word for anyone preaching a false gospel (Galatians 1:8), and once declares he would even wish himself cut off—*anathema*—for the sake of his Jewish kin (Romans 9:3). In 16:22, the warning targets insiders in the church who do not love the Lord, not outsiders. It is neither a magical hex nor permission for Christians to curse others; it is a sober warning that failing to love Christ leaves one outside grace. Some scholars think the line echoes an early eucharistic call inviting those who belong to Christ to come forward and warning those who do not to repent.

**Why does an Aramaic prayer appear inside a Greek letter?** Corinthian believers did not speak Aramaic. The fact that they preserved "Maranatha" untranslated shows it originated with the very earliest Aramaic-speaking Jewish disciples in Judea and grew so beloved that it traveled untranslated across the empire. Strikingly, those first Jewish believers were already calling Jesus "Lord" and praying directly to Him—proving that worship of Jesus was never a late pagan invention, but flourished from the very beginning inside the Jewish church.

**Verse 16:2 is not a rigid tithing law.** Paul specifies no fixed percentage, simply "in keeping with his income." Nor does he mention dropping cash into an offering plate during worship; he says "set aside a sum" at home so it is ready when he arrives. Many churches rightly draw inspiration from this verse for regular Sunday giving, yet in context it specifically addresses a special relief fund for poor saints in Jerusalem rather than pastoral salaries or church overhead.

**"Be men of courage" does not address men exclusively.** The Greek verb in 16:13 derives from the root word for "man," yet in Greek idiom it simply meant "act courageously, stand firm." Paul addresses the entire congregation—men and women alike—much as he urged them earlier, "in your thinking be mature" (14:20). He is calling them to quit acting like squabbling children and stand up like grown-ups.

## What Commentators Say

**The Didache**, an extraordinarily early Christian manual likely from the late first century, records a prayer following the Lord's Supper: "May grace come and this world pass away... If anyone is holy, let him come; if anyone is not, let him repent. Maranatha! Amen." Placing *Maranatha* inside public worship right beside a warning to the unworthy mirrors the exact sequence of 16:22. The manual also instructs believers to gather on the Lord's Day to break bread. *(Didache — The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, chapters 10 and 14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the Maranatha eucharistic prayer in Didache chapter 10 and Sunday assembly in chapter 14 are famous; comparing them to 16:22 is standard scholarly observation -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century Greek preacher, applied 16:2 by urging listeners to keep a small charity box right inside their homes beside their place of prayer. Each time they prayed, they could drop a coin inside, turning their home into a sanctuary and making generosity an ingrained habit instead of waiting for sudden surpluses. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Chrysostom recommending a home alms box near one's prayer spot while preaching on 16:2; exact details and homily number need verification -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, notes that Paul selected the first day of the week because believers gathered then, when hearts were naturally lifted toward charity and fellow believers. He also commends the wisdom of small weekly savings: poorer Christians can contribute their modest share without embarrassment, allowing the whole church rather than only the wealthy to take part. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 16 verse 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin connects the first day of the week with Christian assembly; exact phrasing regarding poorer contributors uncertain -->

**Philo of Alexandria**, first-century Jewish philosopher, describes how Diaspora Jewish communities collected temple funds for Jerusalem: every city maintained a treasury, and at appointed times they selected citizens of the highest integrity to escort the funds to Jerusalem. That practice closely parallels Paul's arrangement in 16:3 and would have felt completely familiar to Jewish believers in Corinth. *(On the Special Laws, Book 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Philo describes collecting sacred funds and selecting respected envoys in Book 1 of this treatise; he does not discuss Corinthians, the parallel is the editor's -->

**Larry Hurtado**, American New Testament scholar who taught in Edinburgh, regarded "Maranatha" as one of the strongest pieces of evidence that Aramaic-speaking Jewish Christians were already praying to Jesus as divine Lord in the earliest years after His resurrection. He argued that devotion to Jesus erupted remarkably early inside strict Jewish monotheism rather than evolving slowly under pagan influence. *(Lord Jesus Christ: Devotion to Jesus in Earliest Christianity, 2003)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Hurtado uses the Maranatha acclamation as a cornerstone argument for early high Christological devotion in this book -->
