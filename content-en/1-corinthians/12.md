# 1 Corinthians 12 — One Body, Many Members

## Story

The Corinthians' next question concerns spiritual gifts. Paul reminds them that when they were pagans, somehow or other they were influenced and led astray to mute idols. Therefore he wants them to know: no one speaking by the Spirit of God says, "Jesus be cursed," and no one can say, "Jesus is Lord," except by the Holy Spirit (12:1–3). The Corinthians were familiar with ecstatic frenzies in pagan shrines. Paul hands them a clear test: the true mark of the Spirit is not how intense an ecstatic experience feels, but the confession that Jesus is Lord.

There are different kinds of gifts, but the same Spirit; different kinds of service, but the same Lord; different kinds of working, but the same God works all of them in all people. To each one the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good (12:4–7). Spirit, Lord, God: Paul names all three across three consecutive sentences, long before the church coined the word "Trinity."

To one there is given through the Spirit a message of wisdom, to another a message of knowledge by the same Spirit; to another faith, to another gifts of healing, to another miraculous powers, to another prophecy, to another distinguishing between spirits, to another speaking in different kinds of tongues, and to still another the interpretation of tongues. All these are the work of one and the same Spirit, who distributes them to each one just as He wills (12:8–11). Speaking in tongues—the very gift the Corinthians prized above all others—is placed near the bottom of Paul's list.

Just as a body, though one, has many parts, and all its many parts form one body, so it is with Christ. For we were all baptized by one Spirit into one body—whether Jews or Greeks, slave or free—and we were all given the one Spirit to drink. The body is not made up of one part, but of many (12:12–14). Remarkably, Paul does not say "so it is with the church," but "so it is with Christ": the church is His very own body.

If the foot should say, "Because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body," it would not for that reason cease to be part of the body. If the ear should say, "Because I am not an eye, I do not belong to the body," it would not for that reason cease to belong. If the whole body were an eye, where would the sense of hearing be? If the whole body were an ear, where would the sense of smell be? In fact, God has arranged the parts in the body, every single one of them, just as He wanted them to be. If they were all one part, where would the body be? As it is, there are many parts, yet one body (12:15–20). These words speak to believers who lack flashy gifts and fear they do not really belong.

The eye cannot say to the hand, "I have no need of you!" Nor can the head say to the feet, "I have no need of you!" On the contrary, the parts of the body that seem weaker are indispensable; the parts we think less honorable we treat with special honor, and the unpresentable parts are treated with special modesty, whereas our presentable parts need no special treatment. God has composed the body giving greater honor to the parts that lacked it, so that there should be no division in the body, but that its parts should have equal concern for one another. If one part suffers, every part suffers with it; if one part is honored, every part rejoices with it (12:21–26). And these words speak to believers with prominent gifts who think they need no one else.

Now they are the body of Christ, and each one of them is a part of it. God has appointed in the church first apostles, second prophets, third teachers, then miracles, then gifts of healing, helping, administration, and various kinds of tongues. Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all teachers? Do all work miracles? Do all have gifts of healing? Do all speak in tongues? Do all interpret? Eagerly desire the greater gifts. And yet Paul will show them a still more excellent way (12:27–31). In Greek, every single question expects the answer "no." That still more excellent way is the next chapter.

## Key Takeaways

**The true mark of the Spirit is confessing Jesus as Lord, not emotional intensity or exotic displays.** Coming out of pagan temples full of ecstatic trances, the Corinthians needed a clear standard: no one can sincerely confess "Jesus is Lord" apart from the Holy Spirit (12:1–3).

**Spiritual gifts are diverse, yet all flow from one God and exist for the common good.** There are varied gifts, varied ministries, and varied workings, yet all come from one Spirit, one Lord, and one God (12:4–6). The Spirit distributes gifts sovereignly "for the common good," never for personal spotlight (12:7–11).

**The church is one body with many members, leaving no room for either inferiority or pride.** Quietly serving believers may not write themselves off because they are not hands or eyes (12:15–20), and prominently gifted believers may never tell others, "I have no need of you" (12:21). Instead, God designed the body to honor its seemingly weaker parts so that every member shares one another's pain and joy (12:22–26).

**No single gift is required of every Christian, and one path stands higher than every gift.** The rapid-fire questions of 12:29–30 make plain that not everyone is an apostle, prophet, healer, or speaker in tongues. Paul encourages desiring gifts that build up, then opens the door to "a still more excellent way"—love in chapter 13 (12:31).

## Deeper Meaning

**Testing the Spirit by the confession of Jesus parallels 1 John.** When Paul says no one can confess Jesus as Lord except by the Holy Spirit (12:3), he sets forth the same standard the apostle John gives for testing spirits: "Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (1 John 4:1–2). Every spiritual experience must always be measured by the truth about Jesus.

**Baptism in one Spirit erases ethnic and social barriers.** Verse 12:13 declares that whether Jews or Greeks, slaves or free, all were baptized by one Spirit into one body. That echoes Paul's liberating declaration in Galatians: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28).

**One body with many members is Paul's signature image for church life.** Just as he calls the Corinthians "the body of Christ, and each of you is a member of it" (12:27), he tells the Romans, "so in Christ we who are many are one body, and each member belongs to one another" (Romans 12:4–5). Differences among Christians are not defects to flatten out, but the very way God makes His church whole.

**The harmony of Spirit, Lord, and God in 12:4–6 resonates strongly with Ephesians.** Verses 12:4–6 lay the foundation for faith in one Triune God working seamlessly in the church, echoing the call to unity in Ephesians: "There is one body and one Spirit... one Lord, one faith, one baptism; one God and Father of all" (Ephesians 4:4–6).

## Notes & Nuances

**"Baptized by one Spirit" in 12:13 is read in two main ways across Christian traditions.** Most Reformed and evangelical traditions read this verse as describing the universal experience of conversion: every single believer is placed by the Spirit into Christ's body the moment they are born again. Classical Pentecostal traditions distinguish conversion from a subsequent empowering experience called baptism in the Holy Spirit (commonly accompanied by speaking in tongues), while still recognizing that 12:13 describes all believers entering one body. Under either view, 12:30 clearly states that not every Christian speaks in tongues.

**Whether miraculous gifts such as healing and tongues continue today remains a point of Christian debate.** Cessationist churches hold that sign gifts belonged uniquely to the foundational era of the apostles. Continuationist churches believe the Holy Spirit still bestows all of these gifts today as He wills, provided they are exercised in love and order according to chapters 12 through 14.

**The gift lists in chapter 12 are neither exhaustive catalogs nor rankings of personal worth.** The list in 12:8–10 differs from 12:28, and both differ from the lists in Romans 12 and Ephesians 4. Paul deliberately places tongues near the end to humble Corinthian boasting, while insisting in 12:22–24 that seemingly modest parts are indispensable and worthy of special honor.

**Chapter 12 is only the opening movement of a single continuous section running through chapter 14.** Reading chapter 12 in isolation can turn it into a mere inventory of gifts; reading all three chapters together reveals Paul's tight design: chapter 12 teaches unity in diversity, chapter 13 establishes love as the supreme standard of every gift, and chapter 14 regulates orderly worship in the assembly.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, observes that the body metaphor in 12:14–26 heals two opposite maladies in the church at once: the envy and inferiority of those who think their gift is small (such as a foot or an ear), and the arrogance of those with showcase gifts (such as an eye or the head). **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, focuses on the purpose of every gift in 12:7: "for the common good." Anyone who uses God-given talents or spiritual gifts for self-display rather than building up fellow believers is stealing for private profit what belongs to the whole church, because the Spirit never gives a gift purely for an individual's own sake. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 12 verse 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin strongly stresses 12:7 that every spiritual gift is directed toward the common benefit of the church -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, echoes 12:3 in teaching that no one can believe in Jesus Christ or confess Him as Lord by human reason or strength alone unless the Holy Spirit calls and enlightens them. Thus simple faith confessing Jesus is already the Spirit's greatest miracle in every believer. *(Small Catechism, Explanation of the Third Article of the Apostles' Creed)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Luther's Third Article explanation closely reflects 12:3 in attributing faith in Jesus wholly to the Holy Spirit; uncertain whether he explicitly cites 1 Corinthians 12:3 there -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, explains on 12:13 that every Christian has received the one Spirit upon entering Christ's body, leaving no room for anyone to claim elite status as a "Spirit-filled" tier above fellow believers. At the same time, writing from a Pentecostal background, he defends the ongoing reality of the Spirit's gifts listed in chapter 12 in the church today. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 12 verses 1–13)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee is well known for reading 12:13 as universal Christian initiation while defending the continuation of spiritual gifts in NICNT -->

**Anthony Thiselton**, British New Testament scholar, points out that the body metaphor was a staple of ancient Greco-Roman political rhetoric, where elites used it to persuade lower classes to stay quietly in their subordinate place. Paul turns that old argument completely upside down: instead of reinforcing hierarchy, he uses it to lift up the weakest parts and call every member to share pain and honor equally (12:22–26). *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NIGTC, 2000, on chapter 12 verses 12–26)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: contrasting Paul's body metaphor with ancient Greco-Roman political fables is a standout contribution of Thiselton's commentary -->
