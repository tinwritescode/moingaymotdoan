# 1 Corinthians 7 — Marriage and Singleness

## Story

From this point forward, Paul answers questions the Corinthians had sent him by letter. The first touches on marriage: "It is good for a man not to touch a woman." Many scholars recognize this as a slogan from a faction in Corinth who believed truly spiritual people should abstain even from marital relations. Paul responds: because of the danger of sexual immorality, each man should have his own wife, and each woman her own husband. The husband must fulfill his marital duty to his wife, and likewise the wife to her husband. The wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does; likewise, the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does (7:1–4). In the ancient world, only husbands were thought to hold rights over their wives' bodies; Paul places both spouses on completely equal footing.

Do not deprive each other, except by mutual consent and for a set time, so that you may devote yourselves to prayer; then come together again, lest Satan tempt you because of your lack of self-control. Paul says this as a concession, not a command. He wishes everyone were as he is, yet each person has his own gift from God—one has this gift, another has that (7:5–7). Paul views his own singleness as a gift from God, and marriage as a gift from God as well.

To the unmarried and widows, Paul says it is good for them to remain single as he is. Yet if they cannot exercise self-control, they should marry, for "it is better to marry than to burn with passion" (7:8–9).

To the married, Paul gives a command—not from himself, but from the Lord: a wife must not separate from her husband (and if she does, she must remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband), and a husband must not divorce his wife (7:10–11). Here Paul cites Jesus' own teaching directly (Mark 10:9–12).

To the rest, Paul specifies that he is speaking, not a quoted command of the Lord: if a believer has an unbelieving spouse who is willing to live with them, they must not divorce. The unbelieving spouse is sanctified through the believing spouse; otherwise their children would be unclean, whereas in fact they are holy. Yet if the unbeliever leaves, let them go; in such circumstances the believing brother or sister is not bound. God has called us to live in peace. How do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband? Or how do you know, husband, whether you will save your wife (7:12–16)? Jesus had not explicitly addressed religiously mixed marriages because that situation arose only once the gospel spread into the Gentile world.

Paul states a guiding principle: each person should live as the Lord has assigned them and in the condition in which God called them. This is the rule he lays down in all the churches. Was a man already circumcised when called? He should not try to erase the marks of circumcision. Was he uncircumcised when called? He should not get circumcised. Circumcision is nothing and uncircumcision is nothing; keeping God's commands is what counts. Were you a slave when called? Do not let it trouble you—though if you can gain your freedom, take the opportunity. For the one called in the Lord as a slave is the Lord's freedman; likewise, the one called as a free person is Christ's slave. You were bought at a price; do not become slaves of human beings. Each person should remain with God in the situation in which he was called (7:17–24). Verse 21 can be translated in two ways—"take the opportunity for freedom" or "make use of your present status as a slave"—with most modern editions including the Berean Standard Bible adopting the first reading.

Regarding the unmarried—literally "virgins"—Paul has no direct command from the Lord, yet he offers his judgment as one who by the Lord's mercy is trustworthy. Because of "the present crisis," he thinks it is good for people to remain as they are. Are you bound to a wife? Do not seek release. Are you unmarried? Do not look for a wife. Yet if you do marry, you have not sinned; and if a virgin marries, she has not sinned. Those who marry will simply face worldly troubles in this life, and Paul wants to spare them that (7:25–28). "The present crisis" may point to a local famine or looming persecution threatening Corinth, or to the hardships of the final era before Christ's return.

The time is short. From now on, those who have wives should live as if they had none; those who mourn, as if they did not; those who rejoice, as if they were not rejoicing; those who buy, as if it were not theirs to keep; those who use the things of the world, as if not engrossed in them—for this world in its present form is passing away. Paul wants them to be free from anxieties. An unmarried man is concerned about the Lord's affairs—how he can please the Lord—whereas a married man is concerned about the affairs of this world—how he can please his wife—and his interests are divided. The same holds true for an unmarried woman and a married woman. Paul says this for their own benefit, not to put a noose around their necks, but so they may live in right order and undivided devotion to the Lord (7:29–35). Paul is not belittling marriage; he is simply acknowledging that it brings real, legitimate responsibilities.

Finally, Paul addresses a man weighing whether to marry off his virgin daughter—or, as many modern translations read it, a man considering whether to marry his betrothed. If he feels he is acting improperly toward her and she is getting past her prime—or his passions are strong—and it ought to be done, let them marry; he does not sin. Yet if he stands firm in his heart without compulsion, mastering his own will and deciding to keep her unmarried, he does well. So the one who marries does well, and the one who refrains does even better. A wife is bound to her husband as long as he lives; if her husband dies, she is free to marry anyone she wishes, only in the Lord. Yet in Paul's judgment she is happier if she remains as she is—and he thinks that he too has the Spirit of God (7:36–40). The Greek of verses 36–38 can be read either as a father deciding for his daughter or as an engaged man deciding with his fiancée.

## Key Takeaways

**Both marriage and singleness are gifts from God, not superior and inferior ranks.** Facing an extreme ascetic faction in Corinth that claimed spiritual believers should swear off marriage altogether, Paul states plainly that marrying is no sin (7:28) and that every person receives their own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another (7:7).

**Within marriage, husband and wife share equal obligation and mutual authority over one another's bodies.** Instead of enforcing the one-sided expectations of Greco-Roman society, Paul places husband and wife completely on par: the wife does not rule her body independently of her husband, and the husband does not rule his body independently of his wife (7:3–5). Even temporary abstinence for prayer requires mutual agreement and a fixed end.

**Marriage is an enduring covenant, yet real-world hardship calls for wisdom and a commitment to peace.** Paul restates the Lord's command that believing spouses must not divorce (7:10–11). Where only one spouse believes, he urges staying if the unbeliever consents, yet releases the believer if the unbeliever insists on departing, because God has called us to peace (7:12–16).

**Because this world in its present form is passing away, believers live freely without being chained to earthly status.** Whether circumcised or uncircumcised, enslaved or free, married or single, what matters is walking with God and keeping His commandments (7:17–24). Knowing that "the time is short" frees believers to hold earthly cares lightly so they can serve the Lord with an undivided heart (7:29–35).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul's rule on the permanence of marriage rests directly on Jesus' own words.** When Paul commands wives not to leave their husbands and husbands not to divorce their wives, he explicitly credits the Lord (7:10–11), echoing Jesus' foundational principle: "Therefore what God has joined together, let man not separate" (Mark 10:9).

**Commending singleness for undivided service parallels Jesus' words without diminishing marriage.** From the opening chapters of Scripture, God declared, "It is not good for the man to be alone," and instituted marriage (Genesis 2:18). In the New Testament, Jesus also opened a special path for those able to embrace singleness "for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 19:12), and Paul carries that vision forward by encouraging those gifted with singleness to use their freedom for the Lord's work (7:7; 7:32–35).

**Viewing enslaved believers as Christ's freedmen planted the seed that would undo slavery from within.** While not calling for armed revolt, Paul tells enslaved Christians to seize freedom if the chance arises, and declares that every believer has been bought at a price and belongs to Christ rather than human masters (7:21–23). Later, when sending the runaway slave Onesimus back to Philemon, Paul appeals to Philemon to welcome him "no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a beloved brother" (Philemon 1:16).

**Living lightly in a passing world is a shared refrain across the New Testament.** When Paul tells the married, the grieving, the joyful, and the buyers not to be mastered by earthly attachments because "this world in its present form is passing away" (7:29–31), he speaks in harmony with the apostle John: "The world is passing away, along with its desires; but whoever does the will of God remains forever" (1 John 2:17).

## Notes & Nuances

**Paul never treats marriage as sinful or second-class.** Many scholars recognize 7:1 as a Corinthian slogan quoted from their letter, not Paul's personal motto. He explicitly affirms that marriage is not a sin (7:28) and is a gift from God (7:7). His preference for remaining unmarried in this chapter is tied directly to "the present crisis" (7:26) and to minimizing distraction in serving the Lord (7:32–35).

**Verse 7:15 on abandonment in a mixed marriage is interpreted in two main ways across churches.** Paul writes that if the unbelieving spouse chooses to leave, let them leave, for the believer "is not bound." Many Protestant commentators take "not bound" to mean the marriage bond itself is dissolved and the abandoned believer is free to remarry. Other interpreters understand the verse as permitting peaceful separation without authorizing remarriage, pointing to 7:39, which states that a wife is bound to her husband as long as he lives.

**Saying in 7:14 that an unbelieving spouse and children are "sanctified" or "holy" does not mean they are automatically saved.** Just two verses later Paul asks, "How do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband?" (7:16), proving the unbeliever still needs personal saving faith. "Sanctified" here means the household stands within the sphere of the believing spouse's grace, so the Christian is not defiled by continuing to live with an unbelieving partner.

**Distinguishing "the Lord's command" from Paul's own words (7:10; 7:12; 7:25) does not weaken the letter's authority.** Paul is simply distinguishing explicit sayings Jesus delivered during His earthly ministry from fresh apostolic guidance required by Gentile church situations. Even so, Paul concludes with quiet confidence that he too speaks as one who "has the Spirit of God" (7:40).

**The counsel to "remain in the calling in which he was called" in 7:20–24 is no endorsement of slavery.** Paul is countering the mistaken notion that conversion demands instantly tearing up one's existing marital or social ties. Right inside 7:21, he tells enslaved believers that if freedom becomes available, they should take it—showing freedom is better than slavery, even while true human dignity rests in belonging to Christ.

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, North African theologian in Carthage around AD 200, wrote two letters to his wife reflecting deeply on 1 Corinthians 7, especially the requirement in 7:39 to remarry "only in the Lord." **Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, drew on 1 Corinthians 7 to hold a steady middle course against two extremes of his day: marriage is genuinely good and instituted by God rather than sinful, while consecrated singleness remains a noble calling. He also highlights the remarkable mutuality of 7:4, where husband and wife bear identical obligations of faithfulness to each other. *(On the Good of Marriage)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine wrote On the Good of Marriage to defend the goodness of marriage while honoring singleness on the basis of 1 Corinthians 7 -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, read 1 Corinthians 7 as liberating consciences from mandatory clerical celibacy. Luther points out that Paul explicitly calls singleness a special gift not given to everyone (7:7) and insists, "I am saying this for your own good, not to restrict you" (7:35); forcing celibacy on those without that gift throws a snare over their consciences. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians 7, 1523)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther published a dedicated commentary on 1 Corinthians 7 in 1523 challenging compulsory clerical celibacy on the basis of 7:7 and 7:35 -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, stresses that 7:1 is not Paul's own maxim, but an ascetic slogan quoted from the Corinthians' letter. The entire chapter is Paul's balanced reply: first affirming full marital intimacy in 7:2–5, and only then presenting the practical advantages of singleness amid "the present crisis" (7:26). *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 7 verses 1–7 and 25–35)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee strongly defends reading 7:1 as a Corinthian slogan and frames the whole chapter around the present crisis -->

**The Mishnah**, the compendium of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, specifies in its tractate *Ketubot* that marital intimacy is a protected obligation that spouses may not arbitrarily withhold, allowing temporary interruption only for fixed periods and weighty reasons such as Torah study or travel. This shows how deeply Paul's discussion of marital duty and temporary mutual consent in 7:3–5 grows out of Jewish safeguards for marriage—while extending completely equal rights to the wife. *(Mishnah, Ketubot, chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Mishnah Ketubot chapter 5 details conjugal obligations rooted in Exodus 21:10, providing close Jewish background to 1 Corinthians 7:3–5 -->
