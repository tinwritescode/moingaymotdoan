# 1 Corinthians 11 — The Lord's Supper

## Story

Paul closes his discussion of idol food with an invitation: "Imitate me, just as I also imitate Christ" (11:1). That line belongs with the previous chapter: he has just shown how he lays aside his rights for others, and that is how he follows Christ.

Then he turns to public worship when the church gathers. He commends them for remembering him in everything and holding to the traditions he passed on. Yet he wants them to understand that the head of every man is Christ, the head of woman is man, and the head of Christ is God. Any man who prays or prophesies with his head covered dishonors his head. Any woman who prays or prophesies with her head uncovered dishonors her head—it is just as though her head were shaved. If a woman does not cover her head, she should cut off her hair; and if it is disgraceful for a woman to have her hair cut off or shaved, she should cover her head (11:2–6). Paul assumes that women pray and prophesy when the church assembles; his concern is the manner in which they do so. The word "head" can mean authority over another, or it can mean source—like the headwaters of a river—and interpreters divide along these two readings.

He draws an argument from creation. A man ought not to cover his head, since he is the image and glory of God; woman is the glory of man. For man did not come from woman, but woman from man; neither was man created for woman, but woman for man. For this reason, and because of the angels, a woman ought to have a sign of authority on her head (11:7–10; Genesis 2:18–23). "Because of the angels" is one of the most puzzling phrases in the letter; many think it means that angels are present in worship assemblies observing their order and reverence.

Yet Paul immediately balances the picture: in the Lord, woman is not independent of man, nor is man independent of woman. For just as woman came from man, so also man is born of woman—and everything comes from God. Judge for yourselves: is it proper for a woman to pray to God with her head uncovered? Does not nature itself teach that if a man has long hair, it is a disgrace to him, whereas if a woman has long hair, it is her glory, for her hair is given to her as a covering? If anyone wants to argue about this, neither the apostles nor the churches of God have any other practice (11:11–16). In Corinth, a married woman appearing uncovered in public was easily mistaken for promiscuous. Churches today apply this passage differently: some retain head coverings, while most see the covering as a first-century cultural custom whose enduring principle is modesty, honor, and order.

On the next topic, however, Paul has no praise to offer: their gatherings do more harm than good. First, he hears that when they come together as a church, there are divisions among them—and to some extent he believes it. There must be factions among them so that those who are approved by God may stand out. When they assemble, it is not the Lord's Supper they are eating, for each one goes ahead with his own meal; one goes hungry while another gets drunk. Do they not have homes to eat and drink in? Or do they despise the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing? Paul cannot commend them for this (11:17–22). In that era, the Lord's Supper took place within a shared meal hosted in a wealthy believer's home. The well-to-do arrived early and dined richly in the main dining room, while slaves and poorer workers arrived after their shift to find only space in the courtyard and little food left.

Paul recounts what he received from the Lord and passed on to them. On the night Jesus was betrayed, He took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and said, "This is My body, which is for you; do this in remembrance of Me." In the same way, after supper He took the cup, saying, "This cup is the new covenant in My blood; do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of Me." For every time they eat this bread and drink this cup, they proclaim the Lord's death until He comes (11:23–26; Luke 22:19–20). This is the earliest surviving written record of the Lord's Supper, penned even before the Gospels.

Therefore, whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty of sinning against the body and blood of the Lord. Each person must examine himself before eating of the bread and drinking of the cup. For anyone who eats and drinks without discerning the body eats and drinks judgment on himself. That is why many among them are weak and sick, and quite a few have fallen asleep—that is, died. If we judged ourselves properly, we would not come under judgment; yet when we are judged by the Lord, we are being disciplined so that we will not be condemned along with the world (11:27–32). "The body" here likely carries two overlapping meanings: Christ's body represented in the bread, and Christ's body the church, whose poor members the wealthy were despising. "Unworthy" describes the manner of eating, not the worthiness of the eater: nobody is worthy on their own, yet everyone must partake in a worthy manner.

So then, when they gather to eat, they must wait for one another. If anyone is hungry, he should eat at home, so that their meetings do not bring judgment. As for the remaining details, Paul will set them in order when he arrives (11:33–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Public worship honors both creation's order and the mutual interdependence of men and women.** Paul affirms that both men and women pray and prophesy in the assembly while calling them to worship with fitting dignity to honor God (11:4–5). Even while appealing to creation's sequence, he immediately balances it: in the Lord, neither woman nor man stands independent of the other, because all things come from God (11:11–12).

**Rich-versus-poor divisions at the shared meal turn the Lord's Supper into an insult to the church.** When wealthy members arrive early to feast and drink while poorer believers go hungry, their worship gathering makes them worse rather than better (11:17–22). Paul tells them bluntly that what they are eating is no longer the Lord's Supper at all, because it contradicts the self-giving unity at the table's heart.

**The Lord's Supper commemorates the new covenant and proclaims Christ's death until He returns.** Paul passes on Jesus' own words from the night He was betrayed: the bread signifies His body given for us, and the cup seals the new covenant in His blood (11:23–25). Each time the church shares the bread and cup, it looks back to the cross and forward to Christ's return (11:26).

**Partaking worthily requires honest self-examination and deep respect for the body of Christ.** Coming to the table while scorning poor brothers fails to discern the Lord's body and invites His discipline (11:27–32). Paul's remedy is both spiritual and wonderfully practical: when you come together to eat, "wait for one another" (11:33).

## Deeper Meaning

**Creation's order in 11:7–12 always stands alongside equal dignity from the very first page of Scripture.** Saying that woman came from man and man is born through woman (11:8; 11:12) never lowers a woman's worth. Right at Genesis 1:27, Scripture proclaims that both bear God's image equally: "So God created man in His own image; in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them."

**The cup of "the new covenant in My blood" fulfills both Sinai and Jeremiah's prophecy.** Long ago Moses sprinkled sacrificial blood on Israel and declared, "This is the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you" (Exodus 24:8). Centuries later, the prophet Jeremiah foretold the day God would establish "a new covenant" with His people (Jeremiah 31:31). At the Lord's table, Jesus' blood inaugurates that promised new covenant (11:25).

**Verses 11:23–25 preserve the earliest written witness to the Lord's Supper in the entire New Testament.** Written in the mid-50s AD—before the Gospels circulated widely—Paul's tradition aligns remarkably closely with Luke's Gospel account (Luke 22:19–20). This proves that the Lord's Supper stood at the unified heart of Christian worship from the earliest years of the church.

**Discipline through sickness in 11:30–32 reflects loving correction meant to rescue believers from final condemnation.** Paul explains that when believers are judged by the Lord now, they are being disciplined "so that we will not be condemned along with the world" (11:32). That theme matches Hebrews directly: "the Lord disciplines the one He loves, and He chastises every son He receives" (Hebrews 12:6).

## Notes & Nuances

**Christians hold differing views on how to apply head coverings in 11:2–16 today.** Some traditions understand "head" as leadership authority and view head coverings as a permanent sign of creation order to be retained in worship. Most modern churches understand head coverings (or the upswept hairstyle worn by married women in Greco-Roman cities) as a first-century cultural symbol of modesty and marital honor, while holding fast to the timeless principles of reverence, propriety, and mutual interdependence (11:11–12). On every reading, 11:5 confirms plainly that women prayed and prophesied in gathered church worship.

**The phrase "because of the angels" in 11:10 remains one of the hardest lines in the letter.** The most widely held explanation is that angels are invisibly present witnessing the church's worship (4:9), calling believers to maintain reverence and order before heaven and earth alike. Other scholars link the line to the order of creation that the angels themselves uphold.

**"Eating or drinking in an unworthy manner" (11:27) does not demand sinless perfection before coming to the table.** If it meant only the sinless could partake, no human being could ever take communion. In context (11:20–22; 11:33–34), "unworthy manner" refers to despising poor believers, eating selfishly in divisive cliques, and ignoring the sacred meaning of Christ's body.

**Verse 11:30 linking sickness and death to abusing the Lord's Supper must be read carefully.** Paul affirms that God can use illness or even death to discipline a congregation openly desecrating the Lord's table and humiliating the poor. That never entitles anyone to reverse the logic and assume that whenever someone falls ill, they must be under divine punishment for personal sin.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the compendium of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, describes in its tractate *Pesachim* the Passover meal with its cups of wine, unleavened bread, and spoken explanations recalling deliverance from Egypt across every generation. **John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, fiercely condemns the rich-poor divide in his exposition of 11:17–22. The Lord's Supper is the Master's common table open equally to all; when the wealthy eat and drink their fill while leaving the poor hungry beside them, they turn the Lord's feast into a selfish private banquet and insult Christ Himself. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians, Homily 27)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's Homily 27 on 1 Corinthians 11 is famous for its stinging indictment of wealth disparity at the Lord's Supper -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, treats head coverings in 11:2–16 as an outward custom that varies across eras and cultures, while the lasting principle is modesty and order in worship. On 11:27–29, he comforts tender-hearted believers that self-examination is never intended to scare the imperfect away from the Lord's table, but to invite them to come in repentance and love for their brothers. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 11 verses 5 and 27–29)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin clearly distinguishes cultural custom from enduring order on 11:2–16 and reassures weak consciences on 11:27–29 -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, emphasizes that failing to "discern the body" in 11:29 points not merely to the bread of communion, but primarily to the church community itself—Christ's body being torn apart as the wealthy humiliate the poor. That is why Paul's practical conclusion in 11:33 is so direct: "wait for one another." *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 11 verses 17–34)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee stresses that "the body" in 11:29 prominently includes the divided church community, linking directly to 11:33 -->
