# 1 Corinthians 10 — Let Him Who Thinks He Stands Take Heed

## Story

Paul has just remarked that even he could be disqualified. Now he turns to Israel's history as a stark warning. Our forefathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea. They were all baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea. They all ate the same spiritual food and drank the same spiritual drink, for they drank from the spiritual rock that accompanied them, "and that rock was Christ." Nevertheless, God was not pleased with most of them, and their bodies were scattered across the wilderness (10:1–5; Exodus 13:21; Exodus 14:22; Exodus 16:4; Exodus 17:6). The cloud and the sea resemble baptism; manna and water from the rock resemble the Lord's Supper. Some Corinthians may have imagined that being baptized and sharing the Supper guaranteed immunity from judgment; Israel enjoyed comparable privileges and still perished.

These things happened as examples for us, to keep us from setting our hearts on evil things as they did. Do not be idolaters, as some of them were, as it is written: "The people sat down to eat and drink, and got up to indulge in revelry." We should not commit sexual immorality, as some of them did—and in one day twenty-three thousand fell. We should not test Christ, as some of them did and were killed by snakes. And do not grumble, as some of them grumbled and were destroyed by the destroying angel (10:6–10; Exodus 32:6; Numbers 25:1–9; Numbers 21:5–6; Numbers 16:41–49). Numbers records twenty-four thousand dead; Paul may be counting those who fell in a single day, or using a round number.

These things happened to them as examples and were written down as warnings for us, on whom the fulfillment of the ages has come. So the one who thinks he is standing firm should be careful not to fall. No temptation has seized them except what is common to mankind. And God is faithful: He will not let them be tempted beyond what they can bear, but when they are tempted, He will also provide a way of escape so that they can stand up under it (10:11–13). Warning and comfort stand side by side: never trust yourself, yet always trust a faithful God.

Therefore, beloved, flee from idolatry. Paul speaks to them as sensible people and asks them to judge for themselves what he says. Is not the cup of blessing that we bless a participation in the blood of Christ? And is not the bread that we break a participation in the body of Christ? Because there is one loaf, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one loaf (10:14–17).

Consider the people of Israel: do not those who eat the sacrifices participate in the altar? Is Paul saying that food sacrificed to an idol is anything, or that an idol is anything? No! Rather, the sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons, not to God, and he does not want them to be participants with demons. They cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons too; they cannot share in the Lord's table and the table of demons. Are we trying to provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than He (10:18–22; Deuteronomy 32:17)? Back in chapter 8, Paul agreed that an idol itself is nothing. Here he adds the crucial reality: real spiritual powers lurk behind pagan worship. Eating meat is fine, yet feasting at a pagan temple ceremony is another matter altogether.

He returns to their slogan: "Everything is permissible," yes, but not everything is beneficial; not everything builds up. No one should seek his own good, but the good of others. Eat anything sold in the meat market without raising questions of conscience, for "The earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof." If an unbeliever invites you to a meal and you want to go, eat whatever is put before you without raising questions of conscience. Yet if someone tells you, "This has been offered in sacrifice," then do not eat it—out of consideration for the one who told you and for conscience' sake, not your own conscience, but the other person's (10:23–29; Psalms 24:1).

Paul voices the protest of the "strong": why should my freedom be judged by another's conscience? If I partake with thankfulness, why am I denounced because of something I thank God for? Rather than debating endlessly, Paul sets down a higher compass: whether you eat or drink or whatever you do, do it all to the glory of God. Give no offense to Jews or to Greeks or to the church of God—just as Paul tries to please everyone in every way, not seeking his own advantage, but that of many, so that they may be saved (10:29–33).

## Key Takeaways

**Religious privileges are never a charm that shields believers from falling.** Israel walked through the sea beneath the cloud and ate and drank spiritual provision from heaven, yet most of them perished in the desert because of idolatry and indulgence (10:1–9). Paul's razor-sharp warning punctures all complacency: "the one who thinks he is standing firm should be careful not to fall" (10:12).

**No believer is ever cornered into sin, because a faithful God always opens a way out.** Temptations facing Christians are ordinary human tests rather than irresistible forces (10:13). Confidence rests not in our own willpower, but in God's faithfulness, which always provides an exit so we can endure without giving in.

**The Lord's Supper is real fellowship with Christ, making pagan temple banquets completely incompatible.** Even though carved wood and stone are nothing in themselves, genuine demonic powers stand behind pagan sacrifices (10:19–20). Those united to Christ through one bread and one cup cannot sit at the table of demons without provoking the Lord to jealousy (10:16–22).

**In everyday choices—down to eating and drinking—the supreme measure is the good of others and the glory of God.** Meat sold in the market or served in a private home belongs to the Creator, so believers may eat freely without anxious inquiries; yet if someone explicitly flags it as sacrificial meat, love abstains for their conscience' sake (10:25–29). Every choice comes home to one clear rule: "whether you eat or drink or whatever you do, do it all to the glory of God" (10:31).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul reads Israel's exodus journey as a living foreshadowing of Christ and the church.** Long ago God instructed Moses to strike the rock at Horeb so water would pour out for His people (Exodus 17:6). Paul looks beneath the surface and sees Christ already present and sustaining His people—"that rock was Christ" (10:4)—just as Jesus later declared Himself "the bread of life" who satisfies every hunger and thirst (John 6:35).

**The warning about demons behind idols draws straight from the Song of Moses.** When Paul writes that "the sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons, not to God" (10:20), he echoes Moses' indictment of Israel when they turned to foreign gods: "They sacrificed to demons, not to God" (Deuteronomy 32:17). The seriousness of idolatry never softened between the Old and New Testaments.

**Freedom to eat market meat rests on the Psalms' confession of the Creator's ownership.** To free believers from superstitious fear over marketplace food, Paul quotes Psalm 24:1: "The earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof" (10:26). Because every creature comes from God's hand, no idol can lay claim to His creation or render it impure.

**Idolatry invariably pulls moral compromise in its wake, from the golden calf at Sinai to Corinth.** Paul cites the scene around the golden calf: "The people sat down to eat and drink, and got up to indulge in revelry" (10:7; Exodus 32:6). History repeated itself in Corinth, where pagan temple banquets blurred smoothly into sexual immorality, warning the church that unfaithfulness in worship inevitably breeds moral decay.

## Notes & Nuances

**"The spiritual rock that accompanied them" in 10:4 reflects a Jewish interpretive tradition familiar in Paul's day.** Because Exodus 17 and Numbers 20 describe water flowing from a rock near the beginning and again near the end of Israel's wandering, Jewish tradition told of a miraculous rock or well that traveled along with Israel across the desert. Paul borrows that well-known image to convey a spiritual reality: Christ Himself accompanied and sustained His people.

**Verse 10:13 is often misquoted as a promise that life will never bring unbearable suffering.** In context, the verse specifically addresses "temptation" to sin—especially the idolatry and immorality just cataloged in 10:6–10. God never promises that hardships will never exceed our personal strength—Paul himself once felt crushed "far beyond our ability to endure" (2 Corinthians 1:8)—but He does promise that we will never be forced into sin, for He always provides an escape.

**Chapter 10 does not contradict chapter 8 regarding food sacrificed to idols.** Paul draws a crisp, practical boundary: attending cultic feasts inside pagan temples entails fellowship with demons and is completely off limits (10:14–22); meat sold in the open marketplace or served at a private dinner in an unbeliever's home may be eaten freely without interrogation (10:25–27), unless someone explicitly identifies it as sacrificial meat, in which case love abstains for the sake of that person's conscience (10:28–29).

**The two questions in 10:29–30 are attributed to two possible speakers.** Many interpreters read "For why should my freedom be determined by someone else's conscience?" as an objection from the "strong" Corinthians that Paul quotes and immediately answers in 10:31–33: a believer's aim is God's glory and the salvation of others. Others read the questions as Paul's own explanation that refraining at the right moment keeps one's freedom from pointless criticism.

## What Commentators Say

**The Tosefta**, an early Jewish rabbinic compilation from around the third century, preserves in its tractate *Sukkah* the oral tradition that a moving rock or miraculous well rolled alongside Israel throughout their forty wilderness years to supply them with water. **John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, unpacks 10:16–17 by emphasizing how deeply sharing the broken bread unites believers both to Christ and to one another. If all share one loaf to become one body, it is unthinkable either to fracture fellowship with brothers or to wander off to share the table of demons. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians, Homily 24)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's Homily 24 on 1 Corinthians 10:16–17 is renowned in Eucharistic theology and church unity -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, presses the lesson of 10:1–5 against trusting blindly in outward rituals. Israel too received spiritual signs corresponding to baptism and the Lord's Supper, yet unbelief still brought judgment in the desert; sacraments convey blessing only when received with living faith and obedience. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 10 verses 1–5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin expounds 10:1–5 pointedly as a warning against mechanical reliance on outward rites apart from faith -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, maps out Paul's clear practical boundary in chapter 10: an absolute ban on temple cult meals (10:14–22), paired with freedom regarding marketplace meat and private dinners (10:25–27). He also emphasizes that 10:13 promises an escape from temptation to sin—specifically idolatry—not immunity from earthly trials. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 10 verses 13–33)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee clarifies both the temple-versus-market distinction and the exact context of 10:13 in NICNT -->

**Richard Hays**, American New Testament scholar, notices that Paul calls ancient Israel "our forefathers" in 10:1 even though most Corinthian Christians were Gentiles.
