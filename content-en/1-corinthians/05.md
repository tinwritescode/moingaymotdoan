# 1 Corinthians 5 — A Little Leaven

## Story

Reports are circulating everywhere that there is sexual immorality in the church at Corinth—a kind not even tolerated among pagans: a man is living with his father's wife. And yet the Corinthians are arrogant! Instead, they should have been filled with grief and put the man who did this out of their fellowship (5:1–2). The woman was almost certainly his stepmother rather than his birth mother; both the Law of Moses (Leviticus 18:8) and Roman law forbade such a union. Paul does not rebuke the woman, likely because she was not a member of the church. What shocks him is not merely the sin itself, but the congregation's attitude: they are proud, perhaps treating their tolerance as a badge of freedom in Christ.

Though absent in body, Paul is present in spirit, and he has already passed judgment on the offender as if he were there. When the church gathers in the name of the Lord Jesus, with Paul's spirit present and the power of the Lord Jesus among them, they are to hand this man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved on the day of the Lord (5:3–5). Handing him over to Satan means putting him outside the church, back into the realm of the world where Satan holds sway. Even so, the final goal is still his salvation, not his ruin.

Their boasting is not good. Do they not know that a little leaven leavens the whole batch of dough? They must get rid of the old leaven so they may be a new unleavened batch—as they really are. For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed. Therefore let us keep the festival, not with the old leaven, the leaven of malice and wickedness, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth (5:6–8). Before Passover, every Jewish household thoroughly searched out and cleared away every last crumb of leaven (Exodus 12:15–20). Paul's point is clear: the lamb has already been slain, so the house must be cleared of leaven.

Paul then clears up a misunderstanding. In an earlier letter, he had told them not to associate with sexually immoral people. He did not mean all the immoral, greedy, swindling, or idolatrous people of this world—otherwise they would have to leave the world altogether. Rather, he meant they must not associate with anyone who claims to be a brother yet is sexually immoral or greedy, an idolater or slanderer, a drunkard or a swindler; with such a person, do not even eat (5:9–11). That earlier letter has been lost, showing that 1 Corinthians was not actually the first letter Paul sent to this church.

What business is it of Paul's to judge those outside the church? Those inside are the ones the church must judge; God will judge those outside. Paul closes with the command of the Law of Moses: "Expel the wicked man from among you" (5:12–13; Deuteronomy 17:7). The Corinthians had gotten everything backwards: they were indulgent toward blatant sin inside the church while imagining they should cut themselves off from sinners outside.

## Key Takeaways

**Tolerating open, unrepentant sin corrupts the entire church rather than demonstrating love or freedom.** What appalls Paul is not only that a man has taken his father's wife, but that the congregation remains puffed up instead of grieving (5:1–2). Ignored, a small trace of sinful leaven quickly works its way through the entire batch (5:6).

**Church discipline is severe, yet its ultimate aim is redemption.** Paul instructs the whole gathered church in the Lord's name to remove the unrepentant offender from fellowship, returning him to the world under Satan's domain (5:3–5). This stern measure is never about venting anger or permanent rejection; it is meant to awaken the sinner so that his spirit may be saved on the day of the Lord.

**Because Christ has been sacrificed as our Passover lamb, the church's entire life is a Feast of Unleavened Bread.** Believers have already been cleansed through Christ's death, so they must live in step with who they already are—casting out malice and evil and walking in sincerity and truth (5:7–8).

**The church is called to guard purity within rather than pass verdict on the outside world.** Paul does not summon Christians to withdraw from unbelievers or abandon society; instead, he draws a sharp boundary: God judges outsiders, while the church must deal honestly and firmly with anyone claiming the name of brother who persists in open sin (5:9–13).

## Deeper Meaning

**Calling Christ our Passover lamb ties the cross directly to Israel's night of deliverance.** When Paul writes, "For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed," he frames the whole Christian life around the Feast of Unleavened Bread (5:7–8). In ancient Israel, every family had to remove all leaven from their homes for seven days to commemorate their rescue from Egypt (Exodus 12:15); now believers must clear the leaven of sin out of God's household.

**Removing an unrepentant offender from fellowship stands directly in line with Jesus' teaching.** Paul calls the whole congregation to assemble, pass judgment, and separate the sinner from their fellowship (5:4–5). Jesus Himself had taught that if a sinning brother refuses even to listen to the church, "treat him as you would a pagan or a tax collector" (Matthew 18:17)—no longer relating to him as part of the household of faith until he turns back.

**The proverb of a little leaven working through the whole lump is a recurring warning in Paul's letters.** Here he uses it to warn about unchecked moral sin spreading through a congregation (5:6). In Galatians, he repeats the very same saying—"A little leaven works through the whole batch of dough"—to warn against false teaching (Galatians 5:9): whether bad living or false belief, leaving a small patch untouched eventually spoils the whole community.

**The chapter's closing line repeats the covenant discipline formula of Deuteronomy.** The charge "Expel the wicked man from among you" (5:13) echoes the recurring verdict in Moses' law whenever grave sin threatened to defile God's people (Deuteronomy 17:7), just as taking one's father's wife stood strictly condemned in the law (Leviticus 18:8). Paul treats the New Testament church as heir to the holiness of God's covenant people, replacing stoning with removal from fellowship.

## Notes & Nuances

**Handing the offender over to Satan "for the destruction of the flesh" in 5:5 has two major interpretations.** Many commentators understand it to mean putting the sinner outside the protective fellowship of the church into the world under Satan's sway, where hardship or the bitter fruit of sin breaks down his proud, sinful nature ("flesh"), leading him to repent and be saved. Several early church fathers and modern scholars take "flesh" literally to mean the physical body—suggesting the man might suffer illness or even physical death (cf. 11:30) while his spirit is spared at the final day. On either view, the sentence is redemptive rather than a curse to hell.

**Church discipline never shuts the door permanently or abandons mercy.** The stated purpose in 5:5 is that the offender's spirit may be saved. Once a disciplined believer truly repents, the church must welcome them back with open arms: in his next letter, Paul urges them to "forgive and comfort him, so that he will not be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow" (2 Corinthians 2:7).

**The command "not even to eat with such a one" in 5:11 applies strictly to someone claiming to be a Christian while persisting in open sin.** Paul explicitly clarifies that he is not forbidding everyday contact with unbelievers, since then believers "would have to leave this world" (5:9–10). Christians still live, work, and share meals warmly with unbelieving neighbors; what is forbidden is casually sharing fellowship meals as though nothing were wrong with a self-professed believer living openly in unrepentant sin.

**This chapter does not contradict "do not judge before the appointed time" in 4:5.** In 4:5, Paul warns against judging the hidden inward motives of God's servants—matters only the Lord can see. Here in 5:12–13, he requires the church to exercise plain discernment regarding public, unmistakable sin inside its own community.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the collection of Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, devotes the tractate *Pesachim* to describing the eve-of-Passover ritual of searching every dark corner of the house by lamplight to clear out and burn every trace of leaven. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, treats 1 Corinthians 5 as the biblical foundation for church discipline. In his view, excommunication is never vengeance, but serves three clear ends: keeping God's name from dishonor, preventing sin from spreading like leaven through the church, and moving the offender to shame and repentance. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 4, chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin sets forth these exact three purposes of church discipline from 1 Corinthians 5 in Institutes Book 4 chapter 12 -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, suggests that "the destruction of the flesh" in 5:5 need not mean physical death, but can denote the breaking of the man's sinful, fleshly orientation once he is placed outside the church's sphere of protection.
