# 1 Corinthians 6 — The Body Is a Temple

## Story

Paul moves on to another disgrace. When one believer has a dispute with another, how dare he take it to be judged by the unrighteous rather than by the saints? Do they not know that the saints will judge the world? And if the world is to be judged by them, are they incompetent to settle trivial cases? Do they not know that we will judge angels—how much more the things of this life (6:1–3)? In Corinth, civil lawsuits were typically the sport of the wealthy, and Roman courts tended to favor status and money.

So if they have disputes over everyday matters, why appoint as judges people who have no standing in the church? Paul says this to shame them. Is there really not a single wise person among them able to arbitrate between brothers? Instead, brother goes to law against brother—and before unbelievers at that (6:4–6). A congregation that prided itself on wisdom could not produce one person wise enough to reconcile two brothers.

The very fact that they have lawsuits among them is already a defeat. Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be defrauded? Instead, they themselves do wrong and cheat—and they do this to their own brothers (6:7–8). Here Paul echoes Jesus' words about turning the other cheek and surrendering one's cloak as well (Matthew 5:39–40).

Do they not know that the wicked will not inherit the kingdom of God? Let no one be deceived. Paul lists the sexually immoral, idolaters, adulterers, men who submit to or perform homosexual acts, thieves, the greedy, drunkards, verbal abusers, and swindlers—none of these will inherit the kingdom of God. Then he adds: "And that is what some of you were. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified, in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (6:9–11). The list targets unrepentant patterns of life rather than a single category of people, and the closing line reveals that the Corinthian church itself was built out of people rescued from just such lives.

Next Paul quotes a slogan the Corinthians liked to repeat: "Everything is permissible for me." He answers: yes, but not everything is beneficial, and he will not be mastered by anything. They also say: "Food for the stomach and the stomach for food, and God will destroy them both." From that they inferred that the body does not matter, so they could do whatever they pleased with it. Paul counters: the body is not meant for sexual immorality, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body. By His power God raised the Lord from the dead, and He will raise us also (6:12–14). Many modern translations place those slogans in quotation marks as Corinthian sayings Paul cites in order to refute them. Because the body will be raised, what we do with it carries eternal significance.

Do they not know that their bodies are members of Christ Himself? Should anyone take the members of Christ and unite them with a prostitute? Never! Whoever unites himself with a prostitute becomes one with her in body, for Scripture says, "The two will become one flesh." But whoever unites himself with the Lord is one with Him in spirit (6:15–17; Genesis 2:24). Corinth was so notorious for prostitution that Greek even had a verb—"to Corinthianize"—meaning to live promiscuously.

"Flee from sexual immorality." Every other sin a person commits is outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body. Do they not know that their body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within them, whom they have received from God, and that they are not their own? They were bought at a price. Therefore glorify God with your body (6:18–20). In chapter 3, the whole church together is God's temple; here, each believer's body is a temple as well.

## Key Takeaways

**Hauling fellow believers before secular courts over material disputes is already a loss before the trial begins.** The church will one day join Christ in judging the world, yet it cannot find one wise peacemaker to resolve small disagreements internally (6:1–6). When Christians sue one another before unbelievers, both sides tarnish Christ's name; Paul states bluntly that it is better to absorb the loss (6:7–8).

**Grace never blurs the boundary between the old life and the new.** The sobering catalog in 6:9–10 makes plain that unjust, unrestrained lifestyles have no share in God's kingdom. Yet right after the stern warning shines magnificent good news: many Corinthians once lived that very way, and now they have been "washed, sanctified, and justified" through Jesus Christ and the Spirit (6:11).

**Christian freedom is not license to do anything one likes, but refusing to be enslaved by anything.** Against the Corinthian motto "Everything is permissible for me," Paul raises two straightforward tests: does it build up, and is it making me a slave (6:12)? Genuine freedom survives only when a believer belongs wholly to the Lord.

**A believer's body belongs to the Lord and is a temple of the Holy Spirit, making bodily purity a sacred matter.** Unlike temporary food for the stomach, the human body was made for the Lord and will be raised by His power (6:13–14). Bought at a great price, believers are no longer free to treat their bodies casually, but are called to honor God in them (6:19–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**The reminder that the saints will judge the world draws on Daniel's vision.** When Paul asks, "Do you not know that the saints will judge the world?" (6:2), he builds on a classic Old Testament expectation: when the Ancient of Days comes, "judgment was given in favor of the saints of the Most High," and they receive the kingdom (Daniel 7:22). That grand destiny must reshape how believers handle small quarrels today.

**Choosing to suffer wrong rather than sue a brother echoes the Sermon on the Mount.** Paul's question "Why not rather be wronged?" (6:7) recalls Jesus' countercultural command: "if someone wants to sue you and take your tunic, let him have your cloak as well" (Matthew 5:40). Christ's followers never place personal advantage above family peace and the witness of the gospel.

**Paul appeals to Genesis 2:24 to show that sexual union is never merely physical.** The Corinthians viewed sex like eating a meal when hungry, yet Paul quotes the creation text: "The two will become one flesh" (6:16; Genesis 2:24). Bodily union reaches deep into the whole person and therefore cannot be divorced from spiritual union with Christ (6:15–17).

**"You were bought at a price" points straight to the cross and embodied worship.** Borrowing the language of redeeming a slave, Paul reminds believers they now belong to a new Master (6:19–20). Peter spells out what that price was—not silver or gold, but "the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Peter 1:18–19)—which is why Paul elsewhere calls believers to "offer your bodies as living sacrifices, holy and pleasing to God" (Romans 12:1).

## Notes & Nuances

**Forbidding believers to sue one another in 6:1–8 never means hiding criminal abuse.** Paul is addressing civil and financial disputes among Christians—"matters of this life" (6:4). Serious crimes such as violence or abuse fall under the authority of civil government, which God appointed to punish wrongdoers (Romans 13:1–4), and must never be swept into private arbitration to save face.

**The two Greek terms concerning same-sex behavior in 6:9–10 are debated among interpreters.** The Berean Standard Bible renders the pair "men who submit to or perform homosexual acts," reflecting the passive and active Greek words (*malakoi* and *arsenokoitai*). The traditional and majority scholarly view understands the pair as referring broadly to male same-sex sexual behavior, continuing the ethic of Old Testament law (Leviticus 18:22). Some modern scholars argue the terms targeted specifically exploitative Greco-Roman practices such as male prostitution or pederasty. Notably, Paul places them right alongside greed, drunkenness, slander, and swindling—and immediately announces in 6:11 that God's grace washes and transforms every kind of past.

**Saying in 6:18 that other sins are "outside the body" does not minimize other sins.** Paul is not suggesting that theft or drunkenness leaves the body untouched; rather, he highlights what makes sexual immorality uniquely damaging: it takes a body that belongs to Christ's members and fuses it as "one flesh" with another, violating the Spirit's temple from within.

**Christian faith honors the human body instead of despising it.** Many currents of Greek thought prized the soul while dismissing the body as a disposable shell, spawning two opposite errors: harsh asceticism or careless indulgence on the assumption that bodily acts leave the soul untouched. Paul dismantles that mindset in 6:14: God will raise our bodies from the dead, proving that the body has eternal worth.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, observes on 6:7 that even if a Christian wins his case in a secular court, he has already lost before God, because he has ruptured brotherhood and tarnished Christ's name before outsiders. He urges readers to look back at 6:11 and remember how much they themselves were forgiven, so they can let go of petty claims. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom preaches forcefully on suffering wrong rather than suing a brother in 6:7; exact homily number needs verification -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, carefully notes that 1 Corinthians 6:1–8 does not prohibit every resort to civil courts when justice or the vulnerable must be defended, but condemns a litigious, self-seeking spirit that disgraces the church before unbelievers. On 6:19–20, he treats "you are not your own" as the foundational principle of the entire Christian life. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 6 verses 1–8 and 19–20)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin is famous for making "we are not our own" central to Christian ethics and taking a measured stance on civil courts -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, identifies "Everything is permissible for me" and "Food for the stomach and the stomach for food" as slogans coined by a Corinthian group to justify temple banquets and visits to prostitutes. Paul quotes their slogans back at them and overturns them with two realities: the resurrection of the body and the indwelling temple of the Spirit. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 6 verses 12–20)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: identifying the Corinthian slogans in 6:12–13 is a central feature of Fee's NICNT exposition -->

**Richard Hays**, American New Testament scholar, observes on the vice list in 6:9–11 that Paul does not single out one sin to condemn outsiders, but sets greed, reviling, drunkenness, and sexual sins side by side before concluding with the good news of transformation in 6:11. He holds that the two Greek terms in 6:9 refer to male same-sex intercourse in continuity with Old Testament ethics, while reminding the church always to read the list in the light of saving grace and humility. *(First Corinthians, Interpretation, 1997, on chapter 6 verses 9–11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Hays treats 1 Corinthians 6:9–11 thoughtfully and thoroughly in both First Corinthians and The Moral Vision of the New Testament -->

**Philo of Alexandria**, a Jewish philosopher living in Egypt in Paul's own era, often compared the righteous soul to a sacred temple in which God dwells. Compared with Philo, Paul's breakthrough in 6:19 is startling: he calls not only the soul, but the physical flesh-and-blood body itself a temple of the Holy Spirit, because Christ was raised bodily from the dead. *(On the Special Laws)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Philo repeatedly uses temple imagery for the soul or mind across his treatises; exact book number of On the Special Laws not recalled -->
