# 1 Corinthians 2 — Hidden Wisdom

## Story

Paul reminds the Corinthians how he first arrived among them. He did not come with lofty speech or impressive wisdom as he proclaimed to them the testimony of God. He resolved to know nothing while he was with them except Jesus Christ, and Him crucified (2:1–2). Just before reaching Corinth, Paul had been in Athens, where he addressed philosophers on the Areopagus and saw only a small response (Acts 17:16–34). Some suspect that by the time he reached Corinth, he deliberately chose not to spar on philosophical terms.

He came to them in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling. His speech and preaching were not with persuasive words of human wisdom, but with a demonstration of the Spirit's power, so that their faith would not rest on human wisdom, but on God's power (2:3–5). Acts tells us that during this stay the Lord appeared to Paul in a vision at night, telling him not to be afraid and to keep speaking, because He had many people in that city (Acts 18:9–10). The Corinthians admired self-assured orators; their apostle arrived trembling.

Even so, Paul is not against all wisdom. He and his fellow workers do speak a message of wisdom among the mature, though not the wisdom of this age or of the rulers of this age, who are coming to nothing. Rather, they speak of God's wisdom in a mystery—a hidden wisdom that God destined before the ages for our glory. None of the rulers of this age understood it, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory (2:6–8). "The Lord of glory" crucified: Paul places two realities that seem impossible to combine inside a single phrase.

As it is written: "No eye has seen, no ear has heard, no heart has imagined, what God has prepared for those who love Him." People often quote this line as a picture of heaven, yet Paul goes straight on to say: God has revealed these things to us through the Spirit, for the Spirit searches all things, even the deep things of God (2:9–10; Isaiah 64:4). What no one had ever seen has now been disclosed.

He illustrates the point with a simple comparison. Only a person's own spirit within him knows his inner thoughts; in the same way, no one knows the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God. We have not received the spirit of the world, but the Spirit who is from God, so that we may understand what God has freely given us. That is why the apostles speak of these gifts not in words taught by human wisdom, but in words taught by the Spirit, "expressing spiritual truths in spiritual words" (2:11–13).

The natural person—someone who possesses only natural life without the Spirit—does not accept the things that come from the Spirit of God; to him they are foolishness, and he cannot understand them, because they are spiritually discerned. The spiritual person, by contrast, makes judgments about all things, yet is not himself subject to anyone's judgment. "For who has known the mind of the Lord, so as to instruct Him? But we have the mind of Christ" (2:14–16; Isaiah 40:13). In the very next chapter, Paul will reveal the sting hidden in the word "spiritual": the Corinthians imagined themselves spiritual, when in truth they were still acting fleshly.

## Key Takeaways

**The center of Christian preaching is not rhetorical polish, but Christ crucified.** Paul purposefully avoided arriving in Corinth like an entertainer winning crowds with dazzling language (2:1–2). If listeners are won over by clever speech, the next sleeker speaker who comes along will sway them right back again.

**Genuine faith rests on the power of God, not on the confidence of the messenger.** Paul makes no attempt to hide the weakness, fear, and trembling he felt while preaching among them (2:3). Precisely because the speaker offered nothing impressive to lean on, those who believed knew their transformation came from the Spirit rather than human persuasion (2:4–5).

**The gospel does not lack wisdom; its wisdom simply belongs to God rather than this age.** The world failed to recognize this wisdom and therefore crucified "the Lord of glory" (2:6–8). What earthly powers assumed was a final execution was in reality the plan God had prepared before time began for the glory of His people.

**Only God's Spirit can open human eyes to grasp what God has done.** Unaided human reason can investigate many matters in this world, yet only God's own Spirit knows the depths of God's heart and makes them known (2:10–12). Consequently, those without the Spirit dismiss the gospel as folly, while those with the Spirit discern the mind of Christ (2:14–16).

## Deeper Meaning

**The messenger's weakness is the very stage where God's power shines brightest.** Paul arrived in Corinth trembling so that their faith would stand on God's strength (2:3–5). Later, in his second letter to this same church, he recounts the Lord's own words to him: "My grace is sufficient for you, for My power is perfected in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9).

**What eye has not seen nor ear heard links back to Isaiah's vision of God's saving action.** In quoting 2:9, Paul draws on Isaiah's praise of a God who works wonders never heard of before on behalf of those who wait for Him (Isaiah 64:4). Yet now that ancient promise is no longer veiled, for God has unveiled it through the Spirit in the cross (2:10).

**Recognizing Jesus never springs from raw intellect, but from revelation from above.** Paul says the natural person cannot receive the things of the Spirit (2:14). When Peter confessed Jesus as the Christ, Jesus likewise told him, "this was not revealed to you by flesh and blood, but by My Father in heaven" (Matthew 16:17), and He promised that the Spirit of truth would guide His disciples into all truth (John 16:13).

**Isaiah's ancient question about the mind of the Lord now receives a startling answer.** The prophet once asked, "Who has directed the Spirit of the LORD, or informed Him as His counselor?" to underscore how small humanity is before God (Isaiah 40:13). Paul repeats that question and then adds a breathtaking claim: "But we have the mind of Christ" (2:16), because the Spirit has given the church insight into Christ's heart.

## Notes & Nuances

**"The rulers of this age" in 2:6–8 has two main interpretations.** Some commentators understand the phrase to refer to earthly authorities such as Pilate, Herod, and the religious leaders who condemned Jesus. Others argue Paul is pointing to dark spiritual powers standing behind them, or to both together. Either way, the core point stands: every ruling power of this present age failed to recognize the Lord of glory and is passing away.

**Verse 2:9 is frequently quoted about heaven, yet the context speaks of the present.** Read in isolation, "no eye has seen, no ear has heard" sounds like a description of the afterlife. Yet in the very next verse (2:10), Paul says God "has revealed it to us by the Spirit." He is referring to the salvation accomplished through the cross, which believers already perceive now, even though its full consummation still lies ahead.

**Distinguishing the "natural" person from the "spiritual" person does not split the church into two tiers.** In 2:14–15, the "natural" person is someone who has only natural life and lacks the Spirit, while the "spiritual" person is any believer who has received the Spirit. Paul is not creating an elite class of enlightened Christians guarding secret knowledge—an illusion he will demolish immediately in chapter 3.

**"Not himself subject to anyone's judgment" in 2:15 does not place a believer above all correction.** The verse sits directly in contrast with the unbeliever without the Spirit in 2:14: someone who cannot grasp the Spirit's work is in no position to pass verdict on the gospel or on those walking by the Spirit. It never exempts believers from mutual accountability and discipline within the church, as Paul himself will insist in chapter 5.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, sees in 2:14 the fundamental boundary of fallen human reason. Human beings remain capable of sharp insight in earthly affairs, yet before the saving truths of God they stand like blind people before the light until the Holy Spirit opens their eyes. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 2 verse 14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin is well known for using 1 Corinthians 2:14 to distinguish human insight in earthly matters from spiritual blindness in divine things -->

**Gordon Fee**, twentieth-century American New Testament scholar, contends that "the rulers of this age" in 2:6–8 refers to the human political and religious authorities who crucified Jesus rather than demonic powers as some interpreters suggest. He also notes that Paul deliberately adopts favorite Corinthian buzzwords like "wisdom" and "spiritual" in order to turn them back as a challenge to the congregation. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 2 verses 6–16)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee argues clearly in NICNT that archontes in 2:6–8 denotes earthly rulers and that Paul echoes Corinthian vocabulary -->

**Richard Hays**, American New Testament scholar, reads 2:16 corporately rather than merely individually. Paul writes "we have the mind of Christ" in the plural: not that individual Christians can claim to be infallible, but that the whole church together learns to view life through the self-giving pattern of the cross. *(First Corinthians, Interpretation, 1997, on chapter 2 verses 6–16)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Hays highlights the communal dimension and cruciform pattern of 1 Corinthians 2; exact wording on 2:16 uncertain -->
