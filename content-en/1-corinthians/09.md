# 1 Corinthians 9 — All Things to All People

## Story

Paul now offers himself as a living illustration of laying down one's rights. Is he not free? Is he not an apostle? Has he not seen Jesus our Lord? Are the Corinthians not the fruit of his labor in the Lord? Even if he is not an apostle to others, surely he is to them, for they themselves are the seal of his apostleship in the Lord. That is his defense to those who sit in judgment on him (9:1–3). Some in Corinth were questioning Paul's apostolic standing—ironically, in part because he refused to accept financial support from them the way other teachers did.

Do Paul and Barnabas not have the right to food and drink provided by the church? Do they not have the right to take along a believing wife, as the other apostles, the Lord's brothers, and Cephas do? Or are only Paul and Barnabas required to work for a living? What soldier ever serves at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat of its fruit? Who tends a flock and does not drink its milk (9:4–7)? Verse 5 casually reveals that Peter was married and traveled with his wife in ministry, and that Jesus' brothers had become active gospel preachers.

Not only common sense says so, the law says so too. The Law of Moses commands, "Do not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain." Is it about oxen that God is concerned? Surely He speaks entirely for our sake! The plowman plows in hope, and the thresher threshes in hope of sharing in the harvest. If the apostles have sown spiritual seed among them, is it too much if they reap a material harvest from them? If others enjoy this right over them, do the apostles not have an even greater claim? Yet Paul never used this right; instead, he endures everything so as not to place any obstacle in the way of the gospel of Christ (9:8–12; Deuteronomy 25:4).

Those who serve in the temple get their food from the temple, and those who attend the altar share in what is offered on the altar. In the same way, the Lord has commanded that those who preach the gospel should make their living from the gospel (9:13–14). Paul is echoing Jesus' own words: "the worker is worthy of his wages" (Luke 10:7). He has not only human precedent, but the Lord's own command backing his right.

Yet Paul has made use of none of these rights, and he is not writing now to secure them. He would rather die than let anyone deprive him of his boast. Preaching the gospel gives him nothing to boast about, because he is compelled to do it—woe to him if he does not preach the gospel! If he preaches voluntarily, he has a reward; if not voluntarily, he is still discharging a stewardship entrusted to him. What then is his reward? That in preaching the gospel he may offer it free of charge, without making full use of his rights in the gospel (9:15–18). Paul had been apprehended by Christ on the Damascus road rather than picking preaching as a career (Acts 9:1–19). The one thing he could freely choose was to preach without charging a penny.

Though he is free and belongs to no one, he has made himself a slave to everyone in order to win as many as possible. To the Jews he became like a Jew, to win the Jews. To those under the law he became like one under the law—though he himself is not under the law—to win those under the law. To those without the law he became like one without the law—though he is not free from God's law, but is under Christ's law—to win those without the law. To the weak he became weak, to win the weak. "I have become all things to all men, so that by all possible means I might save some." He does all this for the sake of the gospel, that he may share in its blessings (9:19–23). This is not spineless compromise: Paul adapts freely on non-essentials like diet and custom, while never bending an inch on the gospel itself.

Do they not know that in a race all the runners run, yet only one receives the prize? Run in such a way as to win it. Every athlete exercises strict self-control in all things; they do it to win a perishable crown, but we do it to win an imperishable one. Therefore Paul does not run aimlessly or box as though beating the air. He disciplines his body and brings it into strict subjection, lest after preaching to others, he himself should be disqualified (9:24–27). Right outside Corinth stood the biennial Isthmian Games, second only to the Olympics in the Greek world. Winners received a wreath of pine or celery leaves that withered within days.

## Key Takeaways

**Paul proves every legitimate right he possesses so that his voluntary surrender of those rights carries full weight.** He stacks up everyday common sense (soldiers, vineyard workers, shepherds), Moses' law about the threshing ox, temple custom, and Jesus' own command that gospel workers earn their living from the gospel (9:7–14). Giving up a right is only real sacrifice when the right genuinely belongs to you.

**True freedom is not clinging tightly to personal rights, but laying them down for the gospel.** Paul chooses to support himself by manual labor and endure hardship "rather than put an obstacle in the way of the gospel of Christ" (9:12). His deepest joy is not a paycheck, but the privilege of offering the gospel free of charge (9:15–18).

**Becoming all things to all people means stooping to meet listeners where they are to save them, never abandoning truth.** Though utterly free, Paul makes himself a servant to everyone—living like a Jew among Jews, like a Gentile among Gentiles, and weak among the weak (9:19–22). Every adjustment serves one consuming goal—winning souls—and remains anchored under "the law of Christ" (9:21–23).

**Following Christ calls for the disciplined self-mastery of an athlete running for a lasting crown.** Paul does not merely lecture others; he trains and masters his own body daily (9:24–27). If Greek athletes endure strict abstinence just for a fading wreath of leaves, believers racing for an "imperishable crown" dare not drift through life carelessly.

## Deeper Meaning

**The principle that ministers are supported by their work runs straight from the Law of Moses to the words of Jesus.** Paul connects "Do not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain" (9:9; Deuteronomy 25:4) with Jesus' instruction when sending out His disciples: "the worker is worthy of his wages" (9:14; Luke 10:7). Scripture regards supporting gospel workers as basic fairness, even though in Corinth's peculiar situation Paul deliberately waived that right.

**Paul's practice of living "like a Jew" to reach Jews shows up vividly in the Book of Acts.** His claim to become like a Jew to win Jews (9:20) was no abstract slogan. On his missionary journeys he circumcised Timothy "because of the Jews who lived in that area" so nothing unnecessary would block their entry into synagogues (Acts 16:3)—even while flatly refusing to circumcise Titus in Galatia when opponents turned circumcision into a requirement for salvation.

**"The law of Christ" in 9:21 ties directly to bearing one another's burdens in Galatians.** Paul clarifies that while he is not under the Mosaic law, he is no outlaw either, because he stands "under Christ's law" (9:21). In Galatians he explains how that law is fulfilled: "Carry one another's burdens" (Galatians 6:2)—the law of self-giving love.

**The metaphor of the race and the imperishable crown stayed with Paul to the very end of his life.** Here he urges the Corinthians to run for "an imperishable crown" (9:25). Years later, facing execution in Rome, he looks back over his ministry using that exact imagery, writing to Timothy that "the crown of righteousness" awaits him and all who long for the Lord's appearing (2 Timothy 4:8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 9 is not a random detour interrupting the discussion of idol food.** On a quick read, Paul can seem to swerve abruptly into apostolic salaries. In reality, chapter 9 is the bridge linking chapters 8 and 10: in chapter 8 he urges strong believers to waive their rights for weak brothers; in chapter 9 he presents his own life as the living model of waiving legitimate rights; and in chapter 10 he warns them not to grow overconfident in their freedom.

**Asking "Is it about oxen that God is concerned?" (9:9) does not mean God ignores animals.** Using a familiar Jewish rabbinic style of reasoning—arguing from the lesser to the greater—Paul means God is not concerned *merely* with oxen; through a humane rule protecting working animals, God also teaches fairness toward human beings, especially those laboring in the gospel.

**Becoming "all things to all men" in 9:22 is neither moral compromise nor two-faced flattery.** Paul explicitly states his boundary in 9:21: he always lives under Christ's law. He adapts freely in customs, meals, and cultural style so listeners can hear the gospel clearly, while never indulging sin or watering down the message of the cross.

**Paul's concern about being "disqualified" in 9:27 is understood in two primary ways.** The Greek term refers to failing a test and being rejected from the contest. Some interpreters understand it as losing one's ministry reward while still being saved, parallel to the builder losing his work in 3:15. Many others see it as a sober warning about the genuine peril of forfeiting salvation through reckless presumption—leading seamlessly into Israel's fatal fall in the wilderness in chapter 10.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, praises the rhetorical skill of Paul's personal example in chapter 9. Had Paul worked with his hands only because he lacked any right to support, his sacrifice would carry little weight; instead, he proves every single claim—right up to the Lord's own command—before revealing that he surrendered all of it for the gospel, leaving Corinthians who clung tightly to their right to eat meat with much to ponder. *(Homilies on 1 Corinthians)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom highlights Paul's strategy of establishing his rights before renouncing them; exact homily number needs verification -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, opened his landmark treatise *The Freedom of a Christian* with a famous two-part paradox drawn directly from 1 Corinthians 9:19: a Christian is a perfectly free lord of all, subject to none; and at the same time a perfectly dutiful servant of all, subject to all. Freedom flows from faith, while service flows from love. *(The Freedom of a Christian, 1520)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's famous opening paradox in The Freedom of a Christian rests directly on 1 Corinthians 9:19 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explains on 9:9 that God certainly cares for animals as Scripture teaches elsewhere, yet Moses' statute was not written for oxen to read—it was written for human beings to learn justice toward laborers. On 9:19–22, he carefully distinguishes loving flexibility from hypocritical compliance with sin. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 9 verses 9–10 and 19–22)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin clearly expounds the lesser-to-greater principle on 9:9 and distinguishes gospel flexibility from hypocrisy on 9:20–22 -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, highlights the local flavor of 9:24–27 for Corinthian readers: the biennial Isthmian Games took place right next door to their city, awarding winners a quickly fading wreath ("a perishable crown"). He reads Paul's warning against being "disqualified" in 9:27 as a serious alert to overconfident Corinthians who assumed they could never fall. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 9 verses 24–27)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee analyzes the Corinthian Isthmian Games setting and treats 9:27 as a serious warning connecting straight into 10:1–12 -->

**Philo of Alexandria**, Jewish philosopher contemporary with Paul, commented on the very same command—"Do not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain" (Deuteronomy 25:4)—arguing that the law instills kindness toward animals precisely to train human beings in fairness and generosity toward fellow humans.
