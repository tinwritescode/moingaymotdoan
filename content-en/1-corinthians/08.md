# 1 Corinthians 8 — Knowledge and Love

## Story

The next question concerns food sacrificed to idols. In Corinth, most meat sold in the marketplace came from animals offered in pagan temples, and guild banquets, family celebrations, and civic feasts were regularly held inside temple precincts. The Corinthians had written to Paul claiming, "We all have knowledge." Paul replies: knowledge puffs up, while love builds up. Anyone who thinks he knows something does not yet know as he ought to know; but whoever loves God is known by God (8:1–3). Paul turns their prized boast upside down: what counts is not how much we know about God, but being known by Him.

Turning to the question itself, Paul agrees with their premise: an idol is nothing at all in the world, and there is no God but one. Even though there are so-called gods in heaven or on earth—as indeed there are many "gods" and many "lords"—yet for us there is but one God, the Father, from whom all things came and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things came and through whom we exist (8:4–6). This is Israel's historic confession, "Hear, O Israel," proclaiming the one God (Deuteronomy 6:4)—yet Paul opens it from within, assigning "God" to the Father and "Lord" to Jesus, placing both squarely inside the confession of the one true God.

Not everyone, however, possesses this knowledge. Some people are still so accustomed to idols from their former lives that when they eat sacrificial meat, they regard it as really offered to a god, and their weak conscience is defiled. Food does not bring us closer to God: we are no worse if we do not eat, and no better if we do (8:7–8).

Be careful, however, that the exercise of your freedom does not become a stumbling block to the weak. If someone with a weak conscience sees a person who "has knowledge" dining inside an idol's temple, will he not be emboldened to eat meat sacrificed to idols? So by your knowledge this weak brother—the one for whom Christ died—is destroyed. When you sin against your brothers in this way and wound their weak conscience, you sin against Christ Himself (8:9–12).

Paul closes with his own personal resolve: if what he eats causes his brother to stumble, he will never eat meat again, so that he will not make his brother fall (8:13). He has the right to eat, and he gladly lays that right down. The next chapter will show that this is the exact pattern by which he lives his whole life.

## Key Takeaways

**Correct knowledge stripped of love harms others instead of building them up.** The Corinthians prided themselves on having "knowledge" about idols, yet Paul sets love firmly above knowledge: bare knowledge easily breeds arrogance, while love builds the church (8:1–3).

**Christian faith places Jesus squarely inside the confession of the one true God.** Surrounded by temples and countless pagan deities, Paul affirms one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things exist and through whom we live (8:4–6).

**The strong may never use their freedom to trample a weaker believer's conscience.** Even though an idol is nothing and food neither commends nor condemns us before God, a new convert emerging from paganism can be drawn into violating his conscience by watching a fellow Christian dine inside a pagan temple (8:7–10). Wounding the fragile conscience of a brother for whom Christ died is sinning against Christ Himself (8:11–12).

**Love gladly surrenders legitimate rights for a brother's good.** Paul leads by example with a resolute vow: if food causes his brother to fall, he would sooner give up meat forever (8:13). True maturity is measured not by how much freedom we dare to claim, but by how much we are willing to give up for others.

## Deeper Meaning

**Verse 8:6 expands Israel's ancient confession of faith to include Jesus Christ.** For generations Israel confessed, "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One" (Deuteronomy 6:4). In 8:6, Paul places both God the Father and Jesus Christ inside that very confession, while attributing creation itself to Jesus ("through whom all things came")—a truth he states just as clearly in Colossians (Colossians 1:16).

**The rule of guarding a weak brother's conscience reappears vividly in Romans.** Addressing food conflicts in Corinth, Paul invokes the cost of the cross (8:11). Later, writing to Roman believers disputing over food and holy days, he brings out the exact same plea: "Do not by your eating destroy your brother, for whom Christ died" (Romans 14:15).

**Wounding a vulnerable believer is a direct offense against Christ.** Verse 8:12 declares that injuring a brother's conscience means "you sin against Christ." This profound bond between Jesus and His humblest followers connects straight back to Jesus' words on the final judgment: whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of Mine, "you did for Me" (Matthew 25:40).

**Paul's pastoral approach stands alongside the Jerusalem Council's decree.** Before Paul wrote this letter, the apostles and elders in Jerusalem had instructed Gentile believers to "abstain from food sacrificed to idols" to preserve harmony with Jewish Christians (Acts 15:29). Rather than issuing a bare rule, Paul uncovers its deeper roots: sacrificial love for one's brother and undivided loyalty to the one Lord.

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 8 is only the first half of Paul's answer about idol food.** Reading chapter 8 in isolation might leave the impression that Paul allows strong Christians to feast inside pagan temples as long as nobody notices. In fact, his reply spans three chapters (chapters 8 through 10): here he confronts loveless arrogance toward weak brothers, while in 10:14–22 he flatly forbids participating in pagan temple feasts because demons stand behind them, even while permitting meat bought at the public market (10:25–27).

**"Becoming a stumbling block to the weak" does not mean catering to every fussy complaint.** In 8:9–10, causing someone to stumble is not merely irritating a picky critic. It means inducing a believer whose conscience is still fragile to imitate your example and commit an act they inwardly believe to be idolatry, wrecking their faith.

**Paul does not ask the strong to deny truth, but to put love ahead of rights.** He grants that their theology is accurate: idols are nothing, and food itself makes no one cleaner or dirtier before God (8:4; 8:8). The wrong lies in using correct doctrine to look down on and injure a brother who has not yet reached the same conviction.

**Verse 8:6 stands as one of the earliest New Testament witnesses to the deity of Jesus.** First Corinthians was written around AD 55, barely twenty-some years after the crucifixion. Yet Paul—a strict Jewish monotheist—already includes Jesus inside the confession of Israel's one God and honors Him as the agent of creation.

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, demonstrates that chapter 8 is not yet discussing meat purchased in the marketplace (which comes up in chapter 10), but rather "strong" Corinthians openly attending feasts right inside pagan temples (8:10). **Richard Bauckham**, British New Testament scholar, regards 1 Corinthians 8:6 as a watershed in early Christian understanding of Jesus.
