# 1 Corinthians 14 — Tongues and Prophecy

## Story

After his hymn to love, Paul returns to spiritual gifts. Pursue love, and eagerly desire spiritual gifts, especially the gift of prophecy. Anyone who speaks in a tongue does not speak to people, but to God, for no one understands him; he utters mysteries in the Spirit. By contrast, the one who prophesies speaks to people for their strengthening, encouragement, and comfort. The one who speaks in a tongue builds up himself, whereas the one who prophesies builds up the church. Paul wishes all of them spoke in tongues, yet he would even rather they prophesied; the one who prophesies is greater than the one who speaks in tongues, unless someone interprets so that the church may be built up (14:1–5). The single yardstick running through this whole chapter is one word: edification. The love of chapter 13 now becomes a simple practical question: does this actually help other people?

If Paul visits them speaking only in tongues without bringing revelation, knowledge, prophecy, or instruction, what good will he be to them? Even lifeless instruments like a flute or harp—if they produce no distinct notes, who can tell what tune is being played? If a trumpet blows an unclear blast, who will get ready for battle? So it is with them: uttering unintelligible words is speaking into thin air. Countless languages exist in the world, each carrying meaning; yet if Paul does not grasp the meaning of someone's language, he is a foreigner to the speaker, and the speaker is a foreigner to him. Since they are eager for spiritual gifts, let them strive to excel in gifts that build up the church (14:6–12).

Therefore the one who speaks in a tongue should pray that he may interpret. When Paul prays in a tongue, his spirit prays, yet his mind is unfruitful. So what shall he do? He will pray with his spirit, and he will also pray with his mind; he will sing with his spirit, and he will also sing with his mind. Otherwise, if someone praises God only in the spirit, how can an uninformed listener say "Amen" to his thanksgiving when he has no idea what was said? The thanksgiving may be fine, yet the other person is not built up. Paul thanks God that he speaks in tongues more than all of them; nevertheless, in church he would rather speak five intelligible words to instruct others than ten thousand words in a tongue (14:13–19). Paul is not hostile to tongues—he practices the gift more than anyone there. He simply refuses to let unintelligible speech crowd out clear teaching in public worship.

Stop thinking like children; in regard to evil be infants, but in your thinking be mature. In the Law it is written that through people of strange tongues and foreign lips the Lord will speak to this people, yet even then they will not listen to Him. Tongues, therefore, are a sign not for believers, but for unbelievers; prophecy, however, is not for unbelievers, but for believers. If the whole church comes together and everyone speaks in tongues, and uninformed visitors or unbelievers walk in, will they not say you are out of your minds? Yet if everyone prophesies and an unbeliever or uninformed person enters, he is convicted by all and called to account by all; the secrets of his heart are laid bare, so he will fall down and worship God, exclaiming, "God is really among you!" (14:20–25; Isaiah 28:11–12). In Isaiah, the foreign speech of Assyrian invaders served as a sign of God's judgment on a stubborn nation that refused to listen. Paul applies that text to show that uninterpreted tongues push outsiders away rather than drawing them near.

What then should happen when they assemble? Each one may bring a hymn, a word of instruction, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation; everything must be done to build up the church. If anyone speaks in a tongue, two—or at most three—should speak, one at a time, and someone must interpret; if there is no interpreter, the speaker should stay silent in the church and speak to himself and to God. Two or three prophets should speak, and the others should weigh carefully what is said. If a revelation comes to someone sitting down, the first speaker should stop. All can prophesy in turn so that everyone may be instructed and encouraged. The spirits of prophets are subject to the control of prophets, for God is not a God of disorder, but of peace (14:26–33). Corinthian worship did not revolve around a single solo preacher; many members contributed. Paul preserves that participation while setting it in clear order. True prophets are never overtaken by an uncontrollable frenzy, unlike ecstatic oracles in pagan shrines.

As in all the churches of the saints, women should remain silent in the churches, for they are not permitted to speak, but must be in submission, as the Law also says. If they want to inquire about something, they should ask their own husbands at home, for it is disgraceful for a woman to speak in church (14:33–35). This paragraph is difficult to fit alongside chapter 11, where women clearly pray and prophesy in public worship. Many interpreters conclude Paul is restricting one specific kind of speech—such as disruptive questioning or publicly weighing prophecies. Several ancient manuscripts place verses 34–35 after verse 40 rather than here, leading some scholars to suggest they originated as a later marginal note.

Did the word of God originate with them, or are they the only ones it has reached? If anyone considers himself a prophet or spiritual person, let him recognize that what Paul writes is the Lord's command; whoever ignores this will himself be ignored. Therefore, eagerly desire to prophesy, and do not forbid speaking in tongues. Yet everything must be done in a proper and orderly manner (14:36–40). In verse 38, some ancient manuscripts read "let him be ignored," while others carry a form meaning "he is not recognized."

## Key Takeaways

**Spiritual gifts are tools for building, not medals to show off.** The Corinthians treated tongues as proof of superior spirituality. Paul changes the question: not "which gift looks highest?" but "which gift helps my brother?" That is why he urges them to seek gifts that excel "for the building up of the church" (14:12). Every gift exists to serve, like a hammer handed to a carpenter to build a house rather than hang on a wall.

**God never asks worshipers to switch off their minds.** Paul exercises the gift of tongues and knows firsthand that when he prays in a tongue, "my spirit prays, but my mind is unfruitful" (14:14). He refuses to choose one side over the other: "I will pray with my spirit, but I will also pray with my mind" (14:15). Healthy worship engages both a fervent heart and an alert intellect.

**Gathered worship must think about the outsider walking through the door.** Paul pictures an unbeliever stepping into the room. If everyone bursts into uninterpreted tongues, the visitor concludes they are "out of your minds" (14:23). If they hear God's word in plain language, their heart is pierced and they bow down confessing, "God is really among you!" (14:25). Anything that leaves guests feeling shut out falls short of true worship.

**Order flows from God's own character.** Paul introduces guidelines for worship not out of rigid legalism, but because "God is not a God of disorder, but of peace" (14:33). At the same time, he refuses to use order to quench the Spirit: "do not forbid speaking in tongues" (14:39). Freedom for many to contribute and order so every contribution builds up belong together (14:40).

## Deeper Meaning

**From Babel to Pentecost.** At the Tower of Babel, God confused human language so people could not understand one another and scattered across the earth (Genesis 11:7). At Pentecost, every visitor "heard them speaking in his own language," drawing scattered nations together (Acts 2:6). In Jerusalem, tongues fostered understanding; in Corinth, uninterpreted tongues drove people apart, which is why Paul insists on interpretation.

**Moses' ancient wish coming alive.** When two men prophesied in Israel's camp, Joshua tried to stop them, and Moses answered, "I wish that all the LORD's people were prophets" (Numbers 11:29). Joel promised that day would come, when "your sons and daughters will prophesy" (Joel 2:28). Paul's assurance that "you can all prophesy in turn" (14:31) shows that ancient promise unfolding inside a local congregation.

**Nations recognizing God among His people.** The unbeliever's confession in 14:25 echoes Isaiah's vision of foreign nations bowing before God's people and declaring, "Surely God is with you" (Isaiah 45:14). The prophets foresaw a day when peoples from everywhere would come because they heard that God dwells with His people (Zechariah 8:23). Paul says that very reality can break through in an ordinary house church gathering.

**Prophecy must always be weighed.** Paul directs that while prophets speak, "the others should weigh carefully what is said" (14:29). He gave the Thessalonians the exact same counsel: "Do not treat prophecies with contempt, but test all things. Hold fast to what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:20–21). John likewise urges testing the spirits because many false prophets have gone out (1 John 4:1). Claiming "God told me" never exempts anyone from discernment.

**Prayers beyond words.** Paul recognizes that one who speaks in a tongue "does not speak to men, but to God," even when no human listener understands (14:2). In Romans, he writes of times when we do not know what to pray for, and the Spirit Himself intercedes for us "with groans too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). Some prayers stretch beyond human vocabulary. Paul never scorns them; he simply notes their proper place is privately between the worshiper and God (14:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verses 14:34–35 on women keeping silent have three main interpretations.** This passage sits in clear tension with chapter 11, where women pray and prophesy openly in worship (11:5). First, some scholars view these two verses as a later marginal note accidentally copied into the text, pointing out that certain manuscripts relocate them after verse 40. Second, many argue Paul is restricting a specific disruption in Corinth—such as noisy interruptions or publicly judging prophecies, directly tied to "weighing" in verse 29. Third, others read it as an enduring principle regarding authoritative teaching in the assembly, alongside "learn in quietness" in 1 Timothy (1 Timothy 2:12). As for "as the Law also says," the Old Testament contains no explicit ban on women speaking in worship; interpreters point either to Genesis 3:16 or to first-century synagogue custom.

**Are tongues human languages or something else?** At Pentecost, listeners immediately recognized their native languages (Acts 2:6). In Corinth, however, "no one understands him" unless someone interprets (14:2). Some believe Corinthian tongues were still foreign human languages unknown to anyone present. Others think they were non-human spiritual utterances, noting Paul's phrase "the tongues of men and of angels" (13:1). Paul never defines the phenomenon because the Corinthians already knew what it sounded like.

**Verse 14:22 can look backwards next to verses 14:23–25.** Paul says tongues are "a sign, not for believers, but for unbelievers," yet immediately adds that unbelievers hearing tongues will think the church is crazy. The standard explanation is that "sign" carries its Old Testament sense from Isaiah 28: tongues act as a negative sign of judgment. Unbelievers who hear incomprehensible speech stay shut out, just as ancient Israel heard foreign invaders and still refused to listen to God (14:21). Prophecy, by contrast, signals God's living presence and draws even outsiders to worship.

**"Prophesying" here does not primarily mean predicting future events.** Paul defines its core function simply: the prophet speaks to people "for their strengthening, encouragement, and comfort" (14:3). Such speech can lay bare "the secrets of his heart" (14:25) and must always be weighed by the congregation (14:29). For that reason, most commentators distinguish congregational prophecy from inspired Scripture: local prophecy may contain mixed elements and must always be tested against Scripture.

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, early Christian writer in Carthage around AD 200, challenged Marcion to produce in his rival group the Spirit's gifts Paul describes in 1 Corinthians 12–14: prophets, psalms, visions, prayers, and interpreted tongues. For Tertullian, the presence of these gifts proved that Paul's God was the same God who spoke through the Old Testament prophets—though he portrayed prophecy as an ecstatic trance, a view many later readers reject in light of 14:32. *(Against Marcion, Book 5, chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Tertullian challenging Marcion with the gifts of 1 Corinthians 12–14 in Book 5 chapter 8; the contrast with 14:32 is editorial commentary -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, wrote that in the early days the Spirit fell upon believers and they spoke in tongues as a sign fitting that opening era—announcing that the gospel would spread across every language on earth. Once that sign accomplished its purpose, it passed away. Asking whether anyone receiving the laying on of hands today still expects to speak in tongues, he answers that the enduring sign of the Spirit is love for one's brothers. *(Homilies on the First Epistle of John, Homily 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this passage in Homily 6 on 1 John is widely quoted in discussions of tongues; Augustine discusses tongues generally there rather than commenting directly on 1 Corinthians 14 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, appealed to 14:16 against conducting liturgy and prayers in Latin before congregations who could not understand it. If listeners cannot understand what is prayed, they cannot truly say "Amen," and such prayer builds up nobody. For Calvin, this verse settles the principle that public worship must be held in the language of the people. *(Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on chapter 14 verse 16)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin uses 14:16 against Latin worship, a standard Reformational argument; exact phrasing uncertain -->

**The Babylonian Talmud** preserves an ancient ruling stating that anyone—including a minor or a woman—may technically be counted among the seven Sabbath Torah readers, yet the sages ruled that a woman should not read before the congregation "out of respect for the dignity of the congregation." This ruling reveals that in ancient synagogue life, women speaking publicly before the whole assembly was viewed as socially improper even when not strictly forbidden—providing illuminating background for "disgraceful" in 14:35. *(Babylonian Talmud, Megillah, folio 23a)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the ruling about the seven readers and "honor of the congregation" in Megillah 23a is well known; the Talmud is later than Paul, and the connection to 14:35 is the editor's -->

**Gordon Fee**, American Pentecostal New Testament scholar, argues that 14:34–35 were not originally written by Paul, but were a marginal gloss later copied into the letter. He rests his case on three points: one textual family places the verses after verse 40, they interrupt the flow concerning tongues and prophecy, and they conflict sharply with 11:5. It is his most famous conclusion on this chapter, though many scholars disagree. *(The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NICNT, 1987, on chapter 14 verses 34–35)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Fee's interpolation thesis on 14:34–35 is widely known and frequently debated -->

**D. A. Carson**, Canadian New Testament scholar teaching in the United States, retains verses 34–35 as authentically Pauline and links them directly to verse 29. On his reading, women remain free to pray and prophesy as in chapter 11, yet are barred from publicly weighing prophecies before the assembly, because judging prophecy constitutes an exercise of authoritative teaching. He also notes that the Corinthians treated tongues as the hallmark of elite spirituality, and chapters 12–14 dismantle that mindset. *(Showing the Spirit: A Theological Exposition of 1 Corinthians 12–14, 1987)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking 14:34–35 specifically to the weighing of prophecies in verse 29 is Carson's signature proposal in this book -->
