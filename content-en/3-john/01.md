# 3 John 1 — Hospitality for the Truth and Three Portraits in the Church

## Story

The elder writes this brief personal note to Gaius, a dearly loved friend whom he genuinely loves in the truth. He prays that Gaius will prosper in every way and enjoy good physical health, just as his soul is already prospering. He shares how overjoyed he was when some brothers arrived and testified to Gaius's devotion to the truth and the way he walks in it; for this aging spiritual father, "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth" (1:1–4).

He commends Gaius's faithfulness in everything he does for traveling brothers—even strangers arriving from elsewhere. Those very brothers stood before the church and testified about Gaius's love. The elder encourages him to keep sending them on their way with provisions in a manner worthy of God. After all, they went out on behalf of Christ's Name and accept nothing from Gentiles; therefore believers ought to welcome and support them, becoming "fellow workers for the truth" right alongside them (1:5–8).

Yet the letter is not all good news. The elder had written a brief note to the church, but Diotrephes—someone "who loves to be first"—refuses to recognize the elder's authority. The elder warns that when he comes, he will expose everything Diotrephes has been doing: spreading malicious slander against the elder and his coworkers, refusing to welcome traveling brothers himself, blocking anyone else who wants to welcome them, and even throwing those people out of the church (1:9–10).

Setting those two sharply opposing examples side by side, the elder urges Gaius never to imitate what is evil, but what is good: whoever does good belongs to God, while whoever does evil has never seen God. Next he commends Demetrius—probably the carrier of this very letter—as a shining model: everyone speaks well of him, the truth itself vouches for him, and the elder's own circle testifies for him too, a testimony Gaius knows is trustworthy (1:11–12).

Just like the close of 2 John, the elder says he has much more to share, yet prefers not to write with pen and ink; he hopes to visit soon and talk face to face. The letter ends with a blessing of peace and an unmistakably warm closing: "The friends here send you greetings. Greet each of our friends there by name" (1:13–14).

## Key Takeaways

**Welcoming and supporting gospel workers means sharing directly in the work of the truth.** Not everyone can leave home to travel from city to city preaching the gospel, yet the believer who opens a door, shares a meal, and helps cover travel expenses for those going out for Christ's Name counts equally as one of the "fellow workers for the truth" (1:5–8).

**Hunger for power can damage a church just as badly as false doctrine.** Unlike 1 John and 2 John, which confront doctrinal error, Diotrephes's problem in 3 John is not theology—it is ego: he "loves to be first." Determined to control everything, he readily slanders an aging leader, turns away faithful brothers, and expels anyone who disagrees with him (1:9–10).

**Three people in one short letter sketch three timeless choices inside the Church.** Gaius embodies quiet, faithful hospitality; Diotrephes embodies controlling ambition and division; Demetrius embodies transparent integrity vouched for both by the community and by the truth itself (1:5–12).

## Deeper Meaning

**The ideal balance between 2 John and 3 John.** Read side by side, these two brief letters form a striking pair: 2 John warns believers not to host false teachers spreading heresy (2 John 1:10), while 3 John urges believers to wholeheartedly welcome genuine coworkers going out for the Name (1:5–8). Discernment without love quickly shrinks into the narrow turf-guarding of Diotrephes; hospitality without discernment leaves the door wide open to deception.

**Calling one another "friends" echoes the Upper Room in John's Gospel.** In the closing verse, the elder addresses believers not merely as brothers or children, but as "friends" (1:14)—echoing Jesus' words to His disciples on the night of the Last Supper, when He said He no longer called them servants, but His friends (John 15:14–15). And his request to "greet each of our friends there by name" recalls the Good Shepherd who calls His own sheep one by one by name (John 10:3).

**Welcoming Christ's messengers is welcoming Christ Himself.** When the elder praises Gaius for supporting those who go out on behalf of the Name without taking money from outsiders (1:7–8), he is carrying forward what Jesus taught when sending out His disciples: whoever welcomes you welcomes Me (Matthew 10:40; John 13:20).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 1:2 is not an automatic promise of material wealth.** The wish—"I pray that in every way you may prosper and enjoy good health, as your soul also prospers"—is frequently ripped out of context as a slogan for prosperity teaching. In reality, wishing someone good health and smooth going in their affairs was a completely standard opening greeting in ancient Greco-Roman letters. What makes this verse striking is that the elder makes Gaius's spiritual health the benchmark for his physical health—plenty of us would feel uneasy if our bodies were only as healthy as our souls!

**3 John is the shortest book in the entire Bible by Greek word count.** Fitting comfortably on a single small sheet of papyrus, it nonetheless opens a remarkably candid window onto late first-century church life: a network of traveling missionaries, openhanded hosts like Gaius, and fierce local power struggles like the one sparked by Diotrephes.

**The lost letter mentioned in 1:9.** When the elder writes, "I have written to the church about this," he refers to an earlier letter sent to Diotrephes's church that was never preserved in Scripture (though some have suggested it might be 2 John). This reminds us that the apostles wrote many everyday pastoral notes, while God preserved the specific letters needed for His Church in every generation.
