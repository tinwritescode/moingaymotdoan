# Isaiah 5 — The Song of the Vineyard and Six Woes

## Story

Chapter 5 opens with what sounds like a gentle tune, as if a minstrel were striking up a song at a harvest festival. Isaiah announces he will sing "for my beloved a song of his vineyard." His beloved owned a vineyard on a fertile hillside. He spared no labor: he dug the soil, cleared out every stone, planted the choicest vines, built a watchtower in the middle, and hewed out a winepress. After such tender care, he naturally looked for sweet grapes; "yet it yielded only bad fruit" (5:1–2).

Speaking in the third person, the vineyard owner suddenly breaks in himself, addressing the people of Jerusalem and Judah as jurors: "What more could have been done for My vineyard than I have already done for it? When I looked for good grapes, why did it yield only bad?" Without waiting for their reply, he announces the sentence on the ungrateful vineyard: he will rip away its hedge so animals devour it, break down its wall to be trampled, leave it unpruned and unhoed until briers and thorns take over. Then the final line jolts every listener awake: "I will also command the clouds that the rain not fall on it." No human farmer can issue orders to the clouds! Instantly the curtain drops: "For the vineyard of the LORD of Hosts is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah are the plant of His delight." God looked for justice, yet saw bloodshed; He looked for righteousness, yet heard cries of distress (5:3–7).

Where the vineyard song speaks broadly of "bad fruit," the next section exposes each bitter cluster through six rapid-fire cries of "Woe!" The first strikes at greed for land: "Woe to you who add house to house and join field to field," buying out neighbors until no room is left for anyone else, as if trying to live alone in the land. Yet the LORD whispers into Isaiah's ear that those grand mansions will stand empty and desolate, while the vast estates suffer catastrophic crop failure: ten acres of vineyard will yield barely a single bath of wine, and a whole homer of seed will produce just one-tenth of a harvest (5:8–10).

The second woe targets drunken self-indulgence: those who rise early to chase strong drink and party late into the night amid lyre, harp, tambourine, and flute, yet "do not regard the deeds of the LORD." As a result, the nation goes into exile for lack of understanding; nobles starve, commoners parch with thirst, and Sheol "enlarges its throat and opens wide its jaws" to swallow the city's splendor, crowds, and noisy revelry. Every proud eye is brought low, leaving only the LORD of Hosts exalted in justice, while the former estates of the rich turn into grazing pasture for lambs and wanderers (5:11–17).

Four more woes follow in quick succession. The third rebukes those who drag sin along with cords of deceit as an ox pulls a cart, then brazenly dare God to hurry up and act so they can see it. The fourth indicts the total inversion of moral truth: "Woe to those who call evil good and good evil, who turn darkness to light and light to darkness, who replace bitter with sweet and sweet with bitter." The fifth condemns those "who are wise in their own eyes." The sixth exposes men who are heroes only at drinking wine and mixing strong drink, who take bribes to "acquit the guilty... and deprive the innocent of justice." Their end is like stubble meeting flame: their roots rot and their blossoms blow away like dust, because they rejected the law and despised the word of the Holy One of Israel (5:18–24).

At the close of the chapter, the anger of the LORD blazes out. He has already stretched out His hand to strike His people, making the mountains quake and leaving corpses like refuse in the streets, yet the sentence is not finished: "Yet for all this, His anger is not turned away; His hand is still upraised." He lifts a banner and whistles for a distant nation from the ends of the earth to march in. That invading army moves like an unstoppable machine: none stumble, none grow drowsy, no belt comes loose, no sandal strap snaps, arrows are sharpened, bows are strung, horses' hooves are like flint, and chariot wheels roar like a whirlwind. They roar like lions snatching prey no one can rescue; their rumble sounds like the pounding sea, while over the land hangs only dark, suffocating distress (5:25–30).

## Key Takeaways

**The greater the grace received, the greater the responsibility to bear fruit.** God demanded nothing unreasonable from His vineyard; He cleared the stones, planted choice vines, built a watchtower, and carved a winepress (5:1–2). Precisely because Judah had been tended so lovingly, yielding only bloodshed and oppression left them without excuse (5:4; 5:7).

**Greed and pleasure-seeking blind people to what God is doing.** Swallowing up the land of the weak to dwell alone in the countryside (5:8) and drinking from morning till night (5:11–12) look like separate vices, yet they grow from the same root: people become absorbed in their own gain and appetite and "do not regard the deeds of the LORD."

**More dangerous than committing sin is renaming sin as virtue.** When a culture starts to "call evil good and good evil" (5:20), deems itself wise in its own eyes (5:21), and sells court verdicts for bribes (5:23), its moral compass has shattered. At that stage, the roots are already rotten inside even if the outward structure still stands (5:24).

**Even the fiercest empires are merely instruments in God's hand.** The foreign army marching against Judah looks utterly invincible (5:26–29), yet it is the LORD who raises the signal flag and whistles for them to come. History never slips from His grip, even in its darkest hours.

## Deeper Meaning

**A gentle trap echoing Nathan's parable to King David.** Isaiah sings about his "beloved's" vineyard so the citizens of Jerusalem nod along in agreement that the owner is justified in tearing it down, before springing the twist: you yourselves are that vineyard (5:1–7). This way of awakening conscience mirrors the prophet Nathan telling David a story of a rich man stealing a poor man's lamb so David pronounced his own sentence before hearing, "You are the man!" (2 Samuel 12:5–7).

**Jesus adopts Isaiah's very own vineyard song.** In the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus tells the parable of a landowner who planted a vineyard, put a wall around it, dug a winepress, and built a watchtower—repeating every single detail of Isaiah 5:1–2—to expose religious leaders who withheld the fruit from God and even killed the owner's Son (Matthew 21:33–34; Matthew 21:40–41). In John's Gospel, Jesus goes even further: where Israel failed by yielding sour fruit, Jesus Himself is "the true vine," so that whoever abides in Him bears the righteous fruit the Father seeks (John 15:1–2).

**The broken-down vineyard in the Psalms and the new vineyard song later in Isaiah.** The picture of Israel as a vine God transplanted from Egypt only to have its walls broken down for passersby to pluck is also the agonizing lament of Psalm 80 (Psalms 80:8–12). Yet Isaiah's final word about the vineyard is not abandonment: in chapter 27, God promises a coming day when His people will sing a different song of "a fruitful vineyard," which the LORD Himself waters and watches over day and night (27:2–3).

**Covenant land laws opposing land grabs.** The woe against those who "add house to house and join field to field" (5:8) goes straight back to the Law of Moses: "The land must not be sold permanently, because the land is Mine" (Leviticus 25:23). Every Israelite family received an ancestral allotment for its livelihood; when wealthy landowners exploited debt to absorb small farms—a sin Isaiah's contemporary Micah likewise denounced fiercely (Micah 2:2)—they destroyed the equitable foundation God had established.

**Warnings against conceit and mocking God's patience.** The rebuke of those wise in their own eyes in 5:21 echoes Proverbs (Proverbs 3:7) and Paul's counsel to the Roman church (Romans 12:16). Meanwhile, the sneering challenge in 5:19—"Let Him hurry and hasten His work so that we may see it!"—foreshadows the scoffers of the New Testament who mock, asking where the promise of His coming is (2 Peter 3:3–4).

## Notes & Nuances

**The most famous wordplay in the book of Isaiah appears in 5:7.** In the original Hebrew, two opposing pairs sound almost identical while meaning the exact opposite: God looked for *mishpat* (justice), and saw *mispach* (bloodshed); He looked for *tsedaqah* (righteousness), and heard *tse'aqah* (a cry of distress). Translation cannot reproduce the sound play, yet knowing it sharpens the prophet's heartbreak: the words differ by barely a syllable, reflecting how razor-thin the line can be between a just society and a brutal one.

**"Bad fruit" in 5:2 does not mean harmless wild grapes.** The Hebrew term literally means *rotten* or *stinking sour berries*—shriveled, bitter fruit unfit to eat and useless for wine. It conveys the utter disappointment of a grower who planted the finest stock only to end up with an unusable crop.

**The measurements in 5:10 reveal total agricultural collapse.** A *bath* held only about twenty-two liters of wine; for ten acres of vineyard—the area ten yokes of oxen could plow in a day—to yield that little meant near-total ruin. Likewise, a *homer* was ten times larger than an *ephah*, meaning farmers who sowed ten measures of seed harvested only one measure of grain—a land exhausted by drought and war.

**The invading army in 5:26–30 is unnamed, yet clearly points to Assyria.** In eighth-century BC history, the Assyrian military was feared across the ancient Near East for its iron discipline, relentless marching speed, and terrifying chariots. The refrain "Yet for all this, His anger is not turned away; His hand is still upraised" in 5:25 will reappear repeatedly in chapters 9 and 10 (9:12), tying chapter 5 directly into the larger arc of judgment ahead.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the fourth-century bishop of Antioch, admires Isaiah's pastoral skill in the vineyard song: instead of attacking his audience head-on from the first line and causing them to shut their hearts, the prophet tells of a generous grower betrayed by his vines so Jerusalem renders a fair verdict against itself. According to Chrysostom, details like clearing stones, building a watchtower, and hewing a winepress recall every blessing God lavished on His people—law, temple, and prophets—leaving them without any excuse. *(Commentary on Isaiah, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Chrysostom's explanation of Isaiah's rhetorical strategy and the symbolic details of vineyard care in chapter 5; not verified word-for-word -->

**Rashi**, the famed eleventh-century Jewish scholar in France, follows ancient Jewish exegetical tradition by linking the vineyard's details to Israel's history: clearing the stones means driving out the Canaanite nations, the watchtower in the midst is the Jerusalem Temple, and the winepress is the altar of sacrifice. On the woe in verse 8 against absorbing houses and fields, he highlights the direct violation of God's original intention in dividing the land fairly among every tribe and household. *(Commentary on Isaiah, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: identifying the tower as the Temple and the winepress as the altar is very well known in Targum and Rashi on Isaiah 5:2; other verses not rechecked against the Hebrew text -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, offers a practical reading of the first woe in verse 8: God does not forbid buying and selling land legitimately, but condemns the bottomless greed that pushes neighbors out so the rich can live "alone in the midst of the land," tearing apart the human community God designed for shared life. Reaching verse 20, Calvin considers calling evil good and turning darkness into light the most hopeless stage of decay, since once conscience warps the standard of right and wrong, even sound rebuke can scarcely break through. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's exposition of greed fracturing community in verse 8 and corrupted conscience in verse 20; not checked against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses over the grower's aching question in verse 4: "What more could have been done for My vineyard than I have already done for it?" He draws the lesson that whenever God's people grow corrupt, the fault never lies in a lack of divine care or grace, but in human hard-heartedness. Henry also points to verse 18: those who haul sin behind them with ropes of falsehood are not reluctant victims swept away by temptation, but willful laborers straining to drag a heavy load of guilt into their own lives. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's comments on human responsibility in verse 4 and dragging sin in verse 18; not verified against the original text -->

**Alec Motyer**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, views chapter 5 as the climax explaining the cause of judgment across Isaiah's entire opening section. In his outline, the vineyard song states the overarching charge, and the six woes in verses 8–24 specify each variety of "bad fruit," from economic greed and decadent partying to judicial corruption. Motyer also stresses that verses 25–30 form a dramatic transition: God had already disciplined Israel directly while they remained stubborn, so now He whistles for an imperial army from afar to carry out the verdict. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: connecting the vineyard song with the six woes and viewing verses 25–30 as a hinge is Motyer's standard structure; exact page numbers not verified -->
