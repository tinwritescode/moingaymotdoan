# Isaiah 15 — A Lament of Tears Over Moab

## Story

Following the oracles directed at Babylon, Assyria, and Philistia in chapter 14, the prophet Isaiah turns his gaze east across the Dead Sea, opening a fresh prophecy titled "The burden against Moab." Catastrophe strikes with the sudden, shattering blow of a nightmare no one saw coming: in a single night, the nation's two greatest strongholds—Ar of Moab and Kir of Moab—are both "laid waste and destroyed" (15:1).

Instantly the whole country plunges into mourning. People climb to the temple shrines and head up to Dibon's high places to weep, wailing over Nebo and Medeba, which have just fallen. Following harsh ancient mourning customs, across the land "every head is shaved and every beard is cut off"; everyone wraps themselves in sackcloth in the streets, climbs onto rooftops, and crowds into public squares, weeping and melting away in tears (15:2–3).

From northern cities like Heshbon and Elealeh, cries carry all the way to Jahaz. Panic grips not only ordinary citizens; even Moab's finest armed warriors lose heart, crying out aloud as their souls tremble within them (15:4).

Suddenly, right in the middle of that devastation, a line halts the reader in their tracks: "My heart cries out over Moab." Rather than gloating over the ruin of a neighbor that so often clashed with Judah, the oracle's speaker aches while watching streams of refugees scatter southward toward Zoar and Eglath-shelishiyah. Weeping crowds trudge up the Ascent of Luhith, raising heartbreaking cries of destruction along the road to Horonaim as their homeland collapses behind them (15:5).

Suffering does not stop at invasion. That once-fertile plateau east of the Jordan suddenly dries out: the waters of Nimrim turn desolate, grass withers, "the tender grass dies out, and nothing green remains." With nothing left to hold onto, the wretched survivors gather whatever scraps of wealth and hard-earned savings remain, hoisting them onto their shoulders to cross "the Brook of the Willows" into foreign territory (15:6–7).

Mourning echoes around Moab's entire border, carrying from Eglaim all the way to Beer-elim (15:8). The final verse closes chapter 15 on its darkest, most chilling note: the waters of Dimon run red with blood. Even then, disaster is not finished—the LORD declares He will bring still more upon Dimon, sending a lion after both Moab's fleeing survivors and whoever remains behind in the land (15:9).

## Key Takeaways

**Prosperity and strong fortresses can collapse overnight.** Moab was famous for its highland citadels, lush vineyards, and sprawling flocks of sheep. Yet when judgment fell, its two chief cities, Ar and Kir, were ruined in a single night, and Nimrim's cool streams ran dry until not a blade of green remained (15:1; 15:6). No border or natural abundance guarantees lasting security when people trust only in themselves.

**The deepest despair comes when people run to idols that never answer.** When crisis hits, Moabites rush up to shrines and high places, shave their heads, trim their beards, and put on sackcloth to plead with their god Chemosh (15:2–3). They weep from the streets to the rooftops, yet no rescue arrives—just as chapter 16 states bluntly that though Moab wears itself out praying at its high place, it accomplishes nothing (16:12).

**God's judgment never comes with cruel gloating.** Amid a severe verdict on a foreign nation, the cry "My heart cries out over Moab" (15:5) reveals a heart deeply moved by human suffering. Whether heard as Isaiah's own grief or God's sorrow speaking through the prophet's lips, this oracle contains not a drop of mockery over a fallen foe, only tears for ordinary families fleeing empty-handed from war.

**Fleeing without turning to God still leaves no escape from disaster.** Moabites scoop up their belongings and hurry south across the Brook of the Willows hoping to outrun ruin, yet the closing verse warns that a lion still overtakes the fugitives (15:7; 15:9). Real shelter for Moab's refugees lies not in the bundles on their backs, but in the invitation to take refuge beneath Zion and David's throne opening right at the start of chapter 16 (16:1–5).

## Deeper Meaning

**Centuries of tangled kinship and rivalry between Israel and Moab.** Moab was not a complete stranger: according to Genesis, Moab was the son of Lot, Abraham's nephew (Genesis 19:36–37). Because of that kinship, when Israel left Egypt the LORD forbade His people to attack Moab, since He had granted that land to Lot's descendants (Deuteronomy 2:9). Over the centuries, however, Moab repeatedly grew arrogant, mocked Israel, and fought against them—prompting prophets like Amos and Zephaniah to pronounce judgment on the nation (Amos 2:1–3; Zephaniah 2:8–9). Remarkably, right across that tense history stands Ruth, a Moabite woman who became King David's great-grandmother, weaving a thread of mercy that connects chapter 15 to the promise of David's throne in chapter 16 (16:5).

**An ancient oracle taken up again by Jeremiah over a century later.** Turning to Jeremiah 48, readers are struck to find an entire string of places and images matching Isaiah 15 almost word for word: refugees weeping up the Ascent of Luhith, cries of ruin along the Horonaim road, wails from Heshbon to Elealeh and Jahaz, Nimrim's dried waters, shaved heads, clipped beards, sackcloth waists, and a heart aching over Moab (Jeremiah 48:5; Jeremiah 48:34–37). As Isaiah 16:13–14 itself suggests, this elegy over Moab was an enduring verdict that God revisited and brought to pass across successive waves of history—from Assyria in the eighth century down to Babylon in the sixth century BC.

**Tears over the judged foreshadow the tears of Jesus.** The cry "My heart cries out over Moab" (15:5), joined with the harp-like sobbing of chapter 16 (16:9; 16:11), shatters the caricature of a cold Old Testament God who delights in punishment. He is too righteous to overlook pride and cruelty, yet He grieves profoundly whenever His creatures reap destruction. That deep divine compassion later appeared in flesh and blood when Jesus looked out over Jerusalem facing imminent judgment and wept over the city (Luke 19:41).

**Halfway through the story: chapter 15 leads directly into chapter 16's open door.** Chapter 15 ends amid blood and the terror of lions (15:9), yet the oracle is only half finished. Right at the opening of chapter 16, Moab's fleeing refugees are urged to send tribute to Mount Zion and seek shelter under David's house, where a faithful, righteous King will sit on the throne (16:1–5). Tragically, Moab's deep-seated pride leads them to turn away from that sole place of refuge (16:6).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who speaks the words "My heart cries out over Moab" (15:5)?** Throughout chapter 15, a first-person speaker ("I" / "my") surfaces only twice: in 15:5 ("My heart cries out over Moab") and in 15:9 ("I will bring still more upon Dimon"). In 15:9, the One sending further calamity and lions is unmistakably the LORD. Regarding 15:5—as well as 16:9 and 16:11 later—commentators split into two main views: some hear the compassionate sob of Isaiah himself appalled by the devastating vision; many others observe that the text signals no change of speaker between 15:5 and 15:9, reading it as God's own aching sorrow voiced through the prophet. Both views fit together naturally: a true prophet always shares the very heartbeat of the One who sends him.

**Why is chapter 15 packed so densely with unfamiliar towns and streams?** In nine short verses, Isaiah drops more than fifteen Moabite place-names: Ar, Kir, Dibon, Nebo, Medeba, Heshbon, Elealeh, Jahaz, Zoar, Eglath-shelishiyah, the Ascent of Luhith, Horonaim, Nimrim, the Brook of the Willows, Eglaim, Beer-elim, and Dimon. Plotted on an ancient map, they are no random list—they trace a clear path from north to south: invaders smash through northern Heshbon and Medeba, sweep through Moab's heartland, and drive fleeing crowds all the way south to Zoar and the Brook of the Willows at the Dead Sea's southern tip, bordering Edom and Judah. The rapid rattle of town names creates the breathless rhythm of emergency dispatches as fortress after fortress falls.

**The wordplay between "Dibon" and "Dimon" in 15:9.** Readers often wonder why 15:2 mentions Dibon whereas 15:9 names Dimon. Hebrew scholars point out a haunting pun: the town's actual name was *Dibon*, yet in 15:9 the prophet deliberately alters one consonant to *Dimon* so it echoes the Hebrew word *dam* ("blood")—as if saying, "the waters of Bloodtown run full of blood." Furthermore, waters turned to blood and dried-up springs (15:6; 15:9) recall brutal ancient siege tactics once actually used against Moab in 2 Kings 3:25, when invaders plugged up springs and ruined fertile farmland.

**Sending "a lion upon the fugitives of Moab" (15:9) feels starkly harsh.** At first glance, the closing verse sounds merciless: the people have already lost home and country—why pile on further disaster and send lions after survivors? It helps to read this within Old Testament prophetic idiom: wild predators roaming a depopulated land are a standard symbol of covenant desolation, while "lion" is also a classic prophetic metaphor for another ruthless invading army sweeping away what was left. Above all, this grim sentence is not God's final word to Moab's people—16:1–5 immediately opens a lifeline for anyone humble enough to seek shelter at Zion, and 16:6 identifies why disaster persisted: stubborn pride refusing to bow.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Latin translator, pays close attention to every place-name in chapter 15 because he lived in the Holy Land and knew the terrain east of the Jordan firsthand. He explains the shift from Dibon to Dimon in 15:9 through its link to the Hebrew word for "blood," while noting the traditional observation that Moabites shaving their heads and weeping on high places (15:2–3) vividly exposes the helplessness of idols under God's judgment. *(Commentary on Isaiah, on chapter 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Jerome's detailed geographical comments on Moab and the Dibon/Dimon wordplay in his Isaiah 15 commentary; not verified against the Latin original -->

**Rashi**, the famed eleventh-century Jewish scholar in France, places the Moab oracle against the historical backdrop of Assyrian imperial conquests. He reads "My heart cries out over Moab" (15:5) as the prophet's own compassion over a neighbor's tragedy, while interpreting the "lion" in 15:9 as a metaphor for a mighty conquering king—such as Nebuchadnezzar or Assyria's king—arriving to complete the ruin of the survivors. *(Commentary on Isaiah, on chapter 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Rashi reading the lion in verse 9 as a conquering king and setting chapter 15 amid imperial invasions; not checked against the Hebrew original -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pauses at length on 15:5 to reflect on Isaiah's heart. That Isaiah weeps over a pagan nation long hostile to God's people teaches God's servants a priceless lesson, Calvin writes: when proclaiming God's righteous judgment, a prophet must never harbor malice or gloat over another's ruin, but carry genuine sorrow for fellow human beings in distress. On 15:9, Calvin also notes that when people refuse to humble themselves under initial trials, heavier judgments inevitably follow. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 15)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's pastoral lesson on 15:5 regarding the prophet's compassion while pronouncing judgment on enemies is a celebrated accent of his Isaiah commentary -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws lessons on the fragility of wealth and the emptiness of idols across chapter 15. Because Moab had lounged in prosperous ease for years, the sudden overnight fall of Ar and Kir (15:1) struck with double shock; all their offerings and tears at pagan shrines proved useless because dead idols cannot save. At the same time, Henry lifts up "My heart cries out over Moab" (15:5) as evidence that those who fear God feel sincere pity even when enemies meet calamity. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's recurring emphases on overnight collapse, futile idol cries, and compassion in verse 5; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, treats Isaiah 15–16 as one unified oracle in which chapter 15 sweeps across the full length of Moab's tragedy from north to south. Motyer highlights the startling honesty of 15:5: right inside a verdict against an arrogant foreign nation, God through His prophet displays a grieving heart utterly unlike the cruel victory hymns of surrounding ancient empires. He also points out the sharp pun in 15:9 altering Dibon to Dimon to evoke "blood." *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's unified structure for chapters 15–16, emphasis on compassion in verse 5, and the Dibon/Dimon wordplay; exact page numbers not verified -->

**John N. Oswalt**, American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 15 as a mirror warning Judah itself against sliding into Moab-like complacency. Moab exemplifies a nation trusting in wealth, natural geographic defenses, and self-made religion—only to watch everything shatter overnight (15:1–7). Oswalt particularly emphasizes that 15:5 reveals the true nature of biblical judgment: God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked, grieving whenever human stubbornness drags people into ruin. *(The Book of Isaiah, Chapters 1–39, NICOT, 1986)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Oswalt reading the Moab oracle as a warning against self-reliance and highlighting divine grief in 15:5 in NICOT; exact page numbers not verified -->
