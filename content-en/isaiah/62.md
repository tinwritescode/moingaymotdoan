# Isaiah 62 — A New Name and the Joy of a Bridegroom

## Story

Before the jubilant song at the end of chapter 61 even fades, a fierce, passionate voice rises again to carry forward the mission for the holy city. The speaker—either the anointed herald from the previous chapter or the LORD Himself—vows never to stay silent for Zion's sake and never to rest for Jerusalem's sake until "her righteousness shines like a bright light, her salvation like a blazing torch." Then every nation and king on earth will see her vindication and glory. Jerusalem will receive a "new name" bestowed directly by the mouth of the LORD—no longer tossed aside at history's edge, she becomes "a crown of glory in the hand of the LORD, a royal diadem in the palm of your God" (62:1–3).

That promise of a new name immediately blooms into joyful wedding imagery. The bitter nicknames once pinned on city and land—"Forsaken" and "Desolate"—are wiped clean. In their place, the city will be called "My Delight Is in Her" (*Hephzibah*) and the once-ruined land named "Married" (*Beulah*), because the LORD genuinely delights in her and binds Himself to her land in marriage. The poetry rises to an unforgettable crescendo: as a young man marries a young woman, "so your sons will marry you; and as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you" (62:4–5).

In the next verses, the scene shifts atop Jerusalem's walls. God announces He has posted "watchmen" along her ramparts who never fall silent day or night. Then the speaker turns straight to those watchmen—calling them by the striking title "you who remind the LORD"—and hands them an audacious charge: take no rest for yourselves, and "give Him no rest" until He reestablishes Jerusalem and makes her "the praise of the earth" (62:6–7).

To reassure a community that had repeatedly lost everything to raiders and foreign armies, the LORD swears solemnly by "His right hand and His mighty arm." That bitter irony of war and exile—Judeans sweating to sow and harvest only for invaders to carry off the crop—will never happen again. He vows never again to hand their grain over to enemies or let foreigners drink the new wine they labored to produce. Instead, those who harvest will eat the fruit of their own labor and praise the LORD, and those who gather grapes will drink new wine in joyous worship right inside "the courts of My sanctuary" (62:8–9).

The chapter finishes with an exhilarating call summoning the entire city to prepare the road for imminent rescue. Everyone is urged to pass out through the gates to clear the path: level the highway for the people, build up the roadway, clear away every obstructing stone, and raise a signal banner high for the nations to spot and gather home. For the LORD's proclamation has already reached the ends of the earth, sending Daughter Zion the greatest news imaginable: "Behold, your Salvation is coming! Behold, His reward is with Him, and His recompense accompanies Him." When that day arrives, both people and city carry luminous new titles: the people are named "The Holy People" and "The Redeemed of the LORD," while Jerusalem is called "Sought Out" and "A City Not Forsaken" (62:10–12).

## Key Takeaways

**God loves His people with radiant delight, not reluctant pity.** Many imagine God saves people merely to keep up appearances, or out of cool, detached sympathy for the weak. Verses 62:4–5 demolish that notion with the word "delight" and the picture of a young bridegroom beaming on his wedding day. In God's eyes, His redeemed people are no chore He tolerates, but a precious crown held proudly in His hand and a cherished bride.

**God Himself invites persistent prayer that reminds Him of His promises.** Telling believers to "give Him no rest" (62:7) sounds astonishingly bold, yet God Himself stations those watchmen on the walls and assigns them this exact task. God is neither forgetful nor sluggish needing human prodding; He opened the chapter swearing He Himself will "not keep still" for Zion (62:1). Inviting tireless prayer pulls His people into sharing His own heartbeat, watching and partnering with what He is already eager to do.

**God's blessing reaches ordinary daily work so honest labor is no longer stolen.** The LORD's oath in 62:8–9 speaks directly to the real grief of Judean farmers: toiling all year only to watch invaders seize the harvest. When God rescues, daily bread and vintage wine return to the hands that produced them—and an ordinary meal turns into glad worship in God's courts. Biblical faith never disconnects spirituality from daily bread and fair work.

**Sure promises do not put believers on the couch—they mobilize us to clear the road.** Even after the LORD swears by His right hand and announces salvation arriving, verse 62:10 fires off four urgent commands in a row: pass through the gates, prepare the way, build up the highway, clear out the stones. Trusting God's faithfulness goes hand in hand with concrete action—clearing away inner and communal stumbling blocks to welcome salvation and raising a banner so others find their way in too.

## Deeper Meaning

**A new name marks a brand-new identity across Scripture.** Throughout the Bible, receiving a new name launches a new life: Abram becomes Abraham, Jacob becomes Israel. Here Jerusalem sheds the label "Forsaken" and receives a "new name" straight from God's lips (62:2; 62:4). That promise echoes all the way to Scripture's closing book, where the risen Christ gives the overcomer a white stone inscribed with "a new name" (Revelation 2:17) and writes upon them the name of God and the New Jerusalem (Revelation 3:12). A shameful past is never the final verdict over anyone belonging to God.

**Marriage between God and Zion leads straight to Christ and the church.** God as husband to His people is no isolated note here; Hosea proclaimed earlier, "I will betroth you to Me forever" (Hosea 2:19–20), and Isaiah 54 already comforted widowed Zion that "your husband is your Maker" (54:4–6). In the New Testament, Paul unfolds Christ's love for the church, giving Himself up to make her radiant and spotless (Ephesians 5:25–27). And in the Bible's climactic vision, the New Jerusalem descends from heaven "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Revelation 21:2).

**Watchmen giving God no rest resonate in Jesus' parable of persistent prayer.** Commanding watchmen to remind the LORD day and night and "give Him no rest" (62:6–7) shares the exact spirit of Jesus' parable of the persistent widow and the unjust judge (Luke 18:1–8). Jesus taught His disciples "that they should always pray and not lose heart"—not because God resembles a selfish judge, but the exact reverse: if even a godless magistrate responds to persistence, how much more surely will a loving God grant justice to His chosen ones crying out day and night!

**The oath over grain and wine reverses the covenant curses of the Pentateuch.** In Deuteronomy, among the harshest penalties for Israel's unfaithfulness was planting vineyards without enjoying their grapes while a foreign nation consumed the harvest of their labor (Deuteronomy 28:30–33). The solemn oath of 62:8–9 declares that era of curse over. God's people return to the original blessing of Mosaic Law: bringing grain and new wine to God's chosen sanctuary to eat and rejoice in His presence (Deuteronomy 14:22–26).

**Clearing the highway and "your Salvation is coming" link both halves of Isaiah and ring on Palm Sunday.** Verses 62:10–11 directly echo the opening overture of Isaiah 40: building the highway (40:3; 57:14) and heralding God's arrival with "His reward with Him, and His recompense before Him" (40:10). Remarkably, when Matthew records Jesus riding a donkey into Jerusalem, he opens with "Say to Daughter Zion"—taken straight from Isaiah 62:11—and joins it seamlessly to Zechariah's prophecy of the gentle King on a colt (Matthew 21:5; Zechariah 9:9). Meanwhile, the title "The Holy People" in 62:12 traces straight from Sinai's covenant (Exodus 19:6) to the New Testament church (1 Peter 2:9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who speaks the opening line, "For Zion's sake I will not keep silent"?** Because the text leaves the speaker unnamed in 62:1, commentators split into two main views. Many identify the speaker as the LORD Himself—the same "I" who posts watchmen on Jerusalem's walls in 62:6. Others hear the continuing voice of the prophet or the anointed herald from chapter 61, since 62:2–3 refers to "the LORD" in the third person. Under either reading, the core message converges: both God and His chosen messenger share an unstoppable passion that refuses rest until Zion shines restored.

**The Hebrew names behind 62:4 and a fascinating historical echo.** The four symbolic names in 62:4 are *Azubah* ("Forsaken"), *Shemamah* ("Desolate"), *Hephzibah* ("My delight is in her"), and *Beulah* ("Married"). Intriguingly, right in Isaiah's own historical period, *Hephzibah* was the actual name of King Hezekiah's queen and King Manasseh's mother (2 Kings 21:1). Perhaps the name carried warm associations with a memorable era of David's house, here tenderly adopted by God as a love-name for Jerusalem itself.

**Why does 62:5 say "your sons will marry you"?** Taken literally, sons marrying their mother city sounds jarring. In ancient Hebrew, texts were originally written with consonants alone, without vowel marks; the exact same consonant cluster can be vocalized either as *your sons* (followed by the Masoretic text and BSB) or as *your Builder / Maker*—mirroring "your Maker" (54:5). Several modern translations adopt the second vocalization—"so your Builder will marry you"—matching the second half of the verse celebrating God's bridegroom joy. Keeping "your sons," many commentators explain the Hebrew verb *marry* in its wider root sense of taking responsible care of and dwelling faithfully in one's homeland, never abandoning it again.

**The unusual image of a "crown of glory in the hand of the LORD" (62:3).** Ordinarily a king wears a crown on his head, so why hold it in his hand? Commentators note two lovely layers here: first, holding a crown up in the open hand displays it proudly for all nations to admire; second, resting inside God's mighty hand guarantees total safety so no enemy can snatch it away.

**Marriage imagery in 62:4–5 never diminishes single or widowed women.** In ancient society, an abandoned or unmarried woman frequently faced severe vulnerability and social stigma, much like untended land falling barren. The prophet borrows a painful reality his hearers knew intimately to depict an entire city moving from abandonment into God's cherished protection—never to measure an individual woman's worth by marital status.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the renowned fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Latin Bible translator, examined each Hebrew name in 62:4 closely, contrasting *Azubah* (forsaken) with *Hephzibah* (where God delights). He read the new name, royal crown, and tireless watchmen on the walls as emblems of the church united to Christ, where apostles and preachers stand guard day and night watching over God's people. *(Commentary on Isaiah, chapter 62)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic attention by Jerome to the Hebrew names and ecclesiological reading in chapter 62; exact Latin lines not verified -->

**Jewish synagogue liturgy (Haftarah)** pairs Isaiah 61:10 through 63:9—spanning all of chapter 62—as the seventh and final *Haftarah of Consolation* right before the Jewish New Year (Rosh Hashanah). Placing chapter 62 at the climax of consolation alongside the Torah reading *Nitzavim* (on Israel returning to God) highlights how deeply Jewish tradition treasures the renaming of "Forsaken" to "My Delight Is in Her" and the bridegroom's joy as the ultimate seal of God's unbreakable covenant. *(Synagogue Haftarah cycle for Parashat Nitzavim)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Isaiah 61:10 through 63:9 serving as the seventh Haftarah of Consolation paired with Nitzavim is a firmly established liturgical fact -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, focused closely on the watchmen of 62:6–7. He saw them first as prophets and ministers posted on the church's ramparts with twin inseparable duties: proclaiming God's word persistently to the people and pleading day and night before God. Explaining "give Him no rest," Calvin noted that God stoops to everyday human speech to embolden believers to pray tirelessly, even though God Himself never sleeps or delays. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 62)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic comments by Calvin on watchmen and the bold anthropomorphism "give Him no rest" in chapter 62; exact text on ccel.org not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, paused warmly over the crown held "in the hand of the LORD" (62:3). God not only prizes His people as a royal diadem, Henry observed, He holds that diadem securely in His own hand—both displaying the glory of His grace before the world and guarding them safely from every foe. On 62:6–7, he called faithful intercessors "the Lord's remembrancers"—not informing God of anything He forgot, but laying His own promises back before Him in prayer. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 62)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: two signature observations by Matthew Henry on the crown in God's hand and the Lord's remembrancers in chapter 62; exact wording on ccel.org not verified -->

**Alec Motyer**, a British Old Testament scholar, observed how chapter 62 continues chapter 61 seamlessly and closes with direct echoes of chapter 40. The highway-clearing commands and the herald announcing the coming Savior bringing His reward (62:10–11) deliberately reprise Isaiah 40's opening refrain, closing a vast structural circle around Zion's restoration and guaranteeing that what God started He will carry all the way until Jerusalem stands known as "A City Not Forsaken." *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Motyer links 62:10–11 structurally back to chapter 40 across chapters 61–62; specific page not verified -->

**John N. Oswalt**, an American Old Testament professor, spotlighted the lovely paradox in 62:6–7: the very God who vowed at verse 1 neither to stay silent nor to rest personally places human watchmen on the walls to cry out and give Him no rest.
