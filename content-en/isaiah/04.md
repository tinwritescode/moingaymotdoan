# Isaiah 4 — The Branch of the LORD and the Cloud Covering Zion

## Story

Chapter 4 contains only six verses, yet it straddles two completely opposite worlds. The opening verse continues the devastation at the end of chapter 3, where Jerusalem's fighting men have fallen in droves by the sword. The toll of war is so grim that "seven women will take hold of one man" begging to be his wives. They gladly waive the basic rights ancient law guaranteed a wife: "We will eat our own bread and provide our own clothes. Just let us be called by your name. Take away our disgrace!" The haughty ladies of chapter 3, who once boasted endless jewelry and silk robes, now long only for a husband's name in a city emptied of men (4:1).

Yet with the very next verse, the oracle suddenly bursts into light as though dark storm clouds have been pulled back. It begins with the same phrase—"In that day"—but now it is a day of restoration: "the Branch of the LORD will be beautiful and glorious," and "the fruit of the land" will become the pride and splendor of Israel's survivors. Every artificial ornament stripped away in chapter 3 is replaced by fresh life that God Himself causes to spring forth (4:2).

Inside the city once branded Sodom and Gomorrah, a new community emerges. Every person left in Zion and remaining in Jerusalem—all "who are recorded among the living"—will "be called holy." No longer a beleaguered minority inside a rotten city, the whole redeemed people are set apart for God (4:3).

How can a bloodstained, arrogant city ever become holy? Verse 4 answers plainly: not by Judah fixing itself up on its own, but when the Lord Himself uses "a spirit of judgment and a spirit of fire" to wash away the filth of the daughters of Zion and "cleanse the bloodstains from the heart of Jerusalem." The searing, fiery judgment of the preceding chapters is the very means by which God purifies the city to prepare it for new life (4:4).

Finally, the vision closes with the sheltering presence of the LORD Himself. Over the entire site of Mount Zion and over all her assemblies, He will create "a cloud of smoke by day and a glow of flaming fire by night." Over all that glory will stretch "a canopy," serving like a shelter to give shade from the daytime heat and "a refuge and hiding place from the storm and the rain" (4:5–6).

## Key Takeaways

**Earthly pride ends in bare humiliation.** The scene of seven women offering to support themselves just to bear a man's name (4:1) is the bitter finale for the proud daughters of Zion in chapter 3. War and judgment strip away every outward badge humanity takes pride in.

**Hope blooms only from what God Himself causes to grow.** When human strength is cut down to the ground, "the Branch of the LORD" sprouts anew (4:2). The true beauty of God's people comes not from gold rings or fine robes, but from the life and glory God gives to the remnant.

**Holiness requires passing through cleansing.** Zion does not become holy by ignoring its bloodstained past, but through God washing every stain away with "a spirit of judgment and a spirit of fire" (4:3–4). God's grace never sweeps sin under the rug; it burns sin away to save the sinner.

**God's own presence is the surest shelter.** The cloud by day, the fire by night, and the canopy against heat and storm (4:5–6) reveal that the greatest gift God promises His restored people is neither wealth nor imperial clout, but Himself dwelling among them and protecting them.

## Deeper Meaning

**"The Branch of the LORD" launches a chain of prophecies about the coming King.** This title (4:2) became a signature designation among later prophets for the Messiah from David's line. Jeremiah promised that God would raise up for David "a righteous Branch" who would reign wisely and justly (Jeremiah 23:5), while Zechariah twice called God's coming servant "the Branch," the One who would build the temple of the LORD (Zechariah 3:8; Zechariah 6:12).

**"Recorded among the living" stretches from Moses to the holy city of Revelation.** The concept of God's people having their names written in a heavenly register (4:3) first appeared when Moses interceded for sinful Israel at Mount Sinai and spoke of "the book You have written" (Exodus 32:32). Daniel likewise promised that everyone whose name is found written in the book will be delivered in the time of distress (Daniel 12:1), and at the close of Scripture, only those written in "the Lamb's book of life" enter the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:27).

**A spirit of judgment and cleansing fire.** The image of God washing filth away through "a spirit of judgment and a spirit of fire" (4:4) echoes John the Baptist's introduction of Jesus in the New Testament: the One coming after him "will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire" (Matthew 3:11). That fire both consumes the chaff of injustice and refines the people who belong to Him.

**A new Exodus spanning all of Mount Zion.** The daytime cloud and nighttime fire (4:5) immediately recall the pillar of cloud and pillar of fire that guided Israel out of Egypt and covered the tabernacle in the wilderness (Exodus 13:21; Exodus 40:34–38). Yet there is an astonishing expansion: in ancient times the cloud of glory rested only over the tent of meeting, whereas now it spreads "over the whole site of Mount Zion and over her assemblies." The entire redeemed community becomes God's dwelling place.

**A sheltering canopy in the new heaven and new earth.** The glorious canopy shielding from sun and storm in 4:5–6 is widened in Revelation to include God's redeemed from every nation: the One seated on the throne "will spread His tabernacle over them," so that "the sun will not beat down on them, nor any scorching heat" (Revelation 7:15–16), for "the dwelling place of God is with men" (Revelation 21:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 4:1 is really the conclusion to chapter 3.** A dramatic hinge falls between 4:1 and 4:2: verse 1 closes the wartime tragedy of the daughters of Zion from chapter 3, while verse 2 begins the promise of renewal. Chapter divisions were added in the Middle Ages and occasionally cut right across the flow of thought. The women's offer—"We will eat our own bread and provide our own clothes"—in 4:1 also reflects the Law of Moses requiring a husband to supply food and clothing for his wife (Exodus 21:10); so desperate are these women that they forfeit every legal entitlement simply to escape the stigma of being unmarried and childless in ancient society.

**"The Branch of the LORD" and "the fruit of the land" have two main interpretations.** Some modern scholars read these parallel phrases in 4:2 literally as restored crops and agricultural abundance after wartime ruin. By contrast, both ancient Jewish tradition and Christian tradition identify "the Branch of the LORD" as the Messiah—the King God brings forth from David's line—with "the fruit of the land" referring either to the land's flourishing under His reign or even to the divine and human natures of the Savior. Within the broader book of Isaiah, the shoot springing from a cut-down stump in chapter 11 (11:1) lends strong support to the messianic reading.

**The word "canopy" in 4:5 evokes a wedding canopy.** In Hebrew, the word *chuppah* is also used for a bridal chamber or the wedding canopy in Jewish marriage ceremonies. Beneath the image of a shelter from storm and rain glows the warmth of covenant love: God protects Zion as a bridegroom cherishes his bride.

## What Commentators Say

**Targum Jonathan**, the ancient Jewish Aramaic paraphrase of the Prophets, translates the phrase "the Branch of the LORD" in verse 2 directly as *the Messiah of the LORD*. **Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century scholar and Church Father renowned for his Latin Bible translation, reads the paired expressions "the Branch of the LORD" and "the fruit of the land" in verse 2 as two facets of the mystery of Christ: He both comes forth from God and grows as fruit from the earth by taking on human flesh. In Jerome's view, the daytime cloud and nighttime fire in verses 5–6 foreshadow God's sheltering presence over the Church amid every earthly tempest. *(Commentary on Isaiah, on chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall the patristic and Hieronymian reading of the two phrases in verse 2 as Christ's divine and human natures; not verified word-for-word -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, cautions readers against forcing an overly rigid division between the two halves of verse 2, yet affirms that the promise centers on Christ, the Church's only true glory. Lingering over verses 3 and 4, Calvin emphasizes that those "recorded among the living" do not make themselves holy; they become holy through the purifying work of the Holy Spirit—a washing and a fire that remove stains human effort can never cleanse. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's Christ-centered reading of verse 2 and emphasis on purifying grace in verses 3–4; not checked against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, highlights the abrupt pivot between verse 1 and verse 2. Just as wartime humiliation drives the daughters of Zion to rock bottom in verse 1, God refuses to let the story end in despair and raises up "the Branch of the LORD" as their new glory and adornment. Henry likens the cloud, fire, and canopy in verses 5–6 to a return of the Exodus, guaranteeing that wherever God's people gather in holiness, He pitches His tent over them against scorching heat and storm. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's connection between restoration in chapter 4, the Exodus, and God's protection of the Church; not checked against the original text -->

**John N. Oswalt**, American Old Testament professor, notes that chapter 4 closes the opening unit that began in chapter 2 with striking symmetry: it opens with Mount Zion exalted (2:2–4) and concludes with Mount Zion covered by the cloud of glory (4:2–6). In Oswalt's reading, verse 4 is the hinge answering the burning question of the first four chapters: how can today's corrupt Jerusalem become tomorrow's holy city?
