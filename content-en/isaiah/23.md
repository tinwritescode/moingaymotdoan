# Isaiah 23 — The Fall and Renewal of Tyre

## Story

After the previous chapter swung its arrow of judgment straight back onto Jerusalem and the official Shebna (22:1–25), chapter 23 closes the entire ten-oracle sequence concerning the nations—begun with Babylon (13:1)—with "The burden against Tyre." Tyre and its sister city Sidon sat along the Phoenician coast (modern southern Lebanon) as the wealthiest maritime hub of the Mediterranean. The prophecy breaks like a lightning bolt across the sea: great merchant ships sailing home from Tarshish receive shocking news at Cyprus (Kittim) that their home port of Tyre has been laid waste—no houses left, no harbor to enter. Along the coast where Sidon's busy merchants once crowded every quay, all bustle drops into stunned silence (23:1–2).

In former days, grain harvested along the Nile (Shihor) poured by ship into Tyre's warehouses, turning the port into "the marketplace of the nations" where sea lanes converged for profit. Now Sidon hangs its head in shame. Even the sea itself—where Tyre's fortified island citadel stood proudly as the ocean's own child—cries out like a mother robbed of every child, lamenting bitterly that she has never labored, given birth, or raised young men and women. When word of Tyre's fall reaches Egypt, the whole country trembles in anguish at losing its premier trading partner (23:3–5).

The oracle tells coastal residents to sail across to Tarshish wailing, then asks a haunting question: is this really your jubilant city of ancient days, whose feet carried it to found colonies in the farthest corners of the sea? Who dared decree such ruin against Tyre—"the bestower of crowns, whose merchants are princes, whose traders are renowned on the earth"? The answer hits like a courtroom gavel: the LORD of Hosts Himself planned it, "to defile the pride of all glory and to disgrace all the renowned of the earth" (23:6–9).

Winds shift across every harbor. Daughter Tarshish—distant colonies once squeezed under Tyre's commercial grip—is free of restraint at last, spreading freely across its land like the Nile overflowing its banks. For the LORD has stretched out His hand over the sea, shaken kingdoms, and commanded the strongholds of Canaan (Phoenicia's coast) to be destroyed. He tells Sidon directly that its noisy revelry is finished; even if its citizens flee across to Cyprus for shelter, they will find no rest there either (23:10–12).

Lest listeners imagine this warning is far-fetched, the prophet points east toward the Chaldeans (Babylon): a once-proud nation whose palaces Assyria turned into siege-battered rubble fit only for desert beasts. If a mighty continental power could be crushed like that, how could a maritime fortress like Tyre hope to stand? The lament closes with its opening refrain: wail, O ships of Tarshish, for your fortress is destroyed (23:13–14).

With the final four verses, the text shifts from poetic dirge to a clear prose forecast. Tyre will be forgotten for seventy years, "the span of one king." When those seventy years run out, Tyre will resemble the popular folk song about a forgotten prostitute: ignored by old patrons, she grabs her harp, strolls through town, strums sweetly, and sings song after song hoping customers remember her and come back (23:15–16).

Then the story takes a totally unexpected turn. After seventy years, the LORD will visit Tyre. The city will revive, reopen its shipping routes, and "prostitute herself with all the kingdoms on the face of the earth"—an ironic way of saying she returns to courting every trading nation for gain. Yet the closing verse delivers the chapter's biggest shock: this time Tyre's profits and wages will no longer be piled into private vaults or hoarded by greed ("they will not be stored or hoarded"), but set apart as holy to the LORD, flowing freely to supply "abundant food and fine clothing" for those who dwell in His presence (23:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Every financial empire eventually falls.** From chapter 13 to chapter 23, Isaiah called military powers like Babylon, Assyria, and Egypt before God's bar, then closed with Tyre—superpower of trade, shipping, and finance. Neither fleets nor overflowing warehouses can hold a city upright when it turns wealth into an idol replacing God.

**Pride is the hidden disease lurking beneath earthly splendor.** Chapter 23 never faults Tyre for knowing how to build ships or conduct commerce. Verse 23:9 pinpoints exactly why God intervened: "the pride of all glory" and the smug self-assurance of earth's elites convinced they hold the world in their palms. Once people believe money and networks guarantee eternal safety, their hearts have already closed against God.

**Global economics also sits in God's hands.** Observers usually credit the rise and fall of ports and markets to luck, geopolitical shifts, or merchant savvy. Yet the question in 23:8 and its answer in 23:9 pull back the curtain: the LORD of Hosts stretches out His hand over the sea, assigns seventy years of decline, and then Himself "visits" the city to restore it (23:17).

**God does not abolish wealth; He redirects it.** Chapter 23 ends remarkably: Tyre does not vanish forever into barren rock. After discipline, its commerce revives—yet its entire aim flips upside down. Instead of stockpiling riches to inflate ego, its earnings sustain the community worshiping God (23:18). Money is not inherently filthy; it becomes holy when lifted out of greed's clutch to serve God and neighbor.

## Deeper Meaning

**The ships of Tarshish from beginning to end in Isaiah.** Back in chapter 2, announcing the Day of the LORD that humbles all pride, Isaiah singled out "every ship of Tarshish" (2:16). Here in chapter 23, that warning unfolds as a dirge for ships losing their home harbor (23:1; 23:14). Yet later in the book, those very ships of Tarshish sail back into view in an utterly transformed scene: leading the procession bringing God's children along with their silver and gold home to Jerusalem to honor the LORD's name (60:9). Isaiah's arc never stops at judgment; it passes through judgment into the redemption of the nations.

**Echoes in Ezekiel and the book of Revelation.** Over a century after Isaiah, Ezekiel devoted three entire chapters to mourning Tyre as a splendid cargo ship sinking into the heart of the sea and unmasking its king's pride for claiming to be a god (Ezekiel 26:1–5; Ezekiel 27:2–4; Ezekiel 28:2–5). Then in Scripture's closing book, sea captains, sailors, and merchants standing far off weeping as a fabulously wealthy capital goes up in smoke in a single hour draw directly on Tyre's lament to depict the fall of Babylon the Great—emblem of every self-exalting global economy opposing God (Revelation 18:11–19).

**A startling reversal of Moses' Law.** Deuteronomy strictly forbade bringing "the wages of a prostitute" into the house of the LORD, declaring it detestable before Him (Deuteronomy 23:18). Yet in Isaiah 23:18—the exact same verse number!—right after comparing Tyre to a harlot collecting wages from all nations (23:17), prophecy announces that her merchandise and wages will be "set apart to the LORD"! It previews the transforming power of grace: things once tainted among the Gentiles will be cleansed for God's service, just as Zechariah foretells a day when even horses' bells and ordinary cooking pots bear the inscription "Holy to the LORD" (Zechariah 14:20–21).

**From ancient King Hiram to the Canaanite woman near Tyre meeting Jesus.** Tyre was not always Israel's enemy. When Solomon built the temple, King Hiram of Tyre blessed the LORD gladly and sent Lebanon cedars and master craftsmen to build God's house (1 Kings 5:1–10). In the New Testament, Jesus cited Tyre and Sidon as pagan cities far readier to repent than stubborn Judean towns (Matthew 11:21–22). And when Jesus personally stepped into the district of Tyre and Sidon, a Canaanite mother knelt before Him in such humble trust that He praised her "great faith" and healed her daughter on the spot (Matthew 15:21–28). The promise of God visiting the Phoenician coast began dawning in His very ministry.

## Notes & Nuances

**Which historical downfall of Tyre is in view here?** Ancient Tyre—spanning both a mainland town and a heavily fortified offshore island—endured repeated sieges by great empires: Assyria's Shalmaneser V and Sennacherib late in the eighth century BC, Babylon's Nebuchadnezzar for thirteen straight years early in the sixth century, and finally Alexander the Great building a causeway to conquer the island in 332 BC. Because 23:13 explicitly notes Assyria's recent devastation of the Chaldeans (likely Sennacherib's campaigns against Babylon around 703 or 689 BC), many scholars locate the oracle's direct backdrop in the Assyrian era near the end of Isaiah's life, while recognizing it unfolded even more profoundly beneath Babylon later on.

**How should "seventy years, the span of one king" be understood?** Rather than a rigid stopwatch tally, the phrase evokes one full human lifespan (Psalms 90:10) or the appointed term of an imperial era, akin to Babylon's seventy years (cf. Jeremiah 25:11). Some commentators match the seventy years to Tyre's eclipse under Assyria (roughly 701 BC until Assyria's collapse around 630 BC); others tie it to Babylonian domination until Persia allowed Phoenician ports to revive trade.

**Why compare international trade to "prostitution"?** To modern ears, the street-song image of a forgotten harlot in 23:16–17 sounds harsh. In Old Testament prophetic speech, calling Tyre's commerce prostitution does not condemn honest business; it exposes Tyre's willingness to strike deals with any power on earth purely for cash, devoid of loyalty or moral principle. Then verse 23:18 flips that jarring metaphor on its head to spotlight grace's power to sanctify profit for God's people.

**Verse 23:13 is famously tricky in the original Hebrew.** One widely followed reading ("Behold the land of the Chaldeans—a people now of no account! Assyria destined it for desert creatures...") takes Assyria's ruin of Babylon as a stark object lesson aimed at Tyre. Some modern scholars consider the ancient text slightly obscure here, translating it instead to mean the Chaldeans (rather than Assyria) will overthrow Tyre. Under either translation, the logic stays rock-solid: another celebrated city has already fallen—proving Tyre's fortress is anything but invincible.

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, draws from chapter 23 the truth that neither commercial superpowers nor island fortresses endure by luck or human brilliance; God brought Tyre down specifically to break the arrogance of wealth. On the closing verse (23:18), Calvin explains it as a promise of Christ's kingdom, when Gentiles entering the covenant consecrate the very riches once serving greed to advance God's work and sustain His people. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's primary exposition of divine sovereignty over commercial pride and Gentile conversion in verse 18; not checked against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws a down-to-earth warning from 23:9 for everyone engaged in commerce: booms and slumps alike sit in God's hand, and wealth swiftly breeds pride whenever people forget the Giver. Henry especially delights in 23:18 because it proves trade itself is no enemy to faith: once hearts are renewed by God, honest profits stop being hoarded selfishly and turn into a holy offering sustaining God's servants. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's pastoral applications regarding commercial pride and consecrated profits; not checked against the original -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, highlights the purposeful symmetry framing chapters 13 through 23: the section opens with Babylon—symbol of imperial military and political arrogance—and closes with Tyre—symbol of economic and commercial arrogance. Both sword and silver must bow before the LORD. He also emphasizes the bold reversal of 23:18 against Deuteronomy's prohibition, illustrating grace's final power to turn the unclean into something holy. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's Babylon-to-Tyre structural frame and comment on verse 18; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, reads the Tyre oracle as a prophetic critique exposing an imperial marketplace that treats the entire globe as its trading floor and imagines itself eternal. The beauty of the closing two verses, he writes, is that God does not wipe out economic life, but rescues it from selfish hoarding ("will not be stored or hoarded") and redirects it into food and clothing nourishing community before God. *(Isaiah 1–39, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: critiquing commercial empire and reorienting economics toward community well-being are signature themes in Brueggemann's Isaiah commentary; exact page numbers not verified -->
