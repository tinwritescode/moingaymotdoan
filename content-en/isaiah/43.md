# Isaiah 43 — You Are Mine and the New Thing in the Wilderness

## Story

Chapter 42 ended amid smoke and judgment flames, as fire scorched Israel all around without waking their hardened hearts (42:25). Yet the instant chapter 43 opens, the mood swings completely on two grace-filled words: "But now, O Jacob!" The Creator who formed Israel speaks tenderly to calm every dread: "Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; you are Mine!" God never promises a life free of rough crossings, yet vows to walk shoulder to shoulder through the fiercest trials: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you; and when you go through the rivers, they will not overwhelm you. When you walk through the fire, you will not be scorched; the flames will not set you ablaze." Because He is the LORD their God, the Holy One and Savior of Israel—because they are "precious and honored in My sight" and loved by Him—He willingly hands over Egypt, Cush, and Seba as their ransom, giving nations in exchange for their life (43:1–4).

Once more God reassures them: "Do not fear, for I am with you." He promises to gather their scattered offspring from all four horizons: bringing them from the east, gathering them from the west, ordering the north "Give them up!" and telling the south "Do not hold them back!" Sons and daughters will come home from the ends of the earth—everyone called by His name, created and formed "for My glory" (43:5–7).

From intimate reassurance, the camera pulls back to a global courtroom. God orders His own people brought forward—addressed with affectionate irony as "a people who have eyes but are blind, and who have ears but are deaf"—to stand opposite all nations of the world. God challenges every nation and pagan deity: which of them foretold what lies ahead or explained former things? Let them bring witnesses to prove themselves right! Turning back to Israel, God declares their true calling: "You are My witnesses, declares the LORD, and My servant whom I have chosen, so that you may consider and believe Me and understand that I am He!" Before Him no god was formed, nor will there be one after Him: "I, yes I, am the LORD, and there is no Savior but Me." He alone proclaimed, saved, and revealed, with no foreign god involved; from eternity He is God, and when He acts, "who can reverse it?" (43:8–13).

To back up that sovereignty, the Redeemer announces an imminent historical rescue: for His people's sake He sends forces against Babylon, bringing down all its bars and turning Chaldean boasts into flight aboard their treasured ships (43:14–15). He recalls His legendary ancient triumph carving a road across the Red Sea and a pathway through mighty waters, drawing out Pharaoh's chariots, horses, and crack army to lie down never to rise again—"extinguished, snuffed out like a wick" (43:16–17).

Then, startlingly, God tells His people to stop living backward in that glorious past: "Do not call to mind the former things; pay no attention to the things of old. Behold, I am about to do something new; even now it is springing up—do you not perceive it?" This new wonder eclipses even the ancient Exodus: He will carve a road straight across the wilderness and send rivers surging across barren desert. Even wild jackals and ostriches will honor Him seeing living water pour out to refresh His chosen people—the people He formed for Himself so "they will declare My praise" (43:18–21).

At verse 22, the tone drops into an aching indictment packed with breathtaking grace. God reminds Jacob they never earned this rescue: they didn't call on Him—worse, "you have grown weary of Me!" They brought Him no sheep for burnt offerings, bought no sweet cane (calamus), nor honored Him with choice sacrifices. God never burdened or wearied them demanding offerings and incense; instead, they "burdened Me with your sins" and "wearied Me with your iniquities" (43:22–24).

Right when every listener braces for a thunderbolt of condemnation, verse 25 flashes with pure, unmerited grace: "I, yes I, am He who blots out your transgressions for My own sake and remembers your sins no more." If Israel still wants to argue its innocence in court, God invites them to step up and state their case! The undeniable truth is that from their very first ancestor onward they sinned, and their spiritual mediators rebelled against Him. That is precisely why He profaned sanctuary princes and handed Jacob over to utter destruction and scorn in exile—so they would grasp deeply that every drop of coming redemption flows solely from God's free mercy (43:25–28).

## Key Takeaways

**Our worth and safety rest on God calling us by name and claiming us as His own.** Twice this chapter speaks "Do not fear"—never promising smooth skies, but grounding peace in four rock-solid truths right in verse 1: God created you, redeemed you, called you by name, and declared "you are Mine" (43:1; 43:5). God does not promise to remove deep rivers or fiery trials from the road, yet He pledges "I will be with you" straight through water and flame (43:2).

**God's witnesses are never flawless experts, but formerly blind people who tasted rescue.** Remarkably, God calls "a people who have eyes but are blind, and who have ears but are deaf" to take the witness stand before the nations (43:8–10). A witness's credibility hangs never on personal polish or perfection, but on lived testimony that there is truly no Savior besides the LORD (43:11–12).

**Never let past memories—whether triumphs or scars—blind you to the new work God is springing up.** Parting the Red Sea anchored Israel's faith for centuries, yet God boldly says, "Do not call to mind the former things" (43:16–19). He isn't erasing history—He is freeing His people from staring backward so they spot fresh creative power sprouting right ahead: the One who split seas can send rivers racing across lifeless desert.

**God's forgiveness is never wages paid for human goodness; He pardons "for My own sake."** Chapter 43's finale strips away any notion that Israel earned return home through pious sacrifices (43:22–24). All they brought Him was the exhaustion of their sins. Yet God declares He blots out transgressions "for My own sake" and remembers them no more (43:25). Real grace always arrives ahead of human deserving.

## Deeper Meaning

**Walking through fire in 43:2 and the three young men in Babylon's furnace.** The promise "When you walk through the fire, you will not be scorched; the flames will not set you ablaze" (43:2) spoke into the shadow of Babylonian exile—and came literally alive right inside Babylon in the book of Daniel: Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego tossed into a blazing furnace walked freely alongside a fourth figure, emerging without a singed hair or even the smell of smoke on their clothes (Daniel 3:25–27). Meanwhile, God calling "you by your name" (43:1) rings tenderly in Jesus calling Himself the Good Shepherd who "calls His own sheep by name and leads them out" (John 10:3).

**"I am He" and the sole Savior echoed in the New Testament.** The solemn phrase "understand that I am He" (43:10) translates the distinctive Hebrew divine self-declaration (*ani hu*), claiming the LORD's exclusive deity. In John's Gospel, Jesus repeatedly takes that exact formula on His own lips: "unless you believe that I am He, you will die in your sins" (John 8:24) and "before Abraham was born, I am!" (John 8:58). Likewise, "there is no Savior but Me" (43:11) resounds in Peter's testimony before Jerusalem's council: "Salvation exists in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given to men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12), while the charge "You are My witnesses" (43:10) passes straight to the early Church in Acts 1:8.

**A new Exodus opening into new creation.** In 43:16–19, God places the ancient Exodus out of Egypt (Exodus 14:21–28) beside "something new" springing up—roads and rivers through wilderness. That "new thing" reaches full radiance in the New Testament, where Paul proclaims anyone in Christ is "a new creation. The old has passed away. Behold, the new has come!" (2 Corinthians 5:17), culminating in Revelation's throne voice: "Behold, I make all things new!" (Revelation 21:5). Even Israel's purpose—"they will declare My praise" (43:21)—is adopted almost verbatim by Peter for the Church, chosen to "proclaim the excellencies of Him who called you out of darkness into His marvelous light" (1 Peter 2:9; cf. Exodus 19:5).

**"Remembers your sins no more" and the New Covenant.** Blotting out transgressions and remembering sins no more (43:25) walks arm in arm with Jeremiah's foundational New Covenant pledge: "I will forgive their iniquity and will remember their sins no more" (Jeremiah 31:34). Hebrews quotes that exact promise to announce that through Jesus' once-for-all sacrifice, God has inaugurated this era of complete forgiveness (Hebrews 8:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**Does giving Egypt, Cush, and Seba "as a ransom" for Israel (43:3–4) sound unfair to other nations?** Modern readers rightly pause wondering if God sacrificed African peoples to favor one nation. Historical backdrop brings crisp clarity: King Cyrus of Persia freed Jewish exiles from Babylon without demanding a single coin of ransom; instead, the Persian Empire under Cyrus and his son Cambyses acquired the wealthy territories of Egypt, Cush (Nubia south of Egypt), and Seba (Red Sea coast). Using vivid ancient commercial metaphor, Isaiah tells crushed exiles how intensely precious they are in God's eyes—worth redrawing entire imperial maps to bring home—without implying God despises other peoples; elsewhere Isaiah explicitly welcomes Egypt and distant nations into shared worship and blessing (19:24–25; 45:14; 56:6–7).

**Why does 43:23–24 mention neglected sheep and "sweet cane" when exiles had no temple?** "Sweet cane" (calamus) was an imported fragrant reed blended into sacred temple anointing oil and incense. Commentators offer two helpful angles on 43:22–24: viewed during Babylonian exile, without a temple they literally offered no sacrifices—reminding them they held zero bargaining chips to claim rescue as earned wages; viewed across pre-exilic Jerusalem life, even while going through outward rituals their hearts had grown thoroughly tired of God. Either way, the verdict stands firm: their sins burdened God, making verse 25's pardon sheer, unmerited grace.

**Who is "your first father" who sinned in 43:27?** Literally "your earliest ancestor" in Hebrew, scholars weigh three candidates: **Jacob**—named directly right beside it in 43:22 and 43:28, famous for his scheming past before God transformed him—**Abraham** as founder of the nation, or **Adam** as ancestor of all humanity. Most modern interpreters lean toward Jacob given the immediate context addressing "Jacob" and "Israel," underscoring that from the nation's roots through all its "spokesmen" (priests and prophets), not one generation stands innocent enough to win a lawsuit against God.

**The startling shift between 43:25 and 43:28.** At a quick glance, readers wonder why God blots out sins in 43:25 only to talk of disgracing sanctuary princes and giving Jacob to destruction in 43:28. In Hebrew, the verbs in 43:28 naturally read in the past tense—God *already* disgraced the princes and *already* handed Jacob over to scorn—explaining the historical cause of the 586 BC catastrophe they are currently suffering, never revoking verse 25's pardon. Explaining plainly *why* exile fell (43:26–28) magnifies how staggering verse 25's free pardon truly is.

## What Commentators Say

**Rashi**, the famed eleventh-century Jewish scholar in France, explains 43:3–4's ransom of Egypt, Cush, and Seba through historical shifts when Assyrian and Babylonian campaigns pivoted toward Egypt and Cush, sparing Israel. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, holds up 43:25 ("I, yes I, am He who blots out your transgressions for My own sake") as one of Scripture's clearest pillars of salvation by pure grace. It demolishes every theory of human merit, Calvin insists: finding zero grounds for forgiveness inside Israel, God finds His sole reason inside His own merciful nature ("for My own sake"). On 43:1–2, Calvin comforts believers that God never promises a storm-free journey, only His presence ensuring neither floods drown nor flames consume them. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 43)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's appeal to Isaiah 43:25 for pure unmerited grace ("for My own sake") is a classic cornerstone both in his Isaiah 43 commentary and in his Institutes -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, writes warmly on 43:1–2 that being called by name and hearing God say "you are Mine" builds a stronger fortress than stone walls: deep waters and fiery trials may startle us, yet can never ruin anyone walking beside the Redeemer. Turning to 43:24–25, Henry marvels at the breathtaking turn of grace: right after God indicts them for wearying Him with iniquity, readers brace for lightning—yet out bursts free forgiveness for His own name's sake. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 43)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's commentary on passing through waters and fire in 43:1–2 and the sudden burst of grace between verses 24 and 25; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the celebrated nineteenth-century English preacher, preached repeatedly on chapter 43, especially 43:2 ("When you pass through the waters, I will be with you") and 43:25 ("for My own sake"). **Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, highlights the dramatic "But now" launching chapter 43 (43:1): never denying the real fire of judgment ending chapter 42, God steps straight into those flames to redeem His own. Motyer also underlines the sweeping pivot of 43:18–19: the Red Sea Exodus stood as the Old Testament's supreme rescue benchmark, yet God bids His people look forward because His coming "new thing"—rivers across wasteland—will eclipse the old miracle and point onward to messianic redemption. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's link between 42:25 and 43:1 and his exposition of the "new thing" surpassing the old Exodus in 43:16–19; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament professor, reads chapter 43 as poetry rekindling imagination inside a worn-out, numb exilic community. He spotlights the healing power of two twin declarations: in 43:1–4, right while empire labels them disposable nobodies, God affirms "you are Mine" and "precious in My sight"; and in 43:18–19, God pries exiles loose from fixating on past griefs so they dare trust a "new thing" springing up right inside the desert. *(Isaiah 40–66, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Brueggemann reading Isaiah 43 as restoring precious identity to exiles and freeing them from past paralysis through 43:18–19; exact page numbers not verified -->
