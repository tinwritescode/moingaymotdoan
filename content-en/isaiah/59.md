# Isaiah 59 — Sin Builds a Wall and the Redeemer Steps In

## Story

At the close of chapter 58, the people still nursed resentment that they fasted while God seemed not to notice (58:3). Entering chapter 59, the prophet answers that complaint head-on. Nothing is wrong with God's strength or hearing: "Surely the arm of the LORD is not too short to save, nor His ear too dull to hear." The dividing wall stands squarely on the human side. Their own iniquities have built a barrier between them and their God, and their sins have hidden His face so that He does not hear (59:1–2).

The prophet proceeds to name each stain clinging to the community. Their hands are defiled with blood and their fingers with iniquity; their lips speak lies and their tongues mutter wickedness. In the courts, no one pleads for justice or argues an honest case; everyone trusts in empty pleas, tells lies, conceives trouble, and gives birth to iniquity (59:3–4).

Shifting from direct address ("you") to the third person ("they"), the indictment turns chilling through two unforgettable images. Their schemes resemble "hatching the eggs of vipers and weaving a spider's web": whoever eats those eggs dies, and crush one open and a viper bursts out; meanwhile, their spiderwebs can never be woven into garments to cover them. Their feet sprint toward evil, rushing to shed innocent blood. Wherever they go, ruin and destruction trail behind; they have made their own paths crooked and never know the way of peace (59:5–8).

Suddenly the voice transforms. The prophet no longer stands outside leveling charges; he steps right into the middle of his people and says "we." Because of these very sins, justice stands far from us and righteousness never reaches us. We look for light and find only darkness; "like the blind, we feel our way along the wall," groping like people without eyes, stumbling at noon as though it were twilight, living among the vigorous like corpses. We growl like bears and moan softly like doves, straining for justice and salvation that stay far out of reach (59:9–11).

Out of collective lament, the community turns face to face with God. Every trace of grumbling and self-defense vanishes: "Our transgressions are multiplied before You, and our sins testify against us." They confess rebellion, denying the LORD, turning their backs on their God, conceiving and uttering lies and oppression from the heart. Inside so broken a society, justice is driven back, righteousness stands afar off, "truth has stumbled in the public square," honesty cannot get through the door, and anyone who turns away from evil promptly becomes easy prey (59:12–15).

Right at that bottom point of despair, the scene shifts upward to heaven. The LORD looks down, displeased that there is no justice anywhere; He sees that no one steps forward and "was amazed that there was no one to intercede." With humanity utterly helpless, God takes matters into His own hands: His own arm brings salvation, and His own righteousness sustains Him. He strides forth like a mighty Warrior strapping on full armor: "He put on righteousness like a breastplate, and the helmet of salvation on His head; He put on garments of vengeance and wrapped Himself in a cloak of zeal." He repays His adversaries according to their deeds near and far—even out to the distant coastlands—until nations from west to east revere His name as He sweeps in like a rushing river driven by the breath of the LORD (59:15–19).

Yet that storm of judgment does not end in destruction—it clears the way for a covenant of redemption. "'The Redeemer will come to Zion, to those in Jacob who turn from transgression,' declares the LORD." God seals that pledge with a lasting covenant: His Spirit resting on His chosen ones and His words placed in their mouth will never depart from their mouth, or from the mouths of their children, or from their descendants forevermore (59:20–21).

## Key Takeaways

**When prayers seem to bounce off the ceiling, never be quick to doubt God's power.** The opening verse sweeps aside humanity's two oldest suspicions: that God is too weak to rescue, or too deaf to listen (59:1). God did not erect the barrier—human sin laid every brick (59:2). Before accusing heaven of silence, we must inspect our own hands and lips.

**Sin never stays private; it rots an entire society.** "Hatching viper eggs and weaving spiderwebs" (59:5–6) exposes deceit's twin curse: deadly to anyone touched by it, yet too flimsy to shelter even its maker. Once lies and violence become normal, truth collapses right in the town square and honest people turn into easy targets (59:14–15).

**True repentance begins when we stop pointing fingers and dare to say "we."** The hinge of chapter 59 arrives when the speaker switches from "you" and "they" to "we" and "our" (59:9–13). Renewal begins in any community the moment nobody stands aloof condemning everyone else, and together people bow low admitting their own sins testify against them.

**When no human can save us, God steps into the breach Himself.** The chapter does not end with people fixing themselves up so God finally rewards them. Humanity is flat on its back without a single intercessor left (59:16). Salvation arrives purely through God's initiative: strapping on the breastplate of righteousness and helmet of salvation, stepping onto the battlefield Himself for His people (59:17–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul borrows chapter 59 to sketch the universal condition of humanity.** In Romans, demonstrating that Jew and Gentile alike stand under the power of sin, Paul quotes 59:7–8 almost verbatim: their feet are swift to shed blood, ruin and misery mark their ways, and the way of peace they have not known (Romans 3:15–17). Isaiah 59 is therefore not merely a diagnosis of one ancient Judean generation, but a mirror of the whole human race apart from God.

**God's own armor becomes the armor gifted to believers.** The LORD putting on righteousness as a breastplate and salvation as a helmet (59:17) directly inspires the armor of God in the New Testament. Paul urges believers to put on the breastplate of righteousness, the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit (Ephesians 6:14–17; 1 Thessalonians 5:8). Believers enter spiritual battle not wearing homemade virtue, but wearing the very armor God Himself wore in triumph.

**Groping at midday fulfills the ancient covenant warning.** Feeling along the wall like the blind and stumbling at noon (59:10) echoes directly the covenant curses of Moses: if Israel disobeys, they will grope at midday like a blind person in darkness with no one to rescue them (Deuteronomy 28:29). Meanwhile, declaring "the arm of the LORD is not too short" (59:1) repeats God's exact words to Moses in the wilderness and to the exiles earlier in Isaiah (Numbers 11:23; Isaiah 50:2). Across Scripture, one truth stays constant: God's strength never shrinks; human faithfulness falters.

**The Redeemer coming to Zion and hope for Israel.** Seeing no one standing to intercede (59:16; cf. Ezekiel 22:30), God's own arm brings deliverance and promises that "the Redeemer will come to Zion" (59:20). Paul quotes 59:20–21 at the climax of his reflection on Israel's future in Romans, declaring that the Deliverer will banish ungodliness from Jacob and establish His covenant removing their sins (Romans 11:26–27). In New Testament light, that Redeemer is Jesus Christ—both standing in the gap as Intercessor and conquering sin single-handedly.

**The covenant of Spirit and Word never pulls apart.** The closing verse (59:21) binds two supreme gifts together: God's Spirit and God's Word, abiding across generations. This connects back to the Spirit resting on the coming shoot of Jesse (11:2) and to Jeremiah's new covenant placing God's law within human hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). Salvation not only cancels past guilt, but creates a living community sustained continuously by both Spirit and Scripture.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 59:20 say "to Zion," while Paul quotes it as "from Zion"?** In the Hebrew text (followed by the BSB), 59:20 states that the Redeemer will come *to* (or *for*) Zion. The ancient Greek Septuagint reads *for the sake of Zion*, while Paul in Romans writes *from Zion* (Romans 11:26). Commentators generally understand Paul as gathering the wider sweep of Scripture—salvation both arrives *to* Zion and radiates outward *from* Zion to all Israel and every nation.

**God wearing "garments of vengeance" and "wrath" (59:17–18) is not selfish rage.** Modern readers often recoil at pictures of God dressing for vengeance. Place these lines directly beside the social breakdown in 59:14–15: a world where justice is kicked out of court and honest people are mugged with nobody defending them. Facing entrenched cruelty like that, cool indifference would be heartless. God's "vengeance" and "zeal" here express fierce, righteous love—determined to halt oppressors and vindicate the defenseless.

**Two well-known renderings of the "rushing river" in 59:19.** The BSB and most modern versions stay with the divine-warrior image: God Himself arrives like a pent-up torrent driven by the breath of the LORD. Older English versions (such as the KJV) split the clause differently: "When the enemy shall come in like a flood, the Spirit of the LORD shall lift up a standard against him." The divergence arises because one Hebrew word can mean either *pent-up/constricted* or *adversary*, and the following verb can mean *drive onward* or *raise a banner*. Either reading affirms the same core truth: unstoppable power belongs to the LORD.

**Who is the singular "you" addressed in 59:21?** Verse 59:20 speaks of "those in Jacob" in the plural, yet 59:21 switches abruptly to masculine singular ("upon you," "your mouth," "your descendants"). Three main views exist: the prophet representing the whole faithful community, Israel personified as one figure, or the anointed Servant who speaks directly at the start of chapter 61. Under every reading, the pledge guarantees that God's Word and Spirit will never vanish from His people.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and translator of the Latin Vulgate, wrote a full commentary on Isaiah and paid close attention to 59:20. Comparing the Hebrew text with the Greek wording Paul cited in Romans, he observed that the promise of the Redeemer coming to Zion for those turning from transgression is fulfilled in Christ, who never abandons Israel yet calls for genuine repentance. *(Commentary on Isaiah, chapter 59)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome compares the Hebrew and Pauline quotation of 59:20 in his Isaiah commentary; exact Latin text in Book 16 not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, regarded 59:21 as one of Scripture's premier statements uniting the Holy Spirit and the Word of God. God has joined His Spirit and His Word with an unbreakable bond, Calvin insisted: preaching stripped of the Spirit's work becomes dead letter, while claiming the Spirit's guidance apart from Scripture chases private delusions. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 59)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: uniting Spirit and Word on the basis of Isaiah 59:21 is one of Calvin's most famous and widely cited exegetical statements -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, lingered over the spiderweb image in 59:5–6. Human schemes and self-excuses resemble webs spun from our own innards—intricate to admire, yet tissue-thin and useless as garments against the cold or God's judgment. He also admired the prophet's humility in switching to "we" from 59:12 onward: true servants of God never stand aloof despising their people, but shoulder shared guilt in intercession before God. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 59)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: two classic points by Matthew Henry on spiderwebs and prophetic solidarity in chapter 59; exact lines on ccel.org not verified -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic compilation of Jewish law and commentary, linked the sequence from 59:15–16 to 59:20 in rabbinic reflections on when the Messiah will arrive. Ancient sages noted how truth vanishes and no intercessor remains right before the Redeemer comes to Zion, reading this as a reminder that history's darkest moral hour stands closest to God's deliverance. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin, section on the signs of the Messiah's coming)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Sanhedrin cites Isaiah 59:15–16 and 59:20 in its discussion of the messianic generation; exact folio 97a or 98a not verified -->

**John N. Oswalt**, an American Old Testament professor, views chapter 59 as the theological hinge of Isaiah's closing movement (chapters 56–66). Chapters 56–58 call God's people to justice and unmask hypocrisy, yet chapter 59 exposes the root impasse: left to ourselves, human beings simply cannot manufacture the righteousness God requires.
