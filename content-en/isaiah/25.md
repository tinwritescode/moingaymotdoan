# Isaiah 25 — The Banquet on God's Mountain and the Day Death Is Swallowed Up

## Story

Chapter 24 just closed with the LORD of Hosts enthroned on Mount Zion, shining in glory before His elders (24:23). Stepping into chapter 25, the atmosphere transforms completely: the crash of collapsing earth gives way to a song of thanksgiving rising from a trusting heart. The prophet addresses God with both intimacy and awe: "O LORD, You are my God!" He exalts and praises God's name because the marvelous things God has just done are no passing stroke of chance, but "plans formed long ago, with perfect faithfulness" (25:1).

The reason for this praise appears immediately. The arrogant city that once crushed the world has been turned by God into a mound of rubble: "You have made the city a heap of rubble, the fortified town a ruin." The foreign citadel is a city no more and will never be rebuilt. Witnessing such righteous power, even strong peoples and cities of ruthless nations that once spread terror turn to honor and revere the LORD (25:2–3).

It turns out God topples the strongholds of the proud precisely to shield the helpless who have no defender. The prophet praises Him as "a refuge for the poor, a stronghold for the needy in distress, a shelter from the storm, and a shade from the heat," especially when the blast of the ruthless lashes like a driving storm against a wall. Just as a sudden cloud drifting over parched ground cools blazing desert heat, God silences the swaggering roar of foreigners and stills the victory song of the ruthless (25:4–5).

The scene then opens into an image of warmth and lavish hospitality unlike anything seen before. Right on Mount Zion, where the LORD of Hosts reigns, He prepares for all peoples on earth "a feast of rich food, a banquet of aged wine—the best of meats and the finest of wines." This is no private banquet for one nation or an exclusive elite, but the richest, choicest table spread wide for every people to celebrate together (25:6).

Right at that table, God performs something greater than hosting a feast: He rips away the funeral shroud that has covered humanity from the beginning. On this very mountain, He destroys the veil shrouding all peoples and "the sheet that covers all nations." Then rings out the brightest promise in the chapter: "He will swallow up death forever. The Lord GOD will wipe away the tears from every face and remove the disgrace of His people from the whole earth." Every word is sealed with absolute certainty: for the LORD has spoken (25:7–8).

Answering that astonishing miracle comes the united cheer of God's people on the day of salvation: "Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him, and He has saved us." Long years of waiting through darkness and reproach have finally reached their goal; their hearts overflow with joy because the One they trusted has come at last to rescue them (25:9).

Chapter 25 ends with a jarring contrast. The LORD's hand rests peaceably upon Mount Zion to guard His people, yet Moab—the neighboring kingdom notorious for its arrogance—meets the exact opposite fate. Moab will be trampled right where it stands "as straw is trampled down in the water of a manure pile." It spreads its hands flailing like a swimmer thrashing in a foul pit, yet every clever stroke fails: the LORD brings down its pride, demolishing its towering fortified walls and flinging them down into the dust (25:10–12).

## Key Takeaways

**God pulls down arrogant power in order to shelter the weak.** In our world, solid fortresses belong to the mighty while the poor stand exposed to wind and sun. In chapter 25, God turns tyrannical strongholds into heaps of rubble while making Himself a fortress, storm shelter, and cooling shade for the needy (25:2; 25:4). God's judgment is never arbitrary; it walks hand in hand with compassion for the oppressed.

**God's salvation opens wide to all nations, never locked inside one border.** Although this song rises on Israel's Mount Zion, the invited guests at the great feast are "all peoples," and the funeral shroud is lifted off "all nations" (25:6–7). Even once-ruthless nations come one day to revere and glorify Him (25:3). Even in the Old Testament, God's dream stretched far beyond a single nation's frontiers.

**Death is not an eternal given; it is an enemy marked for destruction.** People grow accustomed to treating death and grief as inescapable fate. Yet Isaiah pictures death as a mourning veil draped over humanity and heralds the day God will "swallow up death forever" (25:7–8). God does not merely coach people to endure pain; He promises to eradicate pain at its root and personally wipe tears from every single face.

**Waiting on God takes patience, yet it is never wasted.** Twice inside one short verse, God's people echo their confession: "we have waited for Him" (25:9). Much of the life of faith consists of waiting through storms, heat, and disgrace. This chapter promises that overflowing joy belongs at last to those who wait steadfastly on the faithful God whose plans stood firm from long ago (25:1).

**Proud self-salvation collapses under God's hand.** The stark picture of Moab flailing its arms like a swimmer in a manure pit only to sink to the dust is a blunt warning (25:10–12). No matter how towering the walls or how clever the maneuvers, humanity cannot swim free of judgment while clinging stubbornly to pride.

## Deeper Meaning

**From the covenant meal on Mount Sinai to the Marriage Supper of the Lamb.** The lavish banquet of choice food and aged wine on Mount Zion (25:6) connects straight back to the seventy elders of Israel ascending Mount Sinai, beholding God, and eating and drinking in His presence (Exodus 24:9–11). Long ago only the elders of one nation climbed the mountain; now all peoples are welcomed to God's mountain, fulfilling the earlier vision of every nation streaming to the mountain of the LORD (2:2–3). In the New Testament, Jesus echoes this very table when announcing that many will come from east and west to recline at table in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 8:11), and tells the parable of a great banquet whose host sends servants into the streets to bring the poor and crippled until the house is full (Luke 14:15–24). In Revelation, history culminates in the blessing upon all invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9).

**"Swallow up death forever" and the resurrection of Christ.** Verse 25:8 is one of the brightest jewels of the Old Testament. Celebrating the resurrection of believers in 1 Corinthians, the apostle Paul quotes this verse as the climax of his triumph song: when the mortal puts on immortality, the Scripture will be fulfilled, "Death has been swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54–55). In ancient Near Eastern imagery, Death was imagined as an insatiable monster opening wide jaws to swallow mortals alive. Isaiah flips the picture upside down: the swallower of every living thing is itself swallowed forever by God.

**The hand wiping away tears at the close of Scripture.** The tender promise that "the Lord GOD will wipe away the tears from every face" (25:8) resounds word for word twice in Revelation. Before the throne, the Lamb shepherds a vast multitude from every nation and God wipes away every tear from their eyes (Revelation 7:17). Then, as the new heaven and new earth appear, He wipes every tear away and "there will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain" (Revelation 21:4). All the sorrow unleashed when the ground was cursed in Eden (Genesis 3:19) is finally brought to an end by the Creator's own hand.

**The shade and refuge of the poor.** Praising God as a stronghold for the needy and shade from scorching heat (25:4–5) joins the chorus of the Psalms calling the LORD a high refuge for the oppressed in times of trouble (Psalms 9:9; Psalms 14:6). Earlier in Isaiah itself, the sheltering cloud and canopy shielding Mount Zion from heat and storm (4:5–6) recalled the pillar of cloud and fire guarding Israel for forty years in the wilderness.

**Two mountains and two opposite destinies.** Across chapters 24–27, Isaiah sets two cities face to face: the ruined city of pride (24:10; 25:2; 25:12) opposite Mount Zion where the LORD reigns (24:23; 25:6–10). Moab—a neighbor Isaiah earlier rebuked sharply for insolence and empty boasts (16:6)—reappears at the close of chapter 25 not merely as a small kingdom east of the Jordan, but as a concrete emblem of stubborn pride refusing God's banquet of grace.

## Notes & Nuances

**The picture of Moab trampled in a manure pit (25:10–12) feels starkly coarse and jarring.** Right beside a vision of sweeping grace welcoming "all peoples" (25:6–8), three closing verses showing Moab trodden "as straw is trampled down in the water of a manure pile" and flailing inside filth strike modern readers hard. We should face that bluntness honestly rather than glossing over it. In antiquity, people stomped straw into dung to make fuel or mudbricks; the image deliberately applies maximum humiliation to deflate Moab's infamous pride (16:6). Commentators offer two main ways of understanding this abrupt shift: many see Moab named as a symbol for every power stubbornly clinging to arrogance and refusing God's salvation, standing opposite the humbled nations feasting on Zion; some historical scholars view it as an independent oracle against Moab from a concrete conflict, later placed at the end of chapter 25 to illustrate the fall of the proud city announced in verse 2.

**What is "the sheet that covers all nations" (25:7)?** The Hebrew uses two words meaning a "covering" and a "woven sheet" or "shroud," which older translations often rendered as "veil," leading many early interpreters to picture a blindfold of spiritual ignorance lifted from Gentile eyes. Read right next to 25:8 ("He will swallow up death forever"), however, most modern scholars understand it as the burial shroud or mourning cloth draped over the dead and bereaved. Because all humanity lives beneath death's shadow, God removes the universal mourning cloth by annihilating death itself.

**Which city is demolished in 25:2?** Just like "the city of chaos" in chapter 24 (24:10), verse 25:2 leaves the city nameless. Readers across history have proposed Babylon, Nineveh, Susa, or a Moabite fortress. Keeping its name hidden signals that the passage reaches far beyond one past military victory to declare the final collapse of every human empire exalting itself beside God.

**The mention of "aged wine" does not promote drunkenness.** Readers occasionally wonder why Scripture praises a "banquet of aged wine" on God's mountain (25:6) while condemning drunkenness elsewhere. In ancient Mediterranean culture, wine aged on the lees and carefully strained represented the finest festive drink reserved for major celebrations and weddings, symbolizing abundance, joy, and unstinting blessing. Here it pictures God's lavish generosity on the day of redemption, directly opposite the bitter, failing wine of the ruined city in the previous chapter (24:9).

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Latin Bible translator, reads the Mount Zion banquet in verses 6–8 spiritually as foreshadowing the gospel and the Church of Christ, where all peoples are nourished by God's word and sacraments. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, focuses closely on verse 1's confession that God's wondrous deeds are "plans formed long ago, with perfect faithfulness." Amid exile or affliction, God's people easily fear He has forgotten His promises, so the prophet reminds them that God's purpose never shifts with earthly storms. Turning to verses 6–8, Calvin reads the banquet of rich food and fine wine as the abundance of spiritual blessings in Christ's kingdom extended to Gentiles, linking "swallow up death forever" directly to Christ's triumph cited by Paul in 1 Corinthians. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's exposition of God's unchanging counsel in verse 1 and the gospel banquet and defeat of death in verses 6–8; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses warmly over verse 4, noting that God is both "a shelter from the storm" and "a shade from the heat": He shields His people alike from sudden violent persecution and from the grinding glare of daily trial. On verse 8, Henry compares God wiping tears from every face to a tender father soothing a weeping child—not merely drying tears, but erasing the disgrace His people endured in the world. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's pastoral reflections on verses 4 and 8; not checked against ccel.org -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, highlights the tight structural unity of chapter 25 anchored in the threefold refrain "on this mountain" (verses 6, 7, and 10), positioning Mount Zion as the cosmos's true center opposite humanity's crumbled citadels. He explains "the sheet that covers all nations" in verse 7 as death's burial shroud enveloping all humanity since the Fall—proving God resolves humanity's deepest plight, not merely temporary geopolitics. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's structural analysis and burial-shroud reading for chapter 25; exact page numbers not verified -->

Wright**, British New Testament scholar and former Anglican bishop, ranks Isaiah 25:6–8 among the pivotal Old Testament texts unveiling the hope that God will finally vanquish death itself rather than letting death have the last word. Rabbi Eleazar teaches that in the world to come, the Holy One will arrange a circle dance for the righteous in Eden with Himself sitting right in the center, and each person will point straight at Him echoing Isaiah's words: "Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him, and He has saved us."
