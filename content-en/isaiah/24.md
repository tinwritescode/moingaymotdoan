# Isaiah 24 — The Whole Earth Shakes Before the Day the LORD Reigns

## Story

Having finished the cycle of ten oracles judging individual nations one by one from Babylon to Tyre (13:1; 23:18), chapter 24 abruptly pulls the camera far back to take in the entire globe. Naming no specific kingdom or port anymore, the prophet announces an upheaval engulfing all humanity: "Behold, the LORD lays waste to the earth and leaves it in ruins; He will twist its surface and scatter its inhabitants." When His hand drops, every social ladder mortals have built flattens out instantly. Priest fares no differently from layperson, master like servant, mistress like maid, buyer like seller, lender like borrower, creditor like debtor—everyone stands on the exact same footing before the LORD's decisive word (24:1–3).

Withering decay spreads everywhere. The earth mourns, the world languishes, and even the loftiest people on earth fade away. The root cause is no freak accident of nature, but human conduct itself: "The earth is defiled by its people; they have transgressed the laws, violated the statutes, and broken the everlasting covenant." Under piled-up guilt, a curse consumes the earth like fire through dry grass, scorching its inhabitants until only a few survive (24:4–6).

Next, every everyday sound of joy falls dead silent. New wine dries up, vines wilt, and all the merry-hearted groan. The cheerful beat of tambourines stops, the noise of revelers dies out, and the glad lyre falls mute. No one sings over wine anymore; even seasoned drinkers find strong drink turning bitter on their tongues. Inside this bleak landscape stands an unnamed city: "The city of chaos is shattered." Every house is barred shut so none can enter; crowds cry in the streets for wine; city gates lie smashed to pieces; and all gladness across the land sinks into gloom (24:7–12).

Yet right amid ruin that looks total, a thin ray of light breaks through. Across the nations of the earth a tiny remnant remains, just like olives left after the tree is beaten or stray clusters left after grape harvest. Those very survivors raise their voices from the west, shouting in awe at the majesty of the LORD. Calls of praise ripple from the east out across the distant islands of the sea, exalting the name of the LORD, the God of Israel (24:13–15).

From the farthest ends of the earth drifts a song: "Glory to the Righteous One!" Yet even as distant lands sing, Isaiah himself cannot join the chorus. Gazing at the rampant treachery right in front of his eyes, he cries out in anguish: "I waste away, I waste away! Woe is me!" All around him, traitors keep piling treachery upon treachery. Warning of judgment therefore closes back in on earth's inhabitants like three traps laid end to end: "Terror and pit and snare." Whoever flees the sound of terror plunges straight into the pit; whoever scrambles out of the pit gets snared in the net. Nowhere on earth stays safe, because the floodgates of heaven open wide and the foundations of the earth quake (24:16–18).

The whole planet splits apart. Pounding, repetitive Hebrew verbs echo the rumble of collapsing rock: the earth is utterly broken, torn apart, violently shaken. Solid ground—humanity's ultimate symbol of stability—staggers like a drunkard and "sways like a hut" in a hurricane. The sheer weight of human rebellion presses down until it crashes and cannot rise again (24:19–20).

Then the chapter enters its solemn climax with the classic prophetic signpost of the end: "In that day." Judgment reaches not only earth but the heavens above: the LORD will punish both "the host of heaven above" and "the kings of the earth below." All those haughty powers will be herded together like prisoners locked in a dungeon, held for many days before final sentencing. Closing chapter 24 is a dazzling scene inverting all the opening gloom: "The moon will be confounded and the sun will be ashamed," utterly outshone when the LORD of Hosts takes His throne on Mount Zion in Jerusalem, displaying His glory "before His elders" (24:21–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Before God's judgment, every distinction of status disappears.** Verse 24:2 pairs six opposites spanning religion, household, commerce, and finance. Neither priestly office, social prestige, nor a creditor's vault buys immunity when the LORD judges. Before their Creator, all human beings stand on level ground in moral accountability.

**Human sin wounds the earth itself.** This chapter never divorces human ethics from the physical world. When humanity breaks God's law and violates His covenant, the earth itself becomes "defiled," dries up, and reels beneath the weight of guilt (24:5; 24:20). How we live toward God and one another leaves real scars across creation.

**Joy built apart from God eventually turns bitter.** Tambourines, harps, fine wine, and lively feasts inside "the city of chaos" all have an expiration date (24:7–12). When humanity constructs a self-contained civilization that forgets the Giver of life, the very pleasures that intoxicated them turn sour when the storm hits.

**God's people hold distant hope while aching over present evil.** Verse 24:16 captures an achingly real human tension: songs of praise already ring from earth's corners, yet Isaiah cries in pain because deceit still rages around him. Believing in God's ultimate victory never means numbing ourselves to today's suffering and injustice.

**Beyond the fiercest shaking rises God's glorious reign.** Judgment in chapter 24 does not end history in pointless ashes. Earth is overturned to clear away rebellious powers in heaven and on earth, paving the way for the LORD of Hosts to reign righteously from Mount Zion (24:21–23).

## Deeper Meaning

**Un-creation and a second Flood.** Chapter 24's sharpest terms echo the opening chapters of Genesis. Verse 24:1 says the earth turns "waste" and ruined, and 24:10 calls the shattered metropolis "the city of chaos"—using the exact Hebrew word (*tohu*) that described the "formless and void" earth before God brought order (Genesis 1:2). In 24:18, the floodgates of heaven opening repeats Noah's Flood when "the floodgates of the heavens were opened" (Genesis 7:11), while "the everlasting covenant" in 24:5 recalls God's post-Flood covenant with Noah and every living creature (Genesis 9:16). When humanity smashes the Creator's order, creation threatens to slip backward into chaos.

**All creation groaning under human guilt.** Moses' Law warned that innocent bloodshed and wickedness pollute the very land people inhabit (Numbers 35:33). Chapter 24 stretches that principle across the entire planet: earth mourns, vines droop, foundations shake under heavy transgression (24:4–7; 24:20). Centuries later, the apostle Paul named that exact reality, writing that "the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until the present time," eagerly awaiting liberation alongside God's children (Romans 8:19–22).

**Spiritual powers behind earthly empires.** Verses 24:21–22 pull back a veil deeper than ordinary geopolitics: alongside "the kings of the earth below," God rounds up "the host of heaven above" and locks them in a pit awaiting final judgment. The New Testament carries this imagery forward, speaking of sinning angels held in gloomy chains awaiting judgment (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 1:6), and Revelation depicts Satan bound and thrown into the abyss for a long span before his ultimate sentence (Revelation 20:1–3).

**Glory from Mount Sinai to Mount Zion and the New Jerusalem.** The chapter's final line shows the LORD reigning on Mount Zion with radiant glory "before His elders" (24:23). Old Testament readers immediately recall Moses and seventy elders of Israel climbing Mount Sinai, beholding God standing above a pavement like clear sapphire, and eating and drinking in His presence (Exodus 24:9–11)—an image preparing straight for the great mountain banquet of chapter 25. Meanwhile, sun and moon blushing before God's splendor echoes later in Isaiah (60:19) and reaches perfection on Scripture's final pages, where the New Jerusalem needs neither sun nor moon because God's glory gives it light and the Lamb is its lamp (Revelation 21:23).

**Threads linking wider biblical warnings.** A few olives left on a beaten tree in 24:13 repeats Isaiah's earlier picture of Israel's remnant (17:6), now widened to survivors among all nations. The stark proverb of "terror and pit and snare" in 24:17–18 is later borrowed by Jeremiah for Moab's judgment (Jeremiah 48:43–44). And every stratum of society from monarchs to servants confronting divine wrath in 24:2 and 24:21 resurfaces vividly in Revelation, where kings, magnates, and slaves hide in caves before the great day of the One on the throne and of the Lamb (Revelation 6:15–17).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 24 launches chapters 24–27, often called "Isaiah's Little Apocalypse."** Unlike chapters 13–23 naming individual nations—Babylon, Assyria, Egypt, Tyre—chapters 24–27 speak on a cosmic, end-time canvas: the whole planet quakes, death is swallowed up, the dead rise, and the sea monster is slain. Because of this strongly eschatological tone, many modern scholars think this section was composed later than eighth-century Isaiah, perhaps during or after exile. Conversely, conservative interpreters view Isaiah himself as its author, summing up his national oracles by looking right through individual historical empires to God's final universal judgment. Whatever one's dating view, positioning chapters 24–27 immediately behind chapters 13–23 creates a tightly woven theological progression.

**What do "the earth" and "the city of chaos" refer to?** In Hebrew, *eretz* can mean either a specific country (like Judah) or the entire planet. In chapter 24, the global sense clearly dominates, paired with "the world" (24:4), "the peoples" (24:13), "the ends of the earth" (24:16), and "the kings of the earth" (24:21). Likewise, "the city of chaos" (24:10) stays deliberately nameless. Across history, readers might picture Babylon, Nineveh, Tyre, or even unfaithful Jerusalem; leaving it unnamed transforms it into an archetype for every arrogant metropolis humanity constructs in defiance of God's city.

**Which covenant is "the everlasting covenant" in 24:5?** Two leading explanations stand out. Many commentators point to God's covenant with Noah after the Flood, explicitly named "the everlasting covenant" with all flesh on earth (Genesis 9:16), encompassing the sanctity of human life. This neatly explains why all humanity—not Israel alone—stands guilty of breaking it. Others take it more narrowly as the Sinai covenant with Israel, or broadly as the moral order built by the Creator into creation itself.

**Who are "the host of heaven above" in 24:21?** Most contemporary commentators understand these as rebellious spiritual powers operating behind earthly empires, simultaneously dethroning the celestial deities and star-gods pagan nations worshiped. That dovetails neatly with 24:23 right afterward, where moon and sun shrink ashamed before the LORD's splendor. Older interpreters sometimes read the phrase purely poetically for exalted earthly rulers, reinforcing "the kings of the earth" right beside it.

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, lingers over 24:2 to remind readers that neither sacred office nor high status shields anyone who deserts God; priests face the exact same judgment as commoners. Explaining the prophet's sudden cry "I waste away" in 24:16 while distant lands sing, Calvin remarks that faithful believers—even while wholly confident of God's future glory—cannot help grieving deeply when deceit and ruin still grip their homeland. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's exposition of equal accountability in verse 2 and prophetic sorrow in verse 16; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, highlights the six paired social classes in 24:2 as a sobering lesson on how fleeting earthly hierarchies really are: when God acts, rich and poor, master and servant stand equally exposed. He also draws profound comfort from the closing verse: even if sun and moon—creation's brightest lamps—grow dim, God's people never fall into darkness because the LORD of Hosts reigns and radiates His glory among them. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's pastoral observations on verses 2 and 23; not verified against the original -->

**Brevard Childs**, American Old Testament scholar, underlines the hinge function of chapters 24–27 within Isaiah's overall architecture. Right after chapters 13–23 judge historical empires one by one, chapter 24 refuses to break the thread, instead lifting the whole drama to cosmic scale: the nations' guilt is not merely political misconduct, but a rupture of creation's order and the Creator's everlasting covenant, requiring global purification before God establishes His reign on Mount Zion. *(Isaiah, Old Testament Library, 2001)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Childs's canonical reading of chapters 24–27 transitioning from chapters 13–23; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, traces the running contrast across chapters 24–27 between two cities: the nameless "city of chaos"—representing human civilization built apart from God—and Mount Zion where the LORD reigns. He also links God's glory shining "before His elders" in 24:23 directly back to the covenant meal shared by Moses and the seventy elders atop Mount Sinai in Exodus, signaling God's imminent restoration of intimate fellowship with His people. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's two-cities contrast and link between verse 23 and Exodus 24; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Robert Alter**, American Jewish literary scholar and translator of the Hebrew Bible, spotlights the fierce poetic craft of the original Hebrew in this chapter.
