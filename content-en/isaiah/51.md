# Isaiah 51 — Listen to Me and Awake

## Story

At the close of chapter 50, God called those who fear Him to trust Him in the dark rather than light homemade torches. In chapter 51, He speaks straight to that faithful remnant through three consecutive appeals, each opening with an urgent call to listen. First, He addresses "you who pursue righteousness, you who seek the LORD" and tells them to look back to their roots: "Look to the rock from which you were cut, and to the quarry from which you were hewn!" That rock and quarry are Abraham and Sarah. When God called Abraham, he was just one childless old man with a barren wife—yet God blessed him and multiplied him into an entire nation. That very same God now comforts Zion and all her ruins: He will turn her wilderness into "Eden" and her desert into "the garden of the LORD," ringing with "joy and gladness, thanksgiving and the sound of singing" (51:1–3).

The second call widens the horizon across the whole world. God summons His people to pay attention: His law will go out from Him, and He will set His justice "as a light to the nations." His righteousness and salvation are drawing near; His arm will bring justice to the peoples, until distant coasts wait expectantly: "The islands will wait for Me and put their hope in My arm." To show the exiles how unshakable that promise is, God tells them to gaze up at the sky and down at the earth—the two most permanent things imaginable. Even so, "the heavens will vanish like smoke, the earth will wear out like a garment, and its people will die like gnats," whereas "My salvation will last forever, and My righteousness will never fail" (51:4–6).

The third appeal speaks to an everyday dread of living as exiles among foreign empires: human scorn. Addressing those who know what is right and hold "My law in your hearts," God tells them not to fear human reproach or tremble at their insults. Those mockers talk big today, yet they will crumble swiftly like cloth eaten through: "the moth will devour them like a garment, and the worm will eat them like wool." Meanwhile, God's righteousness and salvation endure through every generation (51:7–8).

Hearing such magnificent promises while captivity still drags on, a passionate cry erupts from the people—or the prophet—aimed straight at God: "Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD!" The plea recalls the mighty wonders of Israel's birth: was it not His arm that "cut Rahab to pieces and pierced through the dragon," dried up the deep sea, and "made a road in the depths of the sea for the redeemed to cross over" during the Exodus? Right inside that prayer, hope blazes into a vision of homecoming: "So the redeemed of the LORD will return and enter Zion with singing, crowned with everlasting joy," while "sorrow and sighing will flee away" (51:9–11).

The LORD answers immediately, going straight to the root of their panic: "I, even I, am He who comforts you." He presses them sharply: who are you to "fear mortal man, or a son of man who withers like grass," while forgetting the LORD your Maker who "stretched out the heavens and laid the foundations of the earth"? All day long they cower in terror before the oppressor's rage poised to destroy—yet now God asks with quiet defiance, "Where is the fury of the oppressor?" Then comes crisp good news: the cowering prisoner in chains will soon be released, "neither will he die in the dungeon, nor will he lack bread." For the One standing beside them is the LORD of Hosts, who stirs up the roaring sea. He recalls the close covenant bond resting on His people and His Servant—"I have put My words in your mouth and covered you in the shadow of My hand"—in order to plant the heavens, lay earth's foundations, and declare to Zion, "You are My people!" (51:12–16).

At that point, the cry to wake up flips completely around. Moments earlier, the people begged God's arm to awake; now God Himself calls to the fallen city: "Awake, awake! Rise up, O Jerusalem!" God looks squarely at everything she endured without minimizing a single drop. Jerusalem drank from the LORD's hand "the cup of His fury," draining to the very dregs the goblet that makes people stagger and collapse. Out of all the sons she bore and raised, not a single one was left strong enough to guide her or take her by the hand. Double disaster crashed over her—"ruin and destruction, famine and sword"—leaving no one able to comfort her. Her children lay fainting at the head of every street, trapped helplessly "like an antelope in a net," overwhelmed by the wrath and rebuke of God (51:17–20).

Yet that nightmare has reached its end. Calling Jerusalem "you afflicted one, drunk, yet not with wine"—reeling not from revelry but from an unbearable cup of suffering—God steps forward as the One who "defends His people" and announces He has taken that staggering cup right out of her hand: "never again will you drink it." Not only does Zion's punishment end; God hands that very cup of reeling over to her tormentors—the brutal conquerors who once barked at her, "Bow down so we may walk over you," forcing her to flatten her back to the ground like a street under their boots (51:21–23).

## Key Takeaways

**God's past miracles anchor our faith in the present.** Looking at their shrunken numbers and ruined city, exiles naturally felt nothing could ever grow again. God offers no abstract theory—He points straight back to Abraham and Sarah (51:1–2). If God built an entire nation out of one aging, childless couple, He can easily turn Zion's wasteland back into Eden (51:3).

**Neither sky nor earth nor empire lasts forever—only God's salvation does.** Babylon looked towering, scoffers sounded biting, and heaven and earth felt immovable. Yet God sets everything beside eternity: skies dissipate like smoke, earth frays like old cloth, and mockers get chewed away by moths (51:6–8). Clinging to God's righteousness and saving promise means holding the one thing that never decays.

**Fearing people is a symptom of forgetting how vast God is.** God diagnoses the real source of His people's chronic dread: they magnify fragile mortals who wither like grass while letting the Creator of sky and earth fade from view (51:12–13). The cure for human fear is not pretending enemies are harmless, but remembering who our Maker is.

**God's arm is not asleep—His people need to wake up from despair.** The exiles begged God's arm to wake up as in the days of the Exodus (51:9–10). God answers by telling Jerusalem herself to get up off the ground (51:17). Grace does not leave believers slumped in resignation; once God takes the cup of wrath out of our hands, He calls us to rise and walk into deliverance.

**God's discipline has a boundary, while His mercy endures.** Jerusalem truly drank the bitter consequences of sin through the cup of fury (51:17–20). Yet God still calls Himself the Defender of His people, snatches that cup away, and reverses the humiliation of the crushed (51:22–23). However long suffering lasts, it is never God's final word over His own.

## Deeper Meaning

**From one aging man to a vast multitude.** God's invitation to look back at Abraham and Sarah when he was "but one" (51:2) links straight to the covenant's beginning in Genesis, when God promised to make Abram a great nation and Sarah a mother of nations even in extreme old age (Genesis 12:1–2; 17:15–17). Centuries later, Hebrews looks back to that very miracle to celebrate God's power to bring life out of what seemed dead: from one man as good as dead came descendants as countless as the stars and seashore sand (Hebrews 11:11–12).

**Heavens wearing out like a garment and God's law in the heart.** The image of sky and earth growing old "like a garment" while God's salvation stands forever (51:6) echoes Psalms 102, which Hebrews applies directly to Jesus Christ to affirm Him as the unchanging Creator (Psalms 102:25–27; Hebrews 1:10–12). At the same time, addressing those who hold "My law in your hearts" (51:7) resonates with Jeremiah's promise of the new covenant, in which God writes His law directly upon His people's hearts rather than stone tablets (Jeremiah 31:33).

**A new Exodus and the day tears vanish.** The cry recalling God carving a road through the sea for the redeemed (51:9–10) binds the memory of crossing the Red Sea (Exodus 14:21–22) directly to the return from Babylon. Verse 51:11—the redeemed entering Zion with singing—repeats Isaiah 35:10 almost word for word, uniting both halves of Isaiah in a single melody of hope. Looking further still, the promise that "sorrow and sighing will flee away" points ahead to the New Jerusalem at Scripture's end, where God wipes away every tear and mourning and pain are gone forever (Revelation 21:4).

**The cup of wrath taken from God's people.** The "cup of wrath" making nations stagger is a recurring prophetic symbol, prominent in Jeremiah and later in Revelation (Jeremiah 25:15–17; Revelation 14:10). Read from the New Testament, God's pledge to lift that cup out of His people's hand (51:22) gains breathtaking depth beside Jesus' prayer in Gethsemane: "My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me" (Matthew 26:39). So that God's people would never have to drain the dregs of wrath again, the Savior—about to appear in chapter 53—drank that cup to the bottom in their place.

**Human fear under the light of Jesus' teaching.** God's question asking why His people "fear mortal man" (51:12–13) echoes a thread stretching through all of Scripture—from the opening comfort of Isaiah 40 that all flesh is fleeting grass (40:6–8) to Jesus' charge before sending out His disciples: "Do not be afraid of those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Instead, fear the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**"Rahab" in 51:9 is not Rahab of Jericho.** Though spelled the same in English, in Hebrew they are two completely different words. *Rahab* here means "the proud one" or "sea monster of chaos" in ancient Near Eastern poetry, and the Bible frequently uses it as a poetic nickname for arrogant Egypt shattered by God (Isaiah 30:7; Job 26:12; Psalms 89:9–10). Pairing the slaying of Rahab with drying up the sea (51:9–10) fuses God's two greatest triumphs: subduing primeval chaos at creation and defeating Egypt at the Red Sea (cf. Psalms 74:13–14).

**The "cup of staggering" in 51:17 and 51:22.** The Hebrew phrase behind "the cup that makes men stagger" (*kos hattar'elah*, rendered "cup of trembling" or "cup of reeling" in various versions) evokes dizziness, stumbling, and collapsing like someone completely intoxicated. That is why 51:21 immediately explains that Jerusalem is "drunk, yet not with wine": she has reeled and fallen beneath an overwhelming weight of judgment.

**Who is addressed in 51:16?** Verse 51:16—"I have put My words in your mouth and covered you in the shadow of My hand"—uses masculine singular pronouns in Hebrew, shifting away from feminine Jerusalem used across most of the chapter and closely matching the portrait of the Servant in chapter 49 (49:2). Many interpreters understand God to be addressing the Servant or the prophet entrusted with proclaiming Zion's renewal; others take it as addressing all Israel in its vocation as bearer of God's word. Under either reading, the verse underscores that Zion's rebuilding happens through God's own word placed on the lips of His chosen representative.

**Does handing the cup of wrath to oppressors (51:23) promote personal vengeance?** Invaders forcing conquered captives to lie facedown as a human roadway reflects brutal real-world humiliation in ancient warfare. Notice carefully that Jerusalem never takes up a sword for payback; God Himself acts as righteous Judge calling imperial cruelty to account. The passage places justice firmly in God's hands rather than fueling personal hatred.

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, observed that God points back to Abraham at the opening of chapter 51 to cure His people's fixation on grim outward appearances: when Zion looks like hopeless ashes, remembering how God raised an entire nation from one childless old man trains faith to look at God's power rather than visible statistics. **Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, drew a twin lesson from looking back to "the rock" and "the quarry" in 51:1: remembering humble beginnings keeps us modest in prosperity and lifts faith in adversity, since the One who brought us to life from barrenness can surely revive us again. On 51:12–13, he noted crisply that the sole remedy for the fear of man is keeping the living God steadily in view. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 51)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: hallmark pastoral insights by Matthew Henry on verse 1 and verses 12–13; exact wording on ccel.org not verified -->

**Alec Motyer**, a British Old Testament scholar, highlighted the crisp symmetrical design of chapter 51: three summonses to "listen to Me" (verses 1, 4, and 7) pave the way for the dramatic twin cries of "awake, awake." First the people beg God's arm to wake up (verse 9), and God responds by calling Jerusalem herself to wake up (verse 17, echoed again at the start of chapter 52)—proving that God's saving power was never asleep; human hearts were drowsing in despair. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Motyer's structural observation on the three listen calls and the reversal of awake between verses 9 and 17; specific page not verified -->
