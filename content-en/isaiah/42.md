# Isaiah 42 — The Gentle Servant and the Blind Servant

## Story

Chapter 41 just closed its courtroom trial with a stark verdict dismissing molten images as empty wind and chaos (41:29). Entering chapter 42, the LORD presents His own answer through the first of Isaiah's four celebrated Servant Songs: "Here is My Servant, whom I uphold, My Chosen One, in whom My soul delights." God places His Spirit upon Him so He will bring forth justice to the nations. Completely unlike noisy, self-promoting earthly emperors, this Servant neither shouts nor raises His voice in the streets. Toward the frailest, most battered human lives, "A bruised reed He will not break and a smoldering wick He will not extinguish." Patiently and faithfully He establishes justice across the earth without faltering or growing discouraged, until distant coastlands wait eagerly for His teaching (42:1–4).

Next, the LORD—solemnly introduced as the Creator who stretched out the heavens, spread out the earth, and gives breath and spirit to all who walk on it—speaks straight to His Servant. He has called Him in righteousness, takes Him firmly by the hand, keeps Him, and appoints Him "as a covenant for the people and a light for the nations," commissioned to open blind eyes and lead captives out of pitch-black dungeons. God then proclaims His personal name and exclusive majesty: "I am the LORD; that is My name! I will not yield My glory to another or My praise to idols." Former predictions have already come true; now He announces brand-new things before they even spring up (42:5–9).

Hearing of these new deeds, the prophecy immediately summons the entire globe to burst into "a new song" praising the LORD—sailors and sea creatures across the oceans, distant islands and their peoples, wilderness camps, nomadic Kedar villages in the desert, and cliff-dwelling folk of Sela shouting glory to God from mountain peaks (42:10–12).

Why must all creation sing a new song? Because the LORD Himself is stepping onto the field—pictured through two bold metaphors placed cheek by jowl. First, He marches forth "like a mighty man," stirring His zeal "like a warrior" roaring a battle cry against His foes. Yet in the very next verse, His voice shifts into a mother in labor: after holding His peace, keeping quiet, and restraining Himself for a long time, now He cries out, gasps, and pants "like a woman in labor" to bring forth a new beginning for His people. He dries up blocking mountains, rivers, and pools, then gently leads blind travelers along unfamiliar paths they never knew, turning darkness ahead into daylight and rough tracks smooth, vowing "I will not forsake them." Conversely, those trusting carved idols and hailing them as gods will retreat in utter shame (42:13–17).

Suddenly at verse 18, the mood pivots sharply into heartbreak. God calls aloud: "Listen, you deaf; look, you blind, that you may see!" Then He asks a question that stops readers cold: "Who is blind but My servant, or deaf like the messenger I am sending?" Tragically, historical Israel—the very nation chosen as God's servant and messenger—stands blinder and deafer than anyone. They witnessed countless wonders yet retained nothing; their ears stayed wide open yet heard nothing at all (42:18–20).

For His righteousness' sake, the LORD gladly made His law great and glorious for His people. Yet now this people has become plundered and looted, trapped in pits and locked in prisons—easy prey with nobody stepping forward to shout "Return it!" Isaiah asks whether anyone among them will finally listen and lay this lesson to heart for the future. Who handed Jacob over to looters? Was it not the LORD Himself, against whom "we have sinned," whose ways they refused to walk and whose law they would not obey? That is why He poured out His burning wrath and the fury of battle upon them; flames blazed all around them, yet they never understood; fire scorched them closely, yet they still failed to take it to heart (42:21–25).

## Key Takeaways

**God's true strength never blusters and never crushes the fragile.** Earthly empires enforce order with shouting legions and iron fists. God's Servant establishes justice quietly and tenderly: He refuses to snap a bruised reed or pinch out a dimly smoking wick (42:1–4). Steadfast against evil yet gentle with the broken—that is the unmistakable hallmark of God's work.

**The calling of God's people reaches out to all humanity.** God does not elect His Servant to hoard privilege inside one ethnicity, but to serve as "a light for the nations," opening blind eyes and freeing captives from darkness (42:6–7). Precisely because redemption spills beyond Israel's borders, seas, islands, and distant desert tribes alike are invited into the new song (42:10–12).

**God's long silence is never indifference.** Facing exiles frustrated that God waited so long, verse 42:14 reveals He held back in patient restraint until the right hour, then stepped forward combining a warrior's resolve with a mother's birthing labor (42:13–16). When He acts, He even takes blind wanderers by the hand through darkness.

**The gravest tragedy for God's people is losing sight and hearing.** The chapter's close holds up an unsparing mirror to Israel: entrusted with magnificent instruction and called as messengers to the nations, they grew blind and deaf to both God's grace and His discipline (42:18–25). Naming the real cause behind ruin is the necessary first step to stopping excuses and returning to God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Matthew beholds the exact portrait of Jesus in 42:1–4.** When Pharisees plotted to kill Jesus over Sabbath healings, He quietly withdrew, kept healing crowds, and asked them not to make a spectacle of Him. Matthew quotes all of Isaiah 42:1–4—the longest Old Testament citation in his Gospel—showing Jesus is this very Servant: neither quarreling in the streets, breaking a bruised reed, nor snuffing a smoldering wick until He leads justice to victory and Gentiles hope in His name (Matthew 12:18–21). Earlier, at Jesus' baptism, heaven's voice—"This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased"—echoed 42:1 directly (Matthew 3:17).

**"A light for the nations" and liberation across the New Testament.** Holding infant Jesus in the temple, aged Simeon praised God for seeing salvation prepared as "a light for revelation to the Gentiles" (Luke 2:30–32). Launching His ministry in Nazareth, Jesus declared the Lord's Spirit rested on Him to proclaim good news to the poor (Luke 4:18). Later, the apostle Paul claimed "a light for the Gentiles" (42:6; 49:6) as his mandate carrying the gospel across Rome's empire (Acts 13:47), commissioned "to open their eyes, so that they may turn from darkness to light" (Acts 26:18).

**The Breath-Giver inaugurating new creation.** Calling God the One who spreads out earth and "gives breath to its people" in 42:5 recalls the LORD shaping humanity from dust and breathing life into their nostrils at creation (Genesis 2:7). God is doing far more than reshuffling regimes to free exiles from Babylon; His work through the Servant—bound to a new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31)—ushers in new creation itself.

**The new song stretching from Psalms to Revelation.** Summoning all earth to "sing to the LORD a new song" (42:10) echoes the Psalms celebrating God's marvelous salvation (Psalms 96:1). That chorus peaks in Revelation as heaven sings a new song honoring the Lamb whose blood purchased people from every tribe, tongue, people, and nation for God (Revelation 5:9).

**The blind, deaf servant linking straight back to chapter 6.** The piercing question "Who is blind but My servant?" (42:19–20) echoes the sorrowful verdict delivered at Isaiah's call: hearing without understanding, looking without perceiving (6:9–10). Chapter 42's wonder is that such blindness is not the end of the story: the faithful Servant at the chapter's opening comes precisely "to open the eyes of the blind" (42:7), and the LORD personally pledges to lead the blind out of darkness (42:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does one single chapter contain two completely opposite portraits of "My servant"?** In verses 1–7, the servant delights God, bears His Spirit, opens blind eyes, and shines as light to the nations. By verses 18–20, the servant is blind, deaf, and disobedient. Jewish tradition generally sees Israel in both halves: the opening presents Israel's noble ideal and calling, while the close exposes its fallen reality (some sages also identify the opening figure with the faithful remnant or the prophet himself). Christian tradition—following Matthew's Gospel (Matthew 12:18–21)—discerns an intentional tension woven into Isaiah itself: precisely because historical Israel failed in its servant calling, God raises up one uniquely faithful Servant—the Messiah—to fulfill Israel's vocation as covenant and light to the nations on their behalf.

**God pictured "like a woman in labor" (verse 14) right beside "like a warrior" (verse 13).** Readers used solely to masculine imagery for God are often startled seeing the LORD compared to a woman crying and gasping in childbirth. Scripture never boxes God inside human gender; pairing the fiercest male and female images of exertion captures God's full passion to rescue—fighting relentlessly to shatter chains while enduring labor pains to birth new life for His people.

**Admitting God poured wrath and war onto Israel (verses 24–25) feels hard to hear.** Modern readers often wince hearing God handed His own people to plunderers and scorched them with battle flames. Two truths keep the passage clear. First, the prophets insist on this to refute the despairing lie that Babylon's gods outpowered the LORD: exile did not catch God off guard, but unfolded as covenant consequence after generations of rebellion. Second, the prophet never stands coldly aloof—he includes himself in "we have sinned" (42:24), and the very first verse of chapter 43 immediately promises God will walk straight through fire beside His people (43:1–2).

**"Justice" and "law" in verses 1–4 mean far more than courtroom verdicts.** In Hebrew, *mishpat* ("justice") reaches beyond judicial condemnation to restored, healing right-order across a violence-scarred world, while *torah* ("law") means life-giving instruction. That is why distant coastlands await the Servant's teaching in eager hope rather than dread.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the celebrated fourth-century preacher of Antioch and Constantinople, preaching on Matthew's quotation of Isaiah 42:1–4, highlights Jesus' extraordinary gentleness. A bruised reed or faintly smoking wick represents wounded, frail, stumbling human beings whom Christ could easily snap with a word; instead, He refuses to use power to crush them, gently tending them back to wholeness. *(Homilies on Matthew, on chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Chrysostom's exposition of the bruised reed and smoldering wick in his homilies on Matthew 12; exact homily number not verified on ccel.org -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, cherishes verse 42:3 as supreme comfort for believers haunted by weak, flickering faith. God never waits until people stand tall and flawless before accepting them; He delicately shields whatever tiny spark remains lest it go out. Reaching the chapter's end, Calvin contrasts the faithful Servant of verse 1 with blind Israel in verse 19 to remind readers that outward election never prevents hard-heartedness if we refuse to listen. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 42)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's exposition of 42:3 and his contrast of the two servant portraits in chapter 42; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, notes on 42:3 that fragile, guilt-stricken believers often feel useless as a bent reed or a smoking wick giving off irritating smoke rather than bright light—yet Christ neither breaks nor extinguishes them, fanning that tiny ember into clean flame. On 42:14, Henry stresses that God keeping silent "for a long time" never meant neglect, only patient restraint waiting for the appointed hour of deliverance. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 42)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's reflections on the smoking wick in 42:3 and patient silence in 42:14; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Brevard Childs**, American Old Testament scholar, pays close attention to both contrasting portraits of "My servant" standing inside chapter 42. Setting the Spirit-filled Servant opening blind eyes (verses 1–7) beside blind, deaf Israel (verses 18–20) is no editorial accident, Childs argues, but a pivotal theological design across Isaiah's second half: where the historical nation failed its vocation, God accomplishes it through one faithful Servant. *(Isaiah, Old Testament Library, 2001)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading the tension between the faithful Servant in 42:1–7 and blind Israel in 42:18–20 as deliberate theological architecture is a hallmark of Childs's Isaiah commentary -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament professor, pauses at length over the paired metaphors of warrior and laboring mother in verses 13–14. Joining two seemingly opposite pictures shatters tidy human boxes for God: divine liberation carries both the warrior's determination to smash oppression and the mother's agonizing labor birthing new life out of exile's ruins. *(Isaiah 40–66, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Brueggemann's noted commentary on the warrior-and-mother pair in Isaiah 42:13–14; exact page numbers not verified -->

France**, British New Testament scholar, explains Matthew's full quotation of Isaiah 42:1–4 in Matthew 12 as a direct answer to contemporaries disappointed that Jesus was not a loud sword-wielding Messiah ousting foreign rulers.
