# Isaiah 31 — Egypt Is Mere Flesh, While God Is the Protector

## Story

Picking up immediately after chapter 30's warning about the pointless trek south (30:1–5), Isaiah delivers another cry of "Woe"—short, crisp, and sharp as a blade. As Assyrian armies tighten around Judah's borders late in the eighth century BC, Jerusalem's court still quietly courts Egyptian help. The oracle opens with a blunt indictment of those who go down to Egypt banking on vast numbers of chariots and formidable horsemen, yet never lift their eyes to "the Holy One of Israel" or seek the LORD (31:1).

Jerusalem's clever statesmen no doubt congratulated themselves on smart realpolitik—playing one superpower off another. Isaiah's tone drops into icy irony: "Yet He too is wise!" God is hardly naive enough to be outmaneuvered by human schemes. He brings disaster and never retracts His spoken word. He rises against the house of evildoers and strikes down those who abet the wicked as well. Then rings out a wake-up line that shatters every illusion: "Now the Egyptians are men, not God; their horses are flesh, not spirit." The moment the LORD stretches out His hand, Egypt the helper stumbles, Judah the helped falls right beside it, and both perish together (31:2–3).

From rebuking mortals, the text shifts to what the LORD personally told Isaiah. God sets two vivid animal pictures—one fierce, one tender—right side by side. First comes a lion, a young lion growling low over its prey: though an entire band of shepherds runs out shouting at the top of their lungs to drive it off, neither their noise shakes it nor their crowd scares it back. In the exact same way, the LORD of Hosts will descend to wage battle on Mount Zion and its hill, untouchable by any mortal army (31:4).

With the very next verse, that roaring lion suddenly softens into birds hovering overhead with wings outspread to shield their nest. Just like hovering birds guarding their young, the LORD of Hosts will protect Jerusalem. Four stacked verbs drop like four rings of armor around the capital: He will shield it, deliver it, pass over it, and preserve it (31:5).

Facing a God both awesome in might and overflowing in tenderness, Isaiah turns straight to the people with an urgent appeal: "Return to the One against whom you have so blatantly rebelled, O children of Israel" (31:6). That call comes paired with a promise of awakening: in that day, every single person will fling away the silver and gold idols their own sinful hands once crafted (31:7).

At the chapter's close, the curtain lifts on the actual doom awaiting Assyria, the era's supreme menace. Assyria will fall—not by Egyptian spears or warhorses, but "by a sword not of mortals," a sword belonging to no human hand will devour it. Its proud legions will scatter in terror before that unseen blade, and its choicest young warriors will be pressed into forced labor (31:8). Even its rock of refuge will dissolve in panic, its commanders losing all nerve at the sight of the banner. The oracle closes with the majestic signature of the LORD, "whose fire is in Zion, whose furnace is in Jerusalem" (31:9).

## Key Takeaways

**People naturally trust visible power over the unseen Creator.** Rows of gleaming chariots and thundering cavalry look tangibly reassuring during panic. Verse 31:3 restores clean perspective: Egyptians are still mortal humans, and warhorses are merely flesh that tires and dies. Placing ultimate trust in flesh—even the strongest flesh available—is building on sand.

**Political cleverness can never outsmart God.** Isaiah's dry jab—"Yet He too is wise!" (31:2)—exposes a timeless human conceit: assuming shrewd calculations and strong connections secure our future without consulting God. Every blueprint that leaves God out carries its own collapse built inside.

**Unstoppable strength and gentle tenderness dwell in the same God.** Had the text offered only the growling lion (31:4), God might seem distant and intimidating. Had it offered only hovering mother birds (31:5), people might underestimate His power. Placing both side by side, chapter 31 reveals a God possessing might no enemy can budge, yet pouring that exact might into sheltering and holding His people safe.

**Being rescued is never license to stay the same; it is an invitation to return.** Knowing God will shatter Assyria gives Judah zero excuse to stay double-minded. Right between the promise of protection (31:5) and Assyria's collapse (31:8) stands the call to repent and discard every idol (31:6–7). God's grace always invites us to loosen our grip on false supports.

## Deeper Meaning

**Moses' Law barring a return to Egypt for horses.** Centuries earlier, the Law of Moses explicitly warned Israel's king never to pile up horses and never to lead the people back to Egypt—the very land God rescued them from—to buy them (Deuteronomy 17:16). Running back to Egypt begging for chariots wasn't just bad diplomacy; it ran backward against salvation history. The Psalms reinforce the exact same lesson: some trust in chariots and some in horses, yet God's people trust in the name of the LORD, because a warhorse is a false hope for deliverance (Psalms 20:7; Psalms 33:16–17).

**A new Passover night hovering over Jerusalem.** In 31:5, promising that God will "pass over" and preserve Jerusalem, the original Hebrew employs the exact Passover verb (*pasach*) used on the night Israel left Egypt, when the LORD saw blood on the doorposts and "passed over" Israelite homes without letting the destroyer enter (Exodus 12:13; Exodus 12:23; Exodus 12:27). The irony bites deep: while Judah's leaders race back to Egypt begging protection, God reminds them of the very night He defeated Egypt to save them, pledging a fresh Passover shielding Jerusalem.

**Protective wings stretching from Moses to Jesus.** Birds hovering over their nest in 31:5 echo the Song of Moses, comparing the LORD to an eagle fluttering over its young and spreading wide pinions to carry them (Deuteronomy 32:11). Over seven centuries after Isaiah, Jesus wept overlooking Jerusalem using that very warm image: how often He longed to gather her children together as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, yet they were unwilling (Matthew 23:37). God's protective heart spans both Testaments, and human tragedy remains refusing to shelter beneath His wings.

**The sword "not of mortals" fulfilled in history.** The bold prediction in 31:8 that Assyria would collapse beneath an unearthly sword came strikingly true right inside Isaiah's own book. Just chapters ahead, as King Sennacherib's huge army besieged Jerusalem, God pledged to defend the city—and that very night the angel of the LORD struck down 185,000 soldiers in the Assyrian camp, driving the king home to Nineveh in defeat (37:35–37). Not a single Egyptian horse took the field, exactly as Isaiah foretold.

**God's fire glowing in Zion.** The chapter's closing title—the One "whose fire is in Zion, whose furnace is in Jerusalem" (31:9)—links to Scripture's consistent revelation of God as "a consuming fire" (Deuteronomy 4:24; Hebrews 12:29). That sacred flame serves both as the welcoming hearth on the temple altar signaling God's home among His people and as a blazing furnace consuming arrogant invaders who dare touch His city. In that fire's light, human-made idols of silver and gold get thrown out like garbage (31:7; 2:20), affirming anew the curse on anyone making mere flesh their strength (Jeremiah 17:5).

## Notes & Nuances

**The growling lion in 31:4 carries two possible nuances.** In Hebrew, the preposition translated "on Mount Zion" can mean God descends to fight *for* Mount Zion or *against* Mount Zion. Many interpreters pair 31:4 closely with 31:5 so both proclaim defense: God stands like a lion fiercely guarding His own territory (Zion) against shouting shepherds (Assyria's armies), refusing to surrender His own. Other modern scholars note that 31:4 deliberately retains an unsettling edge of judgment: when the Holy One steps down onto Zion, He is nobody's tame mascot—His presence simultaneously sobers stubborn Jerusalem and sweeps out invaders.

**"Yet He too is wise" drips with biting sarcasm.** At first glance, 31:2 might sound curiously modest, saying God "also" has wisdom. Actually, it is classic Isaiah irony. King Hezekiah's royal counselors considered themselves shrewd realists reading geopolitical tides by courting Egypt, sneering at Isaiah's call for "quietness and trust" (30:15) as naive. Isaiah fires back dryly: you think you hold a monopoly on strategy—don't forget God happens to have a bit of wisdom Himself!

**Why bring up throwing away idols right inside a chapter about foreign policy?** Verse 31:7 suddenly mentions silver and gold idols amid talk of Egypt and Assyria. In ancient Near Eastern diplomacy, military treaties routinely included honoring an ally's gods—and deeper still, worshiping idols and banking on Egyptian chariots spring from the exact same root: pinning security on visible things humans can forge or count, rather than entrusting oneself to the unseen God.

**"Fire in Zion" and "furnace in Jerusalem" do not picture hell in the afterlife.** Hearing "fire" and "furnace" in 31:9, modern readers sometimes jump straight to afterlife punishment. In Old Testament context, Zion's fire recalls first the perpetual altar flame inside Jerusalem's temple—marking God's living residence like a host beside His hearth. To faithful citizens, it radiates warmth, protection, and cleansing; to Assyria marching in to plunder, grabbing Jerusalem is plunging bare hands straight into a roaring furnace.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the great fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Latin Bible translator, reads the famous declaration of 31:3—that Egyptians are flesh, not spirit—not only as commentary on an ancient war between Judah and Assyria, but as a timeless spiritual maxim for believers. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pauses carefully over the ironic edge of "Yet He too is wise" in 31:2: when statesmen congratulate themselves on clever maneuvers while brushing aside God's word, they act as though God were less insightful than they are. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, especially cherishes the paired metaphors in 31:4–5. He links the lion fearless before shouting shepherds to God's unstoppable authority over enemies, while hovering birds guarding their nest capture His watchful tenderness toward His own. Believers discover complete peace, Henry writes, knowing their Protector is simultaneously mightier than every foe and gentler than a mother bird shielding her brood. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 31)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's pastoral reflections on the lion-and-birds pair in verses 4–5; not verified against the original -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, highlights a gem of Hebrew vocabulary in 31:5: the verb translated "pass over" in God protecting Jerusalem is the exact Passover verb from Exodus 12. That single word unlocks chapter 31's piercing irony: while Judah runs back to Egypt begging rescue, God reminds them He once passed over Israel to break Egypt's grip—and pledges a new Passover right over Jerusalem itself. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer highlighting the Passover verb in verse 5 in his Isaiah commentary; exact page numbers not verified -->
