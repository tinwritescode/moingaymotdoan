# Isaiah 12 — The Song of Thanksgiving at the Wells of Salvation

## Story

Right after the vision of the LORD clearing a highway across a dried-up River to bring His remnant home at the end of chapter 11 (11:15–16), the prophetic thunder falls silent to make room for a brief, radiant hymn. Chapter 12 opens with a familiar bridge connecting straight to the previous chapter: "In that day you will say..."—a song in the singular voice, speaking as each rescued individual or the whole community gathered into one heart. The singer gives thanks to the LORD with disarming honesty: rather than glossing over a painful past, the voice confesses straight out, "Though You were angry with me," before bursting into relief because "Your anger has turned away, and You have comforted me" (12:1).

Out of that relief of pardon and comfort, the song rises into a steady confession of faith: "Surely God is my salvation; I will trust and not be afraid." Fear dissolves not because of thick city walls or clever military alliances, but because of the One standing beside His people. The singer repeats God's name twice in a row with deep affection and pride: "For the LORD GOD is my strength and my song, and He has become my salvation" (12:2).

Right at the chapter's midpoint, the rhythm widens from singular to plural, addressing the entire redeemed crowd: "With joy you will draw water from the springs of salvation" (12:3). In the parched climate of the Middle East, where a well meant life itself, picturing everyone happily drawing water without an ounce of anxiety captures the overflowing abundance and freshness of God's grace.

A second song then rises, likewise introduced by "And on that day you will say," now sounding as a choral call inviting the whole assembly to praise. Rather than keeping gratitude locked indoors, they urge one another to call on His name, "make known His deeds among the nations," and proclaim how exalted His name is. They summon one another to sing praises to the LORD because He has done "glorious things"—wonders worthy of being "known throughout the earth" (12:4–5).

The final verse brings both this hymn and the entire opening twelve chapters of Isaiah to a soaring close with a shout addressed straight to God's chosen capital: "Cry out and sing, O citizen of Zion!" The reason for such jubilation fits in one dazzling declaration that lights up the entire storyline from chapter 6 onward: "for great among you is the Holy One of Israel" (12:6).

## Key Takeaways

**Grace does not erase the truth about sin; it turns wrath into comfort.** The singer in 12:1 does not pretend nothing ever went wrong, openly admitting, "You were angry with me." True joy runs deepest when we face God's just displeasure honestly, then marvel at the moment His anger turns away and gives place to tender comfort.

**Trusting God is the only cure for living under fear's control.** Throughout chapters 7 to 10, Judah bounced from terror of the Aram-Israel alliance to terror of Assyria, scrambling into disastrous deals. Verse 12:2 supplies the decisive answer: once you know "God is my salvation," trust drives out fear. Real security is not stockpiling more weapons, but having the LORD Himself as your strength and song.

**God's salvation is an open spring always ready for drawing.** God does not dole out one tiny drop of grace and close the tap; He opens "the springs of salvation" (12:3). Our part is not to dig dry cisterns by our own sweat, but to come gladly and draw life from the wells He has already opened.

**Authentic worship naturally overflows into global witness.** The hymn starts in deeply personal territory—"I will praise You" and "my salvation" (12:1–2)—yet refuses to stay private. It immediately broadens into a community "you" (12:3–4) and spills outward to all nations, urging believers to "make known His deeds among the nations" and let them be "known throughout the earth" (12:4–5). Anyone who truly experiences great mercy cannot keep it bottled up.

## Deeper Meaning

**The hymn of the second Exodus, echoing the Song at the Red Sea.** Placing chapter 12 right after the promise of a dry highway across the Euphrates in 11:15–16 mirrors the exact pattern of Exodus: in Exodus 14 Israel crosses the Red Sea on dry ground, and in Exodus 15 Moses and the entire nation erupt in praise. Verse 12:2 actually quotes Moses' ancient lyric nearly word for word: "The LORD is my strength and my song, and He has become my salvation" (Exodus 15:2; cf. Psalms 118:14). The summons in 12:4 likewise harmonizes with the psalm praising God's covenant with Abraham and Israel (Psalms 105:1). God's new rescue deserves a new song rivaling the day Israel marched out of Egypt.

**The terrifying refrain of the earlier chapters is finally reversed.** From chapter 5 through chapter 10, a grim refrain tolled four separate times like a funeral bell over rebellious Israel: "Yet for all this, His anger is not turned away; His hand is still upraised" (5:25; 10:4). In 12:1, that dread sentence breaks open into relieved thanksgiving: "Your anger has turned away, and You have comforted me." That word "comfort" simultaneously sounds the keynote that will open the entire second half of Isaiah many chapters later: "Comfort, comfort My people" (40:1).

**From dread before the Holy One to joy in His midst.** Back in chapter 6, beholding the glory of the thrice-holy Lord, Isaiah fell apart crying, "Woe to me, for I am ruined, because I am a man of unclean lips" (6:3; 6:5). Yet in the closing line of 12:6, that very "Holy One of Israel"—Isaiah's signature title for God—dwells right in Zion's midst without consuming His people, becoming instead the source of their ringing shout of joy. It brings the circle full back to the Child named "Immanuel," meaning God with us (7:14).

**"The springs of salvation" and Jesus' cry at the Feast of Tabernacles.** In chapter 8, Judah was rebuked for rejecting "the gently flowing waters of Shiloah," symbolizing God's quiet provision (8:6). Here in chapter 12, the remnant is invited to "draw water from the springs of salvation" (12:3), paving the way for the later invitation: "Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters!" (55:1). In New Testament times, during the joyful ceremony of drawing water from the Pool of Siloam at the Feast of Tabernacles—a ritual explicitly linked to Isaiah 12:3—Jesus stood up in the temple and cried aloud: "If anyone is thirsty, let him come to Me and drink. Whoever believes in Me, as the Scripture has said: 'Streams of living water will flow from within him'" (John 7:37–38). He made the same promise of a spring welling up to eternal life to the Samaritan woman (John 4:14), and Revelation concludes with the Lamb guiding His people to springs of living water (Revelation 7:17).

## Notes & Nuances

**The word "salvation" in chapter 12 shares the exact root of Jesus' name.** In Hebrew, the word for "salvation" repeated three times across 12:2 and 12:3 is *yeshuah*—from the same root as the name *Yeshua* (Joshua, transliterated into Greek and English as Jesus), meaning "the LORD saves." English translations cannot capture this sound play, yet to Hebrew ears, hearing "God is my *yeshuah*" and "draw water from the springs of *yeshuah*" rings remarkably close to the Savior's own name.

**Why does 12:2 pair the divine name twice ("the LORD GOD")?** The original Hebrew places the short and full forms of the covenant name right beside each other: *Yah YHWH*. This very rare doubling appears only a handful of times in the Old Testament—including later in this book (26:4)—for maximum emphasis that no source of strength or deliverance exists outside the LORD alone.

**"Citizen of Zion" in 12:6 is feminine in Hebrew.** The original expression literally addresses the "female inhabitant of Zion," reflecting the familiar poetic personification of Jerusalem and God's people as "Daughter Zion" (10:32). Once pictured as a corrupted city or a grieving woman under judgment, she is now summoned to burst into song because the Holy One dwells right in her midst.

**Chapter 12 marks a major structural boundary closing the first great section of Isaiah.** Readers moving straight from chapter 12 into chapter 13 feel an abrupt shift: joyous thanksgiving drops straight into "The burden against Babylon" amidst smoke and battle (13:1). That is because chapters 1–12 form the book's first major block—centering on Judah, Jerusalem, the Assyrian crisis, and the messianic hope—while chapters 13–23 open an entirely new section devoted to oracles concerning the surrounding nations.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Latin Bible translator, pays close attention to the repeated Hebrew term for "salvation" in verses 12:2–3, pointing out that the very name of Jesus signifies that God Himself is salvation. Reading the verse through a New Testament lens, he interprets "the springs of salvation" from which believers joyfully draw as the Scriptures and the streams of Christ's grace, where the faithful find living water to nourish their souls. *(Commentary on Isaiah, on chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Jerome linking the Hebrew word for salvation to Jesus' name and interpreting the springs of salvation as Scripture and grace; not verified against the Latin original -->

**The Mishnah and Talmudic tradition**, classical collections of Jewish law and commentary, connect verse 12:3 directly to one of the most exuberant ceremonies in the Jerusalem temple calendar: the water-drawing celebration from the Pool of Siloam during the Feast of Tabernacles (known as the Rejoicing of the Water-Drawing House). Ancient rabbis explained that the people sang and danced through the night at that ceremony specifically to enact Isaiah 12:3—"with joy you will draw water from the springs of salvation"—and the Jerusalem Talmud further interprets drawing water there as receiving the Holy Spirit amid joyful worship. *(Mishnah, Sukkah chapters 4–5; Jerusalem Talmud, Sukkah chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the connection between Simchat Beit HaShoevah at Sukkot and Isaiah 12:3, along with interpreting water as the Holy Spirit in Sukkah traditions, is widely cited; individual passages not rechecked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pauses at verse 12:1 to teach that a believer's proper response to discipline is never resentment, but candid acknowledgment of God's righteous anger followed by gratitude when He turns to comfort us. On verse 12:2, he underlines that genuine faith always produces calm assurance: whoever rests their entire salvation on God can face every threat without panic. Turning to verses 12:4–5, Calvin remarks that salvation never leaves the rescued silent; authentic gratitude inevitably moves God's people to proclaim His mighty deeds so other nations may know Him too. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's exposition of humbly confessing wrath in verse 1, fearless trust in verse 2, and global proclamation in verses 4–5; not verified line by line on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, highlights the brief yet profound wording of verse 12:2: Scripture does not merely say God *gives* salvation, but that God Himself *is* my salvation—to have Him is to possess everything needed for rescue and safety. On "the springs of salvation" in verse 12:3, Henry compares God's promises and grace to deep, inexhaustible wells, with faith and joy serving as the bucket by which believers draw fresh life every day. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's pastoral points on verse 2 and the bucket-and-wells metaphor on verse 3; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, shows how short chapter 12 serves as a masterfully crafted capstone to Isaiah's entire opening division (chapters 1–12). By placing this song immediately after the second Exodus at the close of chapter 11 and echoing Moses' Red Sea song (Exodus 15:2), Isaiah shows that the Messiah's redemption equals and surpasses the deliverance from Egypt, while thoroughly reversing the dreaded refrain "His anger is not turned away" that haunted the earlier chapters (5:25; 10:4). *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's structural analysis of chapter 12 as a new Exodus hymn closing chapters 1–12 and reversing the anger refrain; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, observes that this concise hymn captures the natural outward movement of biblical worship: beginning with an individual's experience of pardon and comfort (12:1–2), widening into shared community joy around the wells of salvation (12:3), and bursting forth into public witness before the whole world (12:4–6). Praise here is no escape from reality, he notes, but a bold announcement that the Holy One dwelling in Zion truly rules history. *(Isaiah 1–39, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Brueggemann tracing the progression from individual to community to global testimony in chapter 12; exact page numbers not verified -->
