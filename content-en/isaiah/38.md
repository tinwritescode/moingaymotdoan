# Isaiah 38 — King Hezekiah's Illness and Song of Thanksgiving

## Story

During the turbulent season when Assyria was threatening Judah, King Hezekiah suddenly fell mortally ill. The prophet Isaiah son of Amoz came to the palace carrying a stark message from the LORD: "Put your house in order, for you are about to die; you will not recover" (38:1).

Hearing that death sentence, Hezekiah did not argue with the prophet; he turned his face toward the wall and prayed straight to the LORD. He asked God to remember how he had walked faithfully and wholeheartedly before Him and done what was good in His sight. Then Hezekiah "wept bitterly" (38:2–3).

An answer came swiftly. The LORD instructed Isaiah to walk right back in and address the king in the name of "the LORD, the God of your father David": He had heard the king's prayer and seen his tears. God promised to add fifteen years to Hezekiah's life, pledging at the same time to rescue both king and Jerusalem from the king of Assyria and defend the city (38:4–6).

To steady the king's faith that this pledge would truly happen, the LORD gave an astonishing sign: on "the stairway of Ahaz"—an outdoor stepped sundial built by his father Ahaz—the descending shadow of the sun would reverse ten steps backward. Sure enough, the sunlight's shadow retreated ten full steps back up the stairs it had just gone down (38:7–8).

Once recovered, King Hezekiah composed a moving psalm capturing both the dread of approaching death and the flood of gratitude upon being spared. Opening his song, he recalls the crushing despair of facing "the gates of Sheol" right "in the prime of my life," robbed of the rest of his years. Bitterly he grieved thinking he would never again see the LORD in the land of the living or look upon fellow human beings on earth (38:9–11).

Hezekiah reaches for vivid everyday images to describe life's fragility. His dwelling is pulled up and stripped away like a shepherd's tent packed off to another pasture; his life is rolled up like cloth sliced right off a weaver's loom. From dusk till dawn, agony racked him until it felt as if God Himself were crushing all his bones like a lion. He could only chirp weakly like a swallow or thrush and moan like a dove, his eyes growing weary staring upward as he pleaded for God to step in as his security in distress (38:12–14).

Then the psalm swings into wonder and gratitude. What more can he even say, since God Himself spoke and God Himself carried it out? For all his remaining years, he vows to walk humbly remembering his soul's bitterness. By God's deeds people live, and in them lies the life of his spirit. Most wonderful of all, crushing bitterness turned straight into peace: "In Your love You have held back my life from the pit of destruction, for You have cast all my sins behind Your back" (38:15–17).

Closing his song, Hezekiah contrasts the grave with the land of the living: "For Sheol cannot thank You; Death cannot praise You," and those going down to the pit can no longer hope for God's faithfulness. Only the living can praise God as the king does today, and only living fathers can tell their children about His faithfulness. Confident that "the LORD will save me," he vows to play stringed instruments and sing praises in the house of the LORD all the days of his life (38:18–20).

Two concluding verses supply brief historical footnotes wrapping up the episode: Isaiah had ordered a "poultice of figs" pressed onto the king's "boil" so he would recover, and beforehand Hezekiah had asked for a sign confirming he would indeed go up to the house of the LORD again (38:21–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Honest, heartfelt prayer genuinely moves God's heart.** The LORD had just pronounced Hezekiah's sickness fatal, yet He never brushed aside the tears of a man turning back to plead with Him. God answered with extraordinary tenderness: "I have heard your prayer and seen your tears" (38:5).

**Faith never asks us to pretend we aren't afraid of death.** Hezekiah stood among Judah's godliest monarchs, yet confronting death in midlife he wept bitterly, trembled, and poured out raw lament without hiding a thing (38:10–14). Scripture preserves his entire song to assure believers we may bring every fear and frailty straight into God's presence.

**The deepest healing always centers on forgiveness.** Looking back over his ordeal, Hezekiah didn't merely celebrate a healed boil. He marveled at God's love pulling his life back from the pit and throwing every one of his sins behind His back (38:17). Only when guilt is lifted does deep bitterness truly mellow into peace.

**Extra years are given not for selfish indulgence, but to praise God and pass faith to the next generation.** Hezekiah's deepest longing for healing was walking back into the temple to sing praise and standing as a father telling his children of God's faithfulness (38:19–22). Life's true value lies not in tallying years, but in how we live those years before God.

**God's miracles work comfortably alongside ordinary medicine.** God both sent the sun's shadow ten steps backward and directed Isaiah to press a fig poultice onto the king's boil (38:8; 38:21). Trusting God's healing power never means despising sound medical care.

## Deeper Meaning

**Hezekiah's illness threatened the Davidic royal promise.** Scripture notes Hezekiah took the throne at twenty-five and reigned twenty-nine years (2 Kings 18:2), placing him around thirty-nine when promised fifteen more years—literally "in the prime of my life" (38:10). Crucially, his successor Manasseh was only twelve when he became king (2 Kings 21:1), meaning Hezekiah still had no heir when struck down! Calling Himself "the God of your father David" (38:5) shows God wasn't merely saving one sick man, but safeguarding His covenant pledge of an enduring Davidic line (2 Samuel 7:12–16).

**The sign on Ahaz's stairway reverses his father's unbelief.** Years earlier during a siege threat, Isaiah had relayed God's offer inviting Hezekiah's father, King Ahaz, to request any sign he wished to strengthen faith—only for Ahaz to decline piously while scheming to summon Assyria instead (7:10–14). Now, right on a structure bearing Ahaz's own name, his son Hezekiah receives a heavenly sign confirming God's faithful care over Jerusalem.

**Casting sins behind His back ranks among Scripture's loveliest pictures of pardon.** Saying "You have cast all my sins behind Your back" (38:17) stands beside two other famous Old Testament images: hurling our iniquities into the depths of the sea (Micah 7:19) and removing transgressions as far as east is from west (Psalms 103:12). What God places behind His back He resolves never to pull out again to condemn us.

**Old Testament shadows of Sheol paving the way for resurrection light.** Hezekiah's line that Sheol cannot thank God (38:18) echoes several older Psalms (Psalms 6:5; Psalms 30:9; Psalms 115:17). In Old Testament times, God had revealed only dim glimpses of life beyond the grave, anchoring devotion tightly to earthly life. Once Jesus rose from the dead, the New Testament declared He "has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Timothy 1:10), allowing Paul to face death not as silent darkness, but as departing to be with Christ (Philippians 1:21–23).

**Reading Kings and Chronicles alongside Isaiah completes Hezekiah's portrait.** The parallel account in 2 Kings supplies fuller dialogue about choosing whether the shadow moves forward or back (2 Kings 20:1–11), whereas Isaiah uniquely preserves the king's personal psalm of thanksgiving. Meanwhile, 2 Chronicles reveals a sobering postscript: after healing, Hezekiah's heart grew proud, requiring him to humble himself all over again (2 Chronicles 32:24–26).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 38 actually occurred before Assyria's final retreat.** Although positioned right after Assyria's slaughter at the close of chapter 37 (37:36–38), God's pledge in verse 6—"I will deliver you and this city from the hand of the king of Assyria"—shows Hezekiah fell sick right during or just before the Assyrian siege. Isaiah arranges chapters 36–39 thematically rather than strictly chronologically: chapters 36–37 wrap up the Assyrian threat, while chapters 38–39 pivot forward toward the coming shadow of Babylon.

**Why do verses 38:21–22 appear after the thanksgiving psalm?** In 2 Kings' parallel record (2 Kings 20:7–8), applying the fig poultice and asking for a sign precede the backward shadow miracle. Here in Isaiah, the compiler inserts Hezekiah's complete song right after verse 8, then appends those two brief narrative details at the end as a concise postscript.

**Did God "change His mind" by adding fifteen years?** Telling Hezekiah he was dying stated the natural trajectory of his lethal illness while sounding an urgent alarm driving the king to seek God. Across Scripture, numerous pronouncements of doom carry an implicit door open for repentance and prayer; God's gracious response does not imply fickleness or surprise, but shows He freely weaves human prayer into His sovereign action.

**What was "the stairway of Ahaz," and how did the shadow move backward?** Literally "the steps of Ahaz" in Hebrew, it was likely an outdoor stepped stone structure serving as a solar clock tracking shadow length across the day. Commentators suggest varied explanations for the miracle in verse 8—from localized light refraction over Jerusalem to an astronomical wonder akin to Joshua's long day (Joshua 10:12–14). The text leaves physical mechanics unspoken, focusing solely on the Creator's power to grant a clear sign steadying a desperate heart.

**Fifteen extra years also carry a sobering lesson.** Added life was immense grace, yet Hezekiah's final fifteen years fell short of the humble pledge in his song. Within those very years he boasted of his vaults to Babylonian envoys in the next chapter, let pride creep into his heart (2 Chronicles 32:25), and fathered Manasseh—the king who later dragged Judah deepest into idolatry (2 Kings 21:1). Yesterday's mercy never automatically shields us from stumbling if we quit staying humble and alert.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, classic compilation of Jewish debate and interpretation, preserves a famous story behind Isaiah's confrontation with Hezekiah at the start of chapter 38. The rabbis explain that Hezekiah drew stern rebuke because he had deliberately refused to marry and father children after foreseeing he would sire wicked Manasseh; Isaiah replied that divine mysteries belong to the Merciful One while human duty is simply obeying God's command.
