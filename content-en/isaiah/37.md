# Isaiah 37 — Hezekiah's Prayer and the Downfall of Assyria

## Story

Hearing his three officials report the full weight of the Rabshakeh's threats and blasphemy from chapter 36, King Hezekiah instantly tore his garments, put on sackcloth, and walked straight into the house of the LORD (37:1). At the same time, he dispatched palace administrator Eliakim, scribe Shebna, and the senior priests—all draped in mourning sackcloth—to the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz (37:2). The king's message made no attempt to disguise total exhaustion: "This is a day of distress, rebuke, and disgrace; for children have come to the point of birth, and there is no strength to deliver them" (37:3). Drained like a mother in labor with no strength left to push, Hezekiah clung to one solitary hope: perhaps the LORD had overheard the Rabshakeh's taunts mocking "the living God," urging Isaiah to lift up a prayer for "the remnant that still survives" (37:4).

The moment the envoys arrived (37:5), Isaiah sent back a firm, reassuring word from the LORD to the king: "Do not be afraid of the words you have heard, with which the servants of the king of Assyria have blasphemed Me" (37:6). God pledged to put a spirit in Assyria's king so he would hear a startling report, hurry back to his own homeland, and "there I will cause him to fall by the sword" (37:7).

Yet the crisis did not evaporate overnight; a second, fiercer wave rolled in first. Learning his master had pulled out from Lachish, the Rabshakeh rejoined Sennacherib while he was fighting against Libnah (37:8). Right then word reached Assyria's king that "Tirhakah king of Cush" had marched out to challenge him. Still refusing to let Jerusalem off the hook, Sennacherib sent messengers bearing a menacing letter straight to Hezekiah (37:9). This time he hammered straight at the king's faith: "Do not let the God in whom you trust deceive you by saying, 'Jerusalem will not be delivered into the hand of the king of Assyria'" (37:10). Reciting Assyria's long streak of annihilating kingdoms, he listed ruined territories and cities one after another—Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, the people of Eden in Telassar, plus the kings of Hamath, Arpad, Sepharvaim, Hena, and Ivvah—sneering whether any of their gods had saved them (37:11–13).

Hezekiah's response became one of Scripture's most moving scenes of prayer. Taking the letter from the messengers and reading it, the king carried it straight up to the house of the LORD, "spread it out before the LORD," and prayed (37:14–15). Rather than launching straight into panic, Hezekiah first lifted his eyes to the Sovereign towering over every empire: "O LORD of Hosts, God of Israel, enthroned above the cherubim, You alone are God over all the kingdoms of the earth. You made the heavens and the earth" (37:16). He begged God to bend His ear and open His eyes to hear Sennacherib's taunts against "the living God" (37:17). Refusing to sugarcoat facts or deny enemy conquests, Hezekiah admitted openly that Assyria's kings really had devastated nations and thrown their gods into the fire—simply because those idols "were not gods, but the work of human hands—wood and stone" (37:18–19). Finally, the king lifted his highest motive in prayer: "Now, O LORD our God, save us from his hand, so that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that You alone, O LORD, are God!" (37:20).

This time Hezekiah did not even have to send envoys: Isaiah sent word directly from the LORD, beginning with warm reassurance: "Because you have prayed to Me concerning Sennacherib king of Assyria" (37:21). Then a majestic poetic oracle rolled forth flipping the entire battlefield upside down: now "Virgin Daughter Zion" despises, laughs at, and shakes her head behind the retreating tyrant's back (37:22). God confronts him point-blank: whom have you mocked and raised arrogant eyes against?—"Against the Holy One of Israel!" (37:23). God replays the Assyrian king's swaggering boasts of driving endless chariots up Lebanon's peaks to fell tall cedars and cypresses, and drying up all Egypt's streams beneath the soles of his feet (37:24–25). Immediately follows a crushing blow to imperial vanity: every conquest he bragged of, God Himself "planned it long ago," permitting him to reduce fortified cities to heaps of rubble and wilt their citizens like scorched rooftop grass (37:26–27). God watches his sitting down, his going out, his coming in, and his raging against Him (37:28). Because his arrogance has reached heaven's ears, God will "put My hook in your nose and My bit in your mouth"—the exact brutal treatment Assyria used to haul away captives—and drag him back along the very road he came (37:29).

Turning gently to Hezekiah, God provides a three-year agricultural sign for Judah's war-trampled fields: this year they will eat whatever grows on its own, the second year what springs from that, and by the third year they will sow, reap, plant vineyards, and enjoy their fruit in peace (37:30). Judah's surviving remnant will once more "take root below and bear fruit above," because "the zeal of the LORD of Hosts will accomplish this" (37:31–32). As for the threatened capital, God's decree leaves zero wiggle room: Assyria's king will not enter this city, shoot a single arrow there, advance before it with a shield, or heap up a siege ramp, but will return by the way he came (37:33–34). The LORD pledges to defend and save Jerusalem "for My own sake and for the sake of My servant David" (37:35).

The climax arrives with chilling speed and stillness in just three closing verses. "Then the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 men in the camp of the Assyrians. When the people got up the next morning, there were all the dead bodies!" (37:36). King Sennacherib immediately broke camp, retreating home to stay in Nineveh (37:37). Later, while he was worshiping inside the temple of his god Nisroch, his own two sons Adrammelech and Sharezer struck him down with the sword and fled to the land of Ararat, while another son, Esarhaddon, took the throne in his place (37:38).

## Key Takeaways

**When strength runs completely dry, the wisest step is spreading the crisis straight before God.** Hezekiah hid none of his helplessness—admitting they had "no strength to deliver" (37:3)—nor scrambled for new foreign alliances. Carrying the threatening letter into the temple and spreading it out before the LORD (37:14) proves real faith never ducks painful facts; it places them unedited into God's hands.

**Mature prayer beholds God's greatness before measuring its own fear.** Kneeling before the menacing scroll, Hezekiah begins by confessing God as Creator of heaven and earth and sole Sovereign over all kingdoms (37:16), lays out the crisis honestly, and concludes with his deepest desire: that all kingdoms on earth know the LORD alone is God (37:20). Once we see how vast God is, immediate threats shrink to size.

**No earthly power ever slips outside God's sovereignty.** Sennacherib imagined himself bending the whole world by sheer muscle, yet God revealed every victory he enjoyed had been "planned long ago" by heaven (37:26). The moment a ruler crosses into arrogant defiance against the Holy One, God pulls him home as easily as leading a beast by a nose ring (37:29).

**The razor-sharp contrast between silent idols and the living God.** Across chapters 36–37, Assyria ridiculed helpless pagan deities unable to save their worshipers (37:12–13). In the end, Assyria's own king died at his sons' hands while kneeling before his god Nisroch inside its very temple (37:38), while "the living God" saved Jerusalem without Judah firing a single arrow or lifting a sword (37:33–36).

## Deeper Meaning

**Praying for God's glory among all nations.** The climax of Hezekiah's prayer—"so that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that You alone, O LORD, are God" (37:20)—beats with the exact pulse of the Old Testament's grandest prayers. Young David stepped out to face Goliath for the very same aim: "so that all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel" (1 Samuel 17:45–47). King Solomon at the temple's dedication likewise asked God to answer prayers so all earth's peoples would know His name (1 Kings 8:43). God rescues His people never out of narrow favoritism, but so all nations may recognize the true Creator.

**A convergence point tying together prophecies across Isaiah's first half.** Reading chapter 37, threads scattered since the book's opening tie cleanly into one knot. "The zeal of the LORD of Hosts will accomplish this" in 37:32 repeats virtually verbatim the promise of the royal Child on David's throne from chapter 9 (9:7). The poem deflating Sennacherib's arrogance in 37:24–29 directly fulfills chapter 10's indictment of Assyria: "Does an axe raise itself above the one who swings it?" (10:12–15). And Assyria's camp falling overnight without Judean blades fulfills the pledge that God would hover over Jerusalem like protective birds while Assyria fell "by a sword not of mortals" (31:5; 31:8).

**Echoes of Passover night and the Davidic covenant.** The angel of the LORD moving through the enemy camp by night (37:36; 2 Kings 19:35) immediately recalls Passover night in Egypt, when God struck the oppressor's realm to deliver His people (Exodus 12:29). Meanwhile, God's motive for sparing Jerusalem in 37:35—"for My own sake and for the sake of My servant David"—anchors straight in God's oath to David that his dynasty would stand forever (2 Samuel 7:16). Jerusalem was spared not for its own spotless record, but through God's faithfulness to that covenant—which blossoms ultimately in Jesus, Son of David.

**A bridge into Isaiah's great polemic against idols in chapters 40–48.** When Hezekiah observes that gods burned by Assyria "were not gods, but the work of human hands—wood and stone" (37:19), he strikes the exact chord that dominates chapters 40 onward. Facing idol-packed Babylon, Isaiah later exposes the absurdity of cutting one piece of timber to cook dinner while carving its other half into an idol and begging it for rescue (44:17–19).

**Resonance in the Psalms and New Testament trust.** Generations of readers have connected 701 BC's astonishing deliverance with Psalms celebrating God dwelling inside Zion so she cannot fall, helping her at break of day (Psalms 46:5–7), and rebuking foes until "both rider and horse fell fast asleep" (Psalms 76:3–6). And Hezekiah's simple act of laying an intimidating letter open before God (37:14) illustrates warmly Peter's New Testament invitation: "Cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7).

## Notes & Nuances

**Reading the death of 185,000 Assyrian soldiers (37:36) with honesty and balance.** One night leaving 185,000 Assyrians dead (37:36; 2 Kings 19:35) raises two questions thoughtful modern readers wrestle with: what happened historically, and how do we face such massive loss of life ethically? Historically, many interpreters affirm a direct supernatural stroke by God's angel overnight; other interpreters and historians—noting Greek historian Herodotus's tale of field mice swarming Sennacherib's camp—suggest God may have triggered a sudden, lethal outbreak of bubonic plague (which Scripture naturally attributes to the angel of the LORD's hand); some scholars also point out that the Hebrew word for "thousand" (*eleph*) in ancient military records occasionally denotes a military unit rather than an exact round number. Ethically, context is crucial: Assyria operated the ancient world's most terrifying war machine, having just leveled dozens of Judean towns while threatening to wipe out Jerusalem and mocking the living God; notice too that the narrative never endorses human bloodshed—Judah never lifted a sword (2 Chronicles 32:20–21)—affirming instead that God alone holds authority to halt ruthless aggression and rescue His people.

**A twenty-year span compressed between 37:37 and 37:38.** Reading the final two verses back to back makes it sound as if Sennacherib was assassinated the moment he stepped back into Nineveh. In reality, ancient Assyrian and Babylonian chronicles date the Judah campaign to 701 BC and Sennacherib's assassination to 681 BC—twenty years later. Isaiah places both events shoulder to shoulder to display the exact completion of God's prophecy in 37:7 ("there I will cause him to fall by the sword"). Remarkably, both Babylonian chronicles and successor Esarhaddon's inscriptions confirm Sennacherib's murder by his own son in a palace coup, aligning closely with the biblical record.

**The three-year sign (37:30) and King Tirhakah (37:9).** God's agricultural sign to Hezekiah—two years living off volunteer grain before normal planting returns in year three (37:30)—fits real wartime disruption after Assyrian troops occupied Judah's fields during sowing season (with some noting a possible Sabbath rest year as year two): the sign promises citizens will survive without starving and resume normal farming once Assyria withdraws permanently. As for "Tirhakah king of Cush" (37:9), Egyptian records date his formal coronation as Cushite Pharaoh around 690 BC, roughly a decade after 701; the standard explanation is that he led forces into battle as royal prince in 701 and the later narrator naturally refers to him by the royal title familiar to readers.

**The 701 BC miracle never meant Jerusalem was untouchable.** Later Judean generations sadly misread chapter 37's miracle into blind superstition—assuming the mere presence of Zion's temple forced God to shield Jerusalem regardless of idolatry or injustice. Isaiah's own structure shatters that illusion immediately: just two chapters ahead, chapter 39 foretells Babylon emptying Jerusalem's treasuries, and over a century later an unrepentant city truly fell. God's rescue in chapter 37 answered Hezekiah's humble faith and prayer—never offering a blank check for religious arrogance.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the great fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Latin Bible translator, lingers over Hezekiah donning sackcloth, entering the temple, and laying the blasphemous letter before the LORD (37:1; 37:14). He holds it up as a timeless blueprint for believers facing slander or persecution: never retaliate in panic or worldly intrigue, but place every grief humbly before the Almighty. **Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, narrates the Assyrian army's collapse outside Jerusalem while citing both Greek historian Herodotus and Babylonian historian Berossus for corroboration. Josephus writes that on the very first night of the siege, God sent a devastating pestilence into the Assyrian camp wiping out 185,000 troops and commanders in answer to Hezekiah's prayer, sending a terrified Sennacherib racing home to Nineveh where his elder sons later murdered him inside his own temple. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Josephus's well-known narrative in Antiquities Book 10 citing Herodotus and Berossus and attributing the 185,000 deaths to a divine plague; exact section numbers not rechecked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, praises the majestic order of Hezekiah's prayer in 37:16–20: instead of drowning in terror, the king first lifts his gaze to the Creator's sovereignty and places the glory of God's name above even his own survival. Commenting on 37:26—where God declares He "planned long ago" Assyria's conquests—Calvin underlines divine providence: tyrants imagine they roam unchecked by their own might, yet cannot budge one inch past what God ordains, and right at the peak of arrogance God slips a hook into their nose to haul them back (37:29). *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 37)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's exposition of the prayer structure in verses 16–20 and divine providence over tyrants in verses 26–29; not checked against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws one of his best-loved pastoral counsels from Hezekiah spreading Sennacherib's letter before God (37:14). Whenever believers receive dreadful news, threats, or crushing burdens, Henry advises taking that very paper or trouble directly into God's presence in prayer and letting Him read it and handle it. Looking at 37:36–38, he observes that God needs zero mortal armies to defend His own, whereas Nisroch couldn't save its own devotee kneeling right inside its own temple. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 37)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's famous pastoral counsel on spreading the letter in verse 14 and the contrast in verses 36–38; not verified against the original -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, hails Hezekiah's prayer in 37:16–20 as the theological summit of chapters 36–37—faith's decisive reply to the Rabshakeh's taunting question "What is the basis of this confidence of yours?" (36:4). He traces how deftly chapter 37 stitches the entire book of Isaiah together: "the zeal of the LORD of Hosts" (37:32) echoes chapter 9's messianic promise (9:7), while Hezekiah's confession of the sole Creator opposite wood-and-stone idols (37:16; 37:19) swings open the door straight into the anti-idol polemics of chapters 40–48. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer tracing structural ties between Hezekiah's prayer, 9:7, and chapters 40–48; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, highlights the devastating satire in 37:22–29: the Assyrian giant that terrified the Near East bragging of logging Lebanon and drying up Egypt gets laughed out of town by "Virgin Daughter Zion" and dragged off by a nose ring like a tamed beast. To Brueggemann, Hezekiah's prayer (37:16–20) models clear-eyed faith facing reality head-on: the king never denies Assyria's real trail of devastation (37:18), yet firmly refuses to let imperial terror outrank the living God. *(Isaiah 1–39, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing anti-imperial satire in verses 22–29 and clear-eyed faith in verses 16–20 is characteristic of Brueggemann; exact page numbers not verified -->
