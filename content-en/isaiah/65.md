# Isaiah 65 — God's Answer and a New Heaven and New Earth

## Story

Scarcely has chapter 64's trembling question faded—will God keep restraining Himself and stay silent forever?—when the LORD Himself speaks straight back. He turns their complaint completely inside out. He has neither hidden nor kept quiet; on the contrary, He has made Himself so openly available that "I revealed Myself to those who did not ask for Me; I was found by those who did not seek Me." Even to a nation that never called on His name, He kept calling warmly, "Here I am! Here I am!" Meanwhile, toward His own people, He "held out My hands all day long to an obstinate people" who insist on walking bad roads after their own imaginations (65:1–2).

The LORD lays bare the brazen practices provoking Him right to His face: sacrificing in pagan gardens, burning incense on brick altars, "sitting among the graves, spending nights in secret places, eating the meat of pigs" and filling bowls with broth of detestable meat. What repulses God most of all is the smug spiritual arrogance paired with those defiled rituals—people strutting around telling others, "Keep to yourself; do not come near me, for I am holier than you!" To God, such people are "smoke in My nostrils, a fire that burns all day long." Answering chapter 64's closing question directly, He declares: "I will not keep silent, but I will repay"—measuring out full recompense into their laps for both their own sins and their ancestors' hilltop idolatry (65:3–7).

Yet judgment will not wipe everyone out. The LORD shifts tone with a warm vineyard image: spotting good juice left inside a cluster of grapes, people say, "Do not destroy it, for it still has blessing in it." God pledges to act the exact same way for His servants' sake, "not destroying them all." Out of Jacob and Judah He will preserve a faithful seed inheriting the land—"My chosen ones" and "My servants" settling securely there. From west to east, "Sharon will become a pasture for flocks, and the Valley of Achor a resting place for herds" for all who genuinely seek Him (65:8–10).

From here, a sharp dividing line splits the destinies of two groups within the very same nation. Those who forsake the LORD, forget His holy mountain, and "set a table for Fortune and fill bowls of mixed wine for Destiny"—two pagan deities named Gad and Meni—He destines for the sword and slaughter, because "when I called, you did not answer; when I spoke, you did not listen," choosing instead what displeased Him (65:11–12). Five razor-sharp contrasts follow one after another: "My servants will eat, but you will go hungry"; "My servants will drink, but you will go thirsty"; "My servants will rejoice, but you will be put to shame"; "My servants will sing for joy of heart, but you will cry out from anguish of heart" and wail in broken spirit. Rebels leave behind only a name used for curses, whereas God will "call His servants by another name." From that day on, whoever invokes a blessing or swears an oath across the land will do so by "the God of truth," because "the former troubles will be forgotten and hidden from My sight" (65:13–16).

God's climactic reply dwarfs every plea to patch cracked walls or rebuild a burned temple in chapter 64. He announces a brand-new creation of the entire cosmos: "For behold, I will create new heavens and a new earth. The former things will not be remembered, nor will they come to mind." He invites His people to rejoice forever because "I will create Jerusalem to be a joy and its people to be a delight." God Himself will rejoice over Jerusalem and delight in His people, and inside her "the sound of weeping and crying will no longer be heard" (65:17–19).

Life inside this renewed world unfolds in vivid, deeply human colors. Gone is the tragedy of infants dying after a few days or elders cut short before fulfilling their years—so long is life that "the youth will die at a hundred years," and failing to reach a hundred seems a sign of judgment. Honest labor is never again plundered by raiders or injustice: God's people will "build houses and dwell in them; they will plant vineyards and eat their fruit," enjoying the full yield of their hands across lifespans stretching "like the days of a tree." They will "not labor in vain or bear children doomed to disaster," because both they and their offspring stand blessed by the LORD (65:20–23).

The chapter closes with two luminous portraits of healed fellowship with God and healed harmony across creation. With God, silence dissolves completely: "Even before they call, I will answer, and while they are still speaking, I will hear." Among earth's creatures, violence and terror cease: "The wolf and the lamb will feed together, and the lion will eat straw like the ox, but the food of the serpent will be dust. They will neither harm nor destroy on all My holy mountain," says the LORD (65:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**The silence came not from God, but from human hearts turning away.** After chapter 64 accused God of hiding His face, verses 65:1–2 flip the picture upside down: God stands right at the doorway calling "Here I am! Here I am!" with arms held open all day, while humanity chases self-will and refuses to answer (65:12). Often when God feels distant, He never stopped calling—we stopped listening.

**Spiritual arrogance and hypocrisy revolt God more than anything else.** The crowd in 65:3–5 dabbled secretly in idol feasts and graveyard divination, yet looked down their noses telling neighbors not to touch their superior "holiness." God compares that religious smugness to stinging smoke burning His nostrils all day. Authentic holiness never shoves others away in contempt; it begins with humble obedience to God.

**God's true people are known by faithfulness, not ancestry alone.** Throughout chapter 65, God neither saves nor condemns in bulk by ethnic label. Like a vintner sparing a cluster still holding good juice amid damaged vines (65:8), God distinguishes cleanly between "My servants" who seek Him and rebels pouring wine to Fortune and Destiny (65:11–15). Belonging to God's historic community is never a free pass for unfaithfulness.

**Biblical hope is not escaping earth, but a renewed heaven and earth brimming with life.** The new heavens and new earth of 65:17–25 are no foggy cloud-realm, but a vibrant world of homes lived in, vineyards harvested, safe families, rich longevity, instant communion in prayer, and creatures resting together in peace. God does not scrap the physical creation He made—He heals and makes all things new.

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul quotes Isaiah 65:1–2 directly to explain Gentile faith.** Wrestling tearfully in Romans with why many Israelites rejected the Gospel while Gentiles embraced it, Paul recalls how boldly Isaiah spoke: "I was found by those who did not seek Me; I revealed Myself to those who did not ask for Me." Yet toward Israel God still waits patiently: "All day long I have held out My hands to a disobedient and obstinate people" (Romans 10:20–21). God's outstretched arms have never pulled back.

**Isaiah 65's "new heavens and new earth" shapes Scripture's next-to-last chapter.** Both Peter and John take this exact promise as redemptive history's finish line. Peter writes that in keeping with God's promise we look forward to "a new heaven and a new earth, where righteousness dwells" (66:22; 2 Peter 3:13). And Revelation launches its eternal vision straight from Isaiah 65:17–19—seeing "a new heaven and a new earth," the New Jerusalem descending from heaven, and God wiping away every tear where "there will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain" (Revelation 21:1–4).

**The Valley of Achor transformed from disaster into peaceful pasture.** In Joshua, the Valley of Achor—meaning *Valley of Trouble*—was where Achan was stoned for greedy theft that cost Israel a battle (Joshua 7:26). Promising to turn Achor into a tranquil resting place for herds (65:10), God reprises Hosea's tender vow: making "the Valley of Achor a door of hope" (Hosea 2:15). Right where history recorded its ugliest failure, God opens a fresh beginning.

**"The God of truth" leads straight to Christ's title "the Amen."** Twice in 65:16, the Hebrew phrase behind "the God of truth" literally reads *the God of the Amen*—the One utterly faithful to every promise. In the New Testament, Paul declares that all God's promises find their "Yes" in Christ, through whom we speak the Amen (2 Corinthians 1:20), and the risen Jesus introduces Himself to Laodicea as "the Amen, the faithful and true Witness" (Revelation 3:14).

**The peaceable kingdom of 65:24–25 gathers promises from Genesis to the Gospels.** God answering even before His people speak (65:24) rings straight in Jesus' teaching on prayer: "Your Father knows what you need before you ask Him" (Matthew 6:8). Wolf grazing beside lamb and lion eating straw echo almost identically the Messiah's kingdom early in Isaiah (11:6–9). Crucially, "the food of the serpent will be dust" recalls Eden's verdict (Genesis 3:14): all creation is freed from violence, while the serpent—emblem of evil—remains permanently defeated, unable to harm anyone ever again. Meanwhile, the smug boast "I am holier than you" (65:5) comes to life vividly in Jesus' parable of the self-righteous Pharisee (Luke 18:9–14).

## Notes & Nuances

**Do 65:1–2 refer to Israel or to Gentile nations?** Within Isaiah's immediate Old Testament context, many scholars read 65:1 and 65:2 as parallel descriptions of stubborn Israel itself: they neither asked nor sought God, yet He stood saying "Here I am!" with hands stretched out all day. Following the Greek Septuagint, however, Paul in Romans 10:20–21 reads 65:1 of Gentiles (non-seekers finding God) and 65:2 of Israel. That application does not overturn the original context, but draws out the expansive grace already pulsing inside Isaiah 65—opening wide toward every nation as chapter 66 soon makes unmistakable.

**Why does 65:20 still mention death and "the sinner" inside the "new heavens and new earth"?** This question naturally arises beside Revelation 21:4's pledge that death is gone forever. Commentators offer two major explanations. First, the prophet paints abundant life and justice using familiar earthly images his generation could picture—calling death at a hundred "dying young" is poetic hyperbole for extraordinary longevity rather than a literal technical map of eternity, with full defeat of death unveiled progressively into the New Testament. Second, premillennial interpreters read 65:20 as depicting Christ's thousand-year earthly reign prior to the eternal deathless new creation of Revelation 21.

**The pagan gods Fortune and Destiny in 65:11 and a biting Hebrew pun.** *Gad* was the Semitic deity of Fortune (good luck), and *Meni* the deity of Destiny (fate), widely worshiped among neighboring peoples. Rather than trusting the LORD, Judeans laid banquet tables and poured mixed wine wooing luck from these gods. In Hebrew, verse 65:12 opens with a stinging pun: God says "I will *destine* you for the sword," using a verb sounding almost identical to *Meni*—a sobering warning that handing life over to Fate delivers only a destiny of ruin.

**The bizarre rites of 65:3–5: garden shrines, sleeping in tombs, eating pork.** Mosaic Law classified pork as unclean food (Leviticus 11:7) and strictly outlawed necromancy. Burning incense on brick altars (violating God's command for uncut stone altars), sacrificing in pagan groves, and spending nights among graves seeking spirit omens were secretive syncretistic cults. The bitter irony is that participants in these defiled rites fancied themselves spiritually elite ("do not come near me, for I am holier than you!").

**The tender promise to parents in 65:23.** Verse 65:23 pledges that God's people "will not labor in vain or bear children doomed to disaster." Behind that concise poetic line sits a deeply human ache from the exile years: parents will never again endure the heartbreak of bringing children into the world only to watch them swept away by war, famine, or sudden tragedy.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century bishop in North Africa, cited Isaiah 65:17–19 directly when reflecting on the final judgment and eternal world. The passing away of the old heavens and earth, Augustine explained, does not mean God annihilates the physical universe He made, but that He purges away corruption to renew creation into a fitting home for resurrected bodies and the unending joy of the City of God. *(The City of God, Book 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Augustine discusses Isaiah 65's new heavens and new earth in Book 20 as transformation rather than annihilation of matter; exact chapter number not verified -->

**Maimonides** (Rabbi Moses ben Maimon), the towering 12th-century Jewish scholar and philosopher, offered a famously sober reading of the wolf lying with the lamb and the lion eating straw—appearing in chapter 11 and repeated almost word for word in chapter 65 (11:6–7; 65:25). Rather than expecting the messianic age to abolish nature's biological laws, Maimonides read these lines as poetic metaphor portraying Israel dwelling safely at peace among formerly fierce nations—a realistic reading that became a cornerstone of Jewish interpretation. *(Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings and Wars, chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Maimonides's famous argument in Hilkhot Melakhim 12.1 reading the wolf and lamb imagery as political peace metaphor rather than suspension of natural law is well established -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, followed Paul in reading 65:1–2 as foretelling the calling of Gentiles into grace while obstinate Israel rejected God's outstretched hands all day long. Addressing the new heavens and earth in 65:17–25—especially century-long lifespans, houses, and vineyards—Calvin understood the prophet as using warm earthly portraits for the sweeping renewal launched at Christ's first coming and brought to complete perfection at His return. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 65)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic comments by Calvin linking 65:1–2 with Romans 10 and reading 65:17–25 symbolically of Christ's kingdom; exact wording on ccel.org not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, saw 65:5 ("Keep to yourself; do not come near me, for I am holier than you!") as the sharpest portrait of self-righteousness in Scripture: those farthest from true holiness most quickly sneer at others, turning their pride into stinging smoke before God. On the grape cluster holding new wine in 65:8, he noted God's recurring principle of mercy across history: for the sake of a faithful remnant carrying blessing within, God spares the wider community from total ruin. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 65)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: two familiar observations by Matthew Henry on verses 5 and 8; exact lines on ccel.org not verified -->

**Alec Motyer**, a British Old Testament scholar, underscored a deep shift throughout Isaiah shining clearly in chapter 65: whereas chapters 42–53 center on one singular "Servant," here the plural "My servants" appears repeatedly (65:8–15)—the faithful community born through that Servant's redemptive work. Motyer also highlighted the earthy concreteness of the new creation in 65:17–25 (houses, vineyards, families, long life), affirming that God's salvation redeems full embodied human existence rather than abstract disembodied spirits. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Motyer's signature focus on the shift from singular Servant to plural servants and earthy realism of the new creation; specific page not verified -->

**John N. Oswalt**, an American Old Testament professor, emphasized how tightly chapter 65 answers the long lament of Isaiah 63:7 through 64:12. Answering "Will You keep silent?" God refuses to sidestep the true cause of the tragedy: He never abandoned them; they chose to walk away despite His outstretched hands. The climax in 65:17–25 reveals a divine remedy far grander than patching old masonry—recreating an entire cosmos where every fracture since Eden is healed. *(The Book of Isaiah, Chapters 40–66, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Oswalt frames chapter 65 as God's direct answer to 63:7–64:12 culminating in healing Eden's curse in verse 25; specific page not verified -->
