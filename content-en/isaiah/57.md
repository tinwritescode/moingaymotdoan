# Isaiah 57 — Idolatry Exposed and the High One Dwelling With the Humble

## Story

Chapter 57 connects straight onto the corrupt, carousing leaders exposed at the close of chapter 56. Inside so callous a society, "The righteous perish, and no one takes it to heart; devout men are swept away, while no one considers that the righteous are guided away from evil." Bystanders view an upright person's early passing as sheer misfortune and barely shrug; yet in God's sight, they are spared coming disaster, entering into peace and resting undisturbed upon their beds (57:1–2).

In sharp contrast to the restful peace of the upright, a crowd chasing false gods is dragged before God's courtroom. The summons hits without sugarcoating: "But come here, you sons of a sorceress, you offspring of adulterers and prostitutes!" God confronts their brazen mockery of the faithful: "Whom are you mocking? At whom do you snicker and stick out your tongue? Are you not a brood of rebels, the offspring of liars?" Then He strips bare the cruel, sordid pagan rituals they practice in secret: burning with fertility-cult lust under every spreading oak and green tree, and worst of all, "slaughtering your children in the valleys, under the clefts of the rocks" as offerings to idols. Smooth stones pulled from dry streambeds have become their gods and portion, receiving their drink offerings and grain offerings devoted to lifeless rock (57:3–6).

The indictment unfolds their unfaithfulness through the graphic image of shameless adultery. They set up a bed atop a lofty mountain and climb up to offer sacrifices; instead of inscribing God's word on their doorposts, they "set up your pagan symbol behind the door and doorpost," uncovering themselves to others, widening their bed, and sealing pacts with foreign deities. They haul oil and heaps of perfume to appease a foreign king or idol, dispatch messengers far afield hunting new alliances and rituals, and sink all the way down to the depths of Sheol like necromancers consulting the dead. Most tragic of all is their blind stubbornness: worn out along their hopeless road, they still refuse to admit it is futile, finding a tiny burst of renewed energy and charging right back in (57:7–10).

The LORD probes straight to the hidden insecurity driving their betrayal: whom did they dread and fear so much that they lied and forgot God completely? Was it because He held His peace so long in patience that they mistook forbearance for weakness and stopped fearing Him? God declares He will expose their bogus righteousness and useless works. When crisis hits, let them cry out to their whole collection of idols and see if any can save them: "The wind will carry them all off, a breath will blow them away." Only those who take refuge in God Himself will truly "inherit the land and possess My holy mountain" (57:11–13).

Abruptly, right out of that heavy verdict, a hopeful herald shouts to clear the road: "Build it up, build it up, prepare the way! Remove every obstacle from the way of My people!" Immediately follows one of the loftiest yet gentlest self-revelations in all of Scripture: "For thus says the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy: 'I dwell in a high and holy place, and with the oppressed and humble in spirit, to restore the spirit of the lowly and to revive the heart of the contrite.'" God explains why He will not keep accusing or stay angry forever: if His wrath went on endlessly, the fragile human spirit and breath of life He Himself created would faint away before Him (57:14–16).

From that eternal height, God stoops in breathtaking grace. He states plainly why He grew angry, struck His people, and hid His face: "by his sinful greed." Yet even under discipline, they stubbornly kept wandering down the path of their own desires. By every human rule, judgment should end right there—yet the LORD flips the entire script: "I have seen his ways, but I will heal him; I will guide him and restore comfort to him and his mourners." God Himself creates praise upon their lips and proclaims a blessing crossing every divide: "'Peace, peace to those far and near,' says the LORD, 'and I will heal them'" (57:17–19).

Yet that healing peace never belongs automatically to those clinging stubbornly to evil. Two chilly final verses draw the curtain on this section: "But the wicked are like the tossing sea, for it cannot be still, and its waves churn up mire and muck. 'There is no peace,' says my God, 'for the wicked'" (57:20–21).

## Key Takeaways

**People exhaust themselves chasing idols that blow away on a single breath.** Judah poured out oil, perfume, dignity, and even their children's lives before false gods and foreign schemes (57:5–10). The tragedy of idolatry is that the harder you chase it, the more exhausted you become while still fooling yourself to keep running—yet in crisis, "a breath will blow them away" (57:13).

**The infinitely exalted God chooses to live alongside broken, humble hearts.** Verse 57:15 overturns every worldly instinct about status. The higher humans climb, the farther they distance themselves from the lowly; yet the God who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy, keeps exactly two addresses—His high and holy throne, and beside crushed, contrite hearts to bring them back to life (57:15).

**Ultimate healing springs from God's merciful initiative, never human self-repair.** Israel was disciplined for greed yet stubbornly kept walking its own way (57:17). Waiting until people fix themselves first would leave zero hope. God steps in first—"I will heal him"—guiding, comforting, and granting peace to both far and near (57:18–19).

**Real peace cannot coexist with defiant evil.** Nobody finds inner quiet while insisting on living against their Creator. The wicked do not even have to wait for outside punishment; their own restless hearts already churn like a stormy sea tossing up mud and mire, robbing them of any true rest (57:20–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**A bitter inversion of Mosaic Law on doorposts and child sacrifice.** Setting up a pagan emblem "behind the door and doorpost" (57:8) makes a heartbreaking parody of the Pentateuch's foundational command: Israel had been charged to write God's words "on the doorposts of your houses and on your gates" to remember the covenant every time they came and went (Deuteronomy 6:9). Replacing God's word with an idol's symbol turned home itself into a pagan shrine. Meanwhile, "slaughtering your children in the valleys" (57:5) violated one of the Law's strictest prohibitions (Deuteronomy 18:10), yet history records that even Judean kings like Manasseh committed this horror by sacrificing a son in the fire and practicing sorcery (2 Kings 21:6).

**God near the brokenhearted and not staying angry forever.** Verses 57:15–16 beat in harmony with the most cherished Psalms: "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted; He saves the contrite in spirit" (Psalms 34:18), the sacrifice He never despises is "a broken and contrite heart" (Psalms 51:17), and He will not always accuse or harbor anger forever (Psalms 103:9). In the New Testament, Jesus opens the Sermon on the Mount with that exact keynote: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted" (Matthew 5:3–4), while James affirms that God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble (James 4:6).

**Paul quoting Isaiah 57:19 for Christ preaching peace to both Gentiles and Jews.** Writing to the church in Ephesus about how Christ's cross tore down the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile, Paul draws directly on 57:19: "He came and preached peace to you who were far away and peace to those who were near" (Ephesians 2:17). Right alongside it, the phrase "fruit of the lips" (BSB: "bringing praise to their lips") in the same verse echoes in Hebrews, urging believers through Jesus to continually offer God "a sacrifice of praise—the fruit of lips that confess His name" (Hebrews 13:15).

**Clearing the highway, the rest of the righteous, and the line against evil.** The shout "Build it up, build it up, prepare the way!" in 57:14 recalls the hopeful overture of Isaiah 40 clearing a desert highway for God to rescue His people (40:3). The peaceful rest of the righteous departing this life in 57:1–2 shines clearer still in Revelation: "Blessed are the dead—those who die in the Lord from this moment on!" for "they will rest from their labors" (Revelation 14:13). Meanwhile, the closing refrain of 57:21 ("There is no peace for the wicked") repeats almost verbatim the close of chapter 48 (48:22), ringing like a solemn bell through the second half of Isaiah.

## Notes & Nuances

**Graphic sexual imagery and child sacrifice in 57:3–8 require candid, contextual reading.** This passage uses blunt, startling terms—calling idolaters sons of a sorceress, offspring of adulterers and prostitutes, exposing themselves on high mountain beds, and slaughtering children in rocky valleys (57:3–8). Scholars recognize two overlapping realities here: first, ancient Canaanite fertility cults genuinely practiced sexual rites under hilltop groves alongside child sacrifice to Molech; second, across the Old Testament God compares His covenant with Israel to marriage, naming idolatry outright as adultery and prostitution. Modern readers may find the figure of an unfaithful woman jarring; Scripture is not demeaning women here, but harnessing the most painful, shocking metaphor available in ancient society to unmask covenant betrayal and brutal cruelty against children.

**Saying the righteous are "guided away from evil" (57:1) is not a formula for every early death.** Amid Judah's downward spiral toward judgment, this verse comforts readers that the quiet passing of godly people does not slip past God—He gathers them into peace ahead of coming calamity. Yet it should never be turned into a glib cliché to explain away every tragic loss today, because Scripture honors life as a precious gift and treats death as a real grief deserving tender compassion.

**Being "oppressed and humble in spirit" (57:15) does not mean self-hatred or low self-esteem.** The Hebrew word rendered "contrite" or "oppressed" literally means crushed or broken. Set directly opposite the proud, stubborn self-reliance of 57:10–12, a contrite and lowly spirit describes honest surrender—dropping illusions of self-salvation and grieving honestly over sin and brokenness. Notice too why God dwells with such people: not to keep crushing them, but "to restore" and "to revive" fresh life and joy inside their souls.

**Two complementary ways to understand "those far and near" (57:19).** In the immediate post-exilic Jewish setting, many scholars view "those near" as Jews already back in Judah and "those far" as Jewish exiles still scattered in Babylon or distant lands—both groups receiving God's peace and healing. Another reading, linking back to the welcome of the nations in chapter 56, sees "those far" as reaching out to Gentiles once far from the covenant. Both readings reinforce each other: the initial promise to scattered Israel was opened to the whole world through Christ in Ephesians 2:17.

**Verse 57:21 marks a major structural milestone leading into chapter 58.** Just as 48:22 closed the first major movement (chapters 40–48), 57:21 ("There is no peace for the wicked") seals the second movement (chapters 49–57) in Isaiah's latter half. From here, chapter 58 shifts the spotlight away from blatant pagan idolatry straight onto hypocritical fasting inside the outwardly devout community itself.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic treasury of Jewish rabbinic commentary, preserves Rabbi Yochanan's celebrated insight linked to Isaiah 57:15: wherever Scripture extols God's transcendent majesty, right beside it Scripture reveals His tender humility—so in the Prophets, the moment 57:15 calls Him "the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy," it adds immediately that He dwells with "the oppressed and humble in spirit." Another passage in the *Babylonian Talmud* even notices the word order in 57:19, proclaiming peace first to "those far" and then to "those near," seeing in this God's warm welcome to repentant wanderers returning from afar even ahead of those who assumed they were always near. *(Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 31a; Berakhot 34b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Rabbi Yochanan's teaching in Megillah 31a citing Isaiah 57:15 on divine majesty paired with humility and Berakhot 34b on the order "far/near" in 57:19 are famous and widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, dwelt deeply on God's twin dwelling places in 57:15—on high in holiness and within humble hearts. If God revealed only overwhelming majesty, sinners could only flee in terror; by stooping to dwell with contrite spirits, He gives faith a refuge while stripping arrogance of every foothold. On 57:17–18, Calvin marveled at pure sovereign grace: disciplined Israel still turned stubbornly back to its own desires, proving that God's pledge "I will heal him" flows purely from free mercy rather than human self-reform. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 57)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: two central points in Calvin on 57:15 and 57:17–18; exact lines on ccel.org not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, reflected on 57:1–2 that public indifference when righteous people die signals both a hardened generation and God's quiet kindness ushering the faithful to rest ahead of approaching trouble. On the closing two verses (57:20–21), he observed that the wicked need no external storm to be miserable: restless desires and a gnawing conscience inside turn their hearts into a tossing sea continually churning up filth and robbing them of peace. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 57)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: hallmark exposition by Matthew Henry on 57:1–2 and the turbulent sea inside wicked hearts in 57:20–21; exact wording on ccel.org not verified -->
