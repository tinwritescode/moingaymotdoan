# Isaiah 52 — The Feet of the Messenger and the Disfigured Servant

## Story

The summons for Jerusalem to stand up at the end of chapter 51 rings out louder still in the opening verse of chapter 52: "Awake, awake, clothe yourself with strength, O Zion!" The holy city that lay flattened in the dust like a captive is now commanded to put on her festival garments of splendor, because "the uncircumcised and the unclean will no longer enter you." God urges her to "shake off your dust! Rise up and sit on your throne" in honor again, and "remove the chains from your neck" (52:1–2).

The LORD explains the ground of this liberation using the language of slave redemption: "You were sold for nothing, and without money you will be redeemed." Long ago His people went down to Egypt as guests and were forced into slavery; later Assyria oppressed them without cause; and now in Babylon His people have been carried off again for nothing. Their rulers swagger and howl all day long, "constantly blaspheming My name" as if Israel's God were powerless. For the honor of His own name, the LORD acts so that His people will truly know His name and recognize on the day of rescue that He Himself is standing before them saying, "Here I am!" (52:3–6).

Suddenly the camera swings back to the hills encircling ruined Jerusalem. Far off across the ridges, a lone runner races home carrying news of victory. The prophet bursts into rapturous exclamation: "How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of those who bring good news, who proclaim peace, who bring good tidings, who proclaim salvation, who say to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" Atop the broken walls, watchmen catch sight of the news and lift their voices in unison, for "eye to eye they will see the LORD's return to Zion." Even the crumbled ruins of Jerusalem are summoned to break out singing together, because the LORD has comforted His people and redeemed the holy city. No longer is this a private affair of one tiny nation: He has "bared His holy arm in the sight of all the nations; all the ends of the earth will see the salvation of our God!" (52:7–10).

From Zion's walls, the scene shifts back to Babylon, where exiles stand poised to march home. A ringing command sounds forth: "Depart, depart, go out from there! Touch no unclean thing; come out from it." Special charge goes to the priests and Levites about to carry the sacred temple vessels back home: "purify yourselves, you who carry the vessels of the LORD!" Yet unlike that first night fleeing Egypt, when they ate in haste and fled in panic, this time "you will not leave in a hurry nor flee in haste." The march proceeds in dignity and total security, because "the LORD goes before you, and the God of Israel is your rear guard" (52:11–12).

Then, right amid the joyous preparations to march home, the curtain suddenly parts to reveal a familiar yet startling figure: the Servant of the LORD. The closing three verses of chapter 52 open the fourth Servant Song—the most famous and profound of them all, continuing straight through chapter 53. God Himself introduces him with a triple crescendo of exaltation: "Behold, My Servant will prosper; He will be raised and lifted up and highly exalted." Yet the very next verse delivers a breathtaking reversal: crowds stared at him in horror because "His appearance was disfigured beyond that of any man, and His form was marred beyond human likeness." Even so, this very figure battered past recognition will "sprinkle many nations, and kings will shut their mouths because of Him"—the proudest monarchs on earth struck speechless in awe, "for they will see what they have not been told, and they will understand what they have not heard" (52:13–15).

## Key Takeaways

**God's grace restores dignity to the trampled.** In chapter 51, Jerusalem lay flat on the ground as a roadway for invaders. At the start of chapter 52, God calls her to rise, shake off the dust, unfasten the chains at her neck, and dress in splendor to sit enthroned again (52:1–2). God does more than pardon guilt or unlock prison bars; He gives back His people's honor.

**At its heart, the Gospel is a royal announcement: your God reigns.** The runner racing across the hills brings not a list of self-improvement chores, but history-changing news: "Your God reigns!" (52:7). Every ounce of peace, blessing, and salvation flows from the reality that the Creator has personally returned to rule over a shattered world.

**Those freed from bondage are called to live clean and walk unafraid.** As God opens the road out of Babylon, His people need not sneak out in terror because He guards both front and rear (52:12). Yet He calls them to make a clean break with Babylon's defilement and keep themselves pure as bearers of His holy things (52:11).

**God's highest glory travels the road of disfigurement.** Within three brief verses at the end of chapter 52, two extremes collide head-on: the Servant who is "highly exalted" is the very One whose face is "disfigured beyond that of any man" (52:13–14). The world applauds raw power and polished appearances, yet God silences earthly kings in stunned wonder before redemption won through wounds.

## Deeper Meaning

**From "good news" on Zion's hills to the Gospel of the New Testament.** Verse 52:7 stands at the fountainhead of the New Testament word *Gospel* ("good news"), paired with parallel cries in Isaiah 40:9 and Nahum 1:15. Paul quotes this exact line in Romans when describing the proclamation of Christ: "How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!" (Romans 10:15). In Ephesians, he weaves the same picture into the believer's spiritual armor: "with your feet fitted with the readiness of the gospel of peace" (Ephesians 6:15).

**Redemption without silver or gold and the precious blood of Christ.** Saying "without money you will be redeemed" in 52:3 means first of all that God pays no ransom to Babylon to retrieve His people. Centuries later, Peter picks up that exact cadence to describe humanity's deeper redemption: not with "perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Peter 1:18–19).

**A new Exodus and the call to come out of Babylon.** The exiles marching out of Babylon carrying the temple vessels (52:11) came true in concrete history when King Cyrus returned the gold and silver sanctuary articles to the returning community (Ezra 1:7–8). Meanwhile, God going ahead and guarding the rear (52:12) directly recalls the pillar of cloud at the first Exodus moving behind Israel to shield them from Egypt's army (Exodus 14:19–20)—except now without the frantic haste of the first Passover night (Exodus 12:11). In the New Testament, Paul quotes "come out from among them" to call believers to holy separation from idolatry (2 Corinthians 6:17), and the same cry sounds one final time in Revelation before Babylon's fall, paired with the pledge that nothing unclean will ever enter the New Jerusalem (Revelation 18:4; 21:27).

**God's name blasphemed because of His people.** Paul quotes 52:5 early in Romans to confront those who boast in God's law while breaking it: "God's name is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you, as it is written" (Romans 2:24). In both contexts, God cares not only for His people's plight, but for the honor of His name among every nation.

**The Servant lifted to God's throne and revealing what nations never heard.** The triple exaltation of 52:13 ("raised and lifted up and highly exalted") immediately recalls the LORD seated on a "high and lofty throne" in Isaiah's inaugural vision (Isaiah 6:1), and resounds in the Christ hymn of Philippians: "God exalted Him to the highest place and gave Him the name above all names" (Philippians 2:9). Furthermore, Paul adopted 52:15 as the guiding motto of his frontier mission to unreached regions: "Those who were not told about Him will see, and those who have not heard will understand" (Romans 15:21).

## Notes & Nuances

**Modern chapter numbers slice the fourth Servant Song in two.** The closing three verses of chapter 52 (52:13–15) are not a footnote to leaving Babylon; they form the opening stanza of the great poem stretching through all of chapter 53 (52:13–15; 53:1–12). The entire poem consists of five three-verse stanzas, opening with God's voice (52:13–15) and closing with God's voice (53:10–12). Readers do best to read straight from 52:13 through 53:12 without pausing at the chapter break.

**Two famous translations of the verb "sprinkle" in 52:15.** The BSB and traditional renderings follow the standard Hebrew meaning of the verb—*sprinkle*, evoking the Mosaic priest sprinkling water or blood to cleanse the unclean. Under this reading, the Servant acts as priest purifying many nations. Meanwhile, the ancient Greek translation (Septuagint) and several modern versions render the verb *startle* or *astonish*, forming a tight parallel with "kings will shut their mouths because of Him." Both senses fit the flow seamlessly: the breathtaking vindication of the battered Servant simultaneously astounds kings and cleanses the nations.

**The stark wording "disfigured beyond that of any man" in 52:14.** The Hebrew text uses unusually graphic terms for ruin and distortion—marred and beaten past ordinary human recognition. Taking this language at full force lets readers feel the jarring shock of 52:14: the very figure about to be raised and highly exalted has first been battered until barely recognizable as human.

**Verse 52:1 does not bar Gentiles from worshiping God.** The pledge that "the uncircumcised and the unclean will no longer enter you" targets invading foreign armies that trampled and defiled Jerusalem's temple—not God-fearing Gentiles seeking the LORD. Right inside this same chapter (52:10; 52:15), and even more explicitly in chapter 56, God promises that all the ends of the earth will see His salvation and that faithful foreigners will be welcomed right into His house of prayer.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, an early church scholar of the fourth and fifth centuries, applied the beautiful feet of the peace-herald in 52:7 to the apostles carrying the Gospel across the world, and read the closing three verses of chapter 52 as the direct overture to Christ's Passion and glory. He highlighted the razor-sharp contrast between Christ's disfigured face in suffering (52:14) and the exaltation that leaves earthly kings speechless (52:13; 52:15). *(Commentary on Isaiah, chapter 52)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome reads 52:7 of the apostles and 52:13–15 of Christ's suffering and exaltation in his Isaiah commentary; exact Latin wording not verified -->

**Rashi**, an 11th-century Jewish master commentator in France, interpreted the Servant starting at 52:13 as the nation of Israel—specifically the house of Jacob—despised and battered in exile until disfigured in the eyes of the world, yet ultimately exalted by God until foreign kings fall silent in amazement. This reading later became the dominant medieval Jewish interpretation, although earlier Jewish texts such as the Aramaic paraphrase *Targum Jonathan* explicitly identified the Servant in 52:13 as the Messiah. *(Commentary on Isaiah, chapter 52)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Rashi reads the Servant in 52:13–53:12 as Israel while Targum Jonathan adds "the Messiah" at 52:13; exact wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, remarked on 52:7 that the dust-caked feet of a runner hold no beauty in themselves, yet become wondrously lovely because of the message of peace and God's reign they carry to despairing hearts. Reaching 52:13, Calvin expressed regret that later chapter divisions cut right across the opening of the Bible's premier prophecy of Christ's suffering and glory, separating it from chapter 53. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 52)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's remark on 52:7 and critique of the chapter break before 53:1 are well-known points in his commentary; exact wording on ccel.org not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, lingered over "sold for nothing, and without money you will be redeemed" in 52:3: sinners sell themselves to sin for zero real gain, and when God liberated Israel from Babylon He owed the oppressor not a single coin—just as in spiritual redemption the price is not gold or silver but the priceless grace of Christ. On 52:11–12, he noted that with God leading ahead and guarding behind, believers need never flee in panic, but step cleanly and calmly out of defilement. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 52)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic pastoral comments by Matthew Henry on verse 3 and verses 11–12; exact text on ccel.org not verified -->

**N. T. Wright**, a British New Testament scholar, regards 52:7 as the historical key to Jesus' entire proclamation of the "Kingdom of God." When first-century Jews heard Jesus announcing the "good news" that God's kingdom had arrived, they heard the very shout ringing over Zion's hills here—"Your God reigns!"—signaling that the LORD was finally returning as King to end Israel's long exile. *(Jesus and the Victory of God, 1996)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking Jesus' Kingdom of God gospel directly to Isaiah 52:7 is a central thesis throughout Jesus and the Victory of God -->

**Alec Motyer**, a British Old Testament scholar, pointed out that the three verbs of exaltation in 52:13 ("raised and lifted up and highly exalted") directly echo the language describing God's lofty throne in Isaiah 6:1. Thus, right before recounting the Servant's deepest wounds in chapter 53, the prophet places the certain outcome before our eyes: the descent into the depths leads directly to the highest throne of God's glory. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Motyer links the three exaltation verbs in 52:13 with the throne vision of Isaiah 6:1; specific page not verified -->
