# Isaiah 28 — The Precious Cornerstone in Zion and the Farmer's Parable

## Story

Leaving behind the end-time panoramas of chapters 24–27, chapter 28 drops the reader straight back into the suffocating political tension of the eighth century BC, as the Assyrian Empire bears down on both the northern kingdom (Israel/Ephraim) and the southern kingdom (Judah). The chapter opens with a cry of "Woe!"—launching a cycle of six woes stretching through chapter 33. Isaiah takes direct aim at Samaria, the proud capital perched atop a hill overlooking a lush valley, calling it "the majestic crown of Ephraim's drunkards" and "the fading flower of his glorious splendor." A mighty agent sent by the Lord—Assyria—is sweeping down like a driving hailstorm, a destructive wind, and bursting floodwaters. That proud wreath will be trampled underfoot, and the glittering capital will vanish as swiftly as "an early fig before the summer harvest": the instant a passerby spots it and plucks it, he swallows it whole (28:1–4).

Right inside that grim verdict shines a two-verse promise for "that day": once earthly garlands wilt and hit the ground, the LORD of Hosts Himself will become "a crown of glory and a diadem of splendor to the remnant of His people." He will grant a spirit of justice to those seated in judgment and courage to warriors turning back the battle at the city gate (28:5–6).

Lest Jerusalem's rulers watch the north collapse with smug satisfaction, Isaiah whips around straight at them: the leaders here too stagger with wine and reel with strong drink! Worst of all, even priests and prophets are swallowed up by liquor—muddling their visions, stumbling in rendering verdicts, sinking so low that "all the tables are covered with vomit; there is not a place without filth!" When Isaiah challenges them, the drunken officials mock him right back: who does he think he's lecturing? Do we look like toddlers "just weaned from milk, just taken from the breast"? They mimic his preaching as tedious baby-talk drills: "Order on order, order on order, line on line, line on line; a little here, a little there!" (28:7–10).

God answers their sneering with an ice-cold warning. Because this people spurned His gentle, plain invitation—"This is the place of rest, let the weary rest; this is the place of repose"—the LORD will speak to them instead through "mocking lips and foreign tongues," the alien speech of foreign invaders. Then the very word they mocked as nursery rhymes will become a stumbling block causing them to fall backward, be injured, snared, and captured (28:11–13).

Next, the prophet confronts Jerusalem's arrogant scoffers face to face. Facing Assyria's threat, they congratulate themselves on buying safety through clever backroom diplomacy and lies—secretly plotting an alliance with Egypt. Smugly they boast: "We have made a covenant with death; we have fashioned an agreement with Sheol." They feel sure the sweeping scourge will pass right over them because they made lies their refuge and falsehood their hiding place (28:14–15).

Against that counterfeit pact, the Lord GOD announces a true foundation laid by His own hand: "See, I lay a stone in Zion, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, a sure foundation; the one who believes will never be shaken." In God's building, the standard is not cunning deceit, but "justice the measuring line and righteousness the level." The coming hail and floodwaters will sweep away their shelter of lies and annul their hollow pact with death. Every patchwork human scheme ends in absurd discomfort: "The bed is too short to stretch out on, and the blanket too narrow to wrap around you." The LORD will rise up fiercely as He once did at Mount Perazim and the Valley of Gibeon—only this time to perform "His strange work" and "His alien task": instead of fighting enemies to rescue Israel, He must move against His own stubborn people. Isaiah begs them to stop mocking before their chains tighten even harder under the decree of destruction settled over the whole land (28:16–22).

Chapter 28 closes in an utterly changed voice—calm, seasoned, like a wise teacher telling a farmyard parable: "Listen and hear my voice; pay attention and hear what I say!" Isaiah asks: "Does a plowman plow for planting every day?" A farmer never endlessly rips up his soil; once he levels the surface, he knows precisely where to scatter caraway, broadcast cumin, plant wheat in rows, barley in its proper spot, and spelt along the border. Harvest works the same way: nobody rolls heavy cartwheels over fragile spice seeds—"caraway is beaten out with a rod, and cumin with a stick." Even harder wheat is threshed under cartwheels or hooves to loosen the husk without grinding it endlessly into powder. God Himself teaches the farmer that careful skill. If a mortal farmer knows the exact tool, exact timing, and exact moment to stop, how much wiser is the LORD of Hosts in disciplining His people—"wonderful in counsel and magnificent in wisdom" (28:23–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Glory built on indulgence and pride wilts like a cut flower.** Samaria crowned a green hilltop like a radiant wreath, yet inside it rotted with drunkenness and conceit (28:1–4). Every human achievement or splendor paraded apart from God quickly fades and gets swallowed up; only the LORD Himself endures as "a crown of glory" for His people (28:5).

**Mocking God's gentle word invites far harsher lessons.** God offered His people an uncomplicated path: "This is the place of rest, let the weary rest" (28:12). Yet Jerusalem's elite dismissed it as childish drivel beneath their sophisticated geopolitics (28:9–10). When people plug their ears against God's clear, tender voice, they leave themselves open to learning from the "foreign tongues" of painful consequence (28:11).

**Every refuge constructed out of lies turns out too short and too narrow.** Verse 28:20 coins a proverb anyone grasps immediately: a bed too short won't let you stretch out, and a blanket too narrow leaves you shivering whenever you tug it over. Clever tricks, ethical compromises, and alliances with worldly powers promise comfort through the storm—yet when floodwaters hit, they expose their own frailty and leave the soul shivering (28:15–20).

**Genuine faith stands on bedrock and refuses to panic.** Amid frantic scheming for foreign lifelines, God sets a tested, precious, unshakable cornerstone in Zion with a crisp promise: "the one who believes will never be shaken" (or "will not panic in haste") (28:16). Trusting God never removes storms, yet it anchors the heart so we don't frantically grab counterfeit lifejackets.

**God's discipline is never blind rage; it carries a farmer's wisdom and restraint.** The closing parable (28:23–29) balances the scorching warnings earlier in the chapter. A farmer does not plow forever because he hates the dirt—he plows to sow; he does not thresh grain to smash it, but to free the precious kernel. God knows every seed's strength and every person's limit; even His "strange work" of discipline remains governed by magnificent wisdom (28:21; 28:29).

## Deeper Meaning

**The cornerstone in Zion—Jesus Christ.** Verse 28:16 ranks among the Old Testament verses most treasured in the New Testament. Paul weaves Isaiah 28:16 together with Isaiah 8:14 to explain why unbelievers stumble over the gospel while believers are saved: "Everyone who believes in Him will not be put to shame" (Romans 9:33; Romans 10:11). Peter quotes this exact verse—"See, I lay in Zion a stone, a chosen and precious cornerstone; and the one who believes in Him will never be put to shame"—pairing it with Psalm 118:22 to identify Jesus as precious stone to believers yet stumbling rock to the disobedient (1 Peter 2:6–8; Psalms 118:22). In Ephesians, the entire Church rises on the foundation of apostles and prophets with "Christ Jesus Himself as the cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20). And hail and floodwaters testing false refuges in 28:17 echo directly in Jesus' Sermon on the Mount about two houses—one built on rock standing fast, one built on sand collapsing when rain, floods, and wind strike (Matthew 7:24–27).

**"Foreign tongues" cited in 1 Corinthians.** The apostle Paul quotes Isaiah 28:11–12 directly when correcting the Corinthian church's misuse of unintelligible tongues in worship: "By strange tongues and the lips of foreigners I will speak to this people, but even then they will not listen to Me, says the Lord" (1 Corinthians 14:21). Paul reminds believers that in Scripture, hearing unintelligible speech functioned as a judgment sign upon hard hearts refusing clear speech; in church gatherings, intelligible teaching that builds up the body must come first.

**"This is the place of rest" and Jesus' invitation.** Verse 28:12 condenses God's tenderness into an inviting call: "This is the place of rest, let the weary rest." Jerusalem's leaders refused that rest in favor of exhausting foreign intrigues. Seven centuries later, Jesus sounded the exact same call to crowds worn down by religious and earthly burdens: "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28–29).

**God's "strange work" echoing Mount Perazim and the Valley of Gibeon.** Verse 28:21 invokes two celebrated victories in Israel's history: at Baal-perazim God broke through Philistine lines before King David like bursting waters (2 Samuel 5:20; 2 Samuel 5:25), and near Gibeon He hurled great hailstones from heaven crushing Amorite armies for Joshua (Joshua 10:10–12). The heartbreak Isaiah exposes is stark: once God unleashed that awesome power *for* His people, yet because they struck a covenant with lies, He must carry out an "alien task"—turning that same power to discipline His own rebellious city.

**The priestly law of sobriety.** The ugly sight of priests and prophets drunk, stumbling in verdicts, and covering tables with vomit (28:7–8) blatantly broke ancient Mosaic law. Right after the deaths of Aaron's two sons, God issued a permanent statute forbidding priests to touch wine or strong drink while serving in the sanctuary (Leviticus 10:9). Once even a nation's spiritual watchmen drown in liquor, society loses its moral compass completely.

## Notes & Nuances

**The graphic scene of drunken, vomiting priests and prophets (28:7–8) refuses polite euphemisms.** Writing "all the tables are covered with vomit; there is not a place without filth" deliberately skips polite language. This is not casual overdrinking by ordinary folk, but a blistering exposé of total rot among spiritual leaders entrusted with interpreting vision and dispensing justice during national crisis. History confirms Samaria fell to Assyria shortly after this warning in 722 BC (2 Kings 17:5–6). Harsh wording tears off the pious mask to expose real spiritual disgust.

**Verse 28:10 ("order on order, line on line") mimics stammering baby-talk in Hebrew.** In the original text, the line rattles off clipped, repetitive syllables: *tsav latsav, tsav latsav, qav laqav, qav laqav, ze'er sham, ze'er sham*. Commentators note two overlapping layers: literally, drunken leaders mock Isaiah for repeating tedious ABC basics as though lecturing weaned toddlers (rule upon rule, line upon line); acoustically, the choppy syllables sound simultaneously like drunken slurring and infantile babbling. Hence in 28:11–13 God turns their pun right back on them: dismiss My clear speech as babble, and you'll soon hear the baffling foreign chatter of Assyrian troops!

**Was "a covenant with death" (28:15) an occult ritual or biting political satire?** Scholars note two main dimensions. Most hear Isaiah putting words into the mouths of Jerusalem's diplomats to unmask their real mindset: secretly signing a mutual defense treaty with Egypt convinced them they'd bought foolproof insurance against Assyria, essentially bragging they'd cut a deal with Death itself. Other scholars point out that ancient Near Eastern culture also practiced necromantic rites seeking protection from underworld deities—strictly forbidden by Moses' Law. Whether political satire or tinged with superstition, the core sin is identical: seeking safety in falsehood and death rather than the living God.

**"Will not panic in haste" versus "will not be put to shame" in 28:16.** Careful readers notice the Hebrew of 28:16 literally says "the one who believes will not act hastily" (rendered "will never be shaken" in the BSB), whereas New Testament quotations read "will never be put to shame" (Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:6). This slight shift reflects the Hebrew verb meaning "to hurry or flee in panic," which the ancient Greek Septuagint used by the apostles translated idiomatically as "not be embarrassed or disappointed." Rather than clashing, both phrases enrich each other: whoever rests on God's cornerstone neither scrambles in panic during storms nor ends up humiliated in disappointment.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, the sixteenth-century German Reformer, drew one of his most celebrated theological distinctions straight from 28:21: God's "alien work" (*opus alienum*) versus His "proper work" (*opus proprium*). Striking, judging, and breaking human pride is only the "strange work" God undertakes reluctantly first; His true, native work flowing from His very nature is showing mercy, giving life, and saving. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, identifies Zion's precious cornerstone in verse 16 unmistakably as Christ—the Church's sole unshakable foundation amid collapsing political schemes. The promise that believers will not panic in haste urges steady, calm waiting for God's timing rather than rushing into illegitimate escape plans.
