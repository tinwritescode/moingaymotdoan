# Isaiah 6 — The Vision of the High Throne and the Call to Go

## Story

The vision is anchored to a precise moment in history: "In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord seated on a throne, high and exalted; and the train of His robe filled the temple." King Uzziah had ruled Judah for more than fifty years, overseeing a long era of prosperity, so his death around 740 BC left deep anxiety just as the Assyrian Empire was pressing closer. At the very moment Jerusalem's earthly throne fell empty, Isaiah saw the true throne on high still filled with majesty: the hem of God's robe alone filled the entire temple (6:1).

Above Him stood the *seraphim*—blazing angelic beings—each with six wings: with two wings they covered their faces, unable to gaze directly at God's majesty; with two they covered their feet in reverence; and with two they flew. They called back and forth to one another in thunderous antiphonal song: "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Hosts; all the earth is full of His glory." The sound was so mighty that the foundations of the temple thresholds shook, and the whole house filled with smoke (6:2–4).

Before that blinding holiness, Isaiah did not feel elevated or proud to have seen heaven. Instead, he collapsed in terror: "Woe to me, for I am ruined, because I am a man of unclean lips dwelling among a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of Hosts." He realized he did not stand outside the guilt of the nation he had just rebuked in chapter 5; his own lips—a prophet's primary instrument—were defiled before the true King (6:5).

Yet God did not leave him to perish in dread. One of the seraphim flew over, lifted a burning coal with tongs from the altar, touched it straight to Isaiah's mouth, and declared: "See, this has touched your lips; your iniquity is removed and your sin is atoned for." Forgiveness stung like fire against his lips, yet it was entirely God's initiative, coming directly from His own altar of sacrifice (6:6–7).

Only after being cleansed did Isaiah hear the voice of the Lord echoing through the heavenly court: "Whom shall I send? Who will go for Us?" Without a second's hesitation, the man who had just cried "I am ruined!" stepped forward immediately: "Here am I. Send me!" (6:8).

Yet the assignment God handed him was strangely bitter. God told him to go and say to Judah: "Keep listening, but do not understand; keep looking, but do not perceive." He was to preach until their hearts grew calloused, their ears dull, and their eyes closed—otherwise they might see, hear, understand, and turn back to be healed. Hearing a commission that seemed to cut straight against his longing to save his people, Isaiah burst out: "How long, O Lord?" God answered plainly that he must keep proclaiming until cities lie in ruin without inhabitants, houses stand empty, the land lies waste, and the people are sent far away into exile. Even if a tenth remain in the land, they too will be burned over again. Still, the final line preserves a slender yet resilient spark of hope: just as a terebinth or oak leaves a stump standing when felled, "so the holy seed will be the stump in the land" (6:9–13).

## Key Takeaways

**Earthly kings pass away, but the true King never vacates His throne.** King Uzziah's death shook all of Judah, yet this vision reminded Isaiah that history's destiny does not rest on a throne in Jerusalem's palace, but with the One "seated on a throne, high and exalted" (6:1).

**The closer we draw to God, the more clearly we see our own sin.** In chapter 5, Isaiah pronounced "Woe" six times on others; in chapter 6, standing before the Holy One, he instantly cries, "Woe to me!" (6:5). God's holy light leaves no room for anyone to stand smugly above others in condemnation.

**Cleansing grace always comes before sending.** Isaiah did not scrub his own lips to qualify as a prophet; God sent an angel with a glowing coal from the altar to touch his mouth and declare his sin atoned for (6:6–7). Only someone who has tasted forgiveness can gladly answer, "Here am I. Send me!" (6:8).

**Faithfulness to God is not measured by immediate outward success.** God's commission promised Isaiah neither cheering crowds nor mass repentance, but persistent rejection until the country lay in ruins (6:9–12). A servant's worth lies in steady obedience, even when God's word seems to bounce off stone.

**Even the severest judgment leaves behind a stump of life.** Though the nation is chopped down and burned over again, the stump of "the holy seed" remains (6:13). God will restart His people's future from that small surviving remnant.

## Deeper Meaning

**King Uzziah's death is bound up with a lesson in holiness.** Scripture records that late in life Uzziah grew proud, marched into the temple to burn incense—a duty reserved solely for priests—was struck with leprosy on his forehead, and lived in isolation until the day he died (2 Chronicles 26:19–21). Setting Isaiah's vision in the very year that king died underscores a vivid truth: mortals cannot treat lightly the holiness of the One who dwells in the temple.

**A vision of the glory of Jesus Himself.** In John's Gospel, after quoting the prophecy of hardened hearts from Isaiah 6:10 to explain why so many saw Jesus' miracles yet refused to believe, the apostle John adds a breathtaking sentence: "Isaiah said this because he saw Jesus' glory and spoke about Him" (John 12:39–41). The New Testament recognizes the One enthroned on high in the temple as none other than the glory of Jesus Christ.

**The song "Holy, holy, holy" resounds all the way to Revelation.** The chorus of the six-winged seraphim (6:2–3) entered the shared liturgy of both Judaism (the *Kedushah*) and Christianity (the *Sanctus*), and rings out again around heaven's throne in the Bible's closing book, where four six-winged living creatures proclaim day and night without ceasing: "Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God Almighty" (Revelation 4:8).

**A sinner's terror before the Holy One.** Scripture had long taught that no one could see God's face and live (Exodus 33:20), making Isaiah's terror entirely understandable (6:5). Centuries later, witnessing Jesus' miraculous catch of fish, the apostle Peter fell at His knees with the very same cry: "Go away from me, Lord; I am a sinful man!" (Luke 5:8).

**The word of hardening in 6:9–10 marks major turning points in the New Testament.** Jesus quoted these exact two verses when explaining why He spoke in parables to crowds deliberately stopping their ears (Matthew 13:14–15). The apostle Paul likewise closed the book of Acts with these same verses when many Jews in Rome rejected the gospel, opening the next chapter of the gospel's advance to the Gentiles (Acts 28:25–27).

**"Who will go for Us?" and the heavenly council.** The plural pronoun in 6:8 recalls God's words at the creation of humanity (Genesis 1:26) and the scene of the LORD enthroned among the host of heaven asking who will carry out His mission in the story of the prophet Micaiah during Ahab's reign (1 Kings 22:19–20).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Isaiah's call appear in chapter 6 rather than chapter 1?** Readers naturally expect a prophet's call to stand on the first page, as in Jeremiah or Ezekiel. Two main explanations are offered: first, chapters 1–5 are placed ahead of it to lay out Judah's deep corruption so readers understand why God hands Isaiah such a severe commission in chapter 6; second, this vision may not be Isaiah's very first prophetic experience, but a special commissioning launching his direct confrontation with King Ahaz in chapters 7–12.

**Why did God tell Isaiah to "make the hearts of this people calloused, deafen their ears, and close their eyes"?** This is one of the hardest, most unsettling passages in the Old Testament (6:9–10). Some worry that God was actively blocking people from repenting. Commentators commonly highlight two points that clarify the text. First, Judah had already chosen stubbornness and closed ears long before (chapters 1–5); God never forces a longing penitent to perish, but allows willful persistence in sin to ripen into just judgment. Second, in Hebrew idiom, the inevitable outcome of a message is often expressed as an imperative: Isaiah had to brace himself for the fact that the more faithfully he preached, the harder closed hearts would grow—just as the same sun softens wax while baking clay rock-hard.

**Who are the seraphim?** The Hebrew word *seraphim* derives from a root meaning *the burning ones*. Across the entire Bible, only Isaiah 6 uses this name for the angels attending God's throne. The fiery imagery around them and the glowing coal taken from the altar highlight the blazing, unblemished holiness of God.

**Why is "holy" repeated exactly three times?** Ancient Hebrew lacked a superlative form like English "-est"; to express absolute intensity, a word was repeated. Doubling a word was already emphatic; tripling it represents the absolute summit: God is utterly other, set apart from all impurity, and supreme over all creation. Many later Christian interpreters have also heard in this threefold rhythm an echo pointing toward the Triune God, though its direct meaning in context is to exalt the unmatched holiness of the LORD.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the fourth-century bishop of Antioch, preached an entire series of sermons on chapter 6 anchored in its opening line, "In the year that King Uzziah died." He sets King Uzziah's presumption—barging into the temple and being struck with leprosy—side by side with the inviolable majesty of heaven's King. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, cites John 12:41 to affirm that the One Isaiah saw seated on the high throne is Christ Himself, thereby reinforcing faith in His eternal deity. Regarding the thorny question of verses 9–10, Calvin does not shy away, acknowledging that when God's word meets stubborn hearts it becomes the occasion of deeper hardening—not because the word is evil, but because corrupt human nature reacts violently against the light, and God justly uses that response as judgment. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's appeal to John 12:41 to identify the enthroned Lord as Christ and his handling of hardening in verses 9–10 are well-known hallmarks of his exposition of Isaiah 6 -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, traces the spiritual sequence unfolding in Isaiah throughout this chapter: first beholding God's glory, next being struck with horror at his own unworthiness, then receiving forgiveness from the altar, and finally volunteering eagerly to serve. According to Henry, anyone who hopes to speak God's word to others must pass under that cleansing coal first, because no heart answers "Here am I" more readily than one newly assured its sins are forgiven. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the progression of vision, conviction, pardon, and willing service is characteristic of Matthew Henry's pastoral exposition of Isaiah 6; not verified word-for-word -->

**Abraham Joshua Heschel**, the twentieth-century Jewish scholar and teacher in America, observes that Isaiah's encounter with God differs sharply from mystical ecstasies that escape the world. Standing before the Holy One, Isaiah does not forget his nation to sink into private peace; instead, he becomes more acutely aware than ever that he dwells "among a people of unclean lips," and steps forward to carry the bitter burden of a nation heading into ruin. *(The Prophets, 1962)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: contrasting historically engaged prophetic encounter with escapist mysticism is a core theme in Heschel's treatment of Isaiah's call in The Prophets; exact page numbers not verified -->

**R. C. Sproul**, the American pastor and theologian, regards Isaiah 6 as the pivotal passage in Scripture for understanding the nature of God. He notes that nowhere in the Bible is God called *love, love, love* or *power, power, power*, yet the angels cry out "Holy, holy, holy." To Sproul, Isaiah's cry "I am ruined!" captures the trauma of a sinful human being suddenly feeling himself disintegrate when standing exposed before the utterly transcendent and pure God. *(The Holiness of God, 1985)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Sproul's analysis of the threefold "Holy" and Isaiah's sense of coming undone is the famous opening chapter of The Holiness of God -->
