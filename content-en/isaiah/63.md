# Isaiah 63 — The One Who Treads the Winepress and Calling God Father

## Story

Fresh off the wedding jubilation of chapter 62, the atmosphere shifts abruptly into an awe-inspiring, chilling vision. As if peering southeast from a watchtower, the prophet spots a towering figure striding into view and calls out: "Who is this coming from Edom, from Bozrah with crimson-stained garments? Who is this robed in splendor, marching in the greatness of His strength?" The figure replies at once: "It is I, proclaiming vindication, mighty to save" (63:1).

As He draws closer, the watchman realizes that deep crimson staining His garments is no ordinary dye, and presses further—why are His clothes stained red like "one who treads the winepress"? The LORD answers in thundering tones: "I have trodden the winepress alone, and no one from the nations was with Me." In fierce wrath He trampled and crushed His adversaries until "their lifeblood spattered My garments, and all My clothes were stained." He explains why He acted: "For the day of vengeance was in My heart, and the year of My redemption had come." Looking around and finding no helper or supporter, "My own arm brought salvation to Me, and My own wrath sustained Me." He trampled hostile peoples in anger, made them stagger in His fury, and "poured out their lifeblood on the ground" (63:2–6).

Then the scene shifts dramatically once more—from thundering judgment to a hushed, aching communal prayer of lament that stretches all the way through chapter 64. Opening the prayer, the community's spokesperson recounts "the loving devotion of the LORD" and "the abundance of His loving devotion" poured upon Israel in days past. Back then God looked on them with loving trust—"Surely they are My people, sons who will not be disloyal"—and became their Savior. So tightly was God's heart bound to theirs that "in all their distress, He too was afflicted, and the Angel of His Presence saved them." Out of "His love and compassion He redeemed them; He lifted them up and carried them all the days of old" (63:7–9).

Yet warm memory collides at once with painful truth: "Yet they rebelled and grieved His Holy Spirit. So He turned and became their enemy, and He Himself fought against them." From the depths of that rupture, the people gaze back across the heroic days of Moses, aching with longing: where is the One who brought them and the shepherd of His flock up out of the sea, who "put His Holy Spirit among them"? Where is the One who sent "His glorious arm" at Moses' right hand, dividing the Red Sea before them to "make for Himself an everlasting name," leading them across the depths "like a horse in open country so they did not stumble"? Back then the Spirit of the LORD "gave them rest, like cattle going down into the valley," winning Himself a glorious name (63:10–14).

Out of memory, the prayer swells into an urgent plea for the present: "Look down from heaven and see, from Your lofty throne, holy and glorious!" The people ask God directly: "Where are Your zeal and Your might?" Why do His tenderness and compassion now seem held back? Left desolate, they cling fiercely to their deepest bond with Him: "Yet You are our Father, though Abraham does not know us and Israel does not acknowledge us. You, O LORD, are our Father; our Redeemer from of old is Your name" (63:15–16).

The chapter ends in raw inner anguish. The people cry out a heartbreaking question: "Why, O LORD, do You make us stray from Your ways and harden our hearts from fearing You?" They beg Him to return for the sake of His servants, the tribes of His inheritance. For only a little while His holy people possessed the land before "our enemies have trampled Your sanctuary." Looking out over the ruins, they feel as though they have long been "like those You have never ruled, like those not called by Your name" (63:17–19).

## Key Takeaways

**God's redemption never shrugs off evil.** Seeing the crimson-stained Warrior striding home from Edom (63:1–6) jolts the reader, especially right after chapter 62's tender wedding songs. Yet verse 63:4 names why clearly: the day of vengeance and the year of redemption belong together. Inside a world where tyrants crush the innocent, a righteous God cannot whisper soft comforts while letting cruelty roam unchallenged.

**God is no icy monarch on high; He hurts with His people's pain.** Verse 63:9 ranks among the tenderest sentences in the entire Old Testament: in all His people's distress, He too was afflicted, lifting and carrying them like a parent carrying a child. Even when Israel rebelled, verse 63:10 does not depict a petty tyrant bristling over bruised pride—it says they "grieved His Holy Spirit." God's wrath springs from wounded love.

**When the present turns pitch-black, faith rekindles by recalling God's past faithfulness.** Standing amid the ashes of a trampled temple, the intercessor in 63:7–14 neither flees reality nor hides Israel's sin. Instead, they do something wonderfully concrete: recounting every act of grace since Moses crossed the Red Sea. Remembering yesterday is not empty nostalgia—it grounds our plea for God to act once more today.

**Honest prayer dares call God Father and ask our hardest questions.** Verses 63:15–19 conceal zero confusion: why has God gone silent, why does mercy feel dammed up, why do our hearts drift and harden? The worshiper dares bring every raw question before God because one anchor refuses to budge: even if Israel's own patriarchs disown us, the LORD remains our Father and our Redeemer from of old (63:16).

## Deeper Meaning

**The Warrior treading the winepress returns on Scripture's final pages.** Revelation borrows the stark imagery of Isaiah 63:1–3 directly for Christ's return in final judgment: riding a white horse, "dressed in a robe dipped in blood, and His name is The Word of God," He "treads the winepress of the fury of the wrath of God the Almighty" (Revelation 19:13–15; cf. Revelation 14:19–20). Meanwhile, "no one was with Me... My own arm brought salvation" (63:5) directly reprises chapter 59 (59:16), while "day of vengeance" and "year of redemption" (63:4) echo the anointed herald's manifesto in chapter 61 (61:2).

**Paul's charge "do not grieve the Holy Spirit" springs directly from this chapter.** The exact title "His Holy Spirit"—rare across the Old Testament—appears twice in back-to-back verses right here (63:10–11). When Paul writes to the Ephesian church urging, "Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God" (Ephesians 4:30), he draws directly on the phrasing of Isaiah 63:10. That phrasing proves the Holy Spirit is no impersonal energy field, but a personal Presence capable of genuine grief when God's people rebel.

**God's shared affliction points ahead to Christ sympathizing with our weakness.** From the burning bush in Egypt, God told Moses, "I know their sorrows" (Exodus 3:7), sent His angel ahead to guide them (Exodus 23:20–21), parted the Red Sea (Exodus 14:21–22), promised them rest (Exodus 33:14), and carried Israel "as a man carries his son" across the desert (Deuteronomy 1:31). Verse 63:9 gathers that entire journey into one profound truth: God steps directly into His people's suffering. In the New Testament, that truth takes flesh in Jesus, the Great High Priest able to sympathize with our weaknesses because He walked through every trial Himself (Hebrews 4:15).

**Addressing God as "Father" in the Old Testament prepares the way for Jesus' prayer.** Across the Old Testament, God is rarely addressed directly as "Father" in prayer—mainly in a handful of passages celebrating Him as Israel's Maker and Redeemer (64:8; Deuteronomy 32:6). Calling out twice with fierce tenderness "You are our Father" in 63:16 marks a rare summit of Old Testament intimacy. Jesus later placed that very name at the center of every believer's prayer life—"Our Father in heaven" (Matthew 6:9)—and the Holy Spirit places within God's children the intimate cry, "Abba, Father!" (Romans 8:15).

## Notes & Nuances

**The graphic bloodshed of 63:1–6 is an intensely challenging passage.** Picturing God trampling nations in rage until blood splashes His robes understandably unsettles modern readers and raises real questions about God's mercy. Three essential observations help us read honestly. First, Edom and its capital Bozrah were not merely a hostile neighbor that gloated, looted, and ambushed Judean refugees when Jerusalem fell (Obadiah 1:10–14; Psalms 137:7); across Isaiah, Edom grew into an emblem for every brutal power defying God (34:5–6). Hebrew adds sharp wordplay too: *Edom* echoes the word for *red/blood*, while *Bozrah* echoes the *grape harvest*. Second, this is apocalyptic poetry portraying ultimate justice: no real "year of redemption" exists for the crushed unless the machinery of evil is dismantled. Third, notice the emphatic line "I have trodden the winepress alone" (63:3): the passage never tells God's people to pick up swords for revenge; ultimate judgment rests strictly in God's hands alone.

**Two famous ancient readings of 63:9.** In unvocalized ancient Hebrew manuscripts, a tiny two-letter word midway through the verse is spelled *not* (*lo'*), while the traditional Jewish reading tradition pronounces it *to Him* (*lo*, sounding identical). The BSB follows the spoken reading tradition: "In all their distress, He too was afflicted, and the Angel of His Presence saved them." Meanwhile, the Greek Septuagint links *not* to the second clause: "It was neither a messenger nor an angel, but His own Presence that saved them." Walking slightly different grammatical paths, both readings reach the same moving truth: God never delegates rescue from a safe distance, but shares His people's pain and saves them personally.

**Why does the prayer ask God, "Why do You make us stray... and harden our hearts?" (63:17).** On a surface skim, the line can sound like blaming God for human sin. Look closely at the context, though: they never deny guilt—verse 63:10 already admitted they "rebelled," and chapter 64 pours out deep confession. Across Scripture, when people persist in rejecting God, one sobering form of judgment is God lifting His restraining hand and letting them slide into hardened hearts (6:9–10). Thus 63:17 is a desperate confession recognizing that unless God holds our hearts fast, we cannot turn ourselves back—akin to Jesus teaching us to pray, "Lead us not into temptation" (Matthew 6:13).

**What does "though Abraham does not know us and Israel does not acknowledge us" (63:16) mean?** Two insights complement each other. First, Israel's legendary patriarchs Abraham and Jacob (Israel) died over a millennium earlier and cannot step out of history to rescue their children now. Second, the battered post-exilic community feels so diminished and disgraced that even their own ancestors would barely recognize them. Either way, the plea anchors hope firmly: believers' security rests never in ancestry or glorious history, but solely in God as Father.

**The prayer does not end at 63:19.** Later chapter divisions cut right across the middle of this long communal lament. Begun at 63:7, the prayer surges straight over the chapter boundary into 64:1—pleading for God to tear open the heavens and come down—closing only at the end of chapter 64. Reading both chapters together lets the full weight of their grief over the ruined sanctuary land cleanly.

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, the pioneering North African Christian writer around AD 200, drew on the crimson-robed figure coming from Edom and Bozrah in Isaiah 63:1–3 while discussing Christ's Passion, seeing a prophetic emblem of Jesus' own body and blood poured out on the cross. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, pushed back candidly against the ancient allegory identifying the blood on the robes in 63:1–3 as Christ's own blood. The text states unmistakably, Calvin noted, that this is the blood of enemies trampled in judgment rather than Christ's sacrificial blood on the cross; forcing strained allegory obscures God's royal power rescuing His people. On 63:17 regarding hardened hearts, Calvin explained that God is never the author of sin, yet justly withdraws His Spirit and guidance from willful rebels—leaving His people with only one hope: pleading for Him to return. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 63)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: two classic, well-known arguments by Calvin in chapter 63 rejecting the Passion allegory on verses 1–3 and expounding judicial withdrawal in verse 17; exact lines on ccel.org not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, lingered deeply moved over 63:9, reminding readers that every affliction touching God's people pierces His own heart like a father watching his child suffer. On 63:16, he noted that in dark hours, illustrious ancestors like Abraham or Jacob can rescue no one; genuine faith looks past every earthly prop to call God directly Father and everlasting Redeemer. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 63)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: two characteristic themes by Matthew Henry on verses 9 and 16; exact text on ccel.org not verified -->

**Jewish synagogue liturgy (Haftarah)** ends the seventh and final *Haftarah of Consolation* (for *Nitzavim*) right at 63:9—stopping immediately after "He lifted them up and carried them all the days of old," right before 63:10 turns to rebellion. That delicate cutoff shows how Jewish tradition cherishes 63:9—the God afflicted in His people's affliction—as the crowning chord closing the season of consolation ahead of the New Year. *(Synagogue Haftarah cycle for Parashat Nitzavim)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: concluding the Nitzavim Haftarah precisely at Isaiah 63:9 is a well-established liturgical fact -->

**John N. Oswalt**, an American Old Testament professor, placed the Edom Warrior of 63:1–6 right beside 59:15–19 to show one unified theological arc: humanity stands utterly powerless to establish justice ("there was no one to help"), forcing God Himself to act alone. Verse 63:4 unlocks any misunderstanding of divine wrath: the "day of vengeance" is no arbitrary tantrum, but the necessary counterpart to the "year of redemption" defending God's people and ending oppression. *(The Book of Isaiah, Chapters 40–66, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Oswalt connects 63:1–6 with 59:15–19 and highlights the pivotal role of verse 4; specific page not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, regards the prayer launching at 63:7 as one of the bravest, most candid laments in the Old Testament. Rather than hiding behind polite clichés amid a burned temple, the community lays God's past saving wonders (63:7–14) squarely alongside today's abandonment (63:15–19), even daring to confront God in 63:17. Such unflinching honesty, Brueggemann argues, proves mature faith—trusting that God's fatherhood is big enough to hold both His children's agony and their protests. *(Isaiah 40–66, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Brueggemann's characteristic exposition of candid lament in 63:7–19; specific page not verified -->
