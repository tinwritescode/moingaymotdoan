# Isaiah 35 — The Blooming Desert and the Way of Holiness

## Story

Right after the dark, smoke-filled devastation of chapter 34, a door suddenly flings wide onto radiant morning. Isaiah's voice flips from courtroom sentence into jubilant song, addressing the very parched, lifeless terrain just unveiled: "The wilderness and the land will be glad; the desert will rejoice and blossom like a rose" (35:1). Barren waste does not merely sprout a few sparse tufts of grass—it explodes in joyful bloom, singing aloud like a living crowd. The stately majesty of northern Lebanon's cedars and the lush greenery of Mount Carmel and coastal Sharon are bestowed on the desert. Inside that breathtaking transformation, land and people alike behold firsthand "the glory of the LORD, the splendor of our God" (35:2).

From reviving nature, the prophet turns toward God's weary, frightened people—ordinary folk battered by war, exile, or looming empires. He calls on them to hold one another up: "Strengthen the limp hands and steady the feeble knees!" (35:3). To hearts trembling with dread goes out a crisp, fearless charge: "Be strong, do not fear!" Courage rests not on their own fragile stamina, but on the approaching footsteps of God Himself: "Behold, your God will come with vengeance. With divine retribution"—echoing the previous chapter's recompense for Zion—yet ending on the warmest note possible: "He will come to save you!" (35:4).

When God arrives, wonder does not stop at dry soil; it touches broken human bodies one by one. "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped" (35:5). Next come lame legs and silent mouths: "Then the lame will leap like a deer and the mute tongue will shout for joy." Right alongside human healing, the poem returns to the earth as though soil and flesh share a single pulse: waters burst forth in the wilderness, and rushing streams cut straight across the desert (35:6).

Reversal runs all the way to the ground floor. Blistering desert sand that once teased travelers with cruel mirages genuinely becomes a cool pool; cracked thirsty ground gushes with bubbling springs. Most remarkable of all is the next line: "In the haunt where jackals once lay, there will be grass and reeds and papyrus" (35:7). The exact rubble jackals claimed as dens in chapter 34 (34:13) now brims with water thick with green reeds.

Across that reborn wilderness stretches a grand boulevard. "And a highway will be there, and it will be called the Way of Holiness." That highway keeps a clear yet gracious rule: the unclean will not travel it, for it belongs to God's redeemed. At the same time, it runs so plain and straight that whoever walks it—"even fools will not go astray" (35:8). Every ancient terror of wilderness travel vanishes too: no lion prowls there, no ferocious beast climbs up to ambush travelers; only the redeemed walk safely home (35:9).

Chapter 35 concludes with the returning procession marching into God's city amid roaring song. "So the redeemed of the LORD will return and enter Zion with singing, crowned with everlasting joy"—wearers of eternal gladness like festival garlands upon their heads. Joy and gladness overtake and surround them, while "sorrow and sighing will flee away" like midnight shadows scattering before sunrise (35:10).

## Key Takeaways

**Judgment is never God's final word.** Set immediately after chapter 34's chilling ruin, chapter 35 shows God's ultimate aim is always renewal, never sheer destruction. He clears away evil and oppression so fresh life can burst into flower in earth's driest deserts (35:1–2; 35:7).

**Courage comes from knowing who is walking toward us.** Saying "Be strong, do not fear!" is no hollow pep talk telling shaky people to grit their teeth harder. Strength rises from the sure pledge that God Himself is coming personally to save His people (35:3–4).

**God's salvation heals both human bodies and physical creation.** Across chapter 35, blind eyes open, deaf ears hear, lame feet leap like deer, and dry sands gush with streams (35:5–7). God is not rescuing disembodied souls out of a disposable world; He restores both wounded people and scarred earth.

**God's highway is simultaneously holy, plain, and safe.** Calling it "the Way of Holiness" means impurity and cruelty have no lane on it. Yet that holiness is never an elitist riddle reserved for intellectuals: God's road lies so clearly marked that even simple folk never lose their way, and no predator can touch the redeemed (35:8–9).

**The believer's journey ends in unalloyed joy in God's presence.** The pilgrimage of the redeemed does not fizzle out in weary resignation; it arrives singing at Zion's gates as sorrow and sighing vanish forever (35:10).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paired mirror paintings linking chapter 34 and chapter 35.** These two chapters weave together like opposite sides of one tapestry. Chapter 34 announces the LORD's "day of vengeance" and "year of recompense for the cause of Zion" (34:8); chapter 35 heralds God coming with "vengeance" and "divine retribution" to save His people (35:4). In chapter 34, Edom's palaces become "a haunt for jackals" (34:13); in chapter 35, "the haunt where jackals once lay" turns lush with green reeds and water (35:7). Everything dismantled in judgment in chapter 34 gets rebuilt greener than ever by the Creator's hands in chapter 35.

**Reversing the blindness and deafness verdict of chapter 6.** At the outset of Isaiah's call, God spoke a heartbreaking sentence over hard-hearted Israel: ears dull, eyes closed, hearing without understanding, looking without seeing (6:9–10). Here in chapter 35, that verdict reverses completely: blind eyes see, deaf ears open, and mute tongues shout for joy (35:5–6; 29:18). What human stubbornness broke, God arrives personally to mend at its root.

**A new Exodus bridging directly into Isaiah's second half.** Water bursting out in the wilderness recalls Moses striking rock in the desert for thirsty Israel leaving Egypt (Exodus 17:6). Yet Isaiah looks forward even more boldly, heralding the themes of Isaiah's second half: a highway across the wasteland, streams in dry earth, and exiles returning home (40:3–5; 41:18; 43:19–20; 11:16). Most remarkably, closing verse 35:10—the redeemed returning to Zion with singing—reappears virtually word for word deep inside the Book of Comfort (51:11), stitching both halves of Isaiah together with golden thread.

**Jesus invokes chapter 35 directly to answer John the Baptist.** Sitting in prison and wrestling with doubt, John the Baptist sent disciples to ask Jesus whether He was truly the Coming One (Matthew 11:2–3). Jesus did not offer a long lecture; He pointed straight to what was happening before their eyes: "The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear" (Matthew 11:4–5; Luke 7:22). Steeped in Scripture, John instantly heard Isaiah 35 ringing through those words and recognized that God's saving arrival had dawned in Jesus. In Mark's Gospel, when Jesus healed a deaf man with a speech impediment and amazed crowds exclaimed "He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak" (Mark 7:32–37), the rare Greek word for the man's speech difficulty matches the exact word used by the ancient Greek Septuagint in Isaiah 35:6.

**Standing steady on the straight road to the New Jerusalem.** The author of Hebrews quotes 35:3 directly to rally persecuted believers losing heart: "Strengthen your limp hands and weak knees," making straight paths so the lame won't be disabled but healed (Hebrews 12:12–13). Meanwhile, the Way of Holiness barring the unclean and leading to joy-crowned Zion free of sorrow (35:8; 35:10) finds its climax on Scripture's final pages: the New Jerusalem where nothing unclean ever enters and God wipes every tear away so mourning, crying, and pain exist no more (25:8; Revelation 21:4; Revelation 21:27).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is addressing whom in 35:3–4?** In Hebrew, the commands "Strengthen!" and "Say!" stand in the plural without naming a specific speaker or audience. Many commentators hear a general charge laid on the whole community of faith: whoever stands firm must steady the trembling hands and knees of fearful brothers and sisters with words of hope. Others hear God commissioning heralds and prophets to proclaim good news—anticipating the tender opening voices of chapter 40. Either way, biblical courage is never rugged solo bravado, but a shared gift believers nurture in one another.

**Should the healings of 35:5–6 be read literally or metaphorically?** Positioned amid singing deserts and mirages turning into pools, readers naturally ask whether blind, deaf, lame, and mute people symbolize spiritual awakening or promise actual bodily healing. Isaiah certainly used blindness and deafness spiritually elsewhere (6:9–10; 29:18). Yet when Jesus arrived, He literally opened blind eyes, unstopped deaf ears, and healed lame legs (Matthew 11:5; Mark 7:35) while treating those physical miracles as living signs that God's kingdom restores human beings body and soul. Both dimensions reinforce rather than rival each other.

**The desert "rose" in 35:1 and "parched ground" in 35:7.** Traditional English translations (including the BSB) render the blooming desert plant as "a rose." Botanists and several modern translations note that the Hebrew term probably denotes a wild Middle Eastern bulb flower—such as desert crocus or meadow narcissus—that blankets barren slopes in sudden color right after rain. Similarly, "the parched ground" in 35:7 literally evokes shimmering heat mirages on scorched sand: deceptive illusions that once mocked thirsty wanderers transform into genuine pools of cool water. Both details underline that God brings real life, never empty mirage.

**Why does chapter 35 sound so closely akin to Isaiah 40–55?** Attentive readers immediately spot how closely chapter 35—blooming desert, wilderness streams, highway to Zion, and 35:10 matching 51:11 word for word—resonates with Isaiah's second half (from chapter 40 onward), even though four historical chapters about King Hezekiah (chapters 36–39) stand in between. Many modern scholars therefore think chapters 34–35 were crafted during the Babylonian exile to bridge Isaiah's two halves. Traditional commentators hold that Isaiah himself wrote chapter 35 gazing past the Assyrian threat toward future return and the messianic era, with 51:11 deliberately echoing his earlier promise. Under either view, chapter 35 plays a vital structural role: capping the first half in shining hope and bracing hearts for the tense crisis of chapters 36–37.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the great fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Latin Bible translator, reads the blooming wilderness and dry land in 35:1–2 as a figure of the Gentile nations—once barren and arid without knowledge of God, now bursting with life and inheriting Israel's splendor through the gospel. In that rabbinic argument, the dead first rise bearing the physical disabilities they carried in life so everyone recognizes they are genuinely the same individuals rather than brand-new substitutes—and immediately afterward God heals them completely, fulfilling Isaiah 35:6. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall the rabbinic argument citing Isaiah 35:6 regarding resurrection followed by healing in Sanhedrin; exact folio number not rechecked -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, pairs chapter 35 tightly beside chapter 34 to highlight prophecy's balance: right after announcing terrifying doom on foes, God immediately steadies His trembling Church. On 35:3–4, Calvin underlines God's charge that believers prop up one another's limp hands and shaking knees—showing faith never walks solo, needing community encouragement anchored in God's coming. At 35:8, he praises the lucidity of "the Way of Holiness": God lays out salvation so plainly even simple folk never wander off, yet persistent impurity cannot step onto it. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 35)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's exposition of mutual encouragement in verses 3–4 and the clarity of the Way of Holiness in verse 8; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, hails chapter 35 as a glowing picture of Christ's kingdom turning desert hearts green and fruitful by grace. Pausing warmly over 35:8–10, he highlights five sterling qualities of "the Way of Holiness": a public highway rather than a hidden alley; a pure road reserved for the redeemed; a plain path where even simple travelers never lose track; a safe road free of prowling predators; and a singing road leading straight into eternal joy at Zion. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 35)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's structured pastoral meditation on the traits of the Way of Holiness in verses 8–10; not verified against the original -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, treats chapters 34 and 35 as twin canvases closing Isaiah's first half, placing the world under wrath opposite creation reborn. He highlights 35:7—where chapter 34's "jackal haunts" (34:13) bloom into watered reeds—as proof grace can reverse earth's bleakest wastelands. Motyer also stresses 35:10 reappearing virtually intact at 51:11, weaving Isaiah's first half tightly into the new Exodus theme of chapters 40–55. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's structural pairing of chapters 34–35, jackal-haunt note on verse 7, and cross-link to 51:11; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 35 as poetry shattering the numbness of an empire-crushed community. Daring to envision parched deserts cloaked in Lebanon, Carmel, and Sharon's beauty (35:1–2), and broken blind, deaf, lame, and mute bodies restored whole (35:5–6), is no escapist fantasy, Brueggemann writes, but a bold act of faith declaring God's power outweighs cruel present reality. The lion-free highway (35:8–9) opens a secure homecoming road for marginalized exiles singing their way back home. *(Isaiah 1–39, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading poetry of hope against imperial despair and highlighting the restoration of marginalized bodies are signature accents in Brueggemann's commentary; exact page numbers not verified -->
