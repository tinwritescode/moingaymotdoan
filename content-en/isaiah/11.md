# Isaiah 11 — The Shoot from Jesse's Stump and the Kingdom of Peace

## Story

Chapter 10 just ended with the LORD swinging an iron axe to topple Assyria's towering, arrogant forest (10:33–34). As chapter 11 opens, right amid that cleared field of felled trunks, a quiet sign of new life emerges: "Then a shoot will spring up from the stump of Jesse, and a branch from his roots will bear fruit." Upon this coming King, "the Spirit of the LORD will rest," bringing three complete pairs of endowments: wisdom and understanding to discern truth, counsel and strength to act decisively, and knowledge and the fear of the LORD to live in harmony with God (11:1–2).

This King rules unlike any earthly monarch. Because He delights in the fear of the LORD, He never renders verdicts by outward appearances seen by the eye, nor lets rumors and whispers sway His ear. Instead, "with righteousness He will judge the poor, and with equity He will decide for the lowly of the earth." His authority needs no brutal armies: "He will strike the earth with the rod of His mouth and slay the wicked with the breath of His lips." Righteousness and faithfulness serve as the twin belts fastened firmly around His waist, upholding everything He does (11:3–5).

Under this righteous King's reign, even the animal kingdom transforms astonishingly. Predators and prey that once hunted and tore one another now share peaceful shelter: "The wolf will live with the lamb, and the leopard will lie down with the goat; the calf and young lion and fatling will be together," and a little child alone can lead them all. Cows graze beside bears while their young sleep side by side, and the lion eats straw like an ox. Even the ancient dread between humans and serpents vanishes: "The infant will play by the cobra's den, and the toddler will reach into the viper's nest." Across all of God's holy mountain, nothing will hurt or destroy anymore, because "the earth will be full of the knowledge of the LORD as the waters cover the sea" (11:6–9).

The promise does not stop at Israel's borders. In that day, "the Root of Jesse" will stand tall as a banner lifted high for all peoples. Distant nations will seek Him out, and "His resting place will be glorious" (11:10).

At the same time, God launches a new deliverance for His scattered people everywhere. The Lord "will extend His hand a second time to recover the remnant of His people"—gathering them home from every horizon: from Assyria, Egypt, Pathros, Cush, Elam, Shinar, Hamath, and the distant islands of the sea. He will raise a banner among the nations to assemble Israel's exiles and gather Judah's dispersed from the four corners of the earth (11:11–12).

Upon their return, the centuries-long rift between the two brother kingdoms will heal completely. Ephraim's northern jealousy will vanish, and Judah's southern hostility will be cut off; "Ephraim will no longer envy Judah, nor will Judah harass Ephraim." Reunited as one, they will prevail together over the neighbors who so often pressed them hard: swooping down on the Philistine slopes to the west, plundering the eastern peoples, laying hands on Edom and Moab, while Ammon submits to them (11:13–14).

To clear a road for the returning remnant, the LORD will reenact the ancient miracle of the Exodus on an even grander scale. He will dry up the gulf of the Egyptian sea, then wave His hand over the River (the Euphrates) with a scorching wind, splitting the mighty waterway into seven shallow streams so travelers can cross "in sandals." Thus "there will be a highway for the remnant of His people" returning from Assyria, just as God once opened a road for Israel's ancestors on the day they came up out of Egypt (11:15–16).

## Key Takeaways

**God's hope often sprouts from places that look completely dead.** David's royal tree was not merely trimmed; it was chopped right down to the ground, leaving nothing but "the stump of Jesse" (11:1). When dynasties decay and all human pride crashes, God causes a fresh shoot to spring straight from that barren stump. Real salvation does not grow out of booming human might, but from God's power to raise life out of ruin.

**True justice begins with the fear of the LORD and defending the vulnerable.** The King in chapter 11 refuses to judge by surface show or rumor; He looks straight at truth and specifically champions "the poor" and "the lowly of the earth" (11:3–4). Power that genuinely fears God always leans toward protecting the defenseless, whereas arrogant power like Assyria in chapter 10 only tramples the weak.

**Lasting peace springs from knowing God.** The portrait of wolves curled beside lambs and children playing by viper nests is not a naive daydream that nature will somehow mend itself. Verse 11:9 pinpoints the cause: violence and harm vanish "for the earth will be full of the knowledge of the LORD as the waters cover the sea." Once humanity's relationship with its Creator is healed, peace radiates out into human relationships and across all creation.

**God both regathers His own people and flings the door wide for all nations.** In the day of Jesse's Shoot, not only are the scattered remnants of Israel and Judah brought home from the four corners of the earth and reconciled to one another (11:11–13), but that very King stands "as a banner for the peoples" whom the nations seek (11:10). God's redemption was never locked inside one ethnic border; it flows outward from His people to the whole world.

## Deeper Meaning

**From "the stump of Jesse" to "the Root of David" in the New Testament.** Isaiah's description of the coming King as a "shoot" and "branch" from Jesse's roots (11:1) launches a thread running through later prophets, as Jeremiah likewise promises God will raise up for David "a righteous Branch" (Jeremiah 23:5). In Hebrew, the word for "branch" in 11:1 is *netser*—echoing the name of the obscure village of Nazareth, which many scholars see behind Matthew's remark that the Messiah would be called "a Nazarene" (Matthew 2:23). At the close of Scripture, Jesus declares Himself, "I am the Root and the Offspring of David" (Revelation 22:16), and heaven's elders hail Him as the conquering "Root of David" (Revelation 5:5).

**The Spirit resting on Jesus and the power of His word.** Long ago, when David was anointed in Jesse's home, God reminded Samuel that "man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart," and the Spirit of the LORD came powerfully upon David (1 Samuel 16:7; 1 Samuel 16:13). That pattern reaches fullness in Jesus: at His baptism the Holy Spirit descended and remained on Him (Matthew 3:16), and in the Nazareth synagogue He announced that the Spirit of the Lord was upon Him to proclaim good news to the poor (Luke 4:18). The prophecy that He will "slay the wicked with the breath of His lips" (11:4) is echoed by Paul regarding Christ's return to destroy the lawless one by the breath of His mouth (2 Thessalonians 2:8), and Revelation depicts a sharp sword emerging from His mouth to judge the nations (Revelation 19:15). Being belted with righteousness and faithfulness (11:5) later becomes part of the believer's spiritual armor as well (Ephesians 6:14).

**Eden restored and creation freed from groaning.** Lions eating straw like oxen and infants playing beside serpents (11:6–8) carry readers straight back to Scripture's opening pages: before sin entered, God gave green plants to every wild creature for food (Genesis 1:29–30), and only after the Fall did hostility arise between the woman's offspring and the serpent (Genesis 3:15). The end of Isaiah repeats this very portrait in its vision of the new heavens and new earth (65:25), while Habakkuk echoes the promise that the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the LORD's glory as the waters cover the sea (Habakkuk 2:14). In the New Testament, Paul taps into this exact hope when writing that the entire creation groans as it awaits liberation from bondage to decay (Romans 8:19–22).

**Gentiles seeking "the Root of Jesse" fulfilled in the Church.** Verse 11:10 contains an extraordinary detail: in verse 1 the King is a *shoot* sprouting from Jesse's stump, yet in verse 10 He is called *the Root* of Jesse—both descendant and the very source sustaining the whole family tree. Paul quotes 11:10 directly from the ancient Greek version to assure believers in Rome that bringing the Gentiles into salvation was God's plan all along: "The Root of Jesse will appear, One who will arise to rule over the Gentiles; in Him the Gentiles will put their hope" (Romans 15:12).

**A second Exodus and the healing of North and South.** After King Solomon's reign, the ten northern tribes (collectively called Ephraim) broke away from David's house and Judah, sparking centuries of division and bloodshed (1 Kings 12:16–20). God's pledge to reunite Ephraim and Judah (11:13) resonates with Ezekiel's vision of two sticks joined into one in God's hand (Ezekiel 37:15–22). Meanwhile, God splitting the mighty River with a scorching wind so His remnant crosses "in sandals" (11:15–16) mirrors the east wind parting the Red Sea long ago (Exodus 14:21–22), heralding a deliverance even greater than the departure from Egypt.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Isaiah say "the stump of Jesse" instead of "the throne of David"?** Jesse was King David's father, an ordinary Bethlehem farmer rather than a king. Naming "the stump of Jesse" (11:1) sends a double message: first, David's royal dynasty would decline so steeply that all royal splendor would be chopped back to its humblest starting point; second, God is wholly able to begin again from zero and raise up a King even greater than David.

**The traditional "seven gifts of the Spirit" grew out of ancient translations of 11:2.** Counting carefully in the Hebrew text and modern English translations, 11:2 mentions "the Spirit of the LORD" followed by six traits in three pairs: wisdom and understanding, counsel and strength, knowledge and the fear of the LORD (seven expressions in all). The ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) and the Latin Vulgate rendered the "fear" mentioned in verses 2 and 3 as two separate virtues—"piety" and "the fear of the Lord"—giving rise to the Church's famous tradition of the "seven gifts of the Holy Spirit," which many also connect to the "seven Spirits of God" in Revelation (Revelation 4:5).

**Should the wolf lying down with the lamb (11:6–9) be taken literally or symbolically?** Two primary approaches have stood side by side for centuries. Many commentators read the imagery poetically as symbolizing transformed human hearts: fierce, wolf-like and lion-like sinners encounter Christ, grow gentle, and dwell in harmony with the vulnerable. Others hold that alongside inner transformation right now, the promise genuinely foretells the literal renewal of nature itself in the new heaven and new earth, when predation and death cease forever (65:25; Romans 8:19–22). Rather than cancelling each other out, the two readings complement one another across the unfolding stages of God's kingdom.

**How should we read the conquering language of 11:14 right after the peaceful vision of 11:6–10?** Right after portraying a world where nothing harms or destroys and the nations seek Jesse's Root (11:9–10), verse 11:14 shifts abruptly to stark military imagery: God's people swoop down on Philistia's slopes, plunder eastern tribes, and subdue Edom, Moab, and Ammon. Many modern readers feel jolted by that contrast. Set in eighth-century BC context, those five names represent the ring of bordering enemies that repeatedly raided Israel, as well as the historic boundaries of David's golden-age kingdom. Commentators generally note that Isaiah speaks in terms immediately vivid to his contemporaries to guarantee that a reunited Israel will no longer be pushed around—while reading verse 14 in the light of verse 10, where the Messiah's ultimate goal is never blind vengeance, but bringing the nations under God's righteous rule.

**The list of nations in 11:11 extends far beyond eighth-century boundaries.** Alongside Assyria and Egypt—the two great powers looming directly over eighth-century Judah—verse 11:11 adds Pathros (Upper Egypt), Cush (modern Sudan), Elam (southwestern Persia), Shinar (Babylonia), Hamath (Syria), and the Mediterranean islands. Because that geographic spread matches the wider Jewish diaspora after the Babylonian exile, some modern scholars think 11:11–16 was expanded later; more conservative scholars note that Israelites had already been dispersed to distant trade and exile regions in Assyrian times, and the prophecy intentionally sweeps north, south, east, and west to declare that God will reclaim His people no matter how far they have been scattered.

## What Commentators Say

**Irenaeus**, the second-century bishop of Lyon in Gaul, devotes considerable attention to the wolf dwelling with the lamb and the lion eating straw in verses 11:6–9. He readily grants that the images can be read figuratively for people of fierce and gentle temperaments united peacefully in the Church through the gospel. **The Babylonian Talmud**, the foundational rabbinic compilation of Jewish law and interpretation, preserves a dramatic historical episode built around verse 11:3. Because the Hebrew word for "delight" in that verse shares a root with the verb "to smell," ancient rabbis read 11:3 to mean the Messiah would judge by supernatural spiritual intuition—"by scent"—rather than relying on sight and hearing. According to the Talmud, when the second-century revolt leader Bar Kokhba claimed to be the Messiah, the sages tested him with Isaiah 11:3; seeing he lacked that supernatural discernment, they concluded he was not the true Messiah. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 93b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the rabbinic account of testing Bar Kokhba with Isaiah 11:3 in Sanhedrin 93b is a famous and widely cited passage in Jewish exegesis -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, points out the subtlety of naming Jesse rather than King David in verse 11:1: the prophecy foretells that David's dynasty would sink so low it would lose every royal trapping and revert to the obscurity of a Bethlehem farming household, so that when Christ rose from that barren stump everyone would recognize a sheer miracle of God. Regarding the peaceful animals in verses 11:6–9, unlike Irenaeus, Calvin reads them primarily symbolically: Christ's gospel tames fierce, selfish, brutal human natures, turning former predators into brothers and sisters living peacefully under one King. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's explanation of Jesse's stump in verse 1 and his symbolic reading of the animals in verses 6–9 as transformed human character; not checked line by line on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses at verse 11:2 to emphasize that the Spirit of the LORD does not merely visit the Messiah momentarily, but "rests" permanently and fully upon Him, equipping Him with complete wisdom and power to govern while enabling Him to pour out grace from His fullness onto all who belong to Him. Reaching verse 11:13, where Ephraim and Judah lay aside envy and hostility, Henry remarks that one of the finest fruits of Christ's kingdom is healing ancient rifts and rivalries inside God's own people. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's emphases on the Spirit resting abidingly on Christ in verse 2 and reconciliation between Ephraim and Judah in verse 13; not checked against ccel.org -->

**Alec Motyer**, the British Old Testament scholar, underscores the tight link between the closing verses of chapter 10 and the opening verse of chapter 11: Assyria's imperial forest crashes down with no hope of regrowth (10:33–34), whereas David's royal tree, though cut to the stump, retains living roots that send up a new Shoot (11:1). He also analyzes the three paired gifts of the Spirit in 11:2 as complete royal equipping—intellectual insight, practical capacity, and deep devotion to God—capable of leading the whole world back to Eden's harmony. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Motyer's contrast of 10:33–34 with 11:1 and his breakdown of the three Spirit pairs in verse 2; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, reads 11:1–9 as a political vision that completely inverts imperial order. Right after Assyria's swaggering plunder in chapter 10, Isaiah sketches a King who rules neither by brute force nor by favoring the wealthy, making the defense of "the poor" and "the lowly" the true test of justice (11:4). According to Brueggemann, once human rule stops crushing the weak and yields to God's righteousness, even the predatory law of survival across creation itself is broken. *(Isaiah 1–39, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading 11:1–9 as an alternative royal order standing opposite imperial violence is a hallmark of Brueggemann's commentary; exact page numbers not verified -->
