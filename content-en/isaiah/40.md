# Isaiah 40 — Comfort, Comfort My People

## Story

Chapter 39 closed under heavy darkness: Jerusalem's treasures hauled away to Babylon and royal sons drafted into foreign palace service (39:6–7). Stepping into chapter 40 feels like walking straight out of a long night into clear sunrise. Warnings of approaching doom vanish; before us stands a nation that has already endured exile, drained and fearing itself forgotten. Into that exhaustion rings a tender directive from heaven's court—God commissioning His messengers: "Comfort, comfort My people, says your God." Speak tenderly to Jerusalem's heart and announce that her hard term of service is finished, her guilt is pardoned, and she has received from the LORD's hand double—full measure—for all her sins (40:1–2).

Immediately another voice rings out across the heavenly assembly: "Prepare the way for the LORD in the wilderness; make a straight highway for our God in the desert!" It evokes the familiar ancient scene ahead of a king's royal arrival: valleys filled in, hills and mountains lowered, crooked tracks straightened, rough ground leveled smooth. Only this time, the highway carved across the arid waste between Babylon and Judah is built for the LORD Himself to lead His people home. As He passes, His glory will blaze openly, and all humanity together will witness it (40:3–5).

Another voice commands: "Cry out!" The one addressed—likely the prophet himself—asks back: "What shall I cry?" The answer sets mortal fragility beside the durability of God's word. "All flesh is like grass, and all its glory like the flowers of the field"; one scorching wind from the LORD's breath turns grass brown and petals limp. This nation—and every towering empire crushing it—is fleeting as wild grass: "The grass withers and the flowers fall, but the word of our God stands forever!" (40:6–8).

Now the herald bringing good news to Zion and Jerusalem is urged to climb a high peak, lift up a fearless shout at full strength, and tell Judah's ruined towns a headline bigger than any empire's news: "Here is your God!" That announcement juxtaposes two startling snapshots side by side. On one hand, the Lord GOD arrives in sovereign power, His mighty arm ruling, carrying His reward and recompense. On the other, that conquering King turns impossibly gentle: "He tends His flock like a shepherd; He gathers the lambs in His arms and carries them close to His heart. He gently leads the nursing ewes" (40:9–11).

From verse 12 onward, a rapid cascade of soaring questions lifts bruised exiles' gaze off their tiny prison up to the breathtaking scale of God. Who cupped all the oceans in the hollow of His hand, measured the heavens with the span of His fingers, scooped earth's dust in a basket, or weighed mountain ranges on scales? Who ever advised the Spirit of the LORD, or sat Him down to tutor Him in justice and knowledge? Beside Him, mighty empires terrifying the exiles shrink to a stray drop slipping off a bucket or a speck of dust on scales; distant islands lift effortlessly in His palm. Even chopping down all Lebanon's cedars for firewood and offering every forest beast wouldn't suffice for His altar. Every nation on earth counts as less than nothing before Him (40:12–17).

If God stands so beyond compare, what image could anyone ever liken to Him? The oracle pivots into razor-sharp satire over Babylon's bustling idol workshops. Rich patrons pay craftsmen to cast an image, hire goldsmiths to plate it in gold, and add silver chains to anchor it upright. Poorer folk pick rot-resistant wood and hire a skilled carver to fashion an idol propped carefully so it "will not topple" (40:18–20). Isaiah presses the people straight: haven't you heard, haven't you known since earth's foundations were laid? God sits enthroned above the circle of the earth while earth's inhabitants look tiny as grasshoppers; He stretches out skies effortlessly like a sheer canopy or pitching a tent to live in. Pompous princes and earthly judges barely take root before one gust of His breath shrivels them and sweeps them away like chaff. The Holy One asks once more: "To whom will you liken Me, or who is My equal?" Then He invites them to lift their eyes to the starry night sky—the very heavenly bodies Babylon worshiped as gods—to see their Creator marching them out like a disciplined host, calling every single star by name with not one missing (40:21–26).

Only after stretching the whole cosmos open before their eyes does God stoop down to touch His people's quietest wound. He echoes the weary sigh Jacob keeps whispering in exile: "My way is hidden from the LORD, and my claim is ignored by my God." He answers with warm assurance: the LORD is the everlasting God, Creator of earth's farthest ends; He never grows weary or exhausted, and His understanding runs past fathoming. Rather than hoarding strength for Himself, He shares it with the drained. Even the strongest young people stumble and collapse exhausted; "But those who wait upon the LORD will renew their strength; they will mount up with wings like eagles; they will run and not grow weary, they will walk and not faint" (40:27–31).

## Key Takeaways

**True comfort starts with sins genuinely forgiven.** God never pats Jerusalem on the head pretending old sins didn't matter. He names the pain and discipline plainly, then declares the sentence finished and iniquity pardoned (40:1–2). Lasting peace comes not from pretending the past never happened, but from grace closing the ledger forever.

**Unlimited power lives together with deepest gentleness.** People easily assume a God capable of weighing mountains and measuring oceans must feel cold and remote. Chapter 40 intentionally places two images cheek by jowl: the mighty arm conquering nations in verse 10 is the very same arm scooping little lambs against His chest and pacing gently beside nursing ewes in verse 11 (40:10–11).

**God towers infinitely above empires and human-crafted idols.** Exiled amid gleaming Babylonian temples and gilded statues, Judeans easily wondered whether Babylon's gods outmatched Israel's God. This chapter flips perspective upside down: superpowers shrink to specks on a scale and idols need carpenters bracing them so they don't tip over, while God calls every star out by name (40:15–26).

**God never grows tired or loses track of His people.** Long seasons of suffering breed one haunting fear: that God stopped looking or ran out of power to help (40:27). This chapter doesn't answer every riddle about pain, yet anchors us in God's own character: He never fatigues and never drops a single sheep from His flock (40:28–29).

**Lasting endurance flows from waiting on the LORD.** "Waiting" in verse 31 never means idle passivity; it means staking active hope on God once our own batteries die. Youthful stamina always hits a wall, yet those leaning on God receive fresh strength—not just to soar on mountaintop highs, but to run hard in crises and keep walking steadily down ordinary long miles (40:30–31).

## Deeper Meaning

**The wilderness voice opening all four Gospels.** When John the Baptist stepped out in Judea's desert preaching repentance, all four Gospel writers instantly cited 40:3 to identify him as the herald clearing the Lord's highway (Matthew 3:1–3; Mark 1:1–4; Luke 3:4–6; John 1:23). Strikingly, in Isaiah 40 that desert highway is leveled for the LORD Himself—and in the New Testament, the One walking down John's prepared road is Jesus.

**The gentle Shepherd of His flock.** God personally shepherding His people and holding lambs close to His chest (40:11) continues Psalm 23's beloved portrait of the Shepherd (Psalms 23:1–3) and Ezekiel's pledge that God Himself will search out His scattered sheep (Ezekiel 34:11–16). Centuries later, Jesus claimed that exact role outright: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep" (John 10:11).

**God's enduring word identified with the gospel.** The apostle Peter quotes 40:6–8 nearly word for word reminding tested believers that earthly glory wilts like wildflowers while God's word stands eternal—adding immediately, "And this is the word that was proclaimed to you" as good news (1 Peter 1:24–25). James likewise borrows wilting grass and falling blossoms to warn wealthy believers against bragging over fleeting riches (James 1:10–11).

**No one counsels the Almighty.** After tracing God's breathtaking plan of mercy embracing both Jew and Gentile, Paul bursts into doxology quoting 40:13: "Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been His counselor?" (Romans 11:33–36). He repeats the line to Corinth as well, reminding believers that unaided human intellect never tutors God, yet by the Spirit believers share the mind of Christ (1 Corinthians 2:16).

**A new Exodus lifted on eagles' wings.** Promising a road through wilderness (40:3–4) and lifting waiting hearts on eagles' wings (40:31) echoes God's rescue of Israel from Egypt, when He told them at Sinai: "You have seen... how I carried you on eagles' wings and brought you to Myself" (Exodus 19:4). And supplying strength to the weary in 40:29 echoes warmly in Paul's testimony hearing the Lord say His power reaches perfection right inside weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9).

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 40 launches Isaiah's second half and a major scholarly discussion on authorship.** Chapters 1–39 largely occupy eighth-century BC Jerusalem under Assyria's shadow. Abruptly in chapter 40, the prophet addresses exiles already sitting in Babylon over a century later. Traditional interpreters hold that eighth-century Isaiah received prophetic foresight into future exile to comfort coming generations, picking up straight from chapter 39's prediction of Babylon (39:6–7). Most modern scholars view chapters 40–55 as written by an anonymous prophet near the end of exile standing within Isaiah's prophetic tradition, later united into one sweeping scroll. Under either view, the finished book welds chapter 40 tightly both to chapter 39 and to chapter 6's heavenly throne room (6:1–8).

**"Received double for all her sins" (40:2) does not mean God overpunished.** Modern readers sometimes wince, worrying God punished Jerusalem twice as severely as deserved. In ancient Hebrew idiom, "double" can mean full, matching equivalence—like a debt receipt folded double once paid in full to prove the account is closed. Other commentators hear the aching hyperbole of a tender father grieving that his child has already suffered quite enough. No careful reading implies God acted unfairly toward His people.

**Two ways to punctuate "in the wilderness" in 40:3.** Following Hebrew poetic parallelism, verse 3 reads: "A voice of one calling: 'Prepare the way for the LORD in the wilderness; make a straight highway for our God in the desert'"—pairing "in the wilderness" with "in the desert." The ancient Greek Septuagint and all four New Testament Gospels shift the pause earlier: "A voice of one calling in the wilderness, 'Prepare the way for the Lord'" (Matthew 3:3; Mark 1:3; Luke 3:4; John 1:23), spotlighting John the Baptist preaching out in the desert. Both nuances enrich each other without altering the heart of the message.

**"The circle of the earth" (40:22) is vivid poetry, not an astronomy textbook.** Some cite this verse claiming Scripture anticipated a spherical globe; others point out the Hebrew word indicates a flat circular horizon. Look closely at the whole verse: enthroned above earth's circle sits right beside stretching out skies like a sheer curtain and pitching a tent (40:22). The poet draws on everyday visual awe to celebrate the Creator's vast majesty over tiny mortals—never trying to lecture on modern physics.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, preaching on John the Baptist identifying himself as "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" from Isaiah 40:3, crafted a famous contrast between *the voice* and *the Word*. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, cites Isaiah 40:18–20 directly to reject every attempt to sculpt or paint images of God. The majesty of heaven and earth's Creator can never be boxed inside wood, stone, or gold, Calvin argues; carving an image of God shrinks Him down to human imagination. In his Isaiah commentary, Calvin also highlights the shepherd portrait of verse 11 showing God stooping extraordinarily close to carry His frailest ones. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 1, Chapter 11; Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, on chapter 40)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's citation of Isaiah 40:18–20 against images appears clearly in Institutes Book 1, Chapter 11; his comment on verse 11 in Commentary on Isaiah is recalled though not checked against ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, observes on chapter 40's closing verses that Israel's whispered doubt in verse 27—fearing their way lies hidden from God—is a universal temptation during drawn-out trials. He also traces the striking order in verse 31: waiting on God equips believers not only to "mount up with wings like eagles" in exhilarating moments, but also to "run" during urgent races and keep "walking" steadily through ordinary daily obedience without burning out. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Isaiah 40)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's reflections on 40:27 and the eagle-run-walk sequence in 40:31; not verified against ccel.org -->

**Brevard Childs**, American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 40's opening eight verses as reopening the exact heavenly throne-room council first witnessed in Isaiah 6. Where chapter 6's heavenly voice commissioned judgment and hardening until cities lay ruined, chapter 40 issues fresh orders from the same court: the penalty is paid, comfort My people. That hinge reveals Isaiah as one seamless theological tapestry moving from judgment into redemption. *(Isaiah, Old Testament Library, 2001)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Isaiah 40:1–8 as directly reopening the heavenly council of Isaiah 6 is one of Childs's signature and widely cited insights -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament professor, sees Isaiah 40 as daring poetry designed to jolt a paralyzed, exiled community back awake. Exiles in Babylon hadn't just lost real estate—they had lost the imagination that any other future was possible, assuming Babylon's empire and gods held final say. Chapter 40's torrent of cosmic questions shatters that cramped worldview so they dare trust an unthinkable homecoming. *(Isaiah 40–66, Westminster Bible Companion, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading Isaiah 40–55 as poetic imagination breaking exilic paralysis is Brueggemann's governing theme in this volume; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Richard Bauckham**, British New Testament scholar, points to Isaiah 40:12–26's celebration of the Creator as bedrock for ancient Jewish and early Christian monotheism. Israel's God reveals His unique identity through two powers shared with no other being: creating all things entirely alone without counselor, and ruling every nation across history. *(Jesus and the God of Israel, 2008)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing Isaiah 40–55's identity of the sole Creator and Ruler is the central thesis of Bauckham's book -->
