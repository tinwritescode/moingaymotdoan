# Isaiah 66 — Heaven's Throne, a Trembling Heart, and All Nations Gathered

## Story

Chapter 66 brings the entire book of Isaiah to a close with a panorama at once breathtakingly vast and soberingly sharp, opening straight away with a penetrating question about the temple: "Heaven is My throne, and earth is My footstool. What kind of house will you build for Me? Or where will My place of repose be?" The LORD declares that His own hand made everything that exists; what draws His gaze is never imposing stone walls, but a human heart: "This is the one I will esteem: he who is humble and contrite in spirit, who trembles at My word" (66:1–2).

Set directly opposite that humble spirit stands hollow, two-faced worship. When people insist on walking their own paths and delighting in detestable practices, even technically valid sacrifices grow revolting before God: "Whoever slaughters an ox is like one who slays a man; whoever sacrifices a lamb is like one who breaks a dog's neck; whoever presents a grain offering is like one who offers pig's blood; whoever offers frankincense is like one who blesses an idol." Because they refused to answer when God called, ignored Him when He spoke, and deliberately chose what displeased Him, God will bring straight down upon them the very terrors they dread (66:3–4).

God turns tenderly to reassure His faithful remnant—those "who tremble at His word." Right inside their own nation, their own brothers hate and exclude them for the sake of God's name, mocking them with bitter sarcasm: "Let the LORD be glorified, that we may see your joy!" God promises those scoffers themselves will be put to shame. Suddenly an uproar thunders from the city and the temple—the voice of the LORD rising to repay His enemies in full (66:5–6).

Abruptly the scene opens onto a birth miracle unlike anything ever witnessed: Zion "before she was in labor, she gave birth; before she was in pain, she delivered a boy." Astounded, the prophet cries out: "Can a country be born in a day or a nation be delivered in an instant?" Yet the moment Zion goes into labor, she brings forth a throng of children. The LORD assures them that once He brings a birth to the very moment of delivery, He will never shut the womb or stop halfway (66:7–9).

Out of that wonder pours an affectionate invitation to everyone who ever loved and mourned for Jerusalem: rejoice and be glad with her! They will nurse to full satisfaction "at her comforting breasts" and drink deeply from her overflowing abundance. God promises to extend peace to Jerusalem "like a river" and pour the wealth of nations into her "like a flowing stream." Her children will be carried on the hip and bounced tenderly on the knee, crowned by one of the warmest promises in the entire Old Testament: "As a mother comforts her son, so will I comfort you, and you will be comforted over Jerusalem." Their hearts will rejoice and their bones will "flourish like new grass" as God's hand is made known to His servants while His fury blazes against His foes (66:10–14).

Then judgment breaks forth in full majesty. The LORD arrives in fire with chariots churning "like a whirlwind," executing judgment on all flesh by fire and sword. Those who sanctify and purify themselves for pagan garden cults—marching behind a cult leader while "eating the flesh of pigs, rats, and other detestable things"—will perish together (66:15–17).

Right through the smoke of judgment, the horizon widens to earth's farthest shores. The LORD announces the hour has arrived to gather "all nations and tongues" to behold His glory. Setting a sign among them, He dispatches survivors as emissaries to the most remote regions of the ancient map—Tarshish, Put, Lud, Tubal, Javan, and distant coastlands—places that have never heard of His fame or seen His glory, to proclaim His splendor among the nations (66:18–19). In answer, those very nations bring back scattered Israelites from every land as a clean offering to God's holy mountain Jerusalem—on horses, in chariots and wagons, on mules and camels. Then drops the boldest climax of all in verse 21: "And I will also select some of them as priests and Levites, says the LORD" (66:20–21).

Isaiah closes with an eternal horizon placing two starkly opposite destinies side by side. Just as the "new heavens and new earth" God creates endure permanently before Him, so will His people's name and descendants endure; from one New Moon to another and from one Sabbath to another, "all mankind will come to worship before Me" (66:22–23). Yet the final verse pauses on a chilling sight outside the city walls: worshipers step out and look upon the corpses of those who rebelled against God, "for their worm will never die, their fire will never be quenched, and they will be a horror to all mankind" (66:24).

## Key Takeaways

**God's true dwelling is not a grand building, but a humble heart trembling at His word.** The One whose throne is heaven and whose footstool is earth needs no human masonry to house Him (66:1–2). Far above gilded walls, He cherishes the one who is "humble and contrite in spirit, who trembles at My word" (66:2). By contrast, grand sacrifices paired with a heart fond of evil look to God no better than murder or pig's blood (66:3).

**God's love is at once fierce as a warrior and tender as a mother.** Right beside the LORD arriving in fire and whirlwind (66:15), chapter 66 paints one of Scripture's gentlest scenes: carrying His children on the hip, bouncing them on the knee, and pledging, "As a mother comforts her son, so will I comfort you" (66:12–13). Anyone hated or cast out even by religious peers for honoring God's word (66:5) discovers unshakable comfort in His arms.

**God's salvation shatters ethnic barriers and religious monopoly.** Isaiah never ends with an insular Israel gloating over destroyed outsiders. Instead, God dispatches messengers to lands that have never heard His name to proclaim His glory (66:19), gathers every language to worship Him (66:18; 66:23), and even appoints some from among them as "priests and Levites" (66:21)—an earthshaking breakthrough beyond old bloodline restrictions.

**The entire book of Isaiah ends with a decisive fork between two eternal outcomes.** Verses 66:23–24 set two destinies shoulder to shoulder with no gray middle ground: all humanity streaming into endless worship inside the new creation, versus the grim end of defiant rebellion. The closing note never aims at cheap dread, but presses home how gravely our response to God's word matters.

## Deeper Meaning

**Stephen and Jesus quote 66:1–2 to widen our view of temple and humility.** Dragged before the Sanhedrin on charges of speaking against the temple, Stephen quoted Isaiah 66:1–2 directly to prove that the Most High never lives confined inside houses built by human hands (Acts 7:48–50; cf. 1 Kings 8:27; Acts 17:24–25). Earlier, in the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus echoed heaven as God's throne and earth as His footstool when forbidding reckless oaths (Matthew 5:34–35), and His very first beatitude—"Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matthew 5:3)—resonates straight with the "humble and contrite in spirit" whom God esteems (66:2; cf. 57:15). Meanwhile, rejecting hypocritical sacrifices in 66:3 closes a full circle right back to Isaiah's opening chapter (1:11–14).

**Zion's miraculous birth and a new priesthood in the New Testament.** Jerusalem as mother suddenly bearing abundant children beyond imagining (66:7–13) carries straight into Paul's portrait of "the Jerusalem above" as our mother—the once-barren woman now outnumbering the married one (Galatians 4:26–27). Still more striking is 66:20–21: Paul directly invokes this picture of Gentiles presented as a clean offering to God to describe his own missionary ministry—serving as a priest of the Gospel "so that the Gentiles might become an offering acceptable to God" (Romans 15:16). And God's bold promise to select priests and Levites from across the nations (66:21) blooms completely in the New Testament church, which Peter names "a royal priesthood" gathered from every people (1 Peter 2:9).

**The new creation alongside Jesus' sober warning of the undying worm and unquenched fire.** The promise of enduring "new heavens and new earth" (66:22) carries straight into New Testament hope (2 Peter 3:13; Revelation 21:1). At the exact same time, Jesus Himself quotes 66:24 verbatim—"where their worm never dies, and the fire is never quenched"—when solemnly urging disciples to cut off every temptation leading toward hell (Mark 9:48). The most compassionate Person in all Scripture uses Isaiah's closing verse to underscore how deeply serious it is to reject God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Is God forbidding Jews from rebuilding the temple after exile in 66:1–3?** Scholars have debated this passage vigorously. Elsewhere in Isaiah (44:28) and throughout Haggai and Zechariah, God actively commands Jerusalem's temple to be rebuilt! Why then does 66:1–3 ask "What kind of house will you build for Me?" and compare ox and lamb offerings to murder or breaking a dog's neck? Most interpreters clarify that God never rejects the temple or His own ordained worship system, but confronts the *twisted mindset* of builders who imagined constructing a grand house could domesticate God, buy His favor, and cover up secret garden idolatry (66:3; 66:17)—even while despising and expelling faithful brothers who trembled at His word (66:5).

**Selecting "some of them" as priests and Levites in 66:21: who is "them"?** Grammatically in Hebrew, "them" in 66:21 can point back to two antecedents in 66:20: either the *Gentile nations* escorting Israel home, or *your brothers*—non-Levitical Jews returning from dispersion. Under either reading—and most modern scholars favor Gentiles, or both groups united—the declaration is breathtakingly radical beside old covenant law restricting priesthood strictly to Aaron's descendants and the tribe of Levi, throwing open direct priestly access before God's altar to all humanity.

**The stark closing verse (66:24) and a remarkable synagogue tradition.** Many modern readers feel jolted that a book overflowing with soaring comfort ends on corpses, undying worms, and unquenched fire (66:24). Read in its historical and apocalyptic frame, just south of Jerusalem lay the Valley of Hinnom (later giving us *Gehenna*—"hell"), once notorious for child sacrifice and later associated with smoldering refuse and defilement. The prophet invokes that grim picture not to stoke glee over enemies, but as the final seal guaranteeing that rebellion and evil are quarantined forever outside the holy city, unable to spoil the new heavens and new earth. Intriguingly, because 66:24 feels so severe, Jewish synagogue liturgy (noted in the *Masorah*) follows a custom of reading verse 66:23 aloud a second time immediately after 66:24 ("from one Sabbath to another, all mankind will come to worship before Me") so public Scripture reading ends on hope and worship—the same custom practiced at the end of Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, and Malachi.

**Where are the distant lands named in 66:19?** The list maps out the farthest edges of the ancient Mediterranean horizon: *Tarshish* lies in the far west (often linked with southern Spain), *Put* in North Africa, *Lud* in Asia Minor (modern Turkey), *Tubal* northeast near the Black Sea, and *Javan* is Greece along with "distant coastlands" across the Mediterranean. Naming these remote corners declares that the good news of the LORD's glory will reach the farthest rim of the world.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, connected 66:1–2 immediately with Stephen's defense in Acts, observing that the true temple where God delights to dwell is a humble heart trembling at His word. **Rashi**, the renowned 11th-century Jewish scholar in France, read the opening lines (66:1–3) as a fierce rebuke against hypocrisy: building a temple or offering sacrifices while harboring wicked desires makes every animal offering as repulsive before God as murder or slaughtering a dog. On 66:23–24, Rashi noted the liturgical custom of repeating verse 23 after verse 24 so Isaiah closes on comfort and universal worship in the messianic era, while understanding the undying worm and unquenched fire as enduring punishment awaiting rebels after death. *(Commentary on Isaiah, chapter 66)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Rashi's critique of hypocrisy in 66:1–3 and traditional reading of 66:23–24; original Hebrew text not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, prized 66:2 as the golden yardstick separating genuine worship from outward religious show. "Trembling at My word," Calvin explained, is not slavish terror, but deep reverence moving a person to bow in ready obedience to everything God speaks. He also wrote warmly on 66:13 ("As a mother comforts her son, so will I comfort you"): God gladly stoops to borrow humanity's tenderest maternal love so His people can taste mercy far exceeding their imagination. *(Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 66)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: characteristic comments by Calvin on 66:2 distinguishing true worship from show and warm exposition of maternal comfort in 66:13; exact wording on ccel.org not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, drew a poignant lesson from 66:5, where faithful believers are hated and expelled by their own "brothers" in the name of God's glory. The sharpest persecution often comes not from open pagans, Henry remarked, but from religious insiders who despise sincere reverence for God—yet those who tremble at His word need never retaliate, since the LORD Himself will vindicate them. On 66:22–24, Henry underscored the book's crowning contrast between believers' endless joy in worship and rebels' unending ruin. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Isaiah chapter 66)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: hallmark exposition by Matthew Henry on persecution by religious brothers in 66:5 and the final contrast in 66:22–24; exact lines on ccel.org not verified -->

**Alec Motyer**, a British Old Testament scholar, traced the masterful literary ring closing the entire book of Isaiah: chapter 66 intentionally recalls motif after motif from chapter 1—hypocritical sacrifices God loathes (1:11–14; 66:3), New Moons and Sabbaths (1:13; 66:23), pagan garden shrines (1:29; 66:17), and unquenchable fire consuming rebels (1:31; 66:24). In doing so, chapter 66 binds all sixty-six chapters into one unified message: God purges Zion of hypocrisy, gathers all nations into a renewed worshiping family, and establishes an eternal new heaven and new earth. *(The Prophecy of Isaiah, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: identifying the deliberate framing parallels linking Isaiah 1 and Isaiah 66 is a celebrated hallmark of Motyer and Isaiah structural studies -->

**Brevard Childs**, an American Old Testament scholar, stressed that chapter 66 speaks simultaneously to sharp internal post-exilic tensions over rebuilding the temple and flings the door wide open toward the whole world's future. Verse 66:21—electing priests and Levites directly from the nations—marks an unprecedented breakthrough in the Old Testament, shattering closed boundaries to unveil God's ultimate aim: transforming all humanity into one worshiping community before His throne. *(Isaiah, Old Testament Library, 2001)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Childs's analysis of post-exilic conflict in 66:1–5 and the radical universal priesthood of 66:21; specific page not verified -->
