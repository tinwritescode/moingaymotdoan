# Jonah 3 — A Five-Word Sermon and Nineveh's Astonishing Repentance

## Story

Scarcely has Jonah set foot back on dry land when the word of the LORD comes to him "a second time." God's commission stands unchanged to a single syllable: arise, go to "the great city of Nineveh," and proclaim the exact message He commands. This time Jonah neither argues nor buys a boat ticket elsewhere. He gets up and walks straight to Nineveh in obedience to the LORD (3:1–3).

Scripture describes Nineveh as "an exceedingly great city, requiring a three-day journey to cross." Walking just one day into the city, Jonah begins to proclaim his message. The prophet's sermon is astonishingly brief—literally just five words in Hebrew: "Forty more days and Nineveh will be overturned!" No lengthy explanation, no plea to repent, not even the LORD's covenant name or a single hint of mercy (3:3–4).

Yet the response of this entire pagan capital proves the greatest miracle in the whole book. Hearing that stark five-word warning, "the people of Nineveh believed God." At once they proclaim a fast and put on coarse sackcloth to express genuine grief and repentance—from the greatest nobles down to the humblest citizens (3:5).

Word swiftly reaches the king of Nineveh. The mighty monarch of the Assyrian Empire immediately rises from his throne, takes off his royal robes, wraps himself in sackcloth, and sits down in ashes. Next, he and his nobles issue a royal decree across the city: neither human nor beast, neither herd nor flock, may taste a bite of food, graze, or drink water; both people and animals alike must be covered in sackcloth. Far more important than outward display, the king urges everyone to cry out earnestly to God and "let every one turn from his evil way and from the violence in his hands." Refusing to presume on forgiveness, the king clings to humble hope: "Who knows? God may turn and relent; He may turn from His fierce anger, so that we will not perish" (3:6–9).

God looks past mere sackcloth and empty stomachs straight to their transformed actions: He sees "how they turned from their evil ways." Therefore God relents and does not bring upon the city the disaster He had threatened (3:10).

## Key Takeaways

**God is the God of second chances.** The opening sentence of chapter 3—"Then the word of the LORD came to Jonah a second time" (3:1)—overflows with grace. After Jonah's blatant rebellion and flight, God does not toss him aside, but patiently entrusts him with the very same mission again.

**The power of God's word never depends on the preacher's enthusiasm.** Jonah delivered a curt, bare, reluctant sermon, half-hoping the city would still be wiped out. Yet an entire imperial capital fell to its knees in repentance. When God moves in human hearts, His own word carries transforming power—not the messenger's polish or passion.

**True repentance goes beyond tears to change real behavior.** Nineveh wore sackcloth, sat in ashes, and fasted, yet the king grasped exactly what God looks for: turning away "from his evil way and from the violence in his hands" (3:8). And verse 3:10 specifies that God relented not merely because they skipped meals, but because He saw "how they turned from their evil ways."

**God's warnings always carry an unspoken invitation to turn back.** On the surface, "Forty more days and Nineveh will be overturned!" sounded like a sealed death warrant. Yet granting forty full days—instead of instant fire like Sodom—was itself a generous window of grace giving them time to repent.

## Deeper Meaning

**The people of Nineveh will rise as witnesses on judgment day.** In the New Testament, Jesus cites this extraordinary repentance directly to rebuke His own hard-hearted generation: Nineveh repented at Jonah's brief preaching, yet One far greater than Jonah now stands right before them and they still refuse to believe (Matthew 12:41; Luke 11:32).

**God's universal principle of mercy.** God relenting and sparing Nineveh in 3:10 illustrates perfectly the principle later stated explicitly through Jeremiah: whenever God announces He will uproot and destroy a nation, if that nation turns from its evil, God will relent from the disaster He intended to bring upon it (Jeremiah 18:7–8).

**Grace reaches right across the bitterest human enmities.** In Israel's eyes, Assyria stood as their cruelest enemy. Sending a prophet straight into the enemy's capital to rescue them foreshadows the gospel of Christ—good news not reserved for moral insiders or one chosen nation, but thrown wide open to the farthest and most sinful peoples on earth (Ephesians 2:13–17).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does it mean that God "relented" (or "repented" in older translations) in 3:10?** Saying God *relented* never means God made an error and felt remorse, since Scripture clearly states God is not a mortal who changes His mind (1 Samuel 15:29). In Hebrew, the verb expresses *being moved with compassion* and *withholding threatened judgment*. God's righteous and merciful nature never shifts; rather, humanity shifted from arrogance to repentance, shifting their relationship with God from judgment to mercy.

**How huge was Nineveh to require "a three-day journey"?** Ancient Nineveh's inner walled city (modern Mosul, Iraq) spanned roughly twelve kilometers around, while the broader metropolitan district embracing surrounding suburbs stretched across dozens of kilometers. "A three-day journey" likely describes either the time needed to traverse and preach across the entire sprawling greater city or, in ancient diplomatic custom, a standard three-day official visit.

**Why were cattle and sheep forced to fast and wear sackcloth?** Picturing livestock draped in sackcloth in 3:7–8 sounds faintly humorous at first, yet the ancient Greek historian Herodotus confirms that Persians and several ancient Near Eastern peoples genuinely draped mourning cloth over horses and herds during extreme national bereavement. Inside Jonah's story, the detail magnifies Nineveh's sweeping, wholehearted repentance—standing in sharp contrast to Jonah's own stubborn heart.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, marvels at the king of Nineveh's humble hope in 3:9 ("Who knows? God may turn and relent..."). Even without a single promise of mercy spoken by Jonah, Nineveh clung to a slender thread of hope in God's compassion and repented—putting to shame modern listeners who hold crystal-clear gospel promises yet still hesitate to turn back. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Jonah chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's famous contrast between Nineveh's slender hope and the clear promises of the gospel is one of his most widely quoted comments on Jonah 3 -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer, unpacks 3:10 carefully on God *relenting* from disaster. God is never inconsistent, Calvin argues, because every scriptural threat of judgment carries an implicit condition—if sinners repent, He spares them; speaking of God changing His mind accommodates human understanding to help us grasp how our standing before Him shifts when we turn home. *(Commentaries on the Twelve Minor Prophets, on Jonah chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's explanation of conditional judgment warnings and accommodated language for God relenting is a celebrated hallmark of his Jonah 3 commentary -->

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar and Church Father, highlights the stark contrast between Israel repeatedly rejecting prophet after prophet and pagan Nineveh believing a foreign stranger on the spot. He also points out a rich double sense in the Hebrew verb *overturned* in 3:4: Nineveh was not overthrown in physical ruin, yet was completely *turned upside down* from wickedness into righteousness. *(Commentary on Jonah, on chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome is well known for highlighting the double meaning of "overturned" in Jonah 3:4; have not checked exact passage wording -->
