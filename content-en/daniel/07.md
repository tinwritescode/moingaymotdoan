# Daniel 7 — Four Beasts, the Ancient of Days, and the Son of Man

## Story

In the first year of King Belshazzar of Babylon, Daniel had a dream and wrote down the essence of his vision. In the night he watched the four winds of heaven churning up the great sea, and four massive beasts climbed out of the sea, each one completely different from the others (7:1–3).

The first resembled a lion with eagle's wings; its wings were torn off, it was lifted up to stand on two feet like a human, and a human mind was given to it. The second looked like a bear raised up on one side, gripping three ribs in its mouth, while a voice told it to get up and devour much flesh. The third resembled a leopard with four bird wings on its back and four heads, and dominion was given to it. The fourth beast was terrifying, dreadful, and overwhelmingly strong, with huge iron teeth that devoured and crushed and feet that trampled whatever remained; on its head stood ten horns. While Daniel watched closely, a little horn sprouted among them, uprooting three of the earlier horns; this little horn had eyes like human eyes and a mouth speaking boastful things (7:4–8).

Suddenly the scene cuts to a heavenly courtroom. Thrones are set in place, and the Ancient of Days takes His seat: His clothing white as snow, His hair like pure wool, His throne ablaze with fire, its wheels burning flames, and a river of fire flowing out from before Him. Thousands upon thousands attend Him, and ten thousand times ten thousand stand before Him. The court convenes, and the books are opened. Because of the arrogant words spoken by the little horn, the fourth beast is slain and its body thrown into the blazing fire; the other beasts are stripped of authority yet allowed to live for a season (7:9–12).

Daniel watches again as "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds of heaven and approaches the Ancient of Days. He is given authority, glory, and sovereign power so that all peoples, nations, and languages worship and serve Him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion that will never pass away, and His kingdom will never be destroyed (7:13–14).

Deeply troubled in spirit, Daniel asks one of the attendants standing near what all this truly means. The attendant explains: the four great beasts are four kings or kingdoms that will arise on earth, yet ultimately the saints of the Most High will receive the kingdom and possess it forever. Daniel wants to know more about the terrifying fourth beast with iron teeth, bronze claws, ten horns, and the little horn that waged war against the saints and prevailed over them until the Ancient of Days arrived to pronounce judgment (7:15–22).

The angel clarifies further: the fourth beast is a fourth kingdom on earth that will devour and crush the whole world. The ten horns are ten kings emerging from that kingdom, followed by another king who will subdue three kings. He will speak blasphemies against the Most High, wear down the saints, and try to change set times and laws; the saints will be handed over to him for "a time, times, and half a time." Then the heavenly court will sit in judgment, strip away his power, and destroy him completely. At last the kingdom, dominion, and greatness of the kingdoms under the whole heaven will be given to the people of the saints of the Most High, whose kingdom lasts forever. Waking with pale face and troubled thoughts, Daniel kept the whole matter closely in his heart (7:23–28).

## Key Takeaways

**The same history viewed through heaven's eyes.** In chapter 2, Nebuchadnezzar saw four empires as a gleaming metal monument. In chapter 7, Daniel sees those same empires from God's perspective: nothing glorious, but ferocious beasts crawling up out of the chaotic sea, bent on devouring and trampling.

**Evil and persecution operate under God's strict time limit.** The little horn grows bold enough to attack and overpower the saints and attempt to rewrite laws and sacred times (7:21; 7:25). Yet its lease on power is strictly capped: it lasts only *a time, times, and half a time* before heaven's court brings it down permanently.

**The Supreme Court sits in heaven, not in earthly palaces.** Right while the little horn is boasting loudest, the camera cuts quietly to the fiery throne of the Ancient of Days (7:9–10). Every regime imagining itself untouchable on earth will stand accountable before the open books of heaven.

**The everlasting kingdom belongs to the Son of Man and His saints.** In sharp contrast to four savage beasts climbing from the sea, the One who receives the final kingdom bears a human likeness and arrives on the clouds of heaven (7:13–14). And the kingdom He receives is shared with "the saints of the Most High" (7:18; 7:27).

## Deeper Meaning

**The title "Son of Man" that Jesus chose for Himself.** Across all four Gospels, Jesus' favorite self-designation is *the Son of Man*. On the night of His trial before the Sanhedrin, when the high priest demanded whether He was the Christ, Jesus quoted Daniel 7:13 directly: "You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven" (Mark 14:62; Matthew 26:64). His Olivet Discourse likewise repeats the vision of the Son of Man coming on heaven's clouds with great power and glory (Matthew 24:30; Mark 13:26).

**The vision of the risen Christ in Revelation 1.** When the apostle John turns on Patmos, he sees "one like a son of man" with hair white as wool and snow and eyes like blazing fire (Revelation 1:13–14). Astonishingly, Revelation blends traits of both the Ancient of Days (7:9) and the Son of Man (7:13) onto the single person of the risen Jesus.

**The beast from the sea in Revelation 13 and 17.** Revelation's beast rising from the sea fuses all four beasts of Daniel 7 into one creature—body like a leopard, feet like a bear, mouth like a lion, ten horns—speaking blasphemies, waging war against the saints, and granted authority for forty-two months, the exact equivalent of three and a half years or "a time, times, and half a time" (Revelation 13:1–7; 17:3; 17:12). At the final judgment, the books are opened again before the great white throne, directly recalling Daniel 7:10 (Revelation 20:11–12).

## Notes & Nuances

**Major interpretations of the four beasts and the little horn.** Commentators follow two primary identification schemes. In the traditional view (dominant from the Church Fathers through the Reformation and among many conservative scholars), the four beasts correspond to Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome, with the little horn pointing to an antichristian power emerging from Rome or in the end times. In the historical-critical view (widespread among modern critical scholars), the four beasts represent Babylon, Media, Persia, and Greece, making the little horn King Antiochus IV Epiphanes (reigned roughly 175–164 BC), who persecuted the Jews, outlawed Mosaic observance, and desecrated the Jerusalem temple for roughly three and a half years until the Maccabean revolt rededicated it. Many interpreters also embrace a dual fulfillment: partial fulfillment in Antiochus IV or Rome, pointing forward to ultimate fulfillment at the end of history.

**The debate over the dating of Daniel.** From chapter 7 through chapter 12, historical details concerning the Persian and Greek empires become remarkably specific, fueling a long-standing scholarly debate over when the book was written. The traditional view holds that Daniel himself wrote in the sixth century BC, receiving genuine predictive prophecy from God. The critical scholarly view argues the book was compiled around 167–164 BC during the Maccabean persecution, invoking the revered figure of ancient Daniel to fortify believers under fire. Knowing both viewpoints helps readers understand why commentaries interpret chapters 7–12 so differently.

**What does "a time, times, and half a time" mean?** Taking *time* as one year yields one year plus two years plus half a year—three and a half years, matching the forty-two months or 1,260 days in Revelation (Revelation 11:2–3; 12:14). For many readers, three and a half—exactly half of the complete number seven—also carries symbolic force: persecution may be intense, yet it is cut short and strictly bounded by God's control.

**John J. Collins**, the Irish-American Old Testament scholar, explores the ancient Near Eastern literary backdrop behind Daniel 7. He observes that the chaotic sea and cloud-riding figure echo ancient Canaanite poetic motifs, yet Daniel's author thoroughly transforms them to glorify the one true God alone.
