# Daniel 5 — The Hand Writing on the Wall

## Story

Years later, King Belshazzar threw a lavish banquet for a thousand of his nobles and drank wine in front of them. Under the influence of wine, he ordered the gold and silver vessels that Nebuchadnezzar had looted from the Jerusalem temple brought out so that the king, his nobles, his wives, and his concubines might drink from them. As they drank wine from those sacred goblets, they praised gods of gold and silver, bronze, iron, wood, and stone (5:1–4).

In that very hour, the fingers of a human hand appeared and wrote on the plaster of the palace wall right opposite the lampstand. Watching the hand write, the king went pale, terrified thoughts alarmed him, his hip joints went slack, and his knees knocked together. Panicked, he summoned the enchanters, Chaldeans, and astrologers, promising purple robes, a gold chain around the neck, and the third highest rank in the kingdom to anyone who could read and interpret the inscription. Yet none of the king's wise men could read or decipher it, leaving Belshazzar and his nobles even more bewildered and terrified (5:5–9).

Hearing the uproar, the queen mother entered the banquet hall to calm the king. She reminded him that back in Nebuchadnezzar's reign there was a man named Daniel—filled with insight, intelligence, and extraordinary wisdom for solving riddles—who had been appointed chief of the wise men; she urged the king to summon him (5:10–12).

When Daniel was brought in, Belshazzar asked if he was one of the Judean exiles his father had brought back, then repeated the offer of purple clothing, a gold chain, and third place in the kingdom if he could interpret the writing. Daniel replied bluntly: keep your gifts for yourself or give your rewards to someone else, yet I will still read and interpret the writing for you (5:13–17).

Before reading a single word, Daniel recalled the lesson of Nebuchadnezzar: the Most High God gave him sovereign greatness, yet when his heart hardened in pride he was stripped of his throne and driven to live with wild animals until he knew that the Most High rules over human kingdoms. Daniel then indicted Belshazzar directly: you knew all of this, yet you never humbled your heart. Instead, you defied the Lord of heaven by drinking wine from His temple vessels and praising blind, deaf, mindless idols, while refusing to honor the God "who holds in His hand your very breath and all your ways" (5:18–23).

Therefore, the hand was sent from Him to inscribe four words: "Mene, Mene, Tekel, Upharsin" (or "Parsin"). Daniel decoded each term: *Mene* means God has numbered the days of your kingdom and brought it to an end; *Tekel* means you have been weighed on the scales and found deficient; *Peres* (the singular form of *Parsin*) means your kingdom is divided and handed over to the Medes and Persians (5:24–28).

Belshazzar kept his word, clothing Daniel in purple, draping a gold chain around his neck, and proclaiming him third ruler in the kingdom. That very night, however, Belshazzar king of the Chaldeans was slain, and Darius the Mede received the kingdom at about sixty-two years of age (5:29–31).

## Key Takeaways

**Guilt grows heavier when we know history's lessons yet deliberately ignore them.** Nebuchadnezzar sinned in pride, received a warning, was humbled, and was restored. Belshazzar knew that entire story yet refused to learn a shred of humility (5:22). Turning away from known light always brings swift, severe judgment.

**Profaning what belongs to God is a direct challenge to God Himself.** Babylon had no shortage of gold and silver cups for a banquet. Deliberately hauling out Jerusalem's sacred temple vessels to drink to pagan idols was an act of calculated arrogance, boasting that Babylon's gods outranked Israel's God.

**The fragility of those who think themselves untouchable.** Inside massive walls at a roaring banquet of a thousand nobles, a single silent hand sketching a few words on plaster reduced a mighty ruler to knocking knees (5:6). Every human breath rests entirely in the Creator's hand (5:23).

**God's true spokesman cannot be bought with status or perks.** By now Daniel was an elderly man largely forgotten by the new court. Yet offered purple robes, gold, and the third highest post in the empire, he brushed them aside instantly (5:17). He knew this kingdom had only hours left to live; earthly honors mean nothing before God's sentence.

**History has both an expiration date and God's scales.** Three words on the wall sum up how God evaluates every power: days are numbered, true weight is measured, and authority is transferred to another (5:26–28). Resplendent Babylon collapsed in a single night.

## Deeper Meaning

**Babylon's sudden overnight collapse.** Belshazzar's death that very night and Babylon's swift fall fulfill long-standing prophecies by Isaiah and Jeremiah (Isaiah 21:1–9; Jeremiah 51:39). In the New Testament, Revelation borrows this very scene of lavish feasting shattered in a single hour to foretell the doom of *Babylon the Great*, the enduring symbol of the world system aligned against God (Revelation 18:7–10).

**The God who holds our breath in His hand.** Daniel's rebuke to Belshazzar in 5:23 echoes directly in Paul's speech at Athens, declaring that God Himself gives all people life and breath and everything else (Acts 17:25). Belshazzar also resembles the rich fool in Jesus' parable—ready to eat, drink, and celebrate on the very night his life is demanded from him (Luke 12:19–20).

**Weighed on the scales and found wanting.** The scales of judgment deliver a sobering truth to all humanity: weighed against God's righteous standard, no person comes up heavy enough on their own. That is why the New Testament proclaims a righteousness not earned by human performance, but granted freely as a gift through Christ (Romans 3:22–24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who was Belshazzar in Babylonian history?** Because older classical records never mentioned Belshazzar, nineteenth-century skeptics long doubted he existed. Later excavations of Babylonian cuneiform tablets confirmed that Belshazzar was the eldest son of King Nabonidus; while Nabonidus spent years away in Arabia, he entrusted Belshazzar with regency over Babylon. This historical detail also neatly explains why Belshazzar could only offer Daniel *third* place in the kingdom (5:7; 5:16): Nabonidus stood first, and Belshazzar stood second.

**Why is Nebuchadnezzar called Belshazzar's "father"?** In ancient Aramaic and Hebrew, the word *father* extends beyond a biological parent to include a grandfather, ancestor, or royal predecessor. Historically, several kings reigned between Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar.

**What do the words written on the wall actually mean?** In Aramaic, *Mene*, *Tekel*, and *Peres* (plural *Parsin*, with *u-* meaning "and") were originally names of ancient weights or monetary units (a mina, a shekel, and a half-mina). Babylon's wise men could neither read nor grasp the message—perhaps because unvocalized consonants offered multiple readings, or because they saw only a cryptic string of weight names. By God's Spirit, Daniel unlocked the brilliant wordplay: *numbered*, *weighed*, and *divided*—where *Peres* both means *divided* and sounds just like *Persia*.

**Who is Darius the Mede in 5:31?** This remains one of Daniel's knottiest historical puzzles, because extrabiblical sources credit Cyrus of Persia with capturing Babylon in 539 BC and mention no king named "Darius the Mede" at that moment. Scholars have offered several proposals: another name for Gubaru, the governor Cyrus appointed over Babylon; an alternate royal title for Cyrus himself; or, among critical scholars, a later conflation recalling the famous Persian monarch Darius I. No single explanation has won universal consensus.

To Nebuchadnezzar, Daniel offered a compassionate call to repent; to Belshazzar, he delivers an unsparing verdict with no appeal attached.
