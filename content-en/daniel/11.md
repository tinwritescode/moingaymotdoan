# Daniel 11 — Wars Between the King of the North and the King of the South

## Story

Continuing directly from chapter 10, the heavenly messenger unfolds a sweeping, detailed survey of coming history. After the current Persian king, three more kings will arise, followed by a fourth far wealthier than all the rest who uses his riches to stir up everyone against the kingdom of Greece. Out of Greece a mighty king will arise, ruling with great power and doing as he pleases; yet right at his peak his empire will be broken up and parceled out toward the four winds of heaven—not to his own descendants, but to others (11:1–4).

What follows is a long, bloody sequence of power struggles between two rival dynasties sandwiching Judah in between: *the king of the South* (Egypt) and *the king of the North* (Syria). They alternate between war and political marriage alliances: a southern princess is given to the northern king to seal peace, yet the scheme collapses and both princess and entourage perish. A branch from her family line in the south invades in retaliation, storms the northern fortresses, and hauls plunder and captured idols back to Egypt. Next, the northern king's two sons mobilize massive forces, only to be beaten back by the southern king (11:5–12).

Years later, the king of the North returns with an even vaster army. Violent rebels rise against the South while the northern king captures a fortified city and plants himself inside "the Beautiful Land"—Judah. He attempts another political marriage to undermine the South, fails, and turns to conquer coastal regions and islands. There a foreign commander humiliates him and halts his advance; retreating to his own fortresses, he stumbles, falls, and vanishes. His successor merely sends a tax collector through the glorious kingdom before dying quietly without battle (11:13–20).

In his place rises a contemptible figure who was not granted royal majesty, yet seizes the throne through flattery and intrigue. He sweeps away opposing armies, breaks even a "prince of the covenant," acts deceitfully despite treaties, and buys loyalty by distributing plunder. Through treachery right inside the southern king's own household he defeats the South; the two kings sit at the very same banquet table trading lies with hearts bent on evil. Returning home loaded with wealth, his heart turns fiercely against the holy covenant (11:21–28).

At the appointed time he marches south again, only to be confronted by ships from Kittim (the West) that intimidate him into turning back. Enraged, he vents his fury against the holy covenant and favors those who forsake it. His troops desecrate the temple fortress, abolish the daily sacrifice, and set up "the abomination of desolation." With smooth flattery he corrupts covenant-breakers, yet "the people who know their God will stand firm and take action." The wise among the people instruct many; though some fall by sword, flame, captivity, and plunder, this ordeal serves to refine, purify, and make them spotless until the time of the end (11:29–35).

The final section portrays a king who does whatever he pleases, exalting himself above every god and speaking monstrous blasphemies against the God of gods. Ignoring the gods of his ancestors and every other deity, he honors instead *the god of fortresses*—raw military might—and distributes gold, silver, and land to buy followers. At the time of the end, the kings of the South and North clash fiercely again; this king sweeps through many countries, enters the Beautiful Land, and seizes Egypt's treasures. Alarmed by reports from the east and north, he storms out in fury to annihilate many and pitches his royal tents between the seas and the beautiful holy mountain. "Yet he will come to his end, with no one to help him" (11:36–45).

## Key Takeaways

**Imperial politics is an exhausting cycle of ambition and deceit.** Reading through over thirty verses of wars, sham marriages, banquet-table betrayals, and shifting thrones exposes the hollow fragility of worldly power. Even the most formidable conquerors inevitably "stumble and fall and be found no more" (11:19).

**God's people live right at the crossroads of rival empires.** Judah—affectionately named "the Beautiful Land" (11:16; 11:41)—lies squeezed between Egypt to the south and Syria to the north. Whenever superpowers collide, God's people absorb the shockwaves, from crushing taxation to brutal religious persecution.

**Persecution reveals who genuinely knows God.** Caught between seductive flattery and the threat of the sword, the community splits in two: opportunists who abandon the holy covenant, and those who truly know their God, standing firm and instructing others (11:32–33). Suffering is never evidence that God has failed; it acts as a crucible refining His people pure (11:35).

**Every tyrant comes to a lonely end.** The chapter's closing line falls with chilling finality: after exalting himself above every god and trampling nations underfoot, the arrogant ruler reaches his end with no one left to help him (11:45). His authority lasts only until God's "appointed time" runs out (11:27; 11:36).

## Deeper Meaning

**The abomination of desolation and Jesus' warning.** Verse 11:31 explicitly foretells foreign troops desecrating the sanctuary, abolishing the daily offering, and setting up "the abomination of desolation." Centuries later, Jesus echoes this exact expression when foretelling Jerusalem's crisis and summoning His disciples to watchful discernment in dark days (Matthew 24:15; Mark 13:14).

**The ruler who exalts himself above every god.** The sketch of the arrogant king in 11:36—"exalt and magnify himself above every god, and speak monstrous things against the God of gods"—is echoed almost verbatim in Paul's portrait of the *man of lawlessness* (2 Thessalonians 2:4) and Revelation's blasphemous beast (Revelation 13:5–6). Historical tyrants are never merely artifacts of the past; they mirror the recurring spirit of Antichrist across every age.

**Suffering refines believers.** The assurance that trial and persecution serve to "refine, purify, and make them white" (11:35) resonates powerfully across the New Testament: Peter compares fiery trials to a furnace purifying gold so faith shines more precious (1 Peter 1:6–7), and Revelation shows the saints emerging from the great tribulation wearing robes washed spotless white (Revelation 7:13–14).

## Notes & Nuances

**The historical background behind 11:2–35.** Nearly all interpreters recognize how strikingly 11:2–35 traces Middle Eastern history from the fifth to the second century BC: Persian kings through Xerxes (11:2), Alexander the Great and the fourfold division of his realm (11:3–4), the protracted wars between the Ptolemies of Egypt (*king of the South*) and the Seleucids of Syria (*king of the North*), culminating in Antiochus IV Epiphanes (11:21–35). The ships of Kittim in 11:30 match the Roman fleet that humiliated Antiochus and forced him out of Egypt in 168 BC; immediately afterward he vented his rage on Jerusalem, halted sacrifices, and erected an altar to Zeus inside the temple (11:31), igniting the Maccabean resistance.

**Differing interpretations of the chapter's closing section (11:36–45).** Starting at verse 36, the narrative diverges from the historical career of Antiochus IV (for instance, Antiochus never completed a final conquest of Egypt and died in Persia rather than between the sea and Jerusalem's holy mountain). Two major explanations address this shift. First, many traditional commentators (from Jerome and Calvin to numerous modern evangelical scholars) argue that at verse 36 the vision leaps beyond its historical type—Antiochus IV—to the ultimate figure he prefigures: either the Roman Empire or the end-time Antichrist. Second, historical-critical scholars (who date Daniel's composition around 165–164 BC, shortly before Antiochus's death) argue that verses 36–45 still describe Antiochus IV: verses 2–35 match history closely because they survey events already past for the author, while verses 40–45 reflect the author's expectation of the tyrant's imminent downfall, which later turned out differently. Both positions explain the pivot at verse 36 from their own framework and remain central to debates over Daniel's date.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, the fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, devoted the longest section of his commentary to Daniel 11, marshaling numerous ancient Greek historians to demonstrate how 11:2–35 matches the Ptolemaic and Seleucid dynasties and Antiochus IV down to fine details. Reaching 11:36, however, Jerome refuted Porphyry and insisted the prophecy moves beyond Antiochus to depict the end-time Antichrist—the one exalting himself above every god, precisely as Paul echoes in 2 Thessalonians 2. *(Commentary on Daniel, on chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jerome's appeal to Greek historians for verses 2–35 followed by his shift to Antichrist at verse 36 against Porphyry is one of the most famous sections of his commentary -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, read 11:36–45 differently from Jerome: rather than leaping to an end-time Antichrist, Calvin applied these verses to the Roman Empire—the power that superseded the Greek kingdoms and dominated the Mediterranean until Christ's coming. Regardless of historical identification, Calvin stressed the pastoral heart of 11:32–35: the true resilience of God's people under persecution comes from knowing their God. *(Lectures on the Book of Daniel, on chapter 11 verses 32–45)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin interpreting the close of chapter 11 as the Roman Empire rather than Antichrist as Jerome did; have not checked exact wording -->

**Matthew Henry**, the late-seventeenth-century English pastor, pauses at length over 11:32: "the people who know their God will stand firm and take action." Amid a chapter saturated with intrigue, wars, and betrayal, Henry sees this single line shining like a lantern, proving that believers' true strength lies not in political leverage or military force, but in genuine knowledge of and loyalty to God. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Daniel chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: focusing pastoral application on Daniel 11 verse 32 is classic Matthew Henry on this chapter; have not checked original text -->
