# Daniel 6 — Daniel in the Lions' Den

## Story

King Darius divided the kingdom under one hundred and twenty satraps and set three administrators over them to oversee their accounts—and Daniel was one of the three. Because Daniel distinguished himself by an extraordinary spirit, the king planned to place him over the entire kingdom. Jealous, the other administrators and satraps hunted for grounds to charge Daniel with corruption in government affairs, yet they could find not a single flaw, for he was completely faithful and blameless. At last they concluded: we will never find any charge against Daniel unless it has to do with the law of his God (6:1–5).

They marched in as a group, flattered Darius, and urged him to issue a thirty-day edict: anyone who prays to any god or human other than the king must be thrown into the lions' den. They pressed him to sign it immediately under the unchangeable law of the Medes and Persians. Taking the bait, Darius signed the decree (6:6–9).

The moment Daniel learned the decree had been signed, he went home. His upstairs room had windows opening toward Jerusalem. Just as he had always done, three times a day he got down on his knees, praying and giving thanks before his God. The scheming rivals caught him in the act and rushed straight to the king, reminding him of the unalterable law and accusing "Daniel, one of the exiles from Judah," of ignoring the royal edict (6:10–13).

Darius realized too late that he had been tricked and was deeply distressed. Until sundown he exhausted every effort trying to rescue Daniel. Yet the conspirators pressed him relentlessly, insisting that no decree established under Medo-Persian law could ever be revoked. Reluctantly, the king ordered Daniel cast into the lions' den, managing only to say, "May your God, whom you serve continually, deliver you!" A stone was rolled over the mouth of the den and sealed with the signet rings of the king and his nobles (6:14–17).

That night the king returned to his palace fasting, refused all entertainment, and lay awake unable to sleep. At first light of dawn, he hurried to the lions' den and called out in an anguished voice, asking whether the living God Daniel served continually had been able to save him from the lions (6:18–20).

Out of the pit came Daniel's calm reply: "O king, may you live forever!" Daniel explained that God had sent His angel to shut the lions' mouths so they did not hurt him, because he was found innocent before God and had done no wrong before the king either. Overjoyed, Darius ordered Daniel lifted out; not a single scratch was found on him because he had trusted in his God (6:21–23).

At the king's command, Daniel's malicious accusers—along with their wives and children—were thrown into the lions' den, and before they even reached the floor the lions overpowered them and crushed all their bones. Darius then issued a proclamation to all peoples, nations, and languages to tremble in reverence before Daniel's God, for He is the living God who endures forever and whose kingdom will never be destroyed. Daniel prospered throughout the reigns of Darius and of Cyrus the Persian (6:24–28).

## Key Takeaways

**A life of integrity is a believer's first line of defense.** Daniel's enemies scrutinized every corner of his public record and could not uncover a shred of negligence or graft (6:4). Long before facing a showdown over worship, he had proved himself the king's most reliable public servant.

**Daily habits of prayer forge courage for the hour of crisis.** Daniel did not suddenly start praying once the ban passed as an act of political protest. The text explicitly notes he prayed "just as he had done before" (6:10). Now past eighty years old, decades of quiet faithfulness steadied him to face death calmly—neither showing off nor going into hiding.

**Earthly monarchs are trapped by the very laws they make.** Flatterers elevated Darius like a god for thirty days, yet the moment he wanted to save his most trusted friend, the mighty king stood utterly helpless before a sheet of paper he had just signed (6:14–16). Absolute human authority turns out to be remarkably narrow and easily manipulated.

**Deliverance flows from God's presence, not human scheming.** Daniel led no revolt, traded no insults with his rivals, and nursed no resentment toward the king. He entrusted himself to the One who shuts lions' mouths, stepping out of the pit with a gracious blessing for the very monarch who had sent him down (6:21–22).

## Deeper Meaning

**Shutting the mouths of lions through faith.** The book of Hebrews alludes directly to this episode when celebrating believers who through faith "shut the mouths of lions" (Hebrews 11:33). Late in life, standing lonely on trial in Rome, Paul echoed the same imagery when he wrote that the Lord stood beside him, gave him strength, and rescued him from the lion's mouth (2 Timothy 4:17).

**A sealed stone at the pit's mouth and a rush at dawn.** Generations of Christian readers have noticed striking echoes between Daniel's lions' den and Jesus' tomb: an innocent man trapped by envious rivals, a reluctant ruler cornered by legal pressure, a great stone rolled across the opening and sealed with official seals, and a hurried visit at break of dawn discovering life triumphant over death (Matthew 27:62–66; 28:1–6).

**Obeying God rather than human beings.** Daniel honored and served his king wholeheartedly in every civic duty (6:22). Only when human law ordered him to stop worshiping God did he quietly place God first. That is the exact principle the apostles later declared before the Sanhedrin: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Daniel pray with his windows open toward Jerusalem?** This was neither provocation nor showmanship meant for spies. In Solomon's prayer dedicating the temple, he asked that whenever Israel in foreign exile turned toward Jerusalem to pray, God would hear from heaven and show mercy (1 Kings 8:46–49). Facing Jerusalem meant turning toward God's covenant promises.

**The execution of the accusers' wives and children in 6:24.** Modern readers are rightly unsettled seeing the conspirators' entire families hurled into the pit. It is important to remember that collective family punishment was a brutal custom of ancient Persian royal law—whereas Mosaic law explicitly forbade putting children to death for their fathers' crimes (Deuteronomy 24:16). Scripture records honestly what Darius actually did without approving or commending such cruelty.

**Closing the first half of Daniel.** Chapter 6 wraps up the six narrative accounts tracing the lives of Daniel and his three friends across the Babylonian and Persian courts. Starting in chapter 7, the book shifts into symbolic apocalyptic visions narrated by Daniel in the first person.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, asks why Daniel did not simply shut his windows or pray silently in his heart for thirty days to avoid trouble. Calvin answers that because the decree specifically aimed to suppress worship of the true God, concealing his established practice of open prayer would have looked like conceding that the edict held authority over his conscience.
