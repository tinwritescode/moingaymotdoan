# Daniel 9 — The Prayer of Repentance and the Seventy Weeks

## Story

In the first year of King Darius of Median descent, Daniel was studying the prophetic writings and understood from the word of the LORD given to Jeremiah that Jerusalem's desolation would last exactly seventy years. Realizing the appointed time was drawing to a close, Daniel turned his face toward the Lord God, seeking Him with fasting, sackcloth, and ashes in earnest prayer and confession (9:1–3).

He begins by praising the great and awesome God who keeps His covenant of steadfast love with those who love Him. Then, standing in solidarity with his entire nation—kings, princes, ancestors, and all Judah and Israel scattered near and far—he makes an unvarnished confession: we have sinned, rebelled, turned away from Your laws, and refused to listen to Your servants the prophets. Righteousness belongs to You, O Lord, while open shame belongs to us. Every great disaster poured out on Jerusalem has fulfilled the exact warnings written in the Law of Moses; yet even so, the people have not turned back to seek the LORD (9:4–14).

Daniel then pivots to plead for God's mercy. Recalling how God once brought His people out of Egypt with a mighty hand to make a lasting name for Himself, he begs God not on the basis of the people's righteousness—for they have none—but because of "Your great compassion" to turn away His wrath and make His face shine upon His desolate sanctuary and the city that bears His name. His appeal builds in rapid urgency: "O Lord, listen! O Lord, forgive! O Lord, hear and act!" (9:15–19).

While Daniel is still speaking in prayer and confession around the time of the evening sacrifice, the angel Gabriel arrives in swift flight and touches him. Gabriel informs him that the very moment he began praying, a word went out from heaven, and the angel has come to give him insight and understanding because Daniel is "highly esteemed" (9:20–23).

Gabriel then delivers the celebrated prophecy of "seventy weeks" decreed for Daniel's people and holy Jerusalem to accomplish six great goals: finish transgression, put an end to sin, atone for iniquity, bring in everlasting righteousness, seal up vision and prophecy, and anoint the Most Holy Place (or Most Holy One). The angel divides this span into three stages: from the decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem until "the Anointed One, the ruler," there will be seven weeks and sixty-two weeks; Jerusalem will be rebuilt with plaza and trench in troubled times. After those sixty-two weeks, the Anointed One will be cut off and have nothing; the people of the ruler to come will destroy the city and the sanctuary, its end coming like a flood amid war and desolations. Then a ruler will confirm a covenant with many for one week, yet in the middle of that week put an end to sacrifice and offering; on a wing of abominations will come one who makes desolate, until the decreed destruction is poured out on the desolator (9:24–27).

## Key Takeaways

**Reading Scripture leads directly into prayer.** Daniel neither treats Jeremiah's prophecy as dry historical trivia nor sits back passively waiting for the promise to happen on its own. Precisely because he recognizes God's seventy-year promise nearing completion, he fasts and prays fervently for God to fulfill His word (9:2–3).

**A righteous believer stands alongside a sinful nation.** Nowhere in the entire book of Daniel is a single sin of Daniel recorded. Yet when he prays, he never says *they* sinned; he consistently confesses *we* have sinned (9:5; 9:15; 9:20). He takes his place shoulder to shoulder with his people before God.

**Prayer rests on God's mercy and honor, never on human merit.** Verse 9:18 offers one of the profoundest prayers in the Old Testament: "We do not present our petitions before You because of our righteousness, but because of Your great compassion." God forgives not because His people deserve it, but because of His merciful character and covenant name (9:19).

**God's redemptive plan reaches far beyond rebuilding city walls.** Daniel prayed for the seventy-year exile to end and Jerusalem to be restored. God answered with a vision seven times larger—seventy sevens—pointing ahead to the day when not only will stones be rebuilt, but sin itself will be atoned for at the root and everlasting righteousness ushered in (9:24).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jeremiah's prophecy and the return from exile.** Daniel was reading the exact word of the LORD given through Jeremiah regarding seventy years of Babylonian exile (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). Not long after this prayer, King Cyrus issued his decree permitting the Jews to return and rebuild the temple, as recorded in 2 Chronicles and Ezra (2 Chronicles 36:21–23; Ezra 1:1–4).

**The Anointed One cut off and everlasting righteousness.** The Hebrew phrase for "Anointed One" is *Messiah* (in Greek, *Christ*). Generations of Christians have read Daniel 9:24–26 as a direct pointer to Jesus: cut off not for His own guilt (9:26; cf. Isaiah 53:8), through His death putting an end to sin, atoning for iniquity, and bringing in everlasting righteousness (9:24; cf. Hebrews 9:26; 2 Corinthians 5:21).

**The abomination of desolation in Jesus' teaching.** In His Olivet Discourse, Jesus explicitly cites Daniel by name when speaking of "the abomination of desolation standing in the holy place" (Matthew 24:15; Mark 13:14)—a phrase directly linking back to Daniel 9:27 and the visions that follow (cf. 11:31; 12:11). Those words looked both toward Rome's destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 and toward the great tribulations preceding Christ's return.

## Notes & Nuances

**What are "seventy weeks"?** Literally in Hebrew, the phrase means *seventy sevens*—widely understood as seventy sets of seven years, totaling 490 years, recalling the Sabbath-year and Jubilee cycles of Leviticus 25 and 26. Nearly all interpreters agree that each *week* represents seven years, yet they divide sharply over how the calendar starting points and milestones are calculated.

**Major interpretations of the seventy weeks in 9:24–27.** Three leading readings stand out. First, the traditional messianic view (held across the Church Fathers, the Reformation, and many Protestant scholars): counting from the royal decree to rebuild Jerusalem (under Artaxerxes in Ezra or Nehemiah) down to the ministry and crucifixion of Jesus (the Anointed One cut off), followed by Rome's destruction of city and temple in AD 70. Second, the future end-times view (common in Premillennial and Dispensational circles): the first sixty-nine weeks culminate in Christ's death, followed by an extended parenthetical gap (the Church age), after which the final seventieth week unfolds as a seven-year end-time tribulation when the Antichrist makes and breaks a covenant mid-week. Third, the historical-critical view tied to the Maccabean era: the first seven weeks run from Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC to Cyrus (or Joshua the high priest); sixty-two approximate weeks run to the assassination of high priest Onias III (the anointed one cut off in 171 BC); and the final week spans Antiochus IV halting temple sacrifices for roughly three and a half years. A fourth symbolic reading views 70 times 7 as a figure of completeness encompassing all of redemptive history rather than a literal timetable. Each proposal handles certain details smoothly while wrestling with others; readers do best to remain humble on chronologies and focus on the chapter's unmistakable center: God has decreed the day to end sin and establish everlasting righteousness.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, devoted multiple lectures to Daniel's prayer and the seventy weeks. On the prayer, Calvin remarked that Daniel teaches the Church that God's promises should never make us lazy in prayer; on the contrary, promises are the very fuel of prayer. On 9:24–27, he read all seventy weeks continuously without a gap, fulfilled completely in Christ's incarnation, atoning death, and the subsequent destruction of Jerusalem. *(Lectures on the Book of Daniel, on chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's teaching on divine promises fueling prayer and his continuous messianic interpretation of the seventy weeks are celebrated hallmarks of his Daniel lectures -->

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo, wrote to his friend Hesychius about the end of the world and candidly acknowledged how difficult it is to calculate Daniel's seventy weeks year by year. Surveying several chronological schemes current in his day, he warned believers against speculating over the date of Christ's return, urging them instead to hold fast to the settled core truth: Christ has come, and the prophecy about Him stands confirmed. *(Letter 199 to Hesychius, On the End of the World)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Augustine discussing Daniel 9 and chronological uncertainties in Letter 199 to Hesychius; have not checked individual paragraphs -->

**Joyce Baldwin**, the twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, warns that getting bogged down in arithmetic over 9:24–27 easily makes readers miss the chapter's greatest treasure: verse 9:24 is one of the fullest Old Testament summaries of the Messiah's saving work—finishing sin, atoning for guilt, and ushering in everlasting righteousness.
