# Ephesians 5 — Walk as Children of Light

## Story

Having urged believers to forgive as God forgave them, Paul widens the call: "Therefore be imitators of God, as beloved children." Walk in love, just as Christ loved us and gave Himself up for us as a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God (5:1–2). Children learn by watching and copying their parents; God's children learn how to love by looking closely at Christ.

The exact opposite of self-giving love is grasping appetite. Sexual immorality, any kind of impurity, or greed must not even be hinted at among God's holy people. Neither should obscenity, foolish talk, or coarse joking—things completely out of place—but instead thanksgiving. Be sure of this: no sexually immoral, impure, or greedy person—for greed is idolatry—has any inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God. Let no one deceive you with empty words, because on account of these very things God's wrath comes upon the children of disobedience. Therefore do not become partners with them (5:3–7). Placing greed right beside immorality and naming it idolatry exposes their shared root: treating people and possessions alike as objects to seize for oneself.

Once you were darkness, yet now you are light in the Lord. Notice Paul does not merely say they lived *in* darkness or *in* light—they *were* darkness, and now they *are* light. Walk as children of light, for the fruit of light consists in all goodness, righteousness, and truth. Discern what pleases the Lord. Take no part in the fruitless works of darkness, but instead expose them, because it is shameful even to speak of what they do in secret. Everything exposed by light becomes visible, and whatever becomes visible turns into light. That is why it says: "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you." (5:8–14). Those closing lines do not appear verbatim in the Old Testament; most scholars recognize an early Christian hymn—likely sung at baptisms—echoing Isaiah 60:1.

Pay careful attention then to how you walk—not as unwise people, but as wise, making the best use of the time because the days are evil. Do not be foolish, but understand what the Lord's will is. Do not get drunk with wine, which leads to reckless living, "but be filled with the Spirit" (5:15–18). Paul sets wine and the Spirit side by side: both can take charge of a person's life, yet only one brings true life.

What does a Spirit-filled community look like? Believers address one another with psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs, singing and making melody to the Lord from the heart. Always and for everything they give thanks to God the Father in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. And they submit to one another out of reverence for Christ (5:19–21). That closing line opens the door directly into household relationships: before addressing anyone's specific role, Paul summons everyone to mutual submission.

He next addresses marriage directly. Wives, submit to your own husbands as to the Lord, because the husband is head of the wife just as Christ is head of the Church, His body, of which He Himself is Savior. As the Church submits to Christ, so wives should submit to their husbands in everything (5:22–24). For modern readers this ranks among the letter's hardest paragraphs, interpreted across several thoughtful perspectives.

Yet Paul devotes more than twice as many lines to husbands. Husbands, love your wives just as Christ loved the Church and gave Himself up for her, to sanctify her—cleansing her by the washing of water with the word—so He might present the Church to Himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle, holy and blameless. In the same way husbands should love their wives as their own bodies; whoever loves his wife loves himself. Nobody ever hated his own flesh, but feeds and cherishes it, just as Christ cares for the Church, because we are members of His body (5:25–30). In Roman law, a husband wielded nearly absolute legal control over his wife; Paul says nothing about a husband's privileges, pointing him strictly to sacrificial love modeled on the cross.

"Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh." This mystery is profound, Paul adds, and he is speaking about Christ and the Church. Still, let each husband love his wife as himself, and let the wife respect her husband (5:31–33; Genesis 2:24). All the way back to Eden, marriage stood as a living sign—and Paul unveils the ultimate reality it points toward.

## Key Takeaways

**Imitate God through self-giving love.** Christian ethics rests not on a lifeless rulebook, but on Christ giving Himself away for others. Children copy their Father; believers mirror God by loving as Christ loved (5:1–2).

**Grasping appetite is idolatry.** Sexual immorality, impurity, and greed grow from one trunk: seizing what is not ours to take. Paul labels greed "idolatry" because whatever dominates desire steals God's throne—and replaces crude speech with gratitude (5:3–7).

**Believers are light, not merely walking near light.** "Once you were darkness, but now you are light in the Lord." Light never merely dodges shadows; it shines straight into darkness and exposes it, yielding goodness, righteousness, and truth (5:8–14).

**Filled with the Spirit, not intoxicated with wine.** Drunkenness hands control to alcohol; fullness of the Spirit surrenders control to God—overflowing in shared song, constant thanksgiving, and mutual submission out of reverence for Christ (5:15–21).

**Marriage mirrors Christ and the Church.** Wives are called to submit as to the Lord; husbands are called to love with cross-shaped sacrifice even unto laying down their lives. At bottom, Paul declares, marriage stands as a profound living picture of Christ and His Church (5:22–33).

## Deeper Meaning

**A fragrant offering.** Verse 5:2 describes Christ giving Himself as "a fragrant offering and sacrifice," borrowing Old Testament sacrificial vocabulary where a burnt offering rises as "a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (Exodus 29:18). Christ's death fulfills every ancient offering.

**Children of light.** Jesus proclaimed, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12), and told His followers, "You are the light of the world" (Matthew 5:14). John writes, "God is light, and in Him is no darkness at all" (1 John 1:5). Ephesians 5:8 binds those threads together: united with the Lord, believers themselves become light.

**New wine versus the Spirit.** On Pentecost morning, when the Spirit filled the disciples, scoffers sneered, "They are filled with new wine!" (Acts 2:13). Paul pairs wine and the Spirit in 5:18 along the exact same contrast: both grip a person deeply, yet one steals self-control while the other restores true humanity.

**Psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs.** Colossians voices an almost identical charge: "teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs" (Colossians 3:16). Where Colossians credits "the word of Christ," Ephesians credits "the Spirit"—showing Scripture and Spirit work hand in hand within worship.

**God as Israel's faithful husband.** Old Testament prophets pictured God's covenant as marriage: "I will betroth you to Me forever" (Hosea 2:19). Ezekiel depicted God rescuing an abandoned girl, entering covenant with her, and declaring, "I bathed you with water" (Ezekiel 16:8–9). Ephesians 5:26–27 draws straight from that wedding imagery, pointing ahead to "the marriage of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:7).

**Two becoming one flesh.** Verse 5:31 cites Genesis 2:24—a man leaving parents to hold fast to his wife as one flesh. Paul reads that creation verse on two levels simultaneously: earthly marriage, and the eternal union of Christ and the Church—meaning marriage was designed from day one to foreshadow that greater love.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Submission" is challenging and must be read straight out of 5:21.** Verse 5:21 calls everyone to "submit to one another out of reverence for Christ," and in Greek verse 5:22 contains no verb of its own—borrowing "submit" directly from 5:21. Three main Christian views exist: first, complementarians view husbandly headship and wifely submission as an enduring creation design; second, egalitarians anchor the whole passage in mutual submission (5:21) lived out within first-century cultural structures; third, many emphasize how Paul enters familiar Greco-Roman household codes and reshapes them from within—almost unheard-of in antiquity was commanding husbands to love their wives unto death.

**This passage never condones abuse.** Every line addressed to husbands speaks not of privilege, but of Christlike self-sacrifice, cherishing, and nourishing their wives as their own bodies (5:25–29). Not a syllable permits coercion, cruelty, or domination; invoking Ephesians 5 to pressure abuse victims into silent endurance turns the text completely upside down.

**Where does verse 5:14 come from?** "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you" matches no exact Old Testament line, though it echoes Isaiah: "Arise, shine, for your light has come" (Isaiah 60:1). Most scholars recognize a stanza from an early Christian baptismal hymn woven from Isaiah's imagery.

**Greed equals idolatry.** Verse 5:5 equates a greedy person with an idolater, echoed directly in Colossians: "greed, which is idolatry" (Colossians 3:5). Modern readers often view sexual sin as grave while treating financial greed lightly; Paul puts both in the exact same bracket.

**What is "the washing of water with the word"?** Verse 5:26 may refer to Christian baptism paired with the spoken gospel, or recall the ancient bridal bath before a wedding as pictured in Ezekiel 16. The two images harmonize naturally: Christ prepares His Church like a cherished bride.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, archbishop of Constantinople, devoted an entire homily to 5:22–33. Addressing husbands directly, he said: you have heard the measure of a wife's submission; now hear the measure of your love—even if you must die for her or suffer ten thousand hardships, never refuse. He warned husbands never to govern through fear or threats, because Christ never treats the Church that way. *(Homilies on Ephesians, homily 20)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: homily 20 on marriage is famous; core argument recalled though not quoted verbatim -->

**The Mishnah**, Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, preserves Rabbi Akiva's defense of Song of Solomon: all the Scriptures are holy, yet Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies—because Jewish tradition read it as the love song between God and Israel. Paul stands squarely within that covenant marriage tradition when presenting human marriage as an icon of Christ's love for His people. *(Mishnah, Yadayim 3.5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Rabbi Akiva's saying on Song of Songs appears in Yadayim 3.5; pairing it with Ephesians 5 is editorial context -->

**John Calvin**, Reformer in Geneva, analyzed the word "mystery" (*mysterion*) in 5:32. Because the Latin Vulgate translated it *sacramentum*, medieval theology cited the verse to establish marriage as a sacrament. Calvin argued that misread Paul's explicit clarification: Paul says plainly he means Christ and the Church, not that marriage itself is a sacrament. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, book 4, chapter 19)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin critiques the Vulgate sacramentum argument on Ephesians 5:32 in Institutes 4.19 -->

**John Stott**, Anglican pastor in London, highlighted that "be filled with the Spirit" (5:18) is a plural present-tense command addressed to the entire Church—calling for continual filling rather than one isolated peak moment. He traced the four Greek participles following it—speaking in song, making melody to the Lord, giving thanks always, and submitting to one another—arguing these everyday graces, rather than dramatic spectacles, mark true Spirit-fullness. *(The Message of Ephesians, 1979; Baptism and Fullness, 1975)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Stott highlights these four participles following 5:18 across both books -->

**Markus Barth**, Swiss New Testament scholar, read the whole section 5:21–33 under the banner of 5:21. In his view, submission here is the voluntary gift of a free person rather than coerced subjugation of the weak, while the mandate laid on husbands is even steeper—framing the passage as an invitation to sacrificial love rather than a power chart. *(Ephesians 4–6, Anchor Bible, 1974)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: matches Markus Barth's overall interpretation here; exact phrasing unchecked -->

**Andrew Lincoln**, British New Testament scholar, concluded that 5:14 very likely quotes an early Christian baptismal hymn summoning new converts awake from spiritual death. He also compared the household instructions across 5:22–33 and 6:1–9 with classical Greek household-management handbooks tracing back to Aristotle. *(Ephesians, Word Biblical Commentary, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: both observations are widespread in scholarship and recalled from Lincoln; exact degree of certainty in his wording unchecked -->
