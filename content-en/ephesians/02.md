# Ephesians 2 — From Death to Life, From Far to Near

## Story

Paul has just spoken of the power that raised Christ from the dead; now he shows what that same power accomplished for them personally. Formerly they were dead in their trespasses and sins, walking in step with the ways of this world and with the ruler of the kingdom of the air—the spirit now at work in the children of disobedience. Paul does not exclude himself: all of us once lived among them too, gratifying the cravings of our flesh, following the desires of body and mind, and by nature children of wrath like everyone else (2:1–3). That is humanity's stark portrait—not merely ill, but dead; not free, but led along; and standing under wrath.

Then come two words that overturn everything: "But God." Rich in mercy, because of the great love with which He loved us, even while we were dead in our trespasses He made us alive together with Christ. Mid-sentence Paul interjects: by grace you have been saved! He raised us up with Him and seated us with Him in the heavenly realms in Christ Jesus, so that in the coming ages He might display the immeasurable riches of His grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus (2:4–7). In chapter 1, Christ rose and took His seat in heaven; here believers rise and sit enthroned right alongside Him.

"For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not from yourselves—it is the gift of God." Not by works, so that no one may boast. For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand so that we should walk in them (2:8–10). Good works are never the purchase price of salvation; they are the road on which rescued people walk.

Next Paul addresses Gentile believers directly. Remember your former state: Gentiles by physical birth, dubbed "the uncircumcision" by those calling themselves "the circumcision"—an outward cut made in flesh by human hands. Back then you were separate from Christ, excluded from citizenship in Israel, strangers to the covenants of promise, without hope and without God in the world. Yet now in Christ Jesus, you who once were far away have been brought near by the blood of Christ (2:11–13). Five things they lacked; one thing brought everything home.

He Himself is our peace. He made the two groups—Jew and Gentile—into one, tearing down the dividing wall of hostility between them. In His own flesh He set aside the law with its commandments and regulations, so that out of the two He might create in Himself one new humanity, thus making peace. Through the cross He reconciled both to God in one body, putting hostility to death right there (2:14–16). Inside the Jerusalem temple stood a low stone barrier dividing the Court of the Gentiles from the inner precincts, lined with signs threatening death to any Gentile who crossed; first-century readers could hardly miss the image.

He came and preached peace to those far away and peace to those near, because through Him both have access to the Father by one Spirit (2:17–18; Isaiah 57:19). Father, Son, and Spirit meet in a single sentence: through the Son, in the Spirit, straight to the Father.

Consequently you are no longer foreigners or resident aliens, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household—built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus Himself as the cornerstone. In Him the whole structure is joined together and grows into a holy temple in the Lord. In Him you too are being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit (2:19–22; Psalms 118:22). The old temple's dividing wall lies shattered; a brand-new temple rises—built out of the very people the old wall kept apart.

## Key Takeaways

**Humanity is not merely sick, but dead.** Paul does not describe people apart from God as mildly weakened or slightly off course, but dead in trespasses and sins—pulled along by the world's current, hostile spiritual power, and inward cravings. He spares nobody, including himself: "All of us also lived among them" (2:1–3).

**Two words—"But God"—change everything.** Dead people cannot revive themselves. Salvation belongs to God from first breath to last, rooted in His mercy and great love. He makes us alive, raises us up, and seats us with Christ to showcase His overflowing grace across all ages to come (2:4–7).

**Saved by grace in order to walk in good works.** Salvation is pure gift rather than earned wages, leaving zero room for boasting. Yet the very next sentence names its purpose: we are "His workmanship," newly created to walk in good deeds God arranged ahead of time. Good works never earn salvation; they flow from it (2:8–10).

**The cross brings peace along two axes.** Gentiles once stood outside Israel's covenant promises with no hope. In Christ they are drawn close. One single cross reconciles sinners to God vertically and shatters the barrier between Jew and Gentile horizontally, forming "one new humanity" (2:11–18).

**The Church is God's new temple.** God no longer dwells inside a stone building, but inside a living community fitted together upon the foundation of apostles and prophets, anchored by Christ as cornerstone (2:19–22).

## Deeper Meaning

**The valley of dry bones.** Ezekiel looked out over a valley choked with dry bones until God declared, "I will put breath in you, and you will come to life" (Ezekiel 37:5). Paul begins from that exact state of death, where only God can impart life—echoing Jesus' promise that whoever believes "has crossed over from death to life" (John 5:24).

**Those far off and those near.** Verse 2:17 draws on Isaiah: "Peace, peace to the far and to the near" (Isaiah 57:19). While Isaiah originally spoke to scattered Israelites, Paul widens the lens: the far are Gentiles, the near are Jews, and Christ proclaims peace to both alike.

**The stone the builders rejected.** "The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (Psalms 118:22). Isaiah likewise announced God laying in Zion "a precious cornerstone, a sure foundation" (Isaiah 28:16). Peter takes up the same imagery: believers are "like living stones, being built into a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5). The rejected Stone holds the whole structure together.

**The temple of His body.** When Jesus spoke of raising the temple in three days, John noted, "He was speaking about the temple of His body" (John 2:21). Isaiah foretold that God's house "shall be called a house of prayer for all nations" (Isaiah 56:7). Ephesians 2 shows how that promise unfolds: a living temple welcoming Gentiles inside.

**Paul and James in harmony.** James writes, "Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead" (James 2:17). Ephesians 2:8–10 shows the two apostles never clash: Paul insists works are not the root of salvation, while James insists genuine faith produces fruit. Verse 2:10 stands right where both meet.

**Divisions undone.** Galatians proclaims, "There is neither Jew nor Greek," for "you are all one" (Galatians 3:28). Ephesians 2 reveals how that happens: not by Gentiles turning into Jews or Jews ceasing to be Jews, but by God fashioning both into one new humanity in Christ.

## Notes & Nuances

**The dividing wall was a real stone barrier.** Inside Herod's temple, a low stone balustrade separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, marked with Greek inscriptions threatening death to any foreigner crossing over. Archaeologists unearthed one such stone plaque in 1871. Paul himself was arrested in Jerusalem on suspicion of bringing Trophimus—"the Ephesian"—past that barrier (Acts 21:28–29). Ephesian readers encountering 2:14 knew that wall well.

**Who is "the ruler of the kingdom of the air"?** Ancient peoples often viewed the space between earth and sky as the haunt of spiritual beings. Paul adopts that familiar idiom for God's adversary, the devil—"the spirit now at work in the sons of disobedience" (2:2). Rather than sketching spirit geography, he warns that human sin is never merely individual; a dark personal force operates behind it.

**What does "children of wrath" mean?** "Children of" is a Hebrew idiom describing people characterized by something, like "sons of disobedience" in verse 2. Verse 2:3 teaches that left to our fallen condition, humanity stands beneath God's judgment. Following Augustine, many read "by nature" as testimony to original sin, while others take Paul simply to mean how humanity actually lives. Either way, it never means God hates sinners: the very next line declares His immense love for those exact people (2:4).

**In what sense was the law "set aside"?** Verse 2:15 states Christ abolished "the law of commandments expressed in ordinances," whereas elsewhere Paul declares, "We uphold the law" (Romans 3:31). Most interpreters recognize that what Christ ended is the law functioning as a boundary wall—ceremonial markers such as circumcision, dietary laws, and festival calendars distinguishing insiders from outsiders—while the law's core demands of righteousness and love abide.

**Gentiles do not replace Israel.** Paul never claims the Church displaces Israel; instead, Gentiles become "fellow citizens with the saints" (2:19). In Romans he pictures wild olive branches "grafted in," reminding Gentiles bluntly: "It is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you" (Romans 11:17–18).

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in North Africa around AD 400, once assumed as a young Christian that faith came from human effort while grace came from God. Later in life he candidly retracted that view, appealing to Ephesians 2:8–9 to insist that even faith itself is God's gift, stripping away every ground for boasting. *(On the Predestination of the Saints)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine explicitly corrects his earlier view citing Ephesians 2:8 in this treatise; exact chapter number not recalled -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, read 2:8 slightly differently from Augustine on grammar: "and this" refers not narrowly to faith alone, but to the entire package of salvation—Paul teaches that salvation as a whole is God's gift rather than our work. Calvin still fully affirmed faith as God's gift, while holding that this exact clause speaks more broadly. *(Commentary on Ephesians)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's grammatical point that "this" points to salvation as a whole rather than faith alone is widely discussed -->

**Flavius Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian and priest, described the stone balustrade ringing the inner temple courts with warning slabs in Greek and Latin forbidding foreigners to enter the holy enclosure. His account matches the inscription recovered in 1871 and shows how vividly concrete the dividing wall of 2:14 felt to early readers. *(The Jewish War, book 5; Antiquities of the Jews, book 15)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus documents the balustrade and warning inscriptions in both works; historical context rather than commentary on Ephesians -->

**Markus Barth**, twentieth-century Swiss New Testament scholar and son of Karl Barth, dedicated an entire book to Ephesians 2 titled *The Broken Wall*. He argued that Jew–Gentile reconciliation is not a secondary by-product of the gospel, but the gospel itself: apart from this one new humanity, there is no Church—and Gentiles are incorporated alongside Israel rather than replacing Israel. *(The Broken Wall, 1959; Ephesians, Anchor Bible, 1974)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this forms the core thesis of both volumes -->

**John Stott**, twentieth-century Anglican pastor in London, read 2:1–3 as picturing fallen humanity squeezed by three forces: the world ("the course of this world"), the devil ("the ruler of the kingdom of the air"), and the flesh (our own cravings). Those three enemies still oppose believers today, yet Christ has broken their mastery. *(The Message of Ephesians, 1979)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: clearly recalled Stott structuring 2:1–3 around the triad of world, flesh, and devil -->

**Watchman Nee**, twentieth-century Chinese Christian teacher, structured Ephesians around three postures: Sit, Walk, Stand—drawing the first from 2:6. Christian life never starts by rushing out to walk, he taught, but by sitting—resting in the finished work Christ has already accomplished, because you only sit down once a job is done. *(Sit, Walk, Stand)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this forms the core teaching of the opening chapter of Nee's book -->
