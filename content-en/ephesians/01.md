# Ephesians 1 — Every Spiritual Blessing in Christ

## Story

Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, writes to the saints in Ephesus—the faithful in Christ Jesus—wishing them grace and peace (1:1–2). Several of the earliest manuscripts omit the words "in Ephesus." Nor does the letter include personal greetings to individual friends, even though Paul spent nearly three years ministering in Ephesus (Acts 20:31). Many scholars therefore think it circulated as a general letter read aloud across several churches in the region.

Right after the greeting bursts a sweeping hymn of praise. In the original Greek, verses 3 through 14 form a single breathless sentence. Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly realms. Before the foundation of the world, He chose us in Him to be holy and blameless before Him. In love He predestined us for adoption as His children through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of His will, to the praise of His glorious grace freely bestowed on us in the Beloved (1:3–6). This opening stanza celebrates the Father who chooses.

The second stanza centers on the Son. In Him we have redemption through His blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of His grace lavished upon us in all wisdom and insight. He has made known to us the mystery of His will, according to His good purpose set forth in Christ: when the fullness of time arrives, He will unite all things in heaven and on earth under one head, Christ (1:7–10). There stands the goal of all history—not the annihilation of creation, but everything brought back into harmony under Christ.

In Him we have also obtained an inheritance, having been predestined according to the plan of Him who works all things after the counsel of His will, so that we who first hoped in Christ might exist for the praise of His glory. And in Him you too—when you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and believed—were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit. The Spirit serves as the down payment guaranteeing our inheritance until the full redemption of God's own possession, to the praise of His glory (1:11–14). The third stanza honors the Spirit—and every stanza resolves into the same refrain: to the praise of His glory.

Praise next glides naturally into prayer. Ever since hearing of their faith in the Lord Jesus and their love toward all the saints, Paul never ceases giving thanks for them and remembering them in prayer. He asks the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, to grant them a spirit of wisdom and revelation so they may know Him deeply (1:15–17). Notice Paul does not ask God to give them additional blessings—he just declared they already possess every spiritual blessing. He prays that they would wake up and see what is already theirs.

He asks God to enlighten the eyes of their hearts so they may grasp the hope to which He called them, the riches of His glorious inheritance among the saints, and the immeasurable greatness of His power toward us who believe. That power is the exact same mighty strength He exerted when He raised Christ from the dead and seated Him at His right hand in the heavenly realms—far above every ruler, authority, power, dominion, and every title invoked, not only in the present age but also in the age to come (1:18–21; Psalms 110:1). The very power that rolled away Jesus' tomb now works inside believers.

God placed all things under His feet and appointed Him head over everything for the Church. The Church is His body—the fullness of Him who fills everything in every way (1:22–23; Psalms 8:6). The sovereign Ruler of the cosmos is given as a gift to the Church—and scattered little house congregations across the Roman Empire are honored as His very body.

## Key Takeaways

**Blessing is already given, not anxiously awaited.** Paul does not beg God to bless the church; he erupts in praise because God "has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing" (1:3). Christian life begins not by scrambling to accumulate spiritual favors, but by realizing what God has already poured out.

**Chosen in love for holiness.** God chose us "before the foundation of the world"—long before we ever lifted a finger (1:4). The motive is solely "in love" (1:5), and the aim is making us "holy and blameless." Election is never a badge for bragging, but love shaping a transformed life.

**Father, Son, and Spirit share one saving work.** The Father chooses and adopts (1:3–6), the Son redeems through His blood (1:7–12), and the Spirit seals and guarantees (1:13–14). Every movement converges toward one chorus: the praise of God's glory.

**History is headed somewhere.** God's purpose is to "unite all things in Christ, things in heaven and things on earth" (1:10). Broken and fractured though the world looks, it is not drifting randomly; all creation is being gathered beneath one Head.

**Praying to see, not merely to get.** Paul asks God to enlighten "the eyes of your hearts" (1:18) so believers recognize the hope, inheritance, and power already surrounding them—the exact power that raised Christ from death (1:19–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**A classic Jewish blessing.** "Blessed be God" (1:3) echoes the standard opening of Jewish daily prayers of praise (*berakhah*). Peter opens his first letter with the exact same wording (1 Peter 1:3), and Paul opens 2 Corinthians the same way (2 Corinthians 1:3)—now gloriously expanded to bless "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ."

**Chosen just as Israel was chosen.** Moses reminded Israel that God set His heart on them not because they outnumbered other nations, "but because the LORD loved you" (Deuteronomy 7:7–8). Paul extends that exact covenant language to the whole Church, Gentiles included: election flows from sheer love, never from the merit of the chosen (1:4–5).

**Seal and down payment.** In 2 Corinthians Paul likewise writes that God "put His seal on us and gave us His Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee" (2 Corinthians 1:22). A seal marks ownership and authenticity; a down payment (*arrabōn*) pledges that the full balance is certain to follow. Today's indwelling Spirit is the first installment of our coming inheritance (1:13–14).

**Seated at God's right hand.** The psalmist's oracle—"Sit at My right hand until I make your enemies your footstool" (Psalms 110:1)—is the Old Testament verse cited most frequently across the New Testament. Paul weaves it directly together with Psalm 8:6, where God puts "all things under his feet" (1:20–22; Psalms 8:6): humanity's original royal calling finds fulfillment in the risen Jesus.

**Head of creation and head of the Church.** Colossians, Ephesians' twin letter, proclaims that all things "were created through Him," and "He is the head of the body, the church" (Colossians 1:16–18). The Creator sustaining galaxies is the very One cherishing and guiding His Church.

## Notes & Nuances

**To whom was this letter addressed?** The words "in Ephesus" (1:1) are missing from some of the oldest and weightiest manuscripts, and the letter contains zero personal greetings despite Paul's nearly three-year stay there. Many interpreters view it as a circular letter sent around churches in Asia Minor—possibly even the "letter from Laodicea" mentioned in Colossians 4:16.

**Who wrote Ephesians?** Since the nineteenth century, a number of scholars have suggested a close disciple of Paul authored Ephesians after his death, citing its flowing, multi-clause sentences and tight overlap with Colossians. Many other scholars uphold direct Pauline authorship from prison—perhaps assisted by a secretary—attributing stylistic differences to the letter's meditative, liturgical aim. Historically the Church has read the opening greeting (1:1) and prison references (Ephesians 3:1; Ephesians 6:20) at face value.

**What does "predestined" mean?** The verb "predestined" (1:5; 1:11) has sparked centuries of theological conversation. Some understand God selecting specific individuals for salvation before time; others understand God choosing Christ and choosing everyone united "in Christ"; still others highlight God's foreknowledge of who will believe (Romans 8:29). Across every viewpoint, one anchor remains clear: Paul celebrates election in a hymn of worship, never as ammunition for quarreling or despair.

**Where are "the heavenly realms"?** This phrase occurs five times in Ephesians (1:3; 1:20; 2:6; 3:10; 6:12). Rather than simply meaning heaven after death, it names the invisible spiritual realm active right now—where Christ sits enthroned and where hostile spiritual powers still operate. Believers already enjoy blessings "in the heavenly realms" even while walking the streets of Ephesus.

**Irenaeus**, late second-century bishop of Lyon, built his entire vision of salvation around "uniting all things in Christ" (1:10). **John Calvin** advised anyone anxious about whether they are elect never to pry into God's hidden decrees, but to look straight at Christ. **Karl Barth**, twentieth-century Swiss theologian, rebuilt the doctrine of election squarely around "in Christ" (1:4). For Barth, Jesus Christ is simultaneously the electing God and the elected human: God first chooses His own Son, taking rejection upon Himself so humanity may receive life, viewing all humankind in Him. *(Church Dogmatics, vol. **Clinton Arnold**, American New Testament scholar, noted that Ephesians stresses divine power more intensely than any other Pauline epistle. Ephesus was renowned for magical arts and devotion to Artemis, where new converts once burned scrolls of spells (Acts 19:19). **John Chrysostom** preached that God chose us *in order to become* holy, not because we already were holy.
