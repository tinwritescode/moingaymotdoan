# Ephesians 6 — The Full Armor of God

## Story

Paul completes his instructions for Christian households. Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. "Honor your father and mother"—the first commandment carrying a promise—"that it may go well with you and that you may live long in the land." And fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord (6:1–4; Exodus 20:12). In Roman society, where a father held sweeping legal authority over his children's very lives, Paul places clear restraint on fathers: parental authority exists to nurture the child.

Next he addresses enslaved believers: obey your earthly masters with respect and trembling, with a sincere heart, as you would Christ—not merely as people-pleasers while someone watches, but as servants of Christ doing God's will from the heart. Serve willingly as serving the Lord rather than human beings, knowing that whatever good anyone does—slave or free—he will receive back from the Lord (6:5–8). Paul does not issue a direct call to abolish slavery here, a tension modern readers rightly feel deeply. Yet he addresses enslaved people as moral agents possessing dignity, conscience, and direct reward from Christ—something Roman law flatly denied them.

Masters likewise must treat enslaved workers in the exact same spirit, giving up threats, knowing that both servant and master answer to the same Master in heaven, who shows zero partiality (6:9). Across all three household pairs—wives and husbands, children and parents, slaves and masters—Paul speaks directly to both sides, holding the powerful accountable before a higher Lord.

Finally Paul addresses the entire congregation: be strong in the Lord and in the strength of His might. Put on the full armor of God so that you may stand firm against the schemes of the devil. "For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood," but against rulers, authorities, cosmic powers over this present darkness, and spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms. Therefore take up the whole armor of God so you may resist in the evil day, and having done everything, stand firm (6:10–13). Our real adversaries are never the human beings opposing us, but the dark forces operating behind them.

Paul names each piece of armor in turn. Stand firm, buckling the belt of truth around your waist, putting on the breastplate of righteousness, and fitting your feet with the readiness of the gospel of peace. In all circumstances take up the shield of faith, capable of extinguishing every flaming dart of the evil one. Take the helmet of salvation and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God (6:14–17; Isaiah 59:17). Guarded by Roman soldiers while writing, Paul had military gear right before his eyes—yet the helmet of salvation and breastplate of righteousness already appear in Isaiah, worn by God Himself.

The closing element is not a piece of armor, but prayer. Pray at all times in the Spirit with every kind of prayer and petition, staying alert with all perseverance as you intercede for all the saints. Pray also for Paul, that words may be given him whenever he opens his mouth to proclaim boldly the mystery of the gospel—for which he serves as an ambassador in chains—so he may speak fearlessly as he ought (6:18–20). Earthly ambassadors normally enjoy diplomatic immunity; Christ's ambassador wears iron chains—and asks not for release, only for courage.

Paul sends Tychicus, his beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord, to share full news about him and encourage their hearts. He signs off wishing peace, love with faith from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, and grace to all who love our Lord Jesus Christ with an undying love (6:21–24; Colossians 4:7–8). Opening with every spiritual blessing in Christ, the letter closes with steadfast love for Him.

## Key Takeaways

**Responsibility runs in both directions.** Children are called to obey and honor parents, yet fathers are simultaneously warned, "Do not provoke your children to anger." Paul addresses not only those under authority, but those exercising it—bringing both under Christ (6:1–4).

**Masters and servants share one impartial Lord.** Enslaved believers are urged to work sincerely as for Christ, while masters are commanded to treat them in the same spirit and abandon threats, knowing both stand before "one Master in heaven" who never plays favorites (6:5–9).

**Our real struggle is never against human beings.** "We do not wrestle against flesh and blood." Behind visible conflict stand unseen forces of spiritual darkness—forbidding believers from treating fellow human beings as their ultimate enemies, even when opposed by them (6:10–12).

**The armor belongs to God, and the mission is to stand firm.** Truth, righteousness, the gospel of peace, faith, salvation, and God's word are gifts supplied by God rather than self-made grit. Repeating "stand" again and again, Paul makes clear our goal is holding ground Christ has already won (6:13–17).

**Prayer powers the entire battle.** Armor alone is not enough; Paul calls for continual, alert intercession for all believers. Even chained in prison, Paul asks not for escape, only for bold speech to share the gospel (6:18–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**A command paired with a promise.** Verses 6:2–3 quote the fifth commandment: "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land the LORD your God is giving you" (Exodus 20:12). Whereas Exodus referred specifically to Canaan, Paul widens "the land" to "the earth," fitting a global Church scattered across all nations.

**God's own warrior armor.** In Isaiah, the Lord Himself steps forth armed for rescue: "He put on righteousness as a breastplate, and a helmet of salvation on His head" (Isaiah 59:17). Of the coming Messianic King, Isaiah adds, "Righteousness shall be the belt of His waist" (Isaiah 11:5). The armor of Ephesians 6 is God's own panoply, now graciously shared with His people.

**Feet shod with peace.** Verse 6:15 fits believers' feet with "the readiness of the gospel of peace," echoing Isaiah's exultant line: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace!" (Isaiah 52:7). God's soldier marches into conflict carrying peace.

**The sword of God's word.** Facing wilderness temptation, Jesus parried every thrust with Scripture: "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). Hebrews likewise portrays God's word as "sharper than any two-edged sword" (Hebrews 4:12)—the lone offensive weapon in Paul's list.

**Resisting the adversary.** James counsels, "Submit yourselves therefore to God; resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7). The sequence mirrors Ephesians: draw strength in God first, then stand firm against the adversary.

**Tychicus and Onesimus.** The commendation of Tychicus in 6:21–22 matches Colossians 4:7–8 nearly word for word—where Tychicus travels alongside Onesimus, a runaway slave who has come to faith (Colossians 4:9). Sending Onesimus back to his master Philemon, Paul appeals for him to be welcomed "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" (Philemon 1:16). The household words in 6:5–9 may well have been read aloud for the first time with Onesimus standing right in the room.

## Notes & Nuances

**Paul does not explicitly command abolition of slavery.** This reality deserves candid reflection. Roman slavery swept up war captives, debt victims, and children born in bondage, stripping them of basic legal rights. Paul instructs enslaved believers to obey and never explicitly orders Christian masters to emancipate them. In nineteenth-century America, pro-slavery apologists weaponized 6:5, whereas enslaved Christians and abolitionists appealed to 6:9, Galatians 3:28, and Philemon. Some interpreters observe that Paul addressed a powerless minority within an empire they could not restructure overnight, planting seeds of equal dignity that eventually dismantled slavery from within; others frankly acknowledge Paul did not press that far socially—and insist modern readers must never twist this text to defend oppression of any kind.

**"Fear and trembling" does not mean terror of a master.** Paul uses that exact phrase to describe reverent obedience toward God: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12). In 6:5 it pairs with "sincerity of heart" and "as to Christ"—signifying earnest reverence before the Lord, not cowering dread of the whip.

**Obeying parents "in the Lord" carries moral limits.** Adding "in the Lord" in 6:1 places parental authority beneath God's higher authority. Whenever parents demand wrongdoing, the apostles' stand before the Sanhedrin governs: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29)—while verse 4 immediately holds parents accountable too.

**Neither spotting demons everywhere nor denying their reality.** Verse 6:12 acknowledges genuine hostile spiritual powers. Ephesian converts lived amid Artemis worship and thriving magical cults, publicly burning pricey spell books upon believing (Acts 19:19). Paul instructs them neither to live terrified of dark spirits nor to chase demons around every corner, but simply to put on God's armor and stand steady.

**The armor draws more from Isaiah than from Roman barracks.** Though chained beside Roman guards who provided vivid visual cues, nearly every piece of Paul's gear springs directly from Isaiah. Avoid over-allegorizing microscopic military mechanics; the main truth is that every piece is a gracious provision supplied by God.

**William Gurnall**, seventeenth-century English Puritan pastor, authored a monumental treatise devoted entirely to 6:10–20, tracing each piece of armor into everyday temptations. **Clinton Arnold**, American New Testament scholar, traced Ephesians' heavy focus on spiritual powers directly to its Ephesian setting—a hub of magic and devotion to Artemis, revered as mistress over lesser spirits. **Watchman Nee**, Chinese Christian teacher, anchored the final section of *Sit, Walk, Stand* in the repeated command "stand" across 6:11–14.
