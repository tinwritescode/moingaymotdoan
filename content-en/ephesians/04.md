# Ephesians 4 — One Body, One New Self

## Story

The second half of the letter pivots on the word "Therefore." Paul, a prisoner for the Lord, urges believers to walk in a manner worthy of the calling they received—with complete humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, and making every effort to preserve the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace. There is one body and one Spirit, just as they were called to one hope; one Lord, one faith, one baptism; one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all (4:1–6). Seven realities, every single one singular. Unity is not something they must manufacture from scratch; it already exists, and their task is to guard it.

Unity never means uniformity. Each of us has received grace measured out according to Christ's gift. Therefore Scripture declares: when He ascended on high He led a host of captives and gave gifts to humanity. Saying "He ascended" implies that He also descended to the lower regions of the earth; the One who descended is the very One who ascended far above all the heavens to fill the entire universe (4:7–10; Psalms 68:18). The psalm pictured a victorious king receiving tribute; Paul sees a conquering King handing out gifts.

And those gifts are people: He gave some to be apostles, some prophets, some evangelists, some pastors and teachers—to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ, until all of us reach unity in the faith and in the knowledge of the Son of God, growing into maturity measured by the full stature of Christ (4:11–13). Church leaders are not appointed to do every task themselves, but to train every member to serve.

Then we will no longer be infants, tossed back and forth by waves and swept away by every wind of doctrine through human cunning and deceitful schemes. Instead, "speaking the truth in love," we grow up in every respect into Him who is the head, Christ. From Him the whole body, joined and held together by every supporting ligament, grows and builds itself up in love as each part performs its proper work (4:14–16). Truth stripped of love bruises the body; love stripped of truth weakens it.

Next Paul contrasts that new life with the old. Insisting in the Lord, he urges them no longer to walk as Gentiles do in the futility of their thinking—darkened in understanding, alienated from the life of God because of inward ignorance and hardened hearts. Having lost all sensitivity, they have given themselves over to sensuality, greedily indulging every form of impurity (4:17–19). The portrait mirrors Romans 1: hard hearts breed darkened minds, which spiral into shameless living.

That is not how they learned Christ! Assuming they truly heard Him and were taught in Him, as truth is in Jesus, they learned to put off the old self belonging to their former way of life—rotting away under deceitful desires—to be renewed in the spirit of their minds, and to put on the new self, created to be like God in true righteousness and holiness (4:20–24). Taking off and putting on sounds like changing clothes, yet the new garment is a freshly created human being.

Paul immediately makes that new self concrete. Lay aside falsehood and speak truth with your neighbor, because we are members of one another. Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no foothold to the devil. Let the thief steal no longer, but instead labor honestly with his own hands so he may have something to share with anyone in need. Let no corrupting talk leave your mouth, only words good for building up as fits the occasion, imparting grace to those who listen (4:25–29; Psalms 4:4). Every command has two sides—put off the vice, put on the opposite virtue. The former thief stops taking and starts giving.

"And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption." Put away all bitterness, wrath, anger, clamor, slander, and every form of malice. "Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you." (4:30–32). The Spirit is no impersonal force; He can be grieved—and bitter words between brothers and sisters grieve Him deeply.

## Key Takeaways

**Live in step with your calling.** The first three chapters declare what God has done; from chapter 4 onward Paul spells out how believers respond. That sequence matters: we do not behave well in order to win God's call, but because He has already called us we walk worthy of it (4:1).

**Unity is guarded, not invented.** The Spirit already knit believers into one; our calling is to "keep" that unity through humility, gentleness, patience, and love. The sevenfold "one" in 4:4–6 anchors the Church's unity in the character of God Himself (4:1–6).

**Every member serves, and leaders equip.** Christ distributes grace to every believer and appoints apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers—not to monopolize ministry, but to equip all the saints so the whole body thrives (4:7–16).

**Maturity pairs truth with love.** Spiritual children drift with every passing teaching fad. Mature believers stand steady, "speaking the truth in love." Truth without love wounds; love without truth misleads (4:14–16).

**Putting off the old and putting on the new takes shape in concrete habits.** Renewal is never purely internal. Paul names lying, lingering anger, stealing, toxic speech, and bitterness—replacing each with honesty, generous work, edifying speech, and Christlike forgiveness (4:17–32).

## Deeper Meaning

**One God, one people.** The sevenfold "one" of 4:4–6 echoes Israel's foundational confession: "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Because there is only one God, His people can only be one family.

**He ascended on high.** Verse 4:8 cites Psalm 68, celebrating God climbing His holy hill as triumphant King leading captives (Psalms 68:18). Paul reads that victory hymn in light of Christ's ascension, echoing Peter's Pentecost sermon: exalted to God's right hand, Jesus "has poured out" the Holy Spirit (Acts 2:33). He ascends in order to give.

**Many gifts, one Spirit.** In 1 Corinthians Paul similarly writes, "There are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit" (1 Corinthians 12:4). Ephesians 4 teaches the same truth while highlighting its goal: every gift exists to build up one unified body.

**Anger and Psalm 4.** Verse 4:26 quotes Psalm 4:4 via the Greek Old Testament used by the early Church. Rendered from Hebrew, the line reads, "Tremble and do not sin; ponder in your hearts on your beds and be silent." Both versions give the same counsel: when heat flares up, keep it from turning into sin and settle your heart before nightfall.

**Grieving the Spirit.** Isaiah recalls Israel in the wilderness: "They rebelled and grieved His Holy Spirit" (Isaiah 63:10). Paul echoes that very warning in 4:30. Far from an impersonal current, the Spirit feels real grief—especially when believers wound one another with harsh words.

**Renewed in God's image.** The new self "created after the likeness of God" (4:24) looks straight back to creation, when "God created humanity in His own image" (Genesis 1:27). Colossians adds that this new self "is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its Creator" (Colossians 3:10). Salvation restores the shattered image of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Paul alters one verb in Psalm 68.** Psalm 68:18 reads that the Lord "received gifts among humanity," whereas Ephesians 4:8 writes that He "gave gifts to humanity." That difference is genuine and worth noting candidly. The Aramaic Targum read in ancient synagogues also renders the verb "gave." Paul may reflect an existing Jewish reading tradition, or summarize the psalm's logic: a victorious king accepts spoils specifically in order to distribute them generously to his people.

**What does "descended into the lower parts of the earth" mean?** Three main readings exist: Christ descending from heaven to earth at His incarnation; Christ descending to the realm of the dead after crucifixion (favored by many Church Fathers); or Christ returning in the Spirit at Pentecost to pour out gifts. Paul leaves it brief, focusing on one sweep: the One who stooped lowest now reigns highest (4:9–10).

**How many offices, and do all continue today?** Some read "pastors and teachers" as one two-sided role, others as two distinct offices. Churches also differ on whether apostles and prophets continue in the same sense today. What 4:12 leaves crystal clear is the shared goal: equipping all believers for ministry so that the entire congregation serves.

**Not every flash of anger is sin.** Saying "be angry and do not sin" (4:26) acknowledges righteous indignation—such as outrage against injustice. Yet five verses later Paul commands putting away "wrath and anger" (4:31). Both belong together: rightful anger can arise, yet it must be resolved before sunset lest festering resentment hand the devil an open door.

**John Calvin**, Reformer in Geneva, sorted the offices of 4:11 into two tiers: apostles, prophets, and evangelists served as extraordinary founding ministries established to launch the Church (raised up again only rarely when needed), whereas pastors and teachers form ordinary, permanent ministries essential for every generation. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, book 4, chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin lays out this two-tier distinction clearly in Institutes 4.3 and in his Ephesians commentary -->

**John Stott**, Anglican pastor in London, stressed that 4:12 omits the comma once inserted by older English versions between "saints" and "the work of ministry." Pastors are not hired performers carrying out every ministry alone; they equip ordinary believers for active service—what Stott called the ministry of the whole people of God. *(The Message of Ephesians, 1979)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Stott repeatedly highlighted the punctuation shift in 4:12 across his commentary and ecclesiology writings -->

**Andrew Lincoln**, British New Testament scholar, argued that changing "received" to "gave" drew upon Jewish exegetical tradition reflected in the Targum tying Psalm 68 to Moses and Pentecost—presenting Christ as the greater Moses who ascends not to bring down stone tablets, but to distribute Spirit-empowered gifts to His Church. *(Ephesians, Word Biblical Commentary, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalled Lincoln analyzing the Psalm 68 Targum background here; exact level of certainty in his wording unchecked -->
