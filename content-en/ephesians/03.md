# Ephesians 3 — The Mystery of Christ

## Story

Paul begins a prayer: "For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you Gentiles..." Then he pauses mid-sentence to unpack his ministry, not resuming the prayer until verse 14 (3:1). Writing from custody, he never calls himself Rome's captive, but the prisoner of Christ Jesus.

Surely they have heard of the stewardship of God's grace entrusted to him for their sake. By revelation the mystery was made known to him, as he has already written briefly; reading that, they can perceive his insight into the mystery of Christ. That mystery was not disclosed to humanity in past generations as it has now been revealed by the Spirit to His holy apostles and prophets: through the gospel, Gentiles are fellow heirs, members of the same body, and sharers together in the promise in Christ Jesus (3:2–6). In Scripture, a "mystery" is never an unsolvable riddle, but a hidden divine plan now brought into open daylight.

Paul became a servant of this gospel by the gift of God's grace given through the working of His power. Calling himself the very least of all the saints, he marvels that this grace fell to him: to proclaim to the Gentiles the unfathomable riches of Christ and bring to light for everyone the plan of the mystery hidden for ages in God, who created all things (3:7–9). The former persecutor of the Church never forgets where he came from (1 Corinthians 15:9); the deeper he sees into grace, the smaller he feels.

God's purpose is that now, through the Church, His multifaceted wisdom might be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly realms, according to the eternal purpose carried out in Christ Jesus our Lord. In Him, through faith in Him, we hold boldness and confident access to God. Therefore Paul begs them not to lose heart over the afflictions he endures for them, because those sufferings are their glory (3:10–13). The Church—where Jews and Gentiles sit down at one shared table—serves as the living showcase through which God displays His wisdom before cosmic powers.

Then Paul picks up the interrupted prayer. For this reason he bows his knees before the Father, from whom every family in heaven and on earth derives its name. He prays that out of His glorious riches the Father would strengthen them with power through His Spirit in their inner being, so that Christ may dwell in their hearts through faith (3:14–17). Jews ordinarily stood to pray; kneeling signals the urgency and gravity of his plea.

He prays that, rooted and grounded in love, they may have strength together with all the saints to comprehend the breadth and length and height and depth—and to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge—so they may be filled to all the fullness of God (3:18–19). Only "together with all the saints" can anyone grasp it: no solitary individual can measure the dimensions of that love alone.

The prayer swells into doxology: now to Him who is able to do immeasurably more than all we ask or imagine, according to His power at work within us, to Him be glory in the Church and in Christ Jesus throughout all generations, forever and ever. Amen (3:20–21). With that praise, the letter's first three doctrinal chapters conclude; the second half turns to how believers live in response.

## Key Takeaways

**The mystery is Gentiles standing as full equals.** What remained veiled until now was not merely that Gentiles would experience blessing, but that they become "fellow heirs, members of the same body," full partners in the promise—never standing in a second row behind Israel, but sharing one equal footing (3:3–6).

**Ministry is pure grace, never earned status.** Paul styles himself "the very least of all the saints." He was not recruited because he outshone others; both his calling and the strength to fulfill it come wholly as God's gift (3:7–9).

**The Church displays God's wisdom to unseen realms.** Jew and Gentile eating at one table demonstrates God's astonishing wisdom even to heavenly powers. The Church preaches not solely through sermons, but through its very existence as a reconciled community (3:10–12).

**Praying to know what outruns knowledge.** Paul does not pray for material wealth or exemption from hardship. He prays for inward strength, Christ's settled residence in their hearts, and deeper acquaintance with a love too vast for human minds to exhaust (3:14–19).

**God exceeds our boldest imagination.** The closing doxology celebrates the God "able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think." Even our most daring prayer falls short of His power (3:20–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**The ancient promise to Abraham.** From the outset God promised Abraham, "All families on earth will be blessed through you" (Genesis 12:3). Gentile blessing itself was no secret; the newly revealed mystery is *how* God fulfills it—not by turning Gentiles into ethnic Jews, but uniting both into one single body in Christ.

**A mystery long kept quiet.** Romans closes with the exact same cadence, praising God for "the revelation of the mystery that was kept secret for long ages but has now been disclosed" to bring all nations to the obedience of faith (Romans 16:25–26). Both epistles celebrate an eternal plan now unveiled.

**The least chosen for the greatest task.** Paul speaks of himself this way repeatedly: "the least of the apostles" for persecuting the Church (1 Corinthians 15:9), and "the foremost" of sinners (1 Timothy 1:15). Here in Ephesians 3:8 he steps lower still—least of all *saints*, not merely apostles. The closer he draws to life's finish, the deeper his humility grows.

**Height, depth, length, and breadth.** The book of Job measures divine wisdom across those same four dimensions—"higher than heaven," "deeper than Sheol," "longer than the earth and broader than the sea" (Job 11:7–9)—to underscore humanity's helplessness to fathom it. Paul borrows those cosmic measurements yet prays that believers may truly know them through love.

**Bold access into God's presence.** Verse 3:12 celebrates coming before God "with boldness and confidence through our faith." Hebrews echoes the invitation: "Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace" (Hebrews 4:16). Under the old temple system, only the high priest stepped into the Most Holy Place once a year; now every believer, Gentile and Jew alike, enters freely.

**Christ making His home in the heart.** Jesus promised anyone who loves Him, "We will come to him and make our home with him" (John 14:23). Verse 3:17 prays that pledge into living reality: the Greek verb "dwell" means settling permanently as homeowner, never stopping overnight like a guest.

## Notes & Nuances

**An interrupted prayer picked back up.** Verse 3:1 starts a prayer that Paul pauses mid-sentence to reflect on his apostolic calling, resuming cleanly at verse 14 with the repeated phrase "For this reason." Such natural digressions reflect dictation to a scribe, following the flow of living thought; linking 3:1 directly to 3:14 reveals the prayer's continuous arc.

**Prisoner of Christ Jesus.** Paul writes from imprisonment—most likely in Rome, though Caesarea or Ephesus have also been suggested. Calling himself Christ's prisoner rather than Rome's, he urges them not to lose heart because his suffering brings them "glory" (3:13), paralleling Colossians where he rejoices in suffering for Christ's body (Colossians 1:24).

**Who are "His holy apostles and prophets"?** Verse 3:5 credits the Spirit with unveiling this mystery to apostles and prophets—here referring primarily to New Testament Christian prophets (cf. 2:20; 4:11) rather than Old Testament seers. Some scholars see the adjective "holy" before "apostles" as the reverent phrasing of a later disciple looking back, factoring into debates over authorship; others observe that "holy" simply means set apart, the exact term Paul applies to every ordinary believer ("saints").

**Heavenly rulers are not earthly politicians.** "The rulers and authorities in the heavenly realms" (3:10) refer to invisible spiritual powers, matching 1:21 and 6:12. Paul does not picture the Church preaching sermons to angels or demons; rather, the very existence of a united, multiethnic Church bears silent, unmistakable witness before them.

**"Filled to all the fullness of God" never means becoming divine.** Verse 3:19 prays that believers be "filled with all the fullness of God." Colossians clarifies the balance: all the fullness of deity dwells bodily in Christ, and "you have been filled in Him" (Colossians 2:9–10). Believers receive God's fullness through union with Christ without ever becoming gods themselves.

**John Chrysostom**, archbishop of Constantinople around AD 400 and the Greek Church's greatest preacher, marveled at the unusual Greek word Paul coined in 3:8—literally "less than the least"—because existing vocabulary felt insufficiently humble.
