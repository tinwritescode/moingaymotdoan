# 1 Chronicles 7 — The Northern Tribes and the Sorrow of Ephraim's House

## Story

Stepping outward from the worship center of Levi in chapter 6, the genealogies turn north to the tribes of central and northern Israel. Issachar comes first, beginning with four sons: Tola, Puah, Jashub, and Shimron (7:1). Tola's branch produced six clan heads who were "mighty men of valor," numbering 22,600 "in the days of David" (7:2). From Tola's eldest son Uzzi through Izrahiah and his four sons, all five men served as clan chiefs commanding 36,000 troops ready for battle, "for they had many wives and children." Including all their fellow clans of Issachar enrolled by genealogy, the tribe counted 87,000 mighty warriors (7:3–5).

Next comes a brief record of Benjamin—a tribe that receives a much fuller genealogy in chapter 8. Here three sons—Bela, Becher, and Jediael—are tallied with their clan heads and fighting men: Bela's line counted 22,034 warriors, Becher's counted 20,200, and Jediael's line through Bilhan counted 17,200 fighting men (7:6–11). Closing this section is a cryptic single verse listing Shuppim and Huppim sons of Ir, along with Hushim son of Aher (7:12). Immediately afterward, Naphtali receives just one verse naming four sons—Jahziel, Guni, Jezer, and Shallum—"the descendants of Bilhah" (7:13).

Turning to the tribe of Manasseh—the western half living west of the Jordan—women stand out surprisingly in the genealogical ledger. Manasseh's "Aramean concubine" bore Asriel and Machir, "the father of Gilead." Machir married Maacah, fathering Peresh and Sheresh, whose line continued through Ulam, Rakem, and Bedan (7:14–17). Within that branch, the text notes Zelophehad, who "had only daughters" (7:15), then mentions Gilead's sister Hammolecheth, who bore Ishhod, Abiezer, and Mahlah, followed by the four sons of Shemida (7:18–19).

When the list reaches Ephraim, the steady cadence of names halts abruptly at a family tragedy. Across several lines from Shuthelah down to Zabad, Ephraim's sons Ezer and Elead "were killed by the native men of Gath when they went down to seize their livestock" (7:20–21). Hearing the news, their father Ephraim "mourned for them many days, and his relatives came to comfort him" (7:22). After his grief, he went in to his wife; she conceived and bore a son, whom he named Beriah—meaning *in misfortune*—"because tragedy had come upon his house" (7:23).

Yet grief did not write the final word for Ephraim's household. In the very next verse, his daughter Sheerah stepped forward and "built Lower and Upper Beth-horon as well as Uzzen-sheerah"—a woman founding three fortified towns that bore her name and guarded a strategic pass of the land (7:24). From that restored household, Ephraim's lineage moved on through Rephah, Resheph, Telah, Tahan, Ladan, Ammihud, Elishama, and Nun to the tribe's most famous son: Joshua (7:25–27). Closing out Joseph's descendants, the author sketches the map of their inheritance stretching across central Israel: Bethel, Naaran, Gezer, and Shechem as far as Gaza, together with the towns bordering Manasseh—Beth-shean, Taanach, Megiddo, and Dor (7:28–29).

Chapter 7 ends with the tribe of Asher along the northern coast. Four sons—Imnah, Ishvah, Ishvi, and Beriah—and their sister Serah open the register (7:30). Through Beriah, the two branches of Heber (with his sister Shua) and Malchiel fan out across numerous clans such as Japhlet, Shomer, Helem, Zophah, Jether, and Ulla (7:31–39). Finally, matching Issachar and Benjamin earlier in the chapter, Asher's genealogy concludes with a military muster: 26,000 "choice and mighty warriors, chief leaders" ready for war (7:40).

## Key Takeaways

**All the northern tribes still belong to the great family of God's people.** When 1 Chronicles was written after the exile, the northern kingdom had fallen to Assyria centuries earlier. Yet the author painstakingly gathers every available scrap of genealogy, troop count, and town list for Issachar, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, and Asher. In God's sight, His people are not merely a small circle around Jerusalem, but all twelve tribes.

**Compassion and renewal can blossom right in the middle of loss.** The brief episode of Ephraim is the tenderest moment in the chapter. Faced with the tragedy of sons killed during a reckless cattle raid, Scripture neither hides the father's long days of mourning nor forgets the relatives who walked over to sit and comfort him (7:21–22). And immediately after the name Beriah marks the disaster, God opens a path to new life: towns are built and a line runs forward to Joshua (7:23–27).

**Women play an indispensable part in sustaining and building the community.** In a male-dominated ancient world, chapter 7 deliberately records the names and achievements of numerous women: Zelophehad's daughters, Maacah, Hammolecheth, Serah, Shua, and especially Sheerah, who built three towns herself (7:15–18; 7:24; 7:30–32). Building God's people has never belonged to one gender alone.

**Scripture tells history candidly, without polishing its ancestors.** Just as it did not hide Reuben's sin in chapter 5, chapter 7 plainly admits that Ephraim's sons were killed by the men of Gath because they went down to steal their livestock (7:21), and notes freely that Manasseh's descendants came through an Aramean concubine (7:14). God works through real, flawed, wounded human beings rather than waiting for flawless heroes to carry salvation history forward.

## Deeper Meaning

**From catastrophe in Ephraim's house to the leader who brought Israel into the Promised Land.** Verses 7:20–27 trace a remarkably moving arc. Everything starts with a botched raid that leaves Ephraim bereaved, naming his newborn son Beriah in memory of the disaster (7:21–23). Yet that very Ephraimite branch passes through Elishama—Ephraim's tribal leader in the wilderness (Numbers 1:10)—to culminate in Joshua son of Nun, the man God chose to succeed Moses and lead all Israel across the Jordan to claim the Promised Land (Joshua 1:1–2). Just as Jabez's painful name in chapter 4 opened into an answered prayer (4:9–10), Israel's story shows again and again that old wounds cannot lock shut the future God grants.

**Zelophehad's daughters and a voice for women's justice.** Verse 7:15 remarks briefly that "Zelophehad had only daughters," yet any reader of the Old Testament immediately recalls the celebrated episode in Numbers. When their father died without sons, his five daughters—Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah—stood boldly before Moses and the entire assembly asking for an inheritance so their father's name would not disappear from his clan. The LORD Himself declared, "The daughters of Zelophehad speak rightly," and established a statute protecting daughters' inheritance rights in Israel (Numbers 27:1–7). One brief line in a genealogy suffices to recall that landmark victory for justice.

**Beth-horon and territories tied to salvation history.** Upper and Lower Beth-horon, built by Sheerah (7:24), guarded the steep road climbing from the coastal plain into the hill country of Judah and Ephraim (Joshua 16:3–5). Along that very descent of Beth-horon, God routed the coalition of five Amorite kings with a massive hailstorm before Joshua (Joshua 10:10–11), and centuries later King Solomon still fortified those twin strongholds to defend the realm (2 Chronicles 8:5). Likewise, the four cities of Beth-shean, Taanach, Megiddo, and Dor (7:29) were places from which Manasseh had once failed to drive out the Canaanites in Joshua's time (Joshua 17:11–12), yet they remain recorded in full on this map of inheritance as a reminder that God's promises to Joseph's house (Genesis 48:19–20; Deuteronomy 33:13–17) were never revoked.

**Traces of heroes and quiet keepers of faith.** Hidden within the unfamiliar rows of names in chapter 7 are threads running across the whole Bible. From Abiezer in Manasseh (7:18) would arise the judge Gideon (Judges 6:11); the names Tola and Puah in Issachar (7:1) recall the judge Tola son of Puah (Judges 10:1), while Issachar would later be praised as men "who understood the times and knew what Israel should do" when rallying to make David king (12:32). Farther ahead in the New Testament, inside the Jerusalem temple when the infant Jesus was presented to the Lord, the aged prophetess Anna—who recognized the Redeemer—belonged to the northern tribe of Asher (Luke 2:36). Even from northern tribes long thought vanished in history, faithful souls still waited for God's salvation.

**An incomplete list pointing toward future fullness.** Careful readers notice that chapter 7 passes over Dan and Zebulun—a gap echoed strikingly in the Bible's final book, where the list of 144,000 sealed from the twelve tribes also omits Dan (Revelation 7:5–8). Yet the Chronicler himself opened the entire genealogical section by naming all twelve sons of Israel in full (2:1–2). Though actual history is scarred by disruption, loss, and scattering, God's covenant still moves toward a complete people gathered together in Him.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why are Dan and Zebulun missing while Benjamin appears in both chapter 7 and chapter 8?** This is one of the best-known puzzles in the opening nine chapters of 1 Chronicles. Two main explanations are offered. First, many commentators note that *Hushim* (7:12) matches the name of Dan's only son in Genesis 46:23—while Hebrew *Aher* means *another*—so verse 7:12 may preserve a tiny fragment of Dan's genealogy placed right before Naphtali, Dan's fellow son of Bilhah (7:13; cf. Genesis 30:5–8). As for Zebulun, several modern scholars hypothesize that 7:6–11 originally came from a Zebulun muster list mistakenly labeled Benjamin by an ancient scribe, since 7:6 lists only three sons for Benjamin (Bela, Becher, Jediael), unlike the five sons in 8:1–2 and Numbers 26:38–40 or ten in Genesis 46:21. The second explanation keeps the text as it stands: the Chronicler worked from whatever archival records survived for the long-exiled northern tribes, giving Benjamin a brief military tally in chapter 7 and devoting all of chapter 8 to the lineage leading to King Saul and the residents of Jerusalem.

**Speaking plainly about polygamy (7:4) and cattle raiding (7:21).** Verse 7:4 remarks that Izrahiah's descendants fielded large numbers "for they had many wives and children." Scripture reports an ancient social reality—when polygamy was practiced to expand clan manpower—without endorsing it as the marriage ideal God established from the beginning (Genesis 2:24). Similarly, verse 7:21 bluntly reports that Ephraim's sons went down to Gath to steal the locals' livestock and were killed in the clash. The text neither excuses nor defends the raid, letting readers see the painful cost of greed and violence.

**How could Ephraim still be alive to mourn descendants six or seven generations down?** Read as a straight father-to-son chain, verses 7:20–21 place Ezer and Elead six or seven generations after Ephraim, making it surprising when verse 7:22 says Ephraim himself mourned them and then fathered Beriah. Commentators typically offer three views: Ezer and Elead were direct sons of Ephraim while Shuthelah's line represents a parallel branch; ancient genealogies occasionally list contemporaneous clan divisions sequentially, as seen in the repeated names Shuthelah and Tahath (7:20–21); or in biblical genealogical style, "Ephraim" may refer to the patriarch still living in Egypt while his sons grazed herds into Canaan and clashed with Gath, or to the tribe of Ephraim collectively.

**Large troop tallies and traces of David's census.** The figures of 87,000 warriors in Issachar (7:5), nearly 60,000 in Benjamin (7:7; 7:9; 7:11), and 26,000 in Asher (7:40) follow the format of a military roster, explicitly dated by verse 7:2 "in the days of David." Even though Joab halted David's late census unfinished and never entered its final total into the royal chronicles (27:23–24), tribal military records from David's era evidently survived in part. Remember too that many scholars note the Hebrew word rendered *thousand* can sometimes designate a clan division or military squad, so Old Testament troop figures remain open to more than one interpretation.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the great Jewish compendium of law and tradition compiled between the third and sixth centuries, preserves a famous story built right around verses 7:21–22. Discussing Ezekiel's vision of the valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37:1–4), one rabbi taught that those bones belonged to the Ephraimites: miscalculating the covenant timeline, they rushed out of Egypt early to march on the Promised Land and were slaughtered by the men of Gath—which is also why God later led Israel away from the Philistine road, lest they see their brothers' bones and lose heart to return to Egypt (Exodus 13:17). **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses at length over Ephraim's bereavement (7:21–23). He remarks candidly that the calamity stemmed partly from the sons' own wrongdoing in going to seize other people's cattle—a sobering warning against injustice. At the same time, Henry deeply values the detail of Ephraim's relatives coming to comfort the grieving father: sharing sorrow with the bereaved is a warm duty of brotherly love, and when God grants the household a new son, Beriah, after their mourning, He shows Himself able to heal and rebuild shattered homes. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Chronicles 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's pastoral observations on the theft in verse 21, brotherly comfort in verse 22, and restoration through Beriah in verse 23 are clearly recalled; exact wording not checked -->

**H. G. M. Williamson**, the British Old Testament scholar, digs into the structural puzzle of chapter 7: why Benjamin appears both here and in chapter 8 while Zebulun and Dan are absent. He carefully evaluates the proposal that verses 7:6–11 originally drew from a military list for Zebulun mislabeled Benjamin, while Hushim (7:12) preserves a trace of Dan. Beyond that, Williamson notes that the military rosters for Issachar, Benjamin, and Asher reflect an archive from David's reign used by the Chronicler to affirm the full vitality and standing of the northern tribes within Israel. *(1 and 2 Chronicles, New Century Bible Commentary, 1982)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Williamson's analysis of the Zebulun hypothesis in 7:6–11, the trace of Dan in 7:12, and Davidic military sources is familiar from his NCBC commentary; exact page not checked -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli scholar of Chronicles, views the Ephraim episode (7:21–24) as a unique window into the Chronicler's historical outlook. Unlike the traditional sequence placing Ephraim's family in Egypt until the Exodus into Canaan, this passage depicts the ancestor Ephraim and his daughter Sheerah living, fighting, and building towns right inside Canaan. For Japhet, by incorporating such clan traditions, the Chronicler underscores the continuous, unbroken bond between Israel and the land God gave them. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Sara Japhet's thesis that 7:21–24 reflects the Chronicler's view of Israel's uninterrupted presence in the land is a renowned signature point of her OTL commentary -->

**Gary N. Knoppers**, the American Old Testament professor specializing in 1 Chronicles 1–9, highlights a striking feature of chapter 7: its remarkable density of female figures—from Manasseh's Aramean concubine, Maacah, Zelophehad's daughters, and Hammolecheth to Sheerah the builder of three cities and the sisters Serah and Shua in Asher. In Knoppers's view, recording both women's achievements and mixed-ancestry lines like Manasseh's Aramean branch reveals a far broader, more inclusive outlook than the older stereotype that Chronicles cared only about pure bloodlines and men. *(I Chronicles 1–9, Anchor Bible, 2004)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: highlighting the prominent role of women and intermarriage in 1 Chronicles 7 is a notable emphasis in Knoppers's Anchor Bible commentary; exact page not verified -->
