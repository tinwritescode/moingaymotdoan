# 1 Chronicles 5 — The Tribes East of the Jordan

## Story

Leaving Judah and Simeon in the south, the genealogy crosses to the eastern bank of the Jordan River to cover the two and a half tribes that had chosen to settle there back in Moses' time: Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh. Before listing a single name, however, the author pauses for two verses to settle an obvious question: why is Reuben, Israel's firstborn, not placed at the head of the genealogies? The answer is blunt: "because he defiled his father's bed, his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph." Even though Joseph was not enrolled as the firstborn in the genealogy, the author explains clearly how honor was divided among the brothers: "Though Judah prevailed over his brothers and a ruler came from him, the birthright belonged to Joseph" (5:1–2).

Reuben's genealogy begins with his four sons and follows one branch down to the tribal leader Beerah, who was later carried into exile by Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria (5:3–6). Living in the rich grazing country of Gilead, their herds multiplied so rapidly that they spread eastward all the way to the edge of the desert near the Euphrates River. As early as the days of King Saul, the Reubenites defeated the Hagrites in battle and occupied their encampments east of Gilead (5:7–10).

Directly north of Reuben lived the tribe of Gad, inhabiting Bashan and Gilead as far as the pasturelands of Sharon. The author names their clan heads—Joel, Shapham, the seven sons of Abihail, and chief Ahi—noting that their genealogical registry was officially compiled during the reigns of Jotham king of Judah and Jeroboam king of Israel (5:11–17).

Suddenly the register shifts from dry names to a sweeping, dramatic war account. All three eastern groups—Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh—mustered 44,760 valiant men "able to bear shield and sword, to draw the bow, and trained for battle," and marched out against a coalition of desert tribes: the Hagrites, Jetur, Naphish, and Nodab (5:18–19). The turning point of the war lay not in blades or arrows, but in a cry for help: "They were helped in fighting them, and God delivered the Hagrites and all their allies into their hands, because they cried out to Him during the battle. He answered their prayers, because they trusted in Him" (5:20). They took an immense spoil—50,000 camels, 250,000 sheep, 2,000 donkeys, and 100,000 captives—"for the battle was of God," and they settled securely in that territory until the exile (5:21–22).

The final section records the numerous half-tribe of Manasseh stretching from Bashan up to Mount Hermon, led by seven clan chiefs who were all "mighty men of valor, famous men" (5:23–24). Yet immediately after that peak of honor comes a steep plunge in just two closing verses. They "were unfaithful to the God of their fathers and prostituted themselves with the gods of the peoples of the land, whom God had destroyed before them" (5:25). Therefore the God of Israel Himself "stirred up the spirit of Pul king of Assyria—that is, Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria," who swept in and carried Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh away into exile at Halah, Habor, Hara, and the river of Gozan (5:26).

## Key Takeaways

**Sin does not erase family identity, yet it can forfeit privilege.** Reuben remained a son of Israel and kept his place in the genealogy of God's people. Still, his reckless sin with his father's concubine cost him both the standing of firstborn and the double portion of inheritance. Wrong choices carry lasting consequences, even after forgiveness is given.

**True victory flows from trusting God, not merely military skill.** More than forty-four thousand eastern warriors were seasoned fighters skilled with shield and bow (5:18). Yet the author does not credit their tactics; he states plainly why they prevailed: right in the middle of combat, they cried out to God and trusted Him, and God Himself intervened (5:20; 5:22).

**Once faithfulness to God is lost, every outward strength collapses.** In verse 5:24, the eastern leaders are praised as mighty warriors, famous men, great in number. One verse later, because they turned away to follow neighboring idols, all those elite armies are swept away into Assyrian captivity. History does not run on might making right, but under the righteous judgment of God.

**God rules even over empires that do not know Him.** The chapter's closing verse delivers a stunning claim: Assyria's emperor did not invade merely on his own whim; rather, "the God of Israel stirred up the spirit" of that king to discipline an unfaithful people (5:26). No superpower stands outside God's sovereign hand.

## Deeper Meaning

**From Jacob's rebuke to the division of the birthright.** Verses 5:1–2 tie together three key threads from Genesis. Reuben had slept with Bilhah, his father's concubine (Genesis 35:22), so on his deathbed Jacob declared that Reuben "shall no longer excel" (Genesis 49:3–4). Instead, Jacob adopted Joseph's two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, as his own (Genesis 48:5), giving Joseph the double inheritance that constituted the firstborn's birthright under Mosaic law (Deuteronomy 21:17), while leadership and the royal scepter passed to Judah.

**An old charge beside the Jordan forgotten.** When Israel first approached the Promised Land, these two and a half tribes asked Moses for permission to settle east of the Jordan because its rich pasture suited their livestock (Numbers 32:1–5). When Joshua later sent them home to their territory, he charged them earnestly to love the LORD, keep His commandments, and hold fast to Him with all their heart (Joshua 22:1–5). The first half of chapter 5 displays the blessing when they remembered that charge; the last two verses reveal the disaster when wealth and surrounding idols drew them away from the covenant.

**"The battle was of God."** That concise explanation (5:22) rings with a classic note of Old Testament faith: just as David declared before Goliath that "the battle belongs to the LORD" (1 Samuel 17:47), and a prophet later assured King Jehoshaphat that "the battle is not yours, but God's" (2 Chronicles 20:15). Along the same line, their cry in crisis answered by God (5:20) fulfills the psalmist's promise: "Call upon Me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you" (Psalms 50:15).

**Idolatry is betrayal in love.** The text does not merely say they adopted new religious customs; it uses stark language—they "prostituted themselves with the gods of the peoples" (5:25). Throughout the Old Testament, especially in Hosea, the bond between God and His people is pictured as a covenant marriage (Hosea 1:2). Chasing other gods is never just an intellectual error; it is unfaithfulness that wounds the heart of the One who redeemed them.

**Assyria as nothing more than a rod in God's hand.** Verse 5:26 declaring that God stirred up the king of Assyria against the unfaithful tribes (cf. 2 Kings 15:29; 17:6) echoes Isaiah's description of Assyria as "the rod of My anger" (Isaiah 10:5–6). This truth helped the post-exilic community see that losing their land did not mean Israel's God was weaker than the gods of Assyria or Babylon; rather, God Himself had disciplined His unfaithful people.

## Notes & Nuances

**Are Pul and Tiglath-pileser (5:26) two different kings or one man?** Some older translations read "Pul king of Assyria and Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria," making it sound like two monarchs invading together. Ancient Assyrian and Babylonian inscriptions reveal that both names belong to the same ruler: Tiglath-pileser III (reigned c. 745–727 BC), who used the throne name *Pulu* (Pul) as king of Babylon. In Hebrew, the conjunction usually translated *and* can also work explanatorily to mean *that is*—hence "Pul king of Assyria, that is, Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria."

**The huge numbers of plunder and captives (5:21).** The victory over the Hagrites yielded 50,000 camels, 250,000 sheep, and 100,000 captives—staggering figures for the ancient desert frontier. Beyond a literal reading of a massive campaign against wealthy nomadic coalitions, many biblical scholars note that the Hebrew word commonly rendered *thousand* (*eleph*) can sometimes refer to a clan group or military unit, or that ancient battle tallies often used rounded epic figures to convey an overwhelming, God-given victory.

**Reading Old Testament warfare narratives fairly.** Reading that Israel captured a hundred thousand people and that "many fell slain, because the battle was of God" (5:21–22) can understandably unsettle modern readers. It helps to keep in mind that the eastern border beyond the Jordan was constantly raided by desert nomads plundering Israelite villages—as seen in the book of Judges—making this a struggle for survival on the frontier rather than a timeless mandate for aggression. Moreover, the chapter's own ending (5:25–26) demonstrates God's complete impartiality: when Israel itself turned unfaithful like the former nations, God showed no favoritism and allowed them to suffer the same loss of their land.

**The chronological note at verse 5:17.** King Jotham of Judah and King Jeroboam II of Israel did not reign at exactly the same time; Jeroboam II died more than a decade before Jotham began his sole reign in Judah. Historians commonly explain this as two separate registrations conducted in neighboring reigns, or a census begun under Jeroboam II and completed early in Jotham's era.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws a sharp contrast between the two halves of chapter 5. First, looking at Reuben (5:1), he warns that sin leaves an enduring stain capable of stripping an entire family of honor. Next, setting the victory of verse 5:20 beside the exile of verse 5:26, Henry observes that as long as God's people knew how to pray in times of trouble, they were invincible; the moment they abandoned God for idols, all their swordsmanship and fame became worthless. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Chronicles 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic moral exposition on Reuben's sin, the battle cry of verse 20, and the downfall through idolatry in verses 25–26 is well recalled; exact wording not checked -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli scholar of Chronicles, draws special attention to the Hebrew verb *ma'al* (rendered "were unfaithful," conveying *covenant treachery or breach of trust*), appearing here for the first time in Chronicles (5:25). She traces how this keynote term links chapter 5 to King Saul's death in chapter 10 and Judah's exile at the close of 2 Chronicles: in the Chronicler's theology, exile is never a random historical accident, but always the direct consequence of unfaithfulness toward God. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing the theological role of the key term ma'al [unfaithfulness] from 5:25 across both books of Chronicles is a celebrated thesis in Sara Japhet's OTL commentary -->
