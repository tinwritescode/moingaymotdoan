# 1 Chronicles 8 — The Tribe of Benjamin and the Line of King Saul

## Story

Where chapter 7 sketched only a few military totals for Benjamin, chapter 8 devotes forty full verses to painting a complete portrait of Israel's youngest tribe. The genealogy opens with Benjamin's five sons in birth order from oldest to youngest: Bela, Ashbel, Aharah, Nohah, and Rapha (8:1–2). The line of the firstborn, Bela, is traced down through nine sons, including Gera, Naaman, Ahoah, and Shephuphan (8:3–5).

The register then shifts to the sons of Ehud—clan heads at Geba—paired with a brief, intriguing event: "they were carried into exile to Manahath," tied to the names Naaman, Ahijah, and Gera (8:6–7). Next comes another family vignette concerning Shaharaim: he fathered children in the country of Moab "after he had divorced his wives Hushim and Baara." By his new wife Hodesh, he fathered seven sons who all became clan heads, in addition to two sons—Abitub and Elpaal—born earlier to Hushim (8:8–11).

Through Elpaal's line, the text records Benjamin's expansion toward the northwestern plains. Shemed, one of Elpaal's three sons, "built Ono and Lod with their surrounding villages" (8:12). Right after him come two brothers, Beriah and Shema: "they were the heads of the families of the inhabitants of Aijalon, and they drove out the inhabitants of Gath" (8:13). It is only a single verse, yet anyone who has just read chapter 7 catches the striking reversal immediately: where the men of Gath had once killed Ephraim's sons and brought tragedy on that house (7:21), Benjamin's descendants at Aijalon drove out those very men of Gath.

The next fifteen verses call the roll of five large clusters of clan heads across the lines of Beriah, Elpaal, Shimei, Shashak, and Jeroham (8:14–27). All those names converge on one central address: "These were the heads of their fathers' houses, leaders according to their generations, and they lived in Jerusalem" (8:28). Right alongside Judah, Benjamin was a longtime resident rooted directly in the holy capital.

At verse 29, the author's focus moves closer to the town of Gibeon and the clan of Israel's first king. The father of Gibeon—named explicitly as Jeiel in chapter 9—and his wife Maacah fathered many sons, among whom the branch of Mikloth and Shimeah likewise "lived opposite their relatives in Jerusalem" (8:29–32). Then the main line leads straight to the royal household: "Ner was the father of Kish, Kish was the father of Saul," and Saul fathered four sons—Jonathan, Malchi-shua, Abinadab, and Esh-baal (8:33).

Far from leaving the impression that Saul's family perished without trace on Mount Gilboa, the genealogy patiently follows Jonathan's descendants for more than ten generations. Jonathan fathered Merib-baal; Merib-baal fathered Micah; from Micah the chain continues through Ahaz, Jehoaddah, Zimri, Moza, Binea, Raphah, and Eleasah down to Azel and his six sons (8:34–38). Beside Azel stood his brother Eshek, father of Ulam, Jeush, and Eliphelet (8:39). Chapter 8—and with it the entire genealogy of the twelve tribes—closes with a vibrant image: the descendants of Ulam numbered "one hundred and fifty in all," every one of them "mighty men of valor, archers" (8:40).

## Key Takeaways

**A small tribe stepped into a pillar role beside Judah.** In the design of 1 Chronicles 1–9, Judah opens the twelve-tribe survey (chapters 2–4), Levi stands at its center (chapter 6), and Benjamin closes the circle (chapter 8). Those very three tribes formed the backbone that sustained the southern kingdom and the community returning from exile. The youngest brother in Jacob's family can still shoulder the weight of upholding the whole community.

**The descendants of a failed king are not blotted out by God.** Even though chapter 10 is about to deliver a stern verdict on King Saul's unfaithfulness, the author still devotes the closing stretch of chapter 8 to tracing his descendants across more than ten generations (8:33–40). God's judgment on an erring father does not shut off His mercy toward the generations that follow.

**Rootedness in Jerusalem is the gathering point of Benjamin's genealogy.** Twice in the chapter, the author emphasizes that Benjamin's clan leaders lived in Jerusalem, facing their relatives (8:28; 8:32). For Jews freshly returned from Babylon who needed to repopulate a ruined city, recalling this ancient heritage of residence encouraged them to keep planting roots around the place where God was worshiped.

**Real strength is forged through resilience across generations.** Benjamin in this chapter weathered plenty of upheaval: forced removal from Geba to Manahath, displacement into Moab, and losing the crown after Saul (8:6–8; 8:33). Yet they still built towns like Ono and Lod, drove out the people of Gath, and finished with 150 skilled archers in Ulam's house (8:12–13; 8:40). God preserves His people through seasons that look bleakest.

## Deeper Meaning

**The three tribes that rebuilt the post-exilic community.** Giving Judah, Levi, and Benjamin the headline spots in 1 Chronicles 1–9 mirrors the actual arc of biblical history. When the ten northern tribes broke away after Solomon's death, Benjamin alone stood shoulder to shoulder with David's house in Jerusalem (1 Kings 12:21). Centuries later, when King Cyrus allowed the Jews to return and rebuild the temple, it was "the family heads of Judah and Benjamin, and the priests and Levites" who rose to go back (Ezra 1:5). Even the twin towns of Ono and Lod built by Shemed (8:12) later became familiar settlements for the returning exiles (Ezra 2:33; Nehemiah 6:2; 11:35).

**Moses' blessing concerning where God dwells.** Before his death, Moses blessed Benjamin with tender words: the beloved of the LORD rests secure in Him, and God "dwells between his shoulders" (Deuteronomy 33:12). When the Promised Land was apportioned, Jebus—that is, Jerusalem—sat right on the border of Benjamin's territory (Joshua 18:28). Chapter 8's repeated reminder that Benjaminite clans lived in Jerusalem (8:28; 8:32) shows that ancient blessing coming true in the capital where God's temple stood.

**Sweet fruit from the covenant of friendship between David and Jonathan.** The entire final stretch of chapter 8 (8:34–40) hangs on one fragile link: Merib-baal, Jonathan's son. In 2 Samuel he is called Mephibosheth—the five-year-old boy crippled in both feet when his nurse dropped him while fleeing news of Saul and Jonathan's deaths (2 Samuel 4:4). By the ruthless customs of ancient dynasties, a prince from the former royal house rarely survived. Yet remembering his covenant with Jonathan, King David sought Mephibosheth out, restored Saul's estates to him, and promised, "You will always eat at my table" (2 Samuel 9:6–7). Because of that steadfast kindness, a disabled boy whose future seemed over became the forefather of more than ten thriving generations, crowned by the 150 warriors of Ulam.

**Benjamin's famous marksmanship across the Old Testament.** The praise of Ulam's descendants as skilled "archers" (8:40) joins a long tribal tradition. Back in the period of the judges, Benjamin produced the left-handed deliverer Ehud son of Gera (Judges 3:15) and seven hundred elite slingers who could sling a stone at a hair and not miss (Judges 20:16). Later in 1 Chronicles 12, Saul's own kinsmen from Benjamin—equally skilled with bow and sling using either hand—would rally to David at Ziklag (12:1–2). When placed in the right service, a family's natural gifts become a shield for God's people.

**From the tribe of Benjamin to Mordecai and the apostle Paul.** Benjamin's stream does not dry up at verse 8:40. During the Persian exile, when the Jewish people faced annihilation, God raised up Mordecai—a Benjaminite from the line of Kish—alongside Queen Esther to rescue the whole nation (Esther 2:5). And in the New Testament, the apostle who carried the gospel to the nations was a man bearing the very name of his tribe's first king: Saul, better known as Paul, who gladly noted his heritage in "the tribe of Benjamin" (Philippians 3:5).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why do Saul's descendants carry *Baal* in their names?** Verses 8:33–34 name Saul's youngest son *Esh-baal* and Jonathan's son *Merib-baal*; earlier in verse 8:30, an uncle of Saul is simply named *Baal*. By contrast, 2 Samuel renders those two names *Ish-bosheth* and *Mephibosheth* (2 Samuel 2:8; 4:4). The shift reflects how language changed over time: in the early monarchy, the Hebrew word *baal* meant simply *master* or *lord*, and Israelites sometimes used it even of the LORD Himself. Later, as the name Baal became inextricably bound to detestable Canaanite idolatry, scribes transmitting Samuel replaced the ending *-baal* with *-bosheth* (meaning *shame*). Chronicles, however, preserves the original older forms untouched.

**Speaking candidly about Shaharaim divorcing two wives (8:8).** The text states matter-of-factly that Shaharaim fathered children in Moab "after he had divorced his wives Hushim and Baara" and married Hodesh, without explaining why or offering praise or blame. This reflects the Old Testament's candid style in genealogies: recording what happened in family history without approving divorce as pleasing to God. Scripture's clear moral stance on marriage appears elsewhere, as when the prophet Malachi relays God's word, "I hate divorce" (Malachi 2:16).

**Small differences between 8:29–38, 9:35–44, and 1 Samuel 14.** Attentive readers notice two points worth comparing. First, verse 8:29 mentions only "the father of Gibeon" without giving his personal name, whereas the parallel text in 9:35–36 supplies his full name—"Jeiel the father of Gibeon"—and includes *Ner* among his sons, a name that dropped out at 8:30. Second, verse 8:33 reads, "Ner was the father of Kish, Kish was the father of Saul," whereas 1 Samuel 14:50–51 lists Kish (Saul's father) and Ner (father of General Abner) as brothers, both sons of Abiel (1 Samuel 9:1). Commentators generally explain that Abiel may also have been called Ner, or that two different generations within the Gibeon clan reused the names Kish and Ner—naming grandsons after grandfathers was very common in ancient genealogies.

**The "exile" in verses 8:6–7 is not the Babylonian exile.** The phrase "carried into exile to Manahath" can easily be mistaken for deportation to Babylon or Assyria. In fact, Manahath was a locality right inside Judah, near Bethlehem and Jerusalem. This refers to an ancient local displacement or clan expulsion whose detailed story was lost over time, leaving only a brief historical footnote in the genealogy.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, explains why Benjamin receives an entire dedicated chapter here after already appearing in chapter 7: Benjamin stood loyally beside Judah when the kingdom split, shared the capital Jerusalem, and returned together from exile. Looking at the long list of Jonathan's descendants (8:34–40), Henry remarks encouragingly that although the crown passed away from Saul's house, God still blessed the line of Jonathan—the loyal son who loved David—granting his family abundance and honor across many generations. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Chronicles 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's pastoral points on Benjamin's loyalty alongside Judah and God's blessing on Jonathan's line at the end of chapter 8 are clearly remembered; exact wording not checked -->

**H. G. M. Williamson**, the British Old Testament scholar, focuses on the geographical notes in 8:12–13 and 8:28–32. He connects Benjamin's building of Ono and Lod and settlement at Aijalon with the actual post-exilic Persian-period boundaries mentioned in Ezra and Nehemiah. At the same time, Williamson traces how smoothly the author moves from Benjaminite clans in Jerusalem to the Gibeon household and King Saul (8:29–40), building a bridge toward repeating Saul's line at the end of chapter 9 and opening directly into Saul's death in chapter 10. *(1 and 2 Chronicles, New Century Bible Commentary, 1982)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: connecting Ono and Lod to post-exilic boundaries and noting the literary bridge to chapters 9–10 are familiar points in Williamson's NCBC commentary; exact page not verified -->

**Martin J. Selman**, the British Old Testament scholar, emphasizes the Chronicler's balanced and gracious treatment of King Saul's family. Rather than attacking Saul or erasing him from the record in order to exalt David, chapter 8 respectfully traces over ten generations of Saul's descendants down to Ulam's 150 valiant archers. For Selman, this affirms that while the crown belonged to David's line, both Saul's household and the tribe of Benjamin retained an honored place within God's covenant people. *(1 Chronicles, Tyndale Old Testament Commentaries, 1994)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: observing the Chronicler's fair treatment of Saul's descendants in 8:29–40 matches Selman's commentary in TOTC; exact page not verified -->
