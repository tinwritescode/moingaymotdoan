# 1 Chronicles 28 — David's Charge and the Plans for the Temple

## Story

With every temple division and national office set in order (23:1–32; 27:1–34), King David convenes a grand assembly in Jerusalem. Every leader of Israel attends: tribal chiefs, division officers serving the king, commanders of thousands and hundreds, stewards over the royal estates and livestock along with the king's sons, court officials, and mighty warriors (28:1). Though advanced in years, David rises to his feet and addresses the nation with warm intimacy: "Listen to me, my brothers and my people!" He shares his long-held dream of building "a house of rest for the ark of the covenant of the LORD, for the footstool of our God," for which he had prepared abundant materials (28:2). Yet God intervened directly: "You are not to build a house for My name, because you are a man of war who has shed blood" (28:3).

Not a trace of bitterness clouds David's voice. Instead, he traces God's unbroken chain of gracious choice so the entire nation will rally behind his successor. Out of all Israel, God chose Judah as leader; within Judah, He chose Jesse's house; among Jesse's sons, He took pleasure in making David king over all Israel (28:4). In that exact same way, out of all the many sons God gave David, He chose Solomon "to sit on the throne of the kingdom of the LORD over Israel" (28:5). God stated plainly that Solomon would build His temple and its courts, that God would be his Father and he God's son, and that if he remained resolute in keeping God's commands and ordinances, his kingdom would endure forever (28:6–7).

Turning to the gathered assembly, David urges them—in the sight of all Israel and in the hearing of God—to observe and seek out all the commands of the LORD their God, so they may possess this good land and pass it down as a lasting inheritance to their children forever (28:8). Then he turns directly to Solomon, delivering a father's most searching parting charge: "Know the God of your father, and serve Him with wholehearted devotion and a willing mind, for the LORD searches every heart and understands the intent of every thought." Right before his son, he draws a life-or-death line: "If you seek Him, He will be found by you; but if you forsake Him, He will reject you forever" (28:9). He closes with an urgent summons: the LORD has chosen you to build a house for the sanctuary—"Be strong and do the work" (28:10).

Right there before the assembly, David hands Solomon the complete architectural plans for the temple: the portico, its buildings, its storerooms, its upper rooms, its inner rooms, and the room for the mercy seat (28:11). He also passes on every plan "that the Spirit had put in his mind" for the courts of the LORD's house, the surrounding chambers, the treasuries of God's house, and the treasuries of dedicated gifts (28:12), together with the divisions of priests and Levites and every task and vessel for temple ministry (28:13).

The blueprint descends to exact weights of gold and silver for every sacred instrument: lampstands and lamps of gold and silver according to their place of service, gold and silver tables for the showbread, pure gold forks, sprinkling bowls, and pitchers, and measured weights for every gold and silver bowl (28:14–17). Crowning the plans are the altar of incense of refined gold and "the model for the chariot of the gold cherubim that spread their wings and overshadowed the ark of the covenant of the LORD" (28:18). David solemnly confirms the divine origin of the entire blueprint: "All this is in writing from the hand of the LORD upon me, and He gave me understanding in all the details of the plan" (28:19).

Finally, David strengthens Solomon one more time with ringing courage: "Be strong and courageous, and do the work. Do not be afraid or discouraged," because the LORD God, my God, is with you—"He will not fail you or forsake you" until every piece of work for the LORD's house is finished (28:20). Beside God's promised presence, David points to a ready community standing shoulder to shoulder with his son: the divisions of priests and Levites, willing skilled craftsmen of every kind, and the officials and entire nation ready to carry out every command (28:21).

## Key Takeaways

**Accepting God's boundaries frees us to prepare the way wholeheartedly for those who follow.** David dreamed for decades of building God's temple, yet when God stopped him because he had shed much blood (28:3), he neither brooded nor checked out. He poured his twilight years into stockpiling supplies, drawing up plans, and lifting Solomon publicly before the nation. Knowing when to step back so the next generation can finish something greater than ourselves marks true spiritual maturity.

**Every human throne really belongs to God.** Presenting Solomon, David does not call it inheriting his father's personal crown; God has set Solomon "on the throne of the kingdom of the LORD" (28:5). Authority—whether on a throne or in any daily stewardship—is never a private prize for self-importance, but a trust held under God.

**True service to God begins inside an undivided heart.** Verse 28:9 ranks among the deepest sentences in the Old Testament: know the God of your father and "serve Him with wholehearted devotion and a willing mind, for the LORD searches every heart and understands the intent of every thought." God looks right past gilded walls and grand ceremonies into the hidden motives and willingness of every heart.

**Divine election always walks hand in hand with faithful responsibility.** Right after celebrating God's choice of Solomon as His son (28:6), David lays two paths squarely before him: seek God resolutely and find Him with lasting strength, or forsake Him and face rejection (28:7; 28:9). Being chosen by God never grants a license for carelessness or presumption.

**Real courage flows from God's promise never to leave us.** Building the temple loomed dauntingly large for a young monarch, yet David does not tell Solomon to rely on cleverness or wealth. The solid ground for "Do not be afraid or discouraged" is knowing for certain that God stands with him and will never walk away before the work is done (28:20).

## Deeper Meaning

**David follows in Moses' footsteps: receiving the temple "pattern" directly from God.** Highlighting that David hands over a Spirit-given "plan" guided by the LORD's hand (28:11–12; 28:19) immediately evokes Moses atop Mount Sinai, where God commanded him to make the tabernacle and all its furnishings strictly according to the pattern shown on the mountain (Exodus 25:8–9; 25:40). Pairing these two moments confirms that Jerusalem's temple was no personal whim of a king, but the direct continuation of the divinely designed tabernacle. In the New Testament, Hebrews cites that exact Sinai word to teach that the earthly sanctuary was "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things," pointing believers to the true sanctuary where Christ ministers (Hebrews 8:5).

**Solomon follows in Joshua's footsteps: a commissioning echoing beside the Jordan.** David's charge to Solomon (28:20)—"Be strong and courageous, and do the work. Do not be afraid or discouraged," backed by "He will not fail you or forsake you"—mirrors nearly word for word Moses' commissioning of Joshua before entering Canaan and God's own words to Joshua (Deuteronomy 31:6–8; Joshua 1:5–6). Just as Joshua led Israel across the Jordan where Moses could not go, Solomon builds the temple where David could not lay the first stone. Later, the New Testament places that identical promise—"Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you"—into the hands of every follower of Christ (Hebrews 13:5).

**The ark as "footstool" and cherubim as "chariot" of the Most High.** Verse 28:2 calls the ark "the footstool of our God" (cf. Psalms 99:5; 132:7), while verse 28:18 depicts the gold cherubim spreading wings above it as a royal "chariot." The picture evokes a cosmic King seated on heaven's throne, with the earthly sanctuary merely the place where His feet rest—just as God declares through Isaiah: "Heaven is My throne, and earth is My footstool" (Isaiah 66:1). The temple never shrinks or cages God; it calls humanity to awe before the transcendent Lord who stoops to dwell among His people.

**The Searcher of hearts and His promise to seekers.** Verse 28:9 weaves together two great threads of Scripture. First, from the day Samuel went to anoint David among his tall brothers—learning that "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7)—to Jeremiah's declaration (Jeremiah 17:10), God always searches human hearts to their depths. Second, the assurance "If you seek Him, He will be found by you" becomes a recurring anthem throughout 2 Chronicles (2 Chronicles 15:2), rings out in Jeremiah (Jeremiah 29:13), and shines in Jesus' own promise: "Seek, and you will find" (Matthew 7:7–8).

**The line of choice from Judah to Messiah—and a quiet shadow over Solomon.** Tracing God's election in verse 28:4 runs back to Jacob's ancient blessing on Judah (Genesis 49:10), narrowing to Jesse's house, David, and Solomon (1 Samuel 16:12–13; 17:11–14). Yet the warning against forsaking God (28:9) carries an aching irony for readers who know what lies ahead: in his old age, Solomon's heart truly turned away from the LORD, no longer wholly devoted like his father David, leading to the tearing apart of the kingdom after his death (1 Kings 11:4; 11:9–11). Solomon's flaw leaves readers longing all the more for David's true Son, Jesus—who lived every single moment in wholehearted obedience to the Father.

## Notes & Nuances

**Where did the succession struggle of 1 Kings 1 go?** Readers of 1 Kings remember David lying frail and bedridden while Prince Adonijah crowned himself, forcing urgent emergency anointing for Solomon (1 Kings 1). Yet here in 1 Chronicles 28:2, David rises to his feet and addresses all Israel's leaders in complete unity. The Chronicler is not erasing history—his readers already knew Kings well—but spotlighting a formal public assembly of transition (or selecting the unified national dimension) to underscore what mattered most for returning exiles: Solomon stood undeniably chosen by God to build the temple.

**Does "you have shed blood" (28:3) mean David's wars were sinful?** Readers often wonder why God bars David from building as "a man of war who has shed blood" (28:3; cf. 22:8–9) when Scripture itself affirms God was with David in battle. Most commentators strike a balanced note: bloodshed—even in ancient defensive warfare—carried ritual defilement and belonged to an age of conflict, whereas the temple was "a house of rest" embodying peace, fittingly entrusted to Solomon (whose name echoes *peace*). Even so, the wording quietly preserves a deep moral truth about violence: war and bloodshed never represent God's highest ideal.

**Does the warning "He will reject you forever" (28:9) clash with chapter 17's unconditional covenant?** Chapter 17 promises God will never withdraw His steadfast love from David's line and will establish his throne forever (17:13–14). Why then do verses 28:7 and 28:9 attach an explicit "If..."? Commentators distinguish two levels of the promise: the covenant with *David's dynasty as a whole* stands unbreakable and unconditional, whereas blessing or judgment within the reign of *any individual monarch*—whether Solomon or later kings of Judah—depends directly on that ruler's own faithfulness.

**Key Hebrew expressions in verses 28:12, 28:18, and 28:19.** Verse 28:12 refers to the temple *treasuries* storing sacred vessels and dedicated funds; verse 28:18 specifies *refined gold*—gold thoroughly purified through fire—for the incense altar. As for verse 28:19 ("All this is in writing from the hand of the LORD upon me"), the Hebrew idiom expresses Spirit-guided inspiration just as verse 28:12 describes—meaning God's hand guided David's understanding as he set down the written blueprint, rather than imagining physical parchment literally dropping out of the sky.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, treasures David's counsel to Solomon (28:9) as a timeless model for every parent passing faith down to a child. He traces its two linked steps: first "know the God of your father," and only then "serve Him with wholehearted devotion and a willing mind"—for no one can truly worship a God they do not know, and genuine service must be undivided (not halfhearted) and glad (not grudging), because God searches every hidden motive.
