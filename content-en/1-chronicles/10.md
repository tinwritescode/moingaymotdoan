# 1 Chronicles 10 — The Death of King Saul

## Story

Through its first nine chapters, 1 Chronicles consists entirely of names and family lines, ending with the genealogy of King Saul's house (9:35–44). Stepping into chapter 10, the tone shifts abruptly: without a single word about Israel's first king in his youth or his years on the throne, the narrative opens straight onto the day he fell in battle. The Philistines fought against Israel; the men of Israel broke ranks and fled, and many fell slain on the slopes of Mount Gilboa. The enemy pressed hard after Saul and his sons, killing all three of his sons—Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchi-shua (10:1–2).

The ring tightened around Saul. "The battle intensified against Saul," and Philistine archers overtook and wounded him (10:3). Refusing to fall into enemy hands, Saul commanded his armor-bearer to draw his sword and run him through, "otherwise these uncircumcised men will come and torture me." Terrified, the armor-bearer could not bring himself to strike the king. So Saul took his own sword and fell on it. Seeing that his master was dead, the armor-bearer fell on his own sword and died beside him. In a single day, Saul, his three sons, and all his house died together (10:4–6).

Word of the disaster spread down into the valley. When the Israelites living nearby saw that the army had routed and that Saul and his sons were dead, they panicked, abandoned their towns, and fled. Without a fight, the Philistines marched in and occupied those cities (10:7).

The next day, when Philistine troops returned to strip the fallen, they found Saul and his sons lying dead on Mount Gilboa. They stripped off his armor, severed his head, and sent messengers throughout Philistia to proclaim the triumph to their idols and their people. They placed Saul's armor in the temple of their gods and fastened his head in the temple of Dagon as a trophy of war (10:8–10).

Yet loyalty had not vanished from Israel. When all the people of Jabesh-gilead heard what the Philistines had done to dishonor the king's body, every valiant man among them rose up, marched out, and recovered the bodies of Saul and his sons. Bringing them back to Jabesh, they buried their bones beneath the great tree in the town and fasted together for seven days in mourning (10:11–12).

Finally, the author pauses the narrative to deliver a crisp, candid verdict on Saul's life. Saul did not die merely by misfortune in war, but "for his unfaithfulness to the LORD, because he did not keep the word of the LORD and even consulted a medium for guidance, and he did not inquire of the LORD." Therefore the LORD Himself put him to death and turned the kingdom over to David son of Jesse (10:13–14).

## Key Takeaways

**Abandoning God's word cuts away the ground beneath our feet.** Saul did not lose his throne for lack of courage or soldiers. The author pinpoints the twin roots that brought down an entire dynasty: failing to keep the word of the LORD, and refusing to seek Him (10:13–14). Once a heart drifts away from the One who raised it up, outward power becomes an empty shell.

**When people stop seeking God, they easily turn toward dark substitutes.** Cornered and desperate, instead of repenting and returning to God, Saul sought out a medium. That was no minor lapse in a hard hour, but the final step down for a soul that refused God's guidance.

**A leader's fall pulls painful consequences onto everyone around them.** Saul's unfaithfulness did not stop with his own life. Three sons—including faithful Jonathan—fell beside him, his armor-bearer died with him, and ordinary families down in the valley fled their homes (10:2; 10:5–7). The greater a leader's trust, the heavier the fallout of reckless choices on those under their care.

**Even in the darkest hour, steadfast gratitude still shines.** The people of Jabesh-gilead did not blow with the wind once the old king lost power. They risked their lives crossing into enemy territory to rescue the bodies of Saul and his sons, bury them with honor, and fast seven days (10:11–12). Real loyalty does not wait until someone is powerful to show itself.

**The kingdom belongs to God, never as anyone's private property.** The closing verse lifts our eyes above swords and arrows: it was the LORD who ended Saul's reign and transferred the kingdom to David (10:14). History does not drift aimlessly at the mercy of Philistine might, but rests in the sovereign hand of God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Why does Chronicles launch its royal history with the day Saul died?** Unlike 1 Samuel, which spends more than twenty chapters tracing Saul's rise and decline, 1 Chronicles skips all of it, stepping directly from Saul's family tree at the end of chapter 9 onto Mount Gilboa. Writing for Jews newly returned from exile, the author sets Saul up as an unmistakable warning sign at the doorway to David's reign: an unfaithful king loses his land to foreign enemies (10:7), just as the eastern tribes had lost theirs earlier (5:25–26). Out of the darkness of verse 10:14, light immediately dawns for David in chapter 11.

**A debt of gratitude from Saul's earliest days as king.** Chapter 10 does not explain why the people of Jabesh-gilead risked so much for Saul, yet 1 Samuel fills in the backstory. Years earlier, when King Nahash of Ammon besieged Jabesh and threatened to gouge out every resident's right eye, the newly crowned Saul rallied Israel, marched through the night, and rescued the city (1 Samuel 11:1–11). However deeply Saul declined and failed in his later years, Jabesh-gilead never forgot the day he saved their lives.

**Saul's two sins looking back to 1 Samuel.** The indictment that Saul "did not keep the word of the LORD" recalls the two times he defied God's explicit commands delivered through Samuel: offering sacrifice himself when he had no right to wait no longer, and sparing Amalekite plunder God had ordered destroyed (1 Samuel 13:13–14; 15:22–23). Consulting a medium points directly to his desperate midnight visit to Endor on the eve of battle (1 Samuel 28:6–7)—a practice strictly forbidden by Moses and called detestable to the LORD (Deuteronomy 18:10–12). Just three chapters later, when David proposes bringing the ark back, he will name this very failure: throughout Saul's reign, Israel did not inquire of God before the ark (13:3).

**The temple of Dagon and the brief boast of an idol.** Fastening the Israelite king's head inside Dagon's temple (10:10) carried heavy irony. Decades earlier, when the Philistines captured the ark of the covenant and placed it beside Dagon's statue, the next morning the idol lay face down on the floor, and by the second morning its head and hands lay broken off on the threshold (1 Samuel 5:2–4). Now the Philistines imagined their god had avenged that humiliation and triumphed over Israel's God. In truth, Dagon was no stronger than before; the LORD Himself had withdrawn His protection from an unfaithful king to raise up another after His own heart (cf. Daniel 2:21).

**The recurring keynote of "unfaithfulness" throughout Chronicles.** The Hebrew term translated "unfaithfulness" (10:13) denotes far more than a casual mistake; it signifies covenant betrayal and breach of faith—the exact same word just used of the eastern tribes' idolatry and exile (5:25). Side by side, those passages drive home Chronicles' central conviction: losing the land never meant God was helpless, but that kings and people had broken faith with Him.

## Notes & Nuances

**Differences between 1 Chronicles 10 and 1 Samuel 31.** Read side by side, 1 Chronicles 10 follows 1 Samuel 31 closely while adjusting a few details. First Samuel records that the Philistines placed Saul's armor in the temple of Ashtoreth, hung his body on the wall of Beth-shan, and that the men of Jabesh burned the bodies before burying the bones (1 Samuel 31:10–13). First Chronicles 10:10–12 mentions Saul's head fastened in Dagon's temple, omits the cremation—perhaps because later Jewish sensibilities viewed burning bodies with discomfort—and condenses verse 10:6 to "all his house died together." Historically, Saul's surviving son Ish-bosheth (Esh-baal) contested the throne against David for two years (2 Samuel 2:8–10), and the closing verses of chapter 9 just traced Saul's descendants for over ten generations (9:39–44). By "all his house," the Chronicler means Saul's ruling dynasty ended decisively at Gilboa, bypassing the protracted civil war to move directly to David's united kingdom.

**The Amalekite who claimed he killed Saul.** At the opening of 2 Samuel, a young Amalekite runs to David boasting that he finished Saul off at the dying king's request (2 Samuel 1:6–10). Both 1 Samuel 31 and 1 Chronicles 10 state clearly that Saul fell on his own sword. The standard explanation is that the Amalekite wandered across the battlefield, looted Saul's crown and armband from his body, and invented the story hoping for a handsome reward from David—a lie that cost him his life.

**Reading Saul's suicide (10:4) and "the LORD put him to death" (10:14) fairly.** This is a somber passage. Regarding Saul's suicide, Scripture reports starkly that he acted out of dread of enemy torture and humiliation; it never praises or endorses taking one's own life. And when verse 10:14 states that the LORD "put him to death," this familiar biblical idiom does not mean God pushed Saul to suicide, but looks past immediate causes to ultimate judgment: because Saul abandoned Him, God lifted His shielding hand and let the defeat on Gilboa close his reign. Notice too that 1 Samuel says Saul did inquire of the LORD on the night before battle, yet God gave no answer (1 Samuel 28:6). Thus verse 10:14's verdict that he "did not inquire of the LORD" sums up the settled posture of his entire reign—turning to God only when cornered, rather than genuinely seeking Him in obedience.

**The heartbreaking death of Jonathan.** Readers inevitably ache seeing Jonathan—upright, courageous, and fiercely loyal to David—fall on the same day as his unfaithful father (10:2). Scripture does not smooth away that grief. In a broken world, the consequences of a father's or ruler's failure frequently spill over onto innocent people standing closest to them; and Jonathan chose filial loyalty beside his father until his dying breath.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, offers a remarkably different angle on Mount Gilboa than the Chronicler's stern tone. He inserts a glowing tribute to Saul's courage: even knowing beforehand that he and his sons were doomed in that battle, the king refused to flee and stood fighting alongside his troops to the end. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, offers a piercing observation on Saul's final moments: he feared Philistine abuse of his body more than he feared standing before God guilty of self-murder. By contrast, Henry warmly commends the gratitude of Jabesh-gilead, calling their daring burial of Saul a noble example reminding believers never to forget kindness once shown to them. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Chronicles 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's moral reflections on Saul's fear of mockery and Jabesh-gilead's gratitude are familiar from his commentary on 1 Chronicles 10; exact wording not checked -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli scholar of Chronicles, explains that the Chronicler begins his historical narrative with Saul's death not to write Saul's biography, but to build a dark backdrop throwing David's reign into bright relief. She traces the keyword *ma'al*—"unfaithfulness" (10:13)—as the red thread accounting for every national catastrophe in the book, while noting how the author's wording "all his house died together" (10:6) seals off Saul's era completely so no rival dynasty complicates David's accession. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Sara Japhet's analysis of chapter 10 as foil to David, the keyword ma'al, and the phrase "all his house" at 10:6 is a hallmark of her OTL commentary -->

Williamson**, the British Old Testament scholar, highlights that the closing two verses (10:13–14) are the Chronicler's own theological commentary appended to the source taken from 1 Samuel 31.
