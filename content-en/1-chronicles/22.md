# 1 Chronicles 22 — David Prepares for the Temple and Charges Solomon

## Story

The instant fire falls from heaven to consume his offering and the angel sheathes his sword on Ornan's threshing floor at the close of the previous chapter (21:26–27), every trace of hesitation vanishes from David. Standing right on the ground spared from judgment, he declares decisively: "This is the house of the LORD God, and this is the altar of burnt offering for Israel" (22:1).

With the site settled, David throws himself into preparations on an unprecedented scale. First, he orders all the "resident foreigners" in Israel gathered together and appoints stonecutters to quarry and dress blocks for God's house. He stockpiles massive quantities of iron for nails and fittings on the doors, accumulates bronze "beyond weight," and receives cedar logs without number hauled in by Sidonians and Tyrians. In his heart, the aging king reflects: "My son Solomon is young and inexperienced, and the house to be built for the LORD must be exceedingly magnificent, famous and glorious throughout all lands; therefore I will make preparation for it." So David lays in vast supplies before his death (22:2–5).

Next, David summons Solomon to his side and formally charges him to build the temple for the LORD, the God of Israel. Opening his heart, he shares his own lifelong dream: he had deeply desired to build a house for God's name, yet the word of the LORD came halting him: "You have shed much blood and waged great wars. You are not to build a house for My name, because you have shed so much blood on the earth before Me." Still, God did not extinguish that vision; He promised that David's son would be a "man of rest," named Solomon—a name echoing peace—so that in his days God would grant "peace and quiet to Israel," untroubled by enemies on any side. That son would build the house for God's name, with a glorious covenant pledge: "He will be My son, and I will be his Father; and I will establish the throne of his kingdom over Israel forever" (22:6–10).

Having recalled that sacred promise, David blesses and counsels his son tenderly. He prays that the LORD will be with Solomon so he may succeed in building the temple just as God promised, and that God will grant him "discretion and understanding" as ruler over Israel so he may "keep the law" of his God. He points straight to the true root of lasting success: carefully obeying the statutes and ordinances the LORD gave Israel through Moses. Then he pours courage into the young prince: "Be strong and courageous! Do not be afraid or discouraged" (22:11–13).

To steady his young son further, David inventories the vast storehouse he has assembled "with great pains" for the LORD's house: 100,000 talents of gold, 1,000,000 talents of silver, bronze and iron beyond weighing, plus timber and dressed stone already waiting—encouraging Solomon to add even more. Beyond materials, David has lined up legions of skilled laborers—stonecutters, masons, carpenters, and master artisans in every craft. Closing his charge to his son, David urges him forward: "Now arise and begin the work, and may the LORD be with you" (22:14–16).

Knowing so monumental a project cannot rest on Solomon's shoulders alone, David turns to command all the leaders of Israel to rally behind his son. He points to the unmistakable blessing right before their eyes: is not the LORD their God with them, having given them rest on every side by delivering the land's inhabitants into David's hand so the territory stands subdued before the LORD and His people? Standing on that foundation of peace, he calls the leaders to action: "Now set your heart and soul to seek the LORD your God. Arise and build the sanctuary of the LORD God," so they may bring the ark of the covenant and the holy vessels into the house built for His name (22:17–19).

## Key Takeaways

**Not getting the starring role is never a reason to walk away.** David yearned to build God's temple with his own hands, yet God told him no (22:7–8). Rather than sulking or leaving the next generation to fend for itself, he poured his final years into gathering gold, silver, timber, stone, artisans, and national backing for his son (22:2–5; 22:14–19). True greatness of soul shows clearest when we labor gladly to prepare a work someone else will finish and get credited for.

**A magnificent temple means nothing without hearts seeking and obeying God.** David wanted the house to be "exceedingly magnificent, famous and glorious throughout all lands" (22:5) so God's name would be honored among the nations. Yet amid mountains of gold, silver, bronze, and cedar, the centerpiece of his charge to Solomon remains "keeping the law" of God (22:12–13), and his charge to the leaders opens with "set your heart and soul to seek the LORD" before a single stone is laid (22:19). Without reverence for God, the grandest structure is an empty shell.

**God treasures life and peace.** The reason God withheld temple building from David deserves quiet reflection: not because David failed or lacked ability, but because he had "shed much blood" in great wars (22:8). Even wars fought to defend borders leave heavy scars of bloodshed, so the sanctuary symbolizing God's dwelling belonged to a season of quietness and a "man of rest" (22:9).

**Trusting God's presence leads straight to concrete action and teamwork.** Charging both Solomon and Israel's leaders, David binds two truths together: the LORD is with them, and therefore they must get to work—"Arise and build!" (22:16; 22:19). Genuine faith never sits idle waiting for effortless miracles, and God's great works are never a solo stage for one individual.

## Deeper Meaning

**David's handover to Solomon is modeled directly on Moses commissioning Joshua.** Reading David's charge to Solomon—keep Moses' law carefully so you will prosper, "Be strong and courageous! Do not be afraid or discouraged," for the LORD is with you (22:11–13)—readers familiar with the Old Testament immediately hear the unmistakable cadence of Moses commissioning Joshua before crossing the Jordan (Deuteronomy 31:6–8; Joshua 1:6–9). Just as Moses led Israel out of Egypt and prepared everything yet remained outside the Promised Land while handing completion to Joshua, David subdued Israel's foes and amassed supplies yet handed temple construction to Solomon. Placing these two transitions side by side shows that building the temple carries foundational weight equal to entering the Promised Land itself.

**From Moses' promised rest to the "Prince of Peace."** Centuries earlier, Moses had foretold that once God granted Israel rest from all surrounding enemies, He would choose one dwelling place for His name (Deuteronomy 12:10–11). David's reminder to the leaders that God has granted rest on every side (22:18) marks that ancient promise coming true, embodied in Solomon's very name—"man of rest" (22:9). Yet the covenant pledge of verse 22:10—"He will be My son, and I will be his Father; and I will establish the throne of his kingdom over Israel forever"—reaches vastly beyond Solomon's finite reign (17:12–14). The New Testament quotes that exact sonship promise of Jesus (Hebrews 1:5)—whom Isaiah heralds as "Prince of Peace" (Isaiah 9:6), and who spoke of His own body as the true temple (John 2:19–21).

**An aging father's prayer plants the seed for Solomon's famous request.** David did not pray for Solomon to amass wealth or conquer empires; he prayed for the LORD to give his son "discretion and understanding" to rule Israel according to God's law (22:12). That fatherly blessing sank deep into the young king's heart: soon after taking the throne, when God appeared asking what he wished to receive, Solomon asked for "a discerning heart" and "wisdom and knowledge" to lead God's people, humbly calling himself "only a little child and do not know how to carry out my duties"—echoing his father's very concern here (1 Kings 3:7–9; 2 Chronicles 1:10).

**An open welcome to the nations begins right on the construction site.** From the opening verse of preparation, David enlists "resident foreigners" in Israel as stonecutters and receives cedar logs from Sidonians and Tyrians (22:2; 22:4), longing for the temple to win fame and glory "throughout all lands" (22:5). Later, Isaiah widens that vision further, proclaiming that God's house will be called "a house of prayer for all nations" (Isaiah 56:6–7). In the New Testament, Paul unveils the finished design: Gentiles no longer stand outside cutting stones; together with Jews they are built on Christ the cornerstone into "a holy temple in the Lord" (Ephesians 2:19–22).

**One lays the foundation, another builds upon it in God's work.** The relay between David and Solomon—one securing the land, resources, and workforce, the other raising the sanctuary—vividly illustrates Paul's principle to the Corinthians: one plants, another waters, one lays the foundation, another builds on it, each playing an assigned part while God gives the growth (1 Corinthians 3:6–10). No single generation completes all God intends, yet one generation's faithfulness lifts the next higher.

## Notes & Nuances

**The staggering figures of 100,000 talents of gold and 1,000,000 talents of silver (22:14).** Attentive readers do a double take at these weights: at roughly seventy-five pounds (34 kilograms) per ancient talent, 100,000 talents of gold equals roughly 3,400 metric tons and a million talents of silver reaches 34,000 metric tons—vastly exceeding the reserves of major ancient empires and dwarfing David's own personal gift recorded at book's end (29:4). Commentators commonly offer three explanations. First, many modern scholars view these figures as elevated epic hyperbole familiar in ancient Near Eastern royal literature—matching the very next clause saying bronze and iron were "beyond weight" (22:14)—intended to impress on post-exilic readers David's unstinting generosity and the first temple's splendor. Second, some suggest *talent* here followed a much smaller weight standard than the later Babylonian talent. Third, others take the figures literally as cumulative war spoils gathered from neighboring nations over decades of David's reign. However one weighs the numbers, the text's purpose is not an audit sheet, but testifying that David gave his utmost best.

**Why does 1 Chronicles 22:8 bar David from building because of bloodshed, whereas 1 Kings 5:3 gives another reason?** In 1 Kings 5:3, Solomon explains to King Hiram that his father could not build the temple "because of the wars waged against him on all sides" until the LORD put his enemies under his feet—highlighting practical wartime pressures. Here in 1 Chronicles 22:8—and again in chapter 28 (28:3)—the explanation goes deeper spiritually: "you have shed much blood and waged great wars." Rather than clashing, both statements illuminate one reality from two angles. Chronicles never condemns David's defensive wars as sinful—verse 22:18 itself affirms that the LORD delivered the land into his hand to grant peace on all sides. Yet even necessary warfare brings bloodshed and death, standing symbolically and ritually at odds with directly building the sanctuary of life and peace.

**How young was Solomon when David called him "young and inexperienced" (22:5)?** This phrase—repeated at 29:1—does not mean Solomon was still a small child. When he succeeded his father soon afterward, he was likely a young man around twenty years old. In biblical idiom, such language often expresses relative inexperience before an immense calling, just as Solomon himself, already enthroned, humbly told God he was "only a little child" (1 Kings 3:7).

**Enlisting "resident foreigners" as stonecutters (22:2) and ancient forced labor.** Verse 22:2 passes briefly over David's gathering resident foreigners in Israel to cut stone, while 2 Chronicles later reports that Solomon counted more than 150,000 foreigners in the land following his father's registry to serve as laborers and quarrymen in the hills (2 Chronicles 2:17–18). With modern eyes, we should recognize plainly that monumental public projects across the ancient world routinely depended on state conscription of non-citizen populations. Chronicles records that historical practice within its ancient setting rather than endorsing it as a timeless ethic, while simultaneously emphasizing the broad international cooperation of Tyre and Sidon in bringing cedar for God's house (22:4).

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, recounts David gathering gold, silver, bronze, iron, timber, and stone before summoning Solomon and Israel's leaders to charge them with temple construction. He notes explicitly that God barred David from building personally because his hands were stained with blood from many wars, reserving the honor for his peaceful son Solomon—and reports David urging both his son and the elders to unite in obeying Moses' law so God would prosper their work. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus in Book 7 recounts David's material preparations, the bloodshed explanation barring him from building, and his charge to Solomon; exact section numbers not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, admires David's rare humility and generosity throughout this chapter. Where many people denied a cherished honor grow envious or indifferent toward their successor, David did the exact opposite: barred from building, he labored all the harder so Solomon would have every resource ready to succeed. Henry also notes the phrase "with great pains"—or "in my affliction" (22:14)—hearing both a memory of David's stormy years and humble understatement—as though even this vast treasure seemed small beside God's majesty. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Chronicles 22)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's moral observations on David's humility paving Solomon's way and his reading of 22:14 are well remembered; exact text not checked -->

**H. G. M. Williamson**, the British Old Testament scholar, demonstrated in an influential study that the Chronicler deliberately modeled David's commissioning of Solomon in chapter 22 (and chapters 28–29) directly after Moses commissioning Joshua in Deuteronomy and Joshua. The same three classic elements recur in identical order: the call to "be strong and courageous," the assurance that the LORD is with him, and the charge to obey the law carefully in order to succeed. For Williamson, this parallel assures post-exilic Jews that building the temple stood on par in salvation history with conquering the Promised Land, with David and Solomon succeeding each other just like Moses and Joshua. *(1 and 2 Chronicles, New Century Bible Commentary, 1982; "The Accession of Solomon in the Books of Chronicles," Vetus Testamentum, 1976)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Williamson's thesis identifying the Moses–Joshua commissioning pattern in Solomon's accession in 1 Chronicles 22 is a landmark scholarly argument from his 1976 Vetus Testamentum article and 1982 NCBC commentary -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli scholar of Chronicles, observes that chapters 22 through 29 form a large block almost entirely unique to Chronicles, without parallels in Samuel or Kings. Examining the bloodshed bar in 22:8, she points out that the Chronicler never treats it as punishment for personal moral guilt—having omitted Bathsheba and Uriah altogether—but as a ritual and theological contrast between bloodshed in war and a house of peace. In this way, the author honors father and son equally as co-founders of the temple: David secures the site, supplies, and workforce, while Solomon builds in peace. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Sara Japhet's analysis of the distinctiveness of 1 Chronicles 22–29, her non-punitive reading of 22:8, and the co-founder role of David and Solomon are core themes in her OTL volume; exact page not verified -->

**Martin J. Selman**, the British Old Testament scholar, underscores the tight link between divine grace and human responsibility throughout chapter 22. Addressing Israel's leaders in the closing verses (22:18–19), David first reminds them that God has already given them rest on every side before summoning them to "set your heart and soul to seek" Him and rise to build: active service always flows as a grateful response to grace already given, never as a wage earning God's favor. Selman also weighs the enormous gold and silver totals (22:14), noting that whether read via smaller ancient weight units or ceremonial emphasis, their heart is wholehearted giving worthy of God's greatness. *(1 Chronicles, Tyndale Old Testament Commentaries, 1994)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Selman's discussion of grace preceding obedience in 22:18–19 and his treatment of the treasure figures in 22:14 fit his TOTC commentary; exact wording not verified -->
