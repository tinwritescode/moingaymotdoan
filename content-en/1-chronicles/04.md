# 1 Chronicles 4 — The Prayer of Jabez and the Tribe of Simeon

## Story

Chapter 4 brings the expansive genealogy of Judah to a close before turning to Judah's southern neighbor and brother, Simeon. The chapter opens by surveying additional clans of Judah around Bethlehem, Tekoa, and nearby towns—familiar places from chapter 2 now fleshed out with further family lines (4:1–8).

Right in the middle of this steady march of names, the text suddenly halts before a figure who appears out of nowhere, with neither father nor mother named: Jabez. The passage gives just two brief facts about him, yet they are unforgettable. First, "Jabez was more honorable than his brothers," even though at his birth his mother named him Jabez—a name sounding like the Hebrew word for *pain*—saying, "I gave birth to him in pain" (4:9). Second, Jabez brought that heavy name straight to God in a simple prayer: "Oh, that You would bless me and enlarge my territory! May Your hand be with me to keep me from harm, so that I will be free from pain." Without adding a single word of commentary, the narrator closes with one crisp sentence: "And God granted his request" (4:10).

After those two treasured verses, Judah's genealogy resumes its flow. Along the way we glimpse notable faces and trades: Othniel son of Kenaz—Israel's first judge and deliverer—and Joab, "the father of Ge-harashim, for they were craftsmen" (4:11–14). Next come the descendants of Caleb son of Jephunneh, followed by the family of Mered, featuring a marriage just as surprising as those in chapter 2: alongside his Judahite wife, he married "Bithiah the daughter of Pharaoh" (4:15–20). Closing out Judah are the descendants of Shelah—families of fine-linen workers and potters who lived near the king to serve the royal court (4:21–23).

At verse 24, the record turns to the tribe of Simeon. The genealogy begins with Simeon's five sons and follows the line down to Shimei. Although Shimei himself had sixteen sons and six daughters, the author states a sobering fact: "his brothers did not have many children, so their whole clan did not become as numerous as the people of Judah" (4:24–27). This modest tribe lived scattered across thirteen towns and five villages in the far south, from Beersheba to Ziklag—"these were their towns until the reign of David" (4:28–33).

Yet Simeon's story does not end in shrinkage. Under thirteen prosperous clan leaders during the days of King Hezekiah, the tribe stirred to seek new land (4:34–38). They marched to the entrance of Gedor looking for pasture for their flocks, found a broad, peaceful valley inhabited by descendants of Ham and the Meunites, and drove them out to settle on the grazing lands (4:39–41). Not long afterward, five hundred other Simeonites led by four brothers, the sons of Ishi, marched to Mount Seir, struck down the surviving remnant of the Amalekites, and settled there permanently (4:42–43).

## Key Takeaways

**A painful past does not dictate a person's future.** Jabez entered the world carrying a name tied to his mother's agony. In ancient times, a name was often treated as an omen of destiny. Yet Jabez refused to resign himself to living as a victim or passing pain along to others. He turned to the God of Israel, and God transformed the life marked "pain" into a man more honorable than all his brothers.

**Sincere prayer needs no elaborate words.** In a single brief sentence (4:10), Jabez asks for four honest things: God's blessing, expanded territory, God's guiding hand, and protection from harm so he will not know pain. There is no intricate ritual or lofty phrasing—only a heart aware of its weakness leaning fully on God.

**Ordinary trades have an honored place in God's family.** Amid lists of clan chiefs and warriors, chapter 4 carefully names the craftsmen in the Valley of Craftsmen, the families who wove fine linen, and the potters working for the king (4:14; 4:21; 4:23). Serving God is not confined to sitting on a throne or standing at the altar; everyday skilled labor belongs to the larger canvas He is painting.

**Even small, struggling tribes are not forgotten by God.** Simeon had far fewer people than Judah and held land carved out from inside Judah's allotment. Yet when they stepped out to secure a livelihood and confronted their ancient enemy Amalek, God preserved them across the centuries.

## Deeper Meaning

**From "son of sorrow" to God's blessing.** Jabez's mother naming him for her painful labor immediately recalls Rachel, who with her dying breath named her newborn Ben-oni (*son of my sorrow*), only for Jacob to rename him Benjamin, meaning *son of the right hand* (Genesis 35:18). In the same spirit, Jabez asks, "May Your hand be with me" (4:10), refusing to let the sorrow of his beginning define the whole of his life.

**Asking rightly of the God who loves to give.** The outcome—"God granted his request" (4:10)—echoes Jesus' promise to His disciples: "Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened to you" (Matthew 7:7). At the same time, the New Testament helps us distinguish genuine prayer from selfish greed: James reminds believers that some prayers go unanswered "because you ask with wrong motives, that you may squander it on your pleasures" (James 4:2–3). Like Solomon asking for a discerning heart to serve God's people (1 Kings 3:9), Jabez places his request squarely under God's guiding hand.

**An old word of judgment softened by grace.** Before he died, Jacob rebuked Simeon and Levi for their violence and foretold that both tribes would be divided and scattered "in Israel" (Genesis 49:5–7). That prediction came true: upon entering the Promised Land, Simeon received no separate territory of its own, only towns situated inside Judah's borders (Joshua 19:1–2; 19:9). Yet Simeon did not vanish; through these migrations to Gedor and Mount Seir (4:39–43), they found room to live and endured.

**The final battle against the Amalekites.** Five hundred Simeonites wiping out the remnant of the Amalekites at Mount Seir (4:42–43) closes a bitter conflict reaching all the way back to Israel's departure from Egypt, when Amalek ambushed the weary travelers in the wilderness and the LORD declared He would "be at war against Amalek from generation to generation" until their memory was blotted out (Exodus 17:14–16). After the campaigns of Saul and David, it fell to a small band from Simeon to write the final chapter against the remaining Amalekites.

**Heroes and outsiders woven into one story.** Chapter 4 places Othniel—whom the Spirit of the LORD came upon to deliver Israel in the days of the judges (Judges 3:9–10)—right beside Bithiah, the daughter of Pharaoh who married into Judah (4:18). In Hebrew, the name *Bithiah* means *daughter of the LORD*, a lovely sign that even an Egyptian princess could enter the household of Israel's God.

## Notes & Nuances

**The prayer of Jabez is not a magic charm for wealth.** In recent decades, verses 4:9–10 became wildly popular, to the point that some recited them every morning like a formula guaranteeing financial success or promotion. Read closely in its Old Testament setting, the prayer is far simpler and deeper. When Jabez asks God to "enlarge my territory," he prays as an Israelite in the Promised Land hoping to dwell securely on his God-given inheritance without being hemmed in by enemies; asking to be kept from harm "so that I will be free from pain" plays directly on his own name (*pain*). The heart of the prayer lies not in a magic incantation, but in trusting relationship with the God of Israel.

**Facing the violence in Simeon's two territorial raids (4:39–43).** Modern readers naturally feel uneasy at verses 4:40–41: the existing inhabitants of Gedor were living "quiet and peaceful" lives, yet the Simeonites came and "destroyed them completely" to take pasture for their flocks. This deserves honest attention. On one hand, the text is recording actual historical migrations and land conflicts from Hezekiah's era rather than prescribing a timeless moral model, while the Amalekites (4:42–43) were longtime military aggressors who had attacked Israel since the Exodus. On the other hand, from the Chronicler's perspective, Simeon's survival and expansion even while Assyria threatened the land showed that God was still preserving all twelve tribes. We can understand that historical backdrop without justifying violence today.

**The obscure remark "These records are ancient" (4:22).** Verses 4:17–23 contain several broken or difficult Hebrew phrases, so much so that the author inserts a brief aside—rendered "These records are ancient" (4:22). This candor reveals that the Chronicler faithfully compiled fragments from genuine old genealogies and archives in his possession, even where some original context had faded with time.

**What does "to this day" mean (4:41; 4:43)?** Certainly not down to our twenty-first century, and many scholars note it may not even mean down to the Chronicler's own post-exilic day. More likely, the author copied the closing line directly from an older source written during Hezekiah's reign or before Jerusalem fell.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic Jewish repository of biblical interpretation, holds Jabez in special affection (4:9–10). In the tractate *Temurah*, the rabbis identify Jabez with the judge Othniel (4:13) and interpret his prayer spiritually: rather than asking for extra farmland, he asked God to "enlarge his territory" with many disciples coming to study God's law, and asked for God's hand to keep him from ever forgetting what he had learned. *(Babylonian Talmud, tractate Temurah)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the Talmudic tradition in Temurah equating Jabez with Othniel and reading "enlarge my territory" as gaining Torah students is very well known; exact folio 16a not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, writes warmly on the two verses about Jabez. He observes that Jabez's true honor stemmed not from money or power, but from reverence for God and a life of prayer: though his life began in his mother's sorrow, it unfolded in God's blessing. For Henry, the finest part of verse 4:10 is Jabez asking to be kept from "harm" or evil—above all sin, since sin alone brings the deepest grief to the human heart. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Chronicles 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's commentary on Jabez in 1 Chronicles 4:9–10 is among his most famous and frequently quoted pages on Chronicles -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the nineteenth-century English preacher, in his sermon on the prayer of Jabez, urges believers not to settle for a dull, passive faith, but to ask boldly for God's full blessing. At the same time, he stresses that biblical "blessing" always comes joined to "Your hand with me"—God's power to live uprightly—and a plea to be kept from evil so as never to grieve God or hurt others. *(Sermon on 1 Chronicles chapter 4 verse 10; exact sermon number on spurgeongems.org to be verified)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon preached a well-known sermon on the prayer of Jabez linking true blessing with God's hand and protection from evil; exact sermon number not recalled -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli scholar of Chronicles, asks why the author devotes the entire second half of chapter 4 to Simeon, a small tribe long since absorbed into Judah. She demonstrates that recording Simeon's territorial expansion under Hezekiah reflects a deliberate theological conviction: not one of Israel's twelve tribes is cast aside, and even the smallest displays remarkable vitality when blessed by God. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: explaining Simeon's genealogy within the Chronicler's vision of all twelve tribes is typical of Japhet's commentary; exact page not checked -->

**Martin J. Selman**, the British Old Testament scholar, compares the brief story of Jabez to a window cut through a dense wall of genealogies, letting readers see straight into the book's central theme: whoever earnestly seeks the God of Israel is heard and blessed by Him.
