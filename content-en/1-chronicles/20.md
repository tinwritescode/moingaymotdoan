# 1 Chronicles 20 — The Fall of Rabbah and the Defeat of the Philistine Giants

## Story

After routing the Aramean coalition in the previous chapter, the war against Ammon remained unfinished because the Ammonites had retreated behind their city walls (19:15; 19:19). The following spring—"at the time when kings march out to war"—Joab leads out the army, lays waste the land of Ammon, and besieges its capital, Rabbah. This time David stays behind in Jerusalem. Joab assaults Rabbah and overthrows it (20:1).

Once the city falls, David takes the crown off the head of the Ammonite king. Weighing a full talent of gold—more than seventy-five pounds—and set with a precious stone, the crown is placed on David's own head. He also brings an immense amount of plunder out of Rabbah (20:2).

For the city's population, the narrative records a stark scene: David brings out the inhabitants and—in a difficult Hebrew phrase rendered either as cutting them with or consigning them to forced labor with—"saws, iron picks, and axes," doing the same to all the Ammonite towns. With the campaign finished, David and the entire army return to Jerusalem (20:3).

From east of the Jordan, the story pivots swiftly west to three final clashes with the Philistines—where surviving warriors "descended from Rapha," the race of giants, still lingered. First, at Gezer, a warrior named Sibbecai the Hushathite strikes down Sippai, one of the descendants of the giants, subduing the Philistines. In another battle, Elhanan son of Jair kills Lahmi, the brother of Goliath the Gittite, whose spear shaft was "like a weaver's beam" (20:4–5).

The final clash erupts right at Gath, Goliath's hometown. Stepping out for the Philistines is a massive champion with a bizarre trait: six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot—twenty-four in all. Just like Goliath years before, he stands forth and "taunted Israel." This time, however, David does not even need to step onto the field: Jonathan, son of David's brother Shimea, steps up and kills him. The author wraps up David's entire military era in one crisp sentence: all these giants born at Gath "fell by the hand of David and his servants" (20:6–8).

## Key Takeaways

**Every lingering threat around Israel's borders has now been silenced.** Brief as its eight verses are, chapter 20 brings three consecutive war chapters (chapters 18–20) to a decisive close. From Ammon's fortified capital Rabbah in the east to the last Philistine giants in the west, Israel's most stubborn adversaries are subdued, clearing the way for peaceful preparations for the Jerusalem temple.

**Courage passed on by a pioneer raises up an entire new generation.** Years earlier, Saul's whole army froze in terror before a single Goliath, leaving only young shepherd David willing to step forward. Now in chapter 20, David no longer fights giants alone: his soldiers and his own nephew—Sibbecai, Elhanan, and Jonathan—walk in his footsteps to topple every remaining giant (20:4–8). A leader's finest achievement is not winning solo battles, but raising others ready to stand in the gap.

**Arrogance and brute strength never prevail over God's people.** The twenty-four-fingered giant at Gath relied on his freakish stature to "taunt Israel" (20:7), repeating Goliath's ancient boast. His downfall drives home an enduring truth: before God's power, towering height and massive spears amount to nothing.

**Earthly military victory always carries a heavy shadow.** This short chapter does not gloss over the harshness of ancient warfare: a ruined capital and conquered towns subjected to brutal slaughter or harsh forced labor (20:1; 20:3). Those campaigns secured the nation's borders, yet they also foreshadow why God would soon tell David he may not build the temple—his hands had shed too much blood (22:8).

## Deeper Meaning

**The shadow of the giants from Moses' day finally lifts forever.** Centuries earlier, ten of the twelve spies sent into Canaan panicked at the sight of the Anakites, "descendants of the Nephilim," feeling like grasshoppers and stalling an entire generation in the desert (Numbers 13:32–33). Later, Joshua drove them out of the hills of Judah and Israel, yet Scripture left one ominous footnote: a remnant still survived in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod (Joshua 11:21–22). The victories at Gezer and Gath in chapter 20 finish that unfinished chapter from Joshua's era: the terror of the giants in the Promised Land ends completely under David (20:8).

**Direct echoes of David's duel with Goliath.** Every image in the closing four verses rings straight back to the Valley of Elah: Lahmi is Goliath's own brother carrying a spear shaft "like a weaver's beam" identical to his brother's weapon (1 Samuel 17:7), and Gath's giant "taunted Israel" in the exact language of Goliath's challenge (1 Samuel 17:10; 17:45). Back then David declared before both armies that "it is not by sword or spear that the LORD saves, for the battle is the LORD's" (1 Samuel 17:47). By chapter 20, the solitary faith David once voiced alone has become the shared courage of his followers.

**Rabbah—which once housed a giant's relic—also falls.** Deuteronomy preserved a striking footnote: the giant iron bedstead, nine cubits long, of King Og of Bashan—the last survivor of the Rephaim giants east of the Jordan—stood on display right inside Rabbah of the Ammonites (Deuteronomy 3:11). Pairing both halves of chapter 20—Rabbah's collapse in the east and the extermination of Gath's giants in the west—shows every terrifying emblem of ancient power bowing before God's kingdom.

**David's gold crown echoes the royal psalms.** Setting a jeweled gold crown upon David's head (20:2) marks the zenith of his royal prestige among surrounding nations. Psalm 21 celebrates God's favor toward His anointed king with closely matching imagery: "You placed a crown of pure gold on his head" (Psalms 21:3). Yet within the Bible's wider sweep, a crown won through bloodshed remains only a temporary shadow pointing ahead to the Messiah—the King who wins lasting peace not by conquest, but by laying down His life.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 1 Chronicles 20 completely omit David's sin with Bathsheba?** This is the single most famous silence when comparing Chronicles with Samuel. Verse 20:1 opens almost verbatim with 2 Samuel 11:1 ("but David remained in Jerusalem"), yet right between those two clauses 2 Samuel inserts two agonizing chapters detailing David's adultery with Bathsheba, the orchestrated death of her husband Uriah, Nathan's confrontation, and the death of their infant son (2 Samuel 11:1; 12:26–31). First Chronicles leaps directly from the siege to Rabbah's fall without a word. This is not dishonest cover-up: Jewish readers already knew Samuel by heart, and in the very next chapter (chapter 21) the Chronicler candidly exposes David's grave sin in taking the census. Rather, the author selects public episodes directly tied to the kingdom and temple, passing over David's private household scandals.

**Verse 20:3 is a famously difficult line: did David torture and execute the Ammonites or put them to forced labor?** Older translations render 20:3 starkly as "he sawed them with saws, iron picks, and axes," whereas the parallel verse in 2 Samuel 12:31 reads "he put them to work with saws, iron picks, and iron axes, and made them work at the brick kilns." That dramatic contrast rests on a single tiny stroke in Hebrew script: the verb in 2 Samuel (*yasem*) means *he set them to work* (assigning war prisoners to heavy timber-cutting, quarrying, and brickmaking), while the verb in 1 Chronicles (*yashar*) differs by one letter and means *he sawed*. Scholars propose two main options. Many modern commentators view 2 Samuel as preserving the original sense of forced labor, with Chronicles reflecting an early scribal letter-slip. Others take 20:3 literally as harsh wartime execution—a grim reality common in ancient Near Eastern warfare, where Ammon itself was later indicted by Amos for atrocious cruelty to civilians (Amos 1:13–14). Either way, Scripture reports what happened without endorsing it as a model; and 1 Chronicles itself soon confirms that shedding much blood in war disqualified David from building the temple (22:8).

**Who killed Goliath: David or Elhanan?** Verse 20:5 states that Elhanan "killed Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite," whereas the parallel text in 2 Samuel 21:19 reads that Elhanan "the Bethlehemite" "killed Goliath the Gittite," causing some readers to think Scripture contradicts David's famous victory in 1 Samuel 17. Placed side by side in Hebrew script, most scholars spot an unmistakable scribal clue: the Hebrew phrases for *the Bethlehemite* and *the brother of Lahmi* look strikingly alike in handwriting. Many conclude that 2 Samuel 21:19 suffered an early copyist error dropping "brother of" and turning *Lahmi* into *Bethlehemite*, while 1 Chronicles 20:5 preserves or clarifies the historical reality: David killed Goliath, and Elhanan later killed Goliath's brother Lahmi. Conversely, some text-critical scholars think the Chronicler deliberately corrected 2 Samuel 21:19 to harmonize it with 1 Samuel 17.

**A seventy-five-pound crown and omitting the fourth giant.** A talent of gold weighs roughly seventy-five pounds (34 kilograms)—far too heavy to wear comfortably for long. Because the Hebrew consonants for *their king* and for the Ammonite god *Milcom* are identical, many commentators suggest the crown originally adorned Milcom's statue, and David either held it briefly over his head in a victory ceremony or set its prized gem into his own crown (20:2). Notice also that 2 Samuel 21:15–22 records four Philistine giants, whereas 1 Chronicles 20:4–8 lists only three, dropping the first—Ishbi-benob—where an exhausted, aging David nearly died and had to be rescued by Abishai (2 Samuel 21:15–17). Omitting that moment of frailty fits the Chronicler's consistent portrait of a strong, triumphant king across chapters 18–20.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, recounts both the capture of Rabbah and the duels with Philistine giants. He describes the talent-weight gold crown set with a dazzling sardonyx stone at its center placed on David's head, and reads verse 20:3 strictly as a harsh punishment inflicted on Rabbah's population. Later he narrates the defeat of the twenty-four-digit giant by David's nephew Jonathan, noting that afterward the Philistines never dared rise up against Israel again. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus in Book 7 recounts the jeweled talent crown, a harsh reading of Rabbah's punishment, and the 24-digit giant; exact chapter and section numbers not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, pauses over 1 Chronicles 20:1's silence regarding David's sin with Bathsheba. Because that sin already stood recorded plainly in Samuel as a solemn warning for all generations, Chronicles had no need to rehearse a forgiven stain—a gentle reminder of God's mercy toward the repentant. Turning to the Philistine giants (20:4–8), Henry observes that even after Goliath falls, fresh giants keep rising to test God's people, yet God continually raises up new faithful servants to bring down every foe in turn. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Chronicles 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's two moral insights on Chronicles bypassing David's forgiven sin and on new giants rising in chapter 20 are well recalled; exact text not checked -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli scholar of Chronicles, analyzes the Chronicler's two major omissions compared with 2 Samuel: passing over the entire Bathsheba–Uriah tragedy between the two halves of verse 20:1, and omitting the encounter with Ishbi-benob where weary David nearly perished (2 Samuel 21:15–17). These deliberate editorial choices present David's military era at its cleanest and strongest, centered firmly on pacifying the land. She also examines verse 20:5 alongside 2 Samuel 21:19, tracing how the name *Lahmi, brother of Goliath* relates to the tradition of David's victory over Goliath. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Sara Japhet's editorial analysis of the two major omissions and comparison of 20:5 with 2 Samuel 21:19 in her OTL volume are well remembered; exact page not checked -->

**H. G. M. Williamson**, the British Old Testament scholar, examines closely the single-stroke difference between the verb *sawed* in 1 Chronicles 20:3 and *set to work* in 2 Samuel 12:31. He favors 2 Samuel's reading—assigning captives to forced labor with saws, picks, and axes—as closer to the original, suggesting Chronicles' reading emerged from an early scribal variant rather than an intent to heighten brutality. He also notes that attaching the three giant battles right after Rabbah's capture cleanly wraps up David's war block (chapters 18–20) before the great turning point of chapter 21. *(1 and 2 Chronicles, New Century Bible Commentary, 1982)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Williamson's textual note on 20:3 and structural observation on chapters 18–20 in NCBC are well recalled; exact page not verified -->
