# 1 Chronicles 6 — The Tribe of Levi, the Temple Singers, and the Levitical Towns

## Story

After closing chapter 5 with the eastern tribes carried away to Assyria, the genealogies arrive at the centerpiece and longest section of the book's first nine chapters: the tribe of Levi. Chapter 6 opens with Levi's three sons—Gershon, Kohath, and Merari—and moves right into the primary line of Kohath through Amram, the father of Aaron, Moses, and Miriam (6:1–3). From Aaron, the list traces his son Eleazar and grandson Phinehas down a long line of high priests across the centuries: from the era of the judges to Zadok, to Azariah—"who served as priest in the temple Solomon built in Jerusalem"—then to Hilkiah and Seraiah (6:4–14). That chain halts at a painful milestone: Jehozadak, who "went into captivity when the LORD sent Judah and Jerusalem into exile by the hand of Nebuchadnezzar" (6:15).

Immediately afterward, the text steps back to survey all three great Levitical branches—Gershon, Kohath, and Merari—by their clans, tracing several generations within each line (6:16–21; 6:29–30). Inside Kohath's branch, readers suddenly meet two names tied to major Old Testament narratives: Korah, who led a famous rebellion in the wilderness, and his descendant Elkanah, the father of the prophet Samuel, followed by Samuel's two sons, Vashni and Abijah (6:22–28).

At verse 31, the genealogical ledger bursts open into a vivid scene of worship from King David's reign: "These are the men David put in charge of the music in the house of the LORD after the ark rested there." Those choirs ministered steadily with song before the tabernacle until Solomon built the house of the LORD in Jerusalem, each performing his assigned service in proper order (6:31–32).

The author introduces three chief musicians representing all three Levitical branches, tracing each man's pedigree cleanly back to Levi himself. Standing in the center from the line of Kohath was Heman "the singer," grandson of the prophet Samuel and a descendant of Korah (6:33–38). Standing at his right hand from the line of Gershon was Asaph (6:39–43). Standing at his left from the line of Merari was Ethan (6:44–47).

Following the musicians comes a crisp summary of the division of duties in the sanctuary. Their fellow Levites were appointed to all the remaining service of the tabernacle of the house of God (6:48). "Aaron and his descendants," however, were the ones who made offerings on the altar of burnt offering and the altar of incense, handled all the work of the Most Holy Place, and "made atonement for Israel, according to all that Moses the servant of God had commanded" (6:49). To underscore the legitimacy of this priestly line, the author briefly recaps Aaron's succession from Eleazar down to Ahimaaz, son of Zadok in David's day (6:50–53).

The second half of the chapter shifts from family trees to a map of where the Levites lived. Having no single tribal region of their own, they were granted towns with surrounding pasturelands allotted by lot from the twelve tribes (6:64–65). First, Aaron's descendants among the Kohathites received thirteen towns in Judah, Simeon, and Benjamin, headed by the city of refuge Hebron—though its surrounding fields and villages remained with Caleb—along with towns like Libnah, Beth-shemesh, Geba, and Anathoth (6:54–60).

The remaining Levitical clans were distributed evenly across the nation, from the central highlands to the north and east across the Jordan. The rest of the Kohathites received ten towns in Ephraim and the half-tribe of Manasseh, highlighted by the city of refuge Shechem (6:61; 6:66–70). The Gershonites received thirteen towns in Issachar, Asher, Naphtali, and Manasseh in Bashan, including Golan and Kedesh (6:62; 6:71–76). Finally, the Merarites received twelve towns in Zebulun, Reuben, and Gad, including Bezer and Ramoth in Gilead (6:63; 6:77–81).

## Key Takeaways

**Worship stands at the heart of God's community.** In the nine genealogical chapters opening 1 Chronicles, Levi sits dead center and takes up the most space. For Jews returning from exile without David's throne, what held them together as God's people was not political might, but true worship offered according to God's instructions.

**God's grace is greater than the stains of the past.** Kohath's line carried the shadow of Korah, who rebelled against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness. Yet God did not slam the door on his descendants' future; out of Korah's very line came the prophet Samuel and the chief musician Heman, standing right at the center of the nation's spiritual life (6:33–38). The grave sins of ancestors never permanently seal the destiny of descendants who turn back to serve the LORD.

**Praising God is an ordered ministry, not an afterthought.** The moment the ark of the covenant came to rest, King David's very first organizational step was establishing choirs representing all three Levitical families (6:31–47). Music in the temple was neither entertainment nor a passing emotional spark, but a serious ministry carried out day after day in good order before God.

**Service to God involves clear roles, and every role matters.** Only Aaron's line could approach the altar and enter the Most Holy Place to make atonement for the people (6:49). Yet their fellow Levites handling all the tabernacle's practical work (6:48) and the musicians praising day and night were equally vital to wholehearted worship. Knowing the boundaries of our assigned calling guards against pride while helping us stay faithful in our place.

**Having no separate territory allowed the Levites to be present everywhere.** Rather than living clustered behind one tribal border, the Levites dwelt scattered in towns woven throughout all twelve tribes (6:54–81). That dispersion kept worship and instruction in God's law close at hand for everyone, from southern Judah up to Galilee and across the Jordan.

## Deeper Meaning

**An ancient sentence turned by grace into a channel of blessing.** Before he died, Jacob rebuked the violence of Simeon and Levi and declared that they would be divided and scattered "in Israel" (Genesis 49:5–7). That scattering did come to pass, yet for Levi it became an honor. After the Levites rallied to the LORD's side during the golden calf crisis (Exodus 32:26–29), they were set apart to serve Him; the LORD Himself became their inheritance (Numbers 18:20), and living dispersed across forty-eight towns throughout the land (Numbers 35:1–8; Joshua 21:41–42) turned an ancient penalty into a mission of spreading God's word across the whole nation.

**The priestly covenant held firm even when the temple fell.** The chain in 6:1–15 starts with Eleazar and Phinehas—who received "a covenant of a perpetual priesthood" for his zeal for God (Numbers 25:10–13)—moves through Zadok in Solomon's reign (1 Kings 2:35), through Hilkiah who found the Book of the Law in Josiah's day (2 Kings 22:8), down to Seraiah who was executed by Babylon at Riblah (2 Kings 25:18–21) and his exiled son Jehozadak (6:15). At first glance the line seems snapped off by national disaster. Yet open Ezra and Haggai, and right away we meet Jehozadak's son, the high priest Jeshua (Joshua), returning from Babylon alongside Zerubbabel to rebuild the altar and the temple (Ezra 3:2; Haggai 1:1), while Ezra the scribe himself traces his lineage directly back to Seraiah and Aaron (Ezra 7:1–5).

**"The descendants of Korah, however, did not die."** When Korah rebelled against Moses and Aaron in the desert (Numbers 16:1–3), judgment fell on him and his followers. Yet Numbers preserves one short, grace-filled sentence: "The descendants of Korah, however, did not die" (Numbers 26:11). In 1 Chronicles 6 we see that verse burst into bloom: Korah's line not only survived, it produced Heman, standing beside Asaph and Ethan at the helm of temple music (15:17–19; 25:1–6). From those choir traditions, the book of Psalms still preserves songs attributed to the sons of Korah, Asaph, Heman, and Ethan—spanning longing for God, cries in the dark, and hymns celebrating His steadfast faithfulness (Psalms 42:1; 73:1; 88:1; 89:1).

**Justice and mercy bound to the homes of God's servants.** Among the towns granted to the Levites were six "cities of refuge"—such as Hebron and Shechem named explicitly in 6:57 and 6:67, along with Golan, Kedesh, Bezer, and Ramoth—where someone who killed another unintentionally could flee for protection from hasty vengeance until receiving a fair trial (Numbers 35:6). Placing shelters for the vulnerable under Levitical care shows that true worship always walks hand in hand with justice and compassion in public life.

**From a long line of mortal priests to one High Priest living forever.** Looking down the succession of priests in 6:1–15, New Testament readers recognize what the Letter to the Hebrews points out: under the old covenant, "there have been many of those priests, since death prevented them from continuing in office," and they had to offer sacrifices day after day (Hebrews 7:23; 7:27). Aaron's entire line making atonement (6:49) was a shadow pointing ahead to Jesus, who holds "a permanent priesthood," offered Himself "once for all," and lives forever to intercede completely for all who draw near to God (Hebrews 7:24–27).

## Notes & Nuances

**Different chapter and verse numbering between the Hebrew Bible and English Bibles.** In the Hebrew Bible, the first fifteen verses of this chapter (6:1–15) are placed at the end of chapter 5, and chapter 6 begins at what English translations number as verse 6:16. The content is identical, yet knowing this avoids confusion when consulting scholarly commentaries that follow the Hebrew verse numbering.

**Why is the prophet Samuel listed as a Levite here, whereas 1 Samuel associates him with Ephraim?** Chapter 6 states plainly that Samuel descended from Kohath in the tribe of Levi (6:22–28; 6:33–38). In 1 Samuel, however, his father Elkanah is introduced from the hill country of Ephraim and called "an Ephraimite" (1 Samuel 1:1). The traditional explanation is that because Levites dwelt scattered within other tribes' territories (6:66–67), a Levitical family settled for generations in the hills of Ephraim could be designated regionally as Ephraimite. Alongside this, many modern scholars propose that later tradition linked Samuel into the Kohathite line to account for his priestly functions and his standing as grandfather of the singer Heman.

**Minor differences in personal names and town lists compared with other books.** Careful readers will notice a few small variations. Samuel's firstborn is written *Vashni* (6:28), whereas 6:33 and 1 Samuel 8:2 name him *Joel*—most commentators observe that the Hebrew word behind *Vashni* means *and the second*, suggesting that the name *Joel* just before it dropped out during ancient copying. Likewise, the high-priestly list in 6:1–15 telescopes several generations compared with other historical books, and the Levitical town list (6:54–81) omits a few names out of the full forty-eight recorded in Joshua 21—for example, not explicitly naming the tribe of Dan in the later section, even though the Danite towns Aijalon and Gath-rimmon still appear (6:69). Such abbreviations and scribal slips are common in ancient archives and leave the chapter's message untouched.

**Why is only Eleazar's line traced as the high-priestly succession?** Verse 6:3 lists all four of Aaron's sons: Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar. Nadab and Abihu died early for offering unauthorized fire before the LORD (Leviticus 10:1–2). Meanwhile, Ithamar's line—which included the priests Eli and Abiathar—does not appear in 6:4–15 because during Solomon's reign Abiathar was removed from office and the high priesthood passed permanently to Zadok's branch of Eleazar's line (1 Kings 2:27; 2:35), the very line that ministered in the second temple after the exile.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, draws a simple yet profound lesson from the appointment of the singers (6:31–47): the instant the ark found rest, David's first arrangement was the praise of God, reminding us that when our lives and communities receive peace, gratitude and worship should come first. **Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, in his surveys of the Jewish high priesthood, likewise traces an unbroken chain of succession from Aaron through Zadok down to the destruction of the first temple by Babylon. For him, the continuity of this priestly office formed one of the foundational pillars preserving Jewish law and identity through centuries of political upheaval. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Books 10 and 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus presents lists of high priests in Books 10 and 20 of Antiquities of the Jews; exact name-by-name comparison with 1 Chronicles 6 not checked -->

**Sara Japhet**, the Israeli scholar of Chronicles, stresses that Levi's genealogy is not placed dead center of the opening nine chapters—and given greater length than any other—by accident. She shows that chapter 6 pivots around two pillars, Aaron's priesthood and David's three musical guilds, while linking Samuel to the Kohathites as grandfather of Heman reflects the author's theological vision: ordered temple worship is the thread binding all of Israel's history together. *(I & II Chronicles, Old Testament Library, 1993)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the central place of Levi's genealogy and the link between Samuel and the temple music system are familiar themes in Sara Japhet's OTL commentary; exact page not verified -->

Williamson**, the British Old Testament scholar, compares the Levitical town list (6:54–81) closely with Joshua 21 and uncovers the Chronicler's purpose in closing the chapter with this geographical map. Beyond preserving an old record of Levitical settlements, mapping towns across all twelve tribes on both sides of the Jordan serves the ideal of *all Israel*: despite kingdom division and exile, in the author's eyes every tribe still belongs to one united people centered around the worship of the LORD. *(1 and 2 Chronicles, New Century Bible Commentary, 1982)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: explaining the Levitical cities parallel to Joshua 21 in light of the "all Israel" motif is characteristic of Williamson's NCBC commentary; exact page not checked -->

Selman**, the British Old Testament scholar, points to the carefully worded phrasing of verse 6:15: the author does not merely say Babylon's king took the people captive, but states that "the LORD sent Judah and Jerusalem into exile by the hand of Nebuchadnezzar."
