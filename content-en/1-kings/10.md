# 1 Kings 10 — The Queen of Sheba and Solomon's Golden Peak

## Story

Reports of Solomon's wisdom—closely linked with the name of the LORD—traveled far south until they reached the Queen of Sheba. She resolved to make the long journey to Jerusalem herself to test him with hard questions. She arrived with a huge retinue, camels laden with precious spices, vast quantities of gold, and precious stones. Coming before Solomon, the queen poured out everything that was on her mind, and the king answered every single question clearly; nothing was too hidden or difficult for him to explain (10:1–3).

Yet what truly stunned the Queen of Sheba was not merely Solomon's sharp answers. When she saw firsthand the full scope of his wisdom, the palace he had built, the food set on his table, the seating of his officials, the disciplined service and attire of his attendants, his cupbearers, and the burnt offerings he presented at the temple of the LORD, it took her breath away. She confessed that every report she had heard back home was true, yet she had not believed it until she came and saw with her own eyes—and even now, "not even half was told me!" His wisdom and prosperity far surpassed every rumor. She exclaimed, "How blessed are your men! How blessed are these servants of yours!" who stood continually before him listening to his wisdom, and then lifted her praise straight to God: blessed be the LORD your God, who delighted in Solomon and, because of His eternal love for Israel, made him king "to maintain justice and righteousness" (10:4–9).

Before departing, the queen presented Solomon with one hundred and twenty talents of gold, precious stones, and an immense abundance of spices—never again did such a quantity of spices arrive in Jerusalem. Woven into this exchange of gifts is a note about Hiram's fleet hauling gold from Ophir: the ships also brought great cargoes of rare almug wood and precious stones. Solomon used that prized timber to craft supports and balustrades for the temple of the LORD and the royal palace, as well as harps and lyres for the singers—wood the likes of which never came to Jerusalem again. In return, King Solomon gave the Queen of Sheba everything she desired and asked for, over and above the generous royal bounty he bestowed on his own initiative. Her visit concluded, she and her retinue set out on the journey back to her own land (10:10–13).

From this visit of a distant monarch, the text broadens into an inventory of Solomon's immense wealth. The gold flowing into his treasury each year weighed six hundred and sixty-six talents—not counting revenue from traveling merchants, trade tariffs, and tribute paid by all the kings of Arabia and the governors of the land. Gold abounded so richly that the king fashioned two hundred large shields of hammered gold, six hundred shekels to each shield, and three hundred smaller shields of three minas each, displaying them in the Palace of the Forest of Lebanon. He also crafted a grand throne of ivory overlaid with pure gold: six steps led up to a rounded back, armrests flanked by two standing lions, and twelve more lions lining both sides of the six steps—nothing like it had ever been made for any other kingdom (10:14–20).

Gold saturated royal daily life until every drinking cup of King Solomon and every vessel in the Palace of the Forest of Lebanon was pure gold; in Solomon's days, "silver was considered of little value." Once every three years the king's fleet of Tarshish ships sailed out alongside Hiram's fleet and returned packed with gold, silver, ivory, apes, and peacocks. Thus King Solomon surpassed every monarch on earth in both riches and wisdom. The whole world sought an audience with him to hear the wisdom God had put in his heart, streaming in year after year with gifts of silver and gold articles, robes, weapons, spices, horses, and mules (10:21–25).

The chapter closes with imperial-scale military buildup and horse trading. Solomon amassed fourteen hundred chariots and twelve thousand horsemen, stationing them across chariot cities and right beside him in Jerusalem. In the capital he made "silver as common as stones" and cedar as plentiful as sycamore-fig trees in the foothills. Notably, Solomon imported horses from Egypt through royal traders who purchased them in droves at fixed prices: a chariot imported from Egypt cost six hundred shekels of silver, and a horse cost one hundred and fifty. Not only did Solomon equip his own army, his royal merchants also exported chariots and horses at those rates to all the kings of the Hittites and the kings of Aram to the north (10:26–29).

## Key Takeaways

**True God-given wisdom turns onlookers' eyes toward God Himself.** What stands out most in the Queen of Sheba's visit is not simply that she admired Solomon's brilliance, but that she recognized the hand of the LORD behind the wisdom and order of his court (10:1; 10:9). When God's gifts are stewarded rightly, outsiders do not stop at praising human talent; they break out in praise of the Giver.

**The true purpose of leadership is maintaining justice and righteousness for the people.** The foreign queen's insight in 10:9 is remarkably crisp: God set Solomon on the throne not so he could lounge on an ivory throne sipping from golden goblets, but because God loves Israel forever and wanted them governed with "justice and righteousness." Power is legitimate only when it serves those beneath it.

**Gold and silver never satisfy the restless reach of the human heart.** The second half of chapter 10 glitters relentlessly with gold—tribute gold, gold shields, gold-plated throne, gold cups, gold vessels—until silver seems as common as street stones (10:14–27). Yet look closely at the closing verses: Solomon does not rest content with what God generously gave; he piles up war chariots, maintains twelve thousand horsemen, and sends agents straight back to Egypt to deal in war horses for neighboring empires (10:26–29). The summit of wealth sits right beside the edge of greed.

**Hearing secondhand about God can never replace coming to see for yourself.** The Queen of Sheba heard glowing reports back home yet stayed skeptical until she made the costly journey, asked her hard questions face to face, and beheld the reality with her own eyes (10:6–7). Living faith works the same way: another person's testimony may spark curiosity, yet only personal encounter truly convinces the heart.

## Deeper Meaning

**The Queen of Sheba's visit answers both Solomon's prayer in chapter 8 and God's promise in chapter 3.** At Gibeon, God promised Solomon wisdom, riches, and honor unmatched by any king (3:12–13), drawing envoys from all nations to hear him (4:34). At the temple dedication, Solomon prayed that God would hear foreigners who came from distant lands because of His great name (8:41–43). The arrival of the Queen of Sheba—a foreign ruler drawn by the LORD's name who leaves blessing His name—shows both promises coming alive right on stage.

**Gold from Sheba and ships of Tarshish become enduring pictures of the Messiah's kingdom.** Later Scripture borrows chapter 10's radiant imagery to portray the ideal coming King: the kings of Tarshish and Sheba will bring gold and tribute before Him (Psalms 72:10; 72:15), and caravans of camels from Sheba will come bearing gold and frankincense, proclaiming the praise of the LORD (Isaiah 60:6). What Solomon's reign reflected only for a fleeting golden hour will stand forever in the kingdom of the Messiah.

**Jesus proclaims Himself "One greater than Solomon."** In the Gospels, confronting a generation demanding signs yet refusing to listen, Jesus reaches directly back to chapter 10: on judgment day the Queen of the South will rise and condemn this generation, because she traveled from the ends of the earth to hear Solomon's wisdom—"and now One greater than Solomon is here!" (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31). The New Testament affirms that Christ Himself has become our wisdom from God (1 Corinthians 1:30). On another occasion, Jesus also cited Solomon's legendary splendor to free His disciples from anxious striving: "Not even Solomon in all his glory was adorned like one of these" wildflowers (Matthew 6:28–29).

**Royal horse-traders heading back to Egypt collide head-on with the Law of Moses.** Few parallels in Scripture are sharper than the closing four verses of chapter 10 (10:26–29) set beside Deuteronomy's law for Israel's king: he must not acquire great numbers of horses, nor send the people back to Egypt to multiply horses—for the LORD had declared, "You are never to go back that way again"—and he must not accumulate large amounts of silver and gold (Deuteronomy 17:16–17). Chapter 10 reports Solomon's wealth and horse trade in a completely neutral, deadpan voice without a single word of censure, leaving readers to see for themselves that two of the three royal boundaries (gold and Egyptian horses) have already been breached—while the third (many wives) is poised to explode in the opening verse of chapter 11.

## Notes & Nuances

**Where was Sheba, and who was the Queen of Sheba?** Most historians and archaeologists today identify Sheba with the ancient kingdom of Saba in southwestern Arabia (modern Yemen), famed for its overland trade in frankincense, rare spices, and gold linking the Indian Ocean to the Mediterranean. Meanwhile, the first-century historian Josephus and ancient Ethiopian Christian tradition link her with East Africa (modern Ethiopia), where the national epic *Kebra Nagast* tells of her bearing a son by Solomon who founded the Ethiopian royal line. Both shores of the Red Sea enjoyed tight commercial and cultural ties in antiquity, though the biblical text itself records no romance or child between them—the encounter in chapter 10 is purely one of diplomacy, wisdom, and faith.

**The annual tribute of 666 talents of gold in 10:14.** In the standard Hebrew text, both parallel accounts specify 666 talents of gold arriving each year (10:14; 2 Chronicles 9:13), totaling well over twenty tons of gold annually (a few older versions show slight copying or translation variations in the numeral). Some New Testament scholars observe that this exact figure reappears as the number of the beast—"six hundred and sixty-six"—in Revelation (Revelation 13:18), suggesting John may have echoed the figure tied to Solomon's tempting apex of gold hoarding to symbolize imperial economic power and hubris. That remains a thought-provoking possibility rather than the only explanation.

**What does "a fleet of ships of Tarshish" in 10:22 mean?** Tarshish was a mineral-rich port in the far western Mediterranean (often placed in southern Spain), whereas Solomon's fleet sailed from the Red Sea (9:26) into the Indian Ocean, returning with ivory, apes, and peacocks—tropical goods from East Africa or India. Most scholars therefore understand "ships of Tarshish" not as ships bound for the city of Tarshish, but as a class of large, sturdy ocean-going vessels engineered for multi-year voyages (much like calling a vessel an "ocean liner" today).

**What kind of timber was "almug wood" (10:11–12)?** The Hebrew word *almuggim* denotes an imported luxury hardwood—dense, richly colored, and aromatic—prized for decorative supports and stringed musical instruments. Biblical botanists most often identify it as red sandalwood imported from India along Red Sea trade routes.

**Why does chapter 10 voice no explicit criticism of Solomon even as he breaks Deuteronomy's rules?** This reflects the restrained narrative artistry of Kings. Rather than breaking off to lecture the reader, the author places the most dazzling spectacle of Solomon's reign right before chapter 11, letting us feel the gap between the chapter's first half (where the Queen of Sheba blesses God for "justice and righteousness") and its second half (towering ivory and gold, relentless revenue, and an arms-and-horse brokerage with Egypt). That blinding shine makes the sudden plunge in the next chapter all the more stark and painful.
