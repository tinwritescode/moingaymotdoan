# 1 Kings 6 — Building the Temple of the LORD

## Story

Four hundred and eighty years after the Israelites came out of Egypt, in the month of Ziv—the second month—of the fourth year of Solomon's reign, construction of the temple of the LORD officially began. The structure had balanced, dignified proportions: sixty cubits long, twenty cubits wide, and thirty cubits high, fronted by a portico twenty cubits wide and ten cubits deep. Along three exterior walls of the temple, Solomon built a three-story annex of side rooms resting on recessed ledges along the outer wall—the lowest story five cubits wide, the middle six, and the upper seven—so the support beams rested on stepped ledges without piercing the main temple walls (6:1–6).

One striking detail amid that massive construction project is carefully recorded: all the stones were finished at the quarry before being brought to the site, "so that no hammer, chisel, or any other iron tool was heard in the temple while it was being built." The entrance to the side chambers stood on the south side, with winding stairs leading up to the middle floor and then to the third. Once the stone walls were finished, Solomon roofed the temple with beams and planks of cedar and framed the side structures with cedar timbers (6:7–10).

Right in the middle of measurements and building specifications, the narrative pauses abruptly to record a direct word from the LORD to Solomon. Concerning this house he was building, God spoke plainly: if Solomon walked in His statutes, carried out His ordinances, and kept all His commandments, then God would fulfill through him the promise made to David, and "I will dwell among the Israelites and will not abandon My people Israel" (6:11–13).

After that reminder, the account moves to the temple's interior finish. The entire interior stonework from floor to ceiling was paneled with cedar planks carved with gourds and open flowers—inside, "no stone was visible"—while the floor was overlaid with cypress planks. At the far rear, a twenty-cubit section was partitioned off as the inner sanctuary—the Most Holy Place—to house the Ark of the Covenant, leaving a forty-cubit main hall in front. The inner sanctuary formed a perfect cube: twenty cubits long, twenty wide, and twenty high. Solomon overlaid the entire interior of the temple with pure gold, plated the cedar altar standing before the inner sanctuary with gold, and stretched gold chains across its entrance (6:14–22).

Inside the Most Holy Place, Solomon placed two towering cherubim carved from olive wood and overlaid with gold, each ten cubits high with a wingspan of ten cubits. Standing side by side, they stretched their wings so that the outer wing of each touched the opposite walls while their inner wings met at the exact center of the room. Across all the walls of both the inner and outer rooms, artisans carved figures of cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers, overlaying the floors of both rooms with gold as well (6:23–30).

Double doors of olive wood stood at the entrance to the inner sanctuary, and folding cypress doors framed the main doorway of the temple, all carved with cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers and covered smoothly in gold. Surrounding the structure was the inner courtyard wall, built of three courses of dressed stone topped with one course of cedar beams. Begun in the second month of the fourth year and finished in every detail in the eighth month of the eleventh year, the temple took Solomon seven years to build (6:31–38).

## Key Takeaways

**No temple, however magnificent, can take the place of an obedient heart.** The LORD's word breaking straight into the middle of stone measurements and gold paneling (6:11–13) forms the beating heart of the chapter. God does not need a building to house Him; He dwells among His people only when king and nation walk within His covenant. A temple is never a lucky charm guaranteeing blessing when hearts turn away from God's commands.

**Silence at the building site expresses reverence before the Holy One.** Through seven years of heavy labor involving tens of thousands of workers, not a single blow of an iron hammer or chisel rang out at the temple site (6:7). All noisy shaping happened far away, leaving only quiet dignity and peace on the sacred ground itself.

**Work offered to God deserves wholehearted care even where no human eye can see.** The Most Holy Place remained sealed in darkness all year round, entered by the high priest alone on one single day each year; yet it received the finest cedar paneling, delicate carvings, and pure gold overlay (6:19–28). True reverence for God does not work for human applause, but before the One who sees in secret.

**The temple extends God's story of redemption.** Chapter 6 does not date the temple merely by Solomon's reign, but counts forward from the day Israel came out of Egypt (6:1). The temple was not a royal monument to showcase one king's prestige; it was the destination of God's rescue—bringing His freed people home to dwell close beside Him.

## Deeper Meaning

**Solomon's temple evokes the Garden of Eden reopened.** Stepping inside the temple, a priest saw no bare stone walls, only a lush golden garden: palm trees, blooming flowers, gourds, and winged cherubim standing guard (6:18; 6:23–29; 6:32). Those motifs reach straight back to Eden—the fruitful garden at the dawn of creation with cherubim stationed at the entrance to God's presence (cf. Genesis 2:8–9; 3:24). The temple reminded Israel that God's deepest longing has always been to dwell among humanity as He did at the beginning.

**The perfect cube of the Most Holy Place points ahead to the New Jerusalem.** The inner sanctuary measured twenty cubits in length, width, and height alike (6:20). On the final pages of Scripture, the holy city of the New Jerusalem descending from heaven shares that exact cubic perfection: its length, width, and height are equal (cf. Revelation 21:16). In the end, no tiny curtained chamber separates heaven from earth—the entire city and renewed creation become the Most Holy Place where God lives with His people.

**The ban on iron tools connects back to the Mosaic altar law.** Both Exodus and Deuteronomy forbade lifting an iron tool against the stones used to build an altar to the LORD (cf. Exodus 20:25; Deuteronomy 27:5–6). Finishing every stone at the quarry so no sound of iron echoed on the temple mount (6:7) carried that ancient reverence forward into the permanent sanctuary.

**The promise "I will dwell among the Israelites" runs from cover to cover of Scripture.** Verse 6:13 echoes the very pledge God gave Moses when instructing him to erect the wilderness Tabernacle: He desired a sanctuary among His people so He could dwell in their midst (cf. Exodus 25:8; Leviticus 26:11–12). In the New Testament, that promise culminates in Jesus Himself—who spoke of His own body as the true temple (cf. John 2:19–21)—and in the Church, where believers are built together as "living stones" into God's spiritual house (cf. 1 Peter 2:5).

## Notes & Nuances

**Scholars interpret the 480-year milestone in 6:1 in two main ways.** Verse 6:1 places the temple's foundation (around 966 BC) 480 years after the Exodus. Many scholars take the figure literally, placing the Exodus around 1446 BC. Many others view 480 as a round symbolic figure—twelve generations multiplied by forty years each—and date the Exodus in the thirteenth century BC to align with certain archaeological findings. Whichever timeline one adopts, the theological point of 6:1 remains clear: building the temple marks the grand culmination of the Exodus journey.

**A biblical "cubit" is much smaller than a yard or meter.** An ancient cubit measured from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger—roughly 18 inches, or about 45 centimeters. Thus a temple 60 cubits long, 20 wide, and 30 high stood only about 90 feet long, 30 feet wide, and 45 feet high (roughly 27 by 9 by 13.5 meters), surprisingly modest next to many modern cathedrals. The reason is simple: an Old Testament temple was not an auditorium for crowds to sit inside, but the sacred throne room housing the Ark of the Covenant; the congregation gathered and sacrificed in the spacious courtyards outside, while priests alone entered the interior.

**Where was the temple built?** Chapter 6 narrates the construction without naming the location. The book of 2 Chronicles adds that Solomon erected the temple on Mount Moriah in Jerusalem, right on the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite where David had purchased land and built an altar (cf. 2 Chronicles 3:1–2)—the mountain biblical tradition also links with Abraham's offering of Isaac.

**Why are the months from the second month of year four to the eighth month of year eleven called seven years?** Counted strictly on the calendar, the span in 6:37–38 equals seven years and six months, yet 6:38 rounds it cleanly to seven years. Rounding regnal spans is standard in biblical writing; moreover, the number seven carries echoes of completeness and the seven days of creation, casting the temple as a miniature cosmos made for God's rest.

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, describes Solomon's temple architecture in meticulous detail in Book 8 of his history. He marvels especially at the precision of the pre-cut stones in 6:7: they fitted together so seamlessly that onlookers imagined nature itself had joined them into a single mass, showing no trace of hammer, chisel, or iron tool on the sacred site. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus devotes an extensive section of Book 8 to describing Solomon's temple and the seamless, tool-free assembly of its stones -->
