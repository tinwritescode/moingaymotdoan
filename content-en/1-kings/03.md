# 1 Kings 3 — The Dream at Gibeon and the Trial of Two Mothers

## Story

As soon as his throne was secure, Solomon made a marriage alliance with Pharaoh king of Egypt, marrying Pharaoh's daughter and bringing her to live in the City of David until he finished building his own palace, the temple of the LORD, and the wall around Jerusalem. Because no temple had yet been built for the LORD's name, the people were still sacrificing at the high places. Solomon loved the LORD and walked in the statutes of his father David, yet he too offered sacrifices and burned incense at the high places. The king went to Gibeon—the greatest of the high places—and offered a thousand burnt offerings on the altar there (3:1–4).

That night at Gibeon, the LORD appeared to Solomon in a dream and said, "Ask for whatever you want Me to give you." Solomon began with gratitude for the great loving devotion God had shown his father David, then humbly confessed: "I am only a little child and do not know how to go out or come in," placed in the midst of God's chosen people, too vast to number. So the king asked God to give him "an understanding heart" to govern His people and to discern between good and evil (3:5–9).

The request deeply pleased the LORD. Because Solomon had not asked for long life, wealth, or the death of his enemies, but for discernment to administer justice, God promised him a wise and discerning heart unlike anyone before or after him. More than that, God granted what he had not asked for—both riches and honor surpassing any king of his day—and promised long life as well if he walked in God's ways as David had done. Awakening and realizing it had been a dream, Solomon returned to Jerusalem, stood before the Ark of the Covenant of the LORD, offered burnt offerings and peace offerings, and held a banquet for all his servants (3:10–15).

Not long afterward, that wisdom was put to the test in a seemingly impossible case. Two prostitutes came and stood before the king. The first woman explained that the two of them lived alone in the same house with no one else present; she gave birth to a son, and three days later the other woman also bore a son. One night, the other woman rolled over on her baby and suffocated him, then got up in the middle of the night, swapped her dead infant into the first woman's arms, and took the living child for herself. When the first woman woke at dawn to nurse her son, she found him dead—but in the bright morning light she looked closely and knew it was not the son she had borne. The other woman vehemently denied it: "No! The living one is my son, and the dead one is yours." Back and forth they argued before the king (3:16–22).

There were no witnesses and no evidence beyond two conflicting claims. Solomon calmly summarized both sides and gave an order: "Bring me a sword." When a sword was brought in, the king pronounced a sentence that stunned the court: "Cut the living child in two and give half to one and half to the other." Instantly, the truth surfaced. Moved with deep compassion for her son, the true mother pleaded, "Please, my lord, give her the living baby! Do not kill him!" The other woman answered coldly, "He shall be neither mine nor yours. Cut him in two!" Then the king spoke decisively, awarding the living child to the first woman, for "she is his mother." News of the verdict spread throughout Israel, and all the people stood in awe of the king, seeing that the wisdom of God was in him to administer justice (3:23–28).

## Key Takeaways

**Small cracks already appear amid bright faithfulness.** Chapter 3 does not paint Solomon as a flawless saint. Right before his glowing dream at Gibeon, verses 3:1–3 mention his political marriage to Pharaoh's daughter and worship at the high places. These quiet details act like hairline cracks in a beautiful wall, foreshadowing the collapse at the end of his reign.

**What we ask for when handed a blank check exposes our real heart.** God placed an open invitation before Solomon: ask for whatever you want (3:5). Surrounded by every temptation a young ruler might crave—long life, gold, victory over foes—Solomon thought of God's people before himself. The prayers that please God most ask for the grace to serve others well.

**True wisdom begins with humility.** Only a chapter earlier, Solomon had looked sharp and ruthless while clearing away his rivals; yet standing before God, he bowed as a little child who did not know how to go out or come in (3:7). Only those who admit they lack wisdom open their hearts to receive wisdom from above.

**God's justice extends to the very bottom of society.** The inaugural test of Solomon's wisdom was not a diplomatic treaty or a military campaign, but a plea from two marginalized women with no husband or family to advocate for them. Before a throne guided by God, the life of one nameless infant still deserves full, careful justice.

## Deeper Meaning

**Seek what belongs to God first, and the rest will be added.** God's response to Solomon at Gibeon (3:11–13) resonates directly in Jesus's famous teaching on the mountain: seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these other things will be added to you as well (cf. Matthew 6:33). The New Testament widens that promise to every believer: if anyone lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault (cf. James 1:5).

**Early warnings from the Law of Moses about foreign marriages and worship sites.** The law had forbidden Israel to intermarry with foreign nations lest they turn the people's hearts after other gods (cf. Deuteronomy 7:3–4), and commanded that burnt offerings be brought only to the place the LORD chose (cf. Deuteronomy 12:13–14). Solomon's marriage to Pharaoh's daughter and his sacrifices at the high places right at the opening of chapter 3 quietly lay the fuse for his tragic downfall in chapter 11.

**A king who defends the vulnerable points ahead to the Messiah.** The wisdom enabling Solomon to see through human hearts rather than judge merely by competing appearances (3:28) foreshadows the coming King foretold by Isaiah: One resting under the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, who will not judge by what His eyes see or His ears hear, but will judge the poor with righteousness and decide with equity for the meek (cf. Isaiah 11:2–4).

**"One greater than Solomon is here."** Even though Solomon received unmatched wisdom (3:12), he remained a finite human capable of stumbling. Scripture teaches that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom (cf. Proverbs 9:10), and in the New Testament Jesus Himself declares that "One greater than Solomon is here" (cf. Matthew 12:42). The apostle Paul goes further still: Jesus Christ does not merely dispense wisdom—He has become for us wisdom from God (cf. 1 Corinthians 1:30).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Solomon sacrifice at the "high place" of Gibeon?** The Law of Moses restricted unauthorized sacrifices across the land, yet verse 3:2 notes the transitional situation of the time: the Jerusalem temple had not yet been built. The book of 2 Chronicles adds why Gibeon was the preeminent high place: while David had brought the Ark of the Covenant to Jerusalem, Moses's ancient Tabernacle and the bronze altar still stood at Gibeon (cf. 2 Chronicles 1:3–6). Even so, the qualifying "only" in 3:3 signals that the narrator viewed scattered worship at the high places as less than ideal.

**"An understanding heart" literally means "a listening heart."** In 3:9, the original Hebrew phrase translates literally as a heart that hears. Biblical wisdom is not clever rhetoric or shrewd scheming; first and foremost, it is the capacity to listen closely to the voice of God and to the unspoken cry of human need.

**Solomon calling himself a "little child" does not mean he was a young boy.** Historians estimate that Solomon was around twenty years old when he took the throne—and likely already married. Describing himself as "a little child" (3:7) was a customary ancient expression of humility, acknowledging his inexperience before the weight of governing a vast nation.

**The blunt description of the two women as "prostitutes" in 3:16.** The Hebrew word leaves no doubt about their trade, even when older translations occasionally softened the term. Reading that detail plainly brings the case's full force into view: these were two women pushed to the margins of society, sharing a house with no male protector, who were nonetheless permitted to walk straight into the royal court and have their plea heard with utmost care by the wisest king on earth.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, preached on the trial of the two mothers as a piercing portrait of genuine love versus envy. He observed that the false mother was willing to see the child sliced in two so long as her rival lost him as well, whereas the true mother preferred to suffer loss herself to keep her child alive. In his debate against the Donatist schismatics of his day, Augustine drew on this image to defend unity: the true Church never consents to tearing the body apart just to claim victory. *(Sermons, Sermon 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Augustine's use of the two mothers in 1 Kings 3 against the Donatists is well known and usually cataloged as Sermon 10, though exact sermon number and wording were not checked against the source -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, recounts the dream at Gibeon with deep admiration: though still young, Solomon rose above all the usual appetites of youth—wealth, fame, and pleasure—to ask solely for wisdom. Retelling the trial of the two mothers, Josephus pictures the royal court initially assuming the king was joking when he ordered the infant cut in two, before everyone marveled that the sword had unlocked the true hearts of both women. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus gives a detailed account of Solomon's prayer and the courtiers' reaction during the trial of the two mothers in Book 8 -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, treats Solomon's petition as a model of God-pleasing prayer because it aimed at the good of the people rather than personal glory. He also pauses over 3:15 to draw a practical lesson: immediately upon waking from the dream, Solomon returned to Jerusalem to offer sacrifices before the Ark—showing that one who truly receives God's grace hastens to give Him thanks. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Kings 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Matthew Henry's two characteristic comments on Solomon's motive in prayer and his thanksgiving in verse 15, though not verified line by line -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, urges readers to look closely at the chapter's first three verses rather than gazing only at the luminous dream at Gibeon. Placing the marriage to Pharaoh's daughter and worship at the high places right before praising Solomon's love for the LORD is a sober narrative stroke: even at its brightest summit, Solomon's reign already carries the seeds of compromise. *(1 & 2 Kings, Smyth & Helwys Bible Commentary, 2000)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting the ambivalence and subtle warning in verses 1–3 before the Gibeon dream is a signature theme in Brueggemann's commentary; page numbers not verified -->
