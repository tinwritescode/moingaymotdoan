# 1 Kings 5 — Preparing to Build the Temple and the Treaty with Hiram

## Story

When Hiram king of Tyre heard that Solomon had been anointed king in his father's place, he sent envoys to congratulate him, for Hiram had always been a devoted friend of David. Solomon seized the occasion to send back an important proposal. He reminded Hiram that his father David had been unable to build a temple for the name of the LORD because of wars surrounding him on every side; now, however, God had granted Solomon "rest on every side, and there is no adversary or crisis." Therefore he intended to build a temple exactly as the LORD had promised David. Solomon asked Hiram to order his woodsmen to cut cedars from Lebanon, promising that his own servants would work alongside them and that he would pay whatever wages Hiram set, candidly admitting that "no one among us knows how to fell timber like the Sidonians" (5:1–6).

Hearing Solomon's message, King Hiram was overjoyed and burst into praise: "Blessed be the LORD today, for He has given David a wise son to rule over this great people!" Hiram immediately agreed to supply all the cedar and cypress timber Solomon wanted. He laid out the logistics cleanly: Tyrian workers would haul the logs down from Lebanon to the sea, lash them into rafts, float them along the coast to whatever landing site Solomon named, and untie them there; in exchange, Solomon would provide food for Hiram's royal household (5:7–9).

The partnership ran smoothly. Hiram supplied all the cedar and cypress timber Solomon needed, while year by year Solomon sent Hiram twenty thousand kors of wheat to feed his household and twenty kors of pure pressed olive oil. Scripture observes that the LORD gave Solomon wisdom just as He had promised; the two kings remained at peace and made a formal treaty together (5:10–12).

To gather materials for the immense undertaking, Solomon mobilized a vast workforce. He conscripted thirty thousand men for forced labor "out of all Israel," placing Adoniram over them and rotating them to Lebanon in three shifts: ten thousand men worked a month in Lebanon and then spent two months at home. Beyond them stood seventy thousand carriers, eighty thousand stonecutters in the hills, and three thousand three hundred foremen overseeing and coordinating the labor (5:13–16).

At the king's command, workers quarried huge blocks of high-grade stone to dress and lay as the foundation of the temple. Solomon's builders, Hiram's craftsmen, and skilled stoneworkers from Gebal worked side by side shaping the timber and stone so everything would be ready for the temple's construction (5:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Each generation plays its own distinct role in God's plan.** David longed to build the temple, yet his calling was to secure the land, subdue its enemies, and gather materials. Solomon inherited the peace won by the generation before him to raise the house itself (5:3–5). No one finishes everything in a single lifetime; humbly building on the labor of those who went before is part of true wisdom.

**Wise leaders recognize their limits and partner fairly with those more skilled.** Israel's king did not let national pride push him into fumbling alone. He openly acknowledged that the Sidonians surpassed his people in timber craft, gladly paid fair wages, and traded Israel's agricultural abundance for Phoenicia's timber (5:6–11).

**Wisdom and integrity lift up God's name among outsiders.** The spontaneous blessing from the foreign monarch Hiram (5:7) shows that when God's people act with trustworthiness, peaceableness, and wisdom, their very conduct moves onlookers to praise the LORD.

**Great work for God requires solid foundations—and careful thought for the burden placed on people.** The narrator notes both the painstaking care given to cutting huge, costly foundation stones for the temple (5:17) and, more quietly, the rise of a thirty-thousand-man forced labor levy (5:13). Doing big things for God always calls for vigilance, lest zeal for building end up pressing too heavily on human shoulders.

## Deeper Meaning

**Moses's ancient promise reaches its hour of fulfillment.** While Israel still camped outside the Promised Land, Moses had foretold a day when God would give them rest from all their surrounding enemies and then choose one place for His name to dwell (cf. Deuteronomy 12:10–11). Solomon's declaration that the LORD had granted "rest on every side" (5:4), together with the promise that a son of peace would build the temple (cf. 1 Chronicles 22:8–10), announces that this historic moment has arrived.

**Gentile hands join in building the house of God.** Hiram had earlier sent cedar and skilled craftsmen to build David's palace (cf. 2 Samuel 5:11), and now Lebanon's timber alongside workers from Tyre and Gebal helped construct the very temple of the LORD (5:6–10; 5:18). This scene anticipates Isaiah's radiant vision of the glory of Lebanon being brought to adorn God's sanctuary (cf. Isaiah 60:13), and finds its full meaning in the New Testament, where Jews and Gentiles alike are joined together into a holy temple in Christ (cf. Ephesians 2:19–22).

**A warning bell sounds in the words "forced labor."** Throughout Scripture, the Hebrew term for forced labor is tied to Israel's bitterest memory—being driven under Egyptian taskmasters to build Pharaoh's store cities (cf. Exodus 1:11–14). Now, to erect a temple for the very God who rescued them from Pharaoh's yoke, Solomon appoints Adoniram and thousands of overseers to muster forced labor from his own people. The narrative subtly casts a shadow across even its brightest achievement.

**Costly foundation stones and the Cornerstone of the new temple.** Solomon did not settle for cheap stone in the hidden foundation beneath the ground; he insisted on huge, costly, carefully dressed blocks (5:17). In the New Testament, the Church is likewise a spiritual temple built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus Himself as the precious Cornerstone holding the whole structure together (cf. Ephesians 2:20–21).

## Notes & Nuances

**The apparent tension between 5:13 and 9:20–22 over conscripting Israelites.** Verse 5:13 reports that Solomon conscripted thirty thousand laborers "out of all Israel," whereas chapter 9 states that Solomon conscripted the surviving Canaanite populations while making none of the Israelites into slaves (cf. 9:20–22). The standard explanation distinguishes two different groups: the thirty thousand Israelites in 5:13 served on a temporary, rotating public levy—one month on duty, two months back home tending their farms—whereas the hundred and fifty thousand carriers and stonecutters in 5:15 were resident non-Israelites drafted for long-term forced labor, as 2 Chronicles clarifies (cf. 2 Chronicles 2:17–18).

**Minor numerical variations compared with 2 Chronicles 2.** In 5:11, Solomon supplies Hiram's household with twenty thousand kors of wheat and twenty kors of pure pressed olive oil, whereas 2 Chronicles 2:10 adds barley, twenty thousand baths of wine, and twenty thousand baths of oil; many scholars explain that Chronicles lists rations paid to the timber crews, distinct from the annual royal payment to Hiram's palace noted here. Likewise, the overseers number 3,300 in 5:16 versus 3,600 in 2 Chronicles 2:18, usually understood as counting or separating 300 higher-ranking supervisors.

**Who were the Sidonians and the men of Gebal?** Both were Phoenician peoples along the coast of modern Lebanon, famous throughout the ancient Mediterranean for seafaring, trade, cedar logging, and fine masonry. Sidon and Tyre were sister port cities, so Israelites commonly referred to Phoenicians collectively as Sidonians. The men of Gebal came from ancient Byblos, north of Tyre, renowned for exceptional stonecutting.

**Does Hiram's praise in 5:7 mean he converted to worshipping the LORD alone?** Not necessarily. In ancient Near Eastern diplomacy, kings routinely expressed goodwill by honoring the patron deity of an allied kingdom while continuing to worship their own gods. Even so, the biblical author records Hiram's words gladly, because Solomon's wisdom caused the LORD's name to be honored beyond Israel's borders.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, reproduces the correspondence between Solomon and Hiram at length in Book 8 of his history, even claiming that copies of those letters were still preserved in the public archives of Tyre in his own day. He offers a practical explanation for why the treaty pleased both sides: island-bound Tyre possessed fine timber and skilled artisans but lacked farmland, so it depended heavily on grain and olive oil from inland Israel. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus records the letters between Solomon and Hiram and mentions the Tyrian public archives in Book 8 of Antiquities -->
