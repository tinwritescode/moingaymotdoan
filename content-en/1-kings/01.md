# 1 Kings 1 — A Shaking Throne and Solomon's Accession

## Story

King David was now very old and frail, and no matter how many blankets were piled over him, he could not keep warm. His servants searched throughout all Israel for a young virgin to care for him and lie in his arms so the king would stay warm. They brought back Abishag, a very beautiful young woman from Shunem. She attended the king devotedly, yet Scripture notes plainly that "the king had no intimate relations with her" (1:1–4).

Seeing his father's failing strength, Adonijah—the son of Haggith, born next after Absalom—exalted himself and declared, "I will be king." He acquired chariots, horsemen, and fifty men to run ahead of him in royal display. Throughout his entire life, his father had never once displeased him by asking, "Why do you behave as you do?" Adonijah won over Joab, David's veteran commander, and the priest Abiathar; but Zadok, Benaiah, the prophet Nathan, and David's mighty men refused to join him. Adonijah went down to the Stone of Zoheleth near the spring of En-rogel, sacrificed cattle and sheep for a feast, and invited his brothers and the royal officials of Judah, while deliberately leaving out Nathan, Benaiah, the mighty men, and Solomon (1:5–10).

The prophet Nathan sensed the danger at once. He went to Bathsheba, Solomon's mother, broke the news that Adonijah had made himself king without David's knowledge, and devised a plan to save the lives of both mother and son. Bathsheba entered the aged king's private chamber while Abishag was attending him. Bowing low, she reminded David of his oath that Solomon would sit on the throne, and reported that Adonijah was holding a coronation feast without inviting Solomon. She spoke bluntly: the eyes of all Israel were waiting for the king's word; otherwise, once David died, she and Solomon "would be treated as criminals" (1:11–21).

While she was still speaking, the prophet Nathan arrived. He bowed face down before the king and asked tactfully whether this succession had been David's own decision without telling his servants. He recounted the banquet down at En-rogel: the king's sons, the army commanders, and Abiathar were eating and drinking and shouting, "Long live King Adonijah!" while Nathan, Zadok, Benaiah, and Solomon had all been excluded (1:22–27).

At that, David snapped back to full alertness. He summoned Bathsheba and swore by the living LORD that that very day he would fulfill his oath to place Solomon on the throne. He called in Zadok, Nathan, and Benaiah, commanding them to set Solomon on the king's own mule and escort him down to the spring of Gihon; there Zadok and Nathan were to anoint Solomon, blow the trumpet, and shout, "Long live King Solomon!" before bringing him back up to take the throne. Benaiah answered, "Amen!" and prayed that the LORD would make Solomon's throne even greater than David's (1:28–37).

Events unfolded swiftly. Zadok took the horn of oil from the Tent and anointed Solomon at Gihon; the trumpet sounded, and all the people cheered and played flutes as they marched up into the city so loudly that the ground shook. Over at Adonijah's feast, the guests had just finished eating when they heard the uproar; Joab caught the blast of the trumpet and grew uneasy. At that moment Jonathan son of Abiathar ran up. Adonijah still hoped he was carrying good news, but Jonathan replied, "Not at all!" He told the whole story: David had made Solomon king, the city was rejoicing, Solomon was already seated on the royal throne, and David himself had bowed in worship on his bed, thanking God that his own eyes had seen his successor (1:38–48).

Before the words had finished, the banquet scattered in panic, every guest fleeing his own way. Terrified, Adonijah ran and clung to the horns of the altar, begging Solomon to swear not to put him to death by the sword. Solomon set a clear condition: if Adonijah proved himself a worthy man, "not a hair of his head will fall to the ground," but if evil was found in him, he would die. Solomon had Adonijah brought down from the altar; the older brother bowed low before the new king and heard the terse command: "Go to your home" (1:49–53).

## Key Takeaways

**Earthly power eventually grows cold.** The book of Israel's kings does not open with military triumph, but with an aged monarch who cannot stay warm beneath piles of blankets. The warrior who once felled Goliath now lies still in a private chamber, unaware that his son is staging a rival court outside. Every human throne has its limit.

**A father's indulgence leaves a heavy cost behind.** Verse 1:6 is brief yet piercing: throughout Adonijah's life, David had never once dared to displease his son by asking why he acted the way he did. When a parent avoids correction while faults are small, the price paid later can be a succession crisis threatening bloodshed between brothers.

**Self-promotion does not make a true king.** Adonijah possessed everything the world admires: striking looks, seniority among the brothers, chariots, fifty runners, and the backing of General Joab and the priest Abiathar. He lacked only one thing—neither God nor his father had chosen him for the throne. His lavish coronation collapsed at the blast of a single trumpet.

**Trusting God does not mean sitting passively while wrong takes over.** The prophet Nathan did not fold his hands and wait for a miracle. He read the moment, consulted with Bathsheba, and timed his appeal to rouse the aging king. God often works through the alertness and courage of faithful people to fulfill His will.

**True mercy always walks alongside clear boundaries.** Newly enthroned, Solomon did not execute the usurper according to royal custom; he offered Adonijah a path to live. Yet he stated the terms plainly: live uprightly and enjoy peace, plot evil and pay the penalty.

## Deeper Meaning

**Absalom's shadow returns in Adonijah.** Anyone coming straight from 2 Samuel recognizes the parallel immediately: Adonijah was born right after Absalom (cf. 2 Samuel 3:4), was likewise handsome, and acquired chariots and fifty runners in the exact image of his older brother years before (cf. 2 Samuel 15:1). Family fractures that are never healed at the root tend to replay themselves in the next generation.

**God's promise stands firm amid human scheming.** Inside a story thick with palace intrigue, the covenant God had sworn to David—that He would raise up his offspring to sit on the throne and establish his kingdom forever (cf. 2 Samuel 7:12–16)—remained unbroken. Solomon had been loved by the LORD from birth (cf. 2 Samuel 12:24–25) and designated to build the temple (cf. 1 Chronicles 22:9–10). David's thanksgiving in 1:48 confirms that God Himself had placed Solomon on the throne.

**A king riding a mule into the city foreshadows the Messiah.** Unlike Adonijah flaunting war chariots and horsemen, Solomon was escorted on King David's own mule (1:33; 1:38)—the dignified mount of a peacetime ruler. Centuries later, the prophet Zechariah announced that the coming Redeemer King would arrive gently, riding on a donkey (cf. Zechariah 9:9), and Jesus fulfilled that very picture as He entered Jerusalem (cf. Matthew 21:5).

**The horns of the altar cannot shield a wicked heart.** Under the Law of Moses, the altar provided refuge for someone who killed unintentionally, not a shield for willful schemers (cf. Exodus 21:13–14). Adonijah clung to the altar's horns out of fear of death rather than genuine repentance. Meanwhile, Solomon's pledge that "not a hair of his head will fall to the ground" echoes the oath the people once swore to spare Jonathan's life (cf. 1 Samuel 14:45).

## Notes & Nuances

**Abishag lying in King David's arms must be read in its ancient context.** Before modern medical care or heating, people in the ancient world believed the body warmth of a healthy young person helped sustain a frail elder's life. Scripture stresses that David did not sleep with her (1:4). This detail both underscores David's physical exhaustion and sets up the tragedy in chapter 2, when Adonijah asks to marry Abishag.

**David's oath to Bathsheba is not recorded in 2 Samuel.** Because no earlier verse quotes that oath, some scholars wonder whether Nathan and Bathsheba were gently steering a forgetful old king. Yet this chapter shows David recalling and repeating the oath decisively on his own (1:29–30), and 1 Chronicles 22:9–10 likewise affirms that God had already designated Solomon to succeed him.

**Why did longtime loyalists like Joab and Abiathar side with Adonijah?** With his older brothers dead, Adonijah was the eldest surviving prince and by ordinary custom appeared the natural heir. Moreover, both Joab and Abiathar feared being sidelined under Solomon's reign, where Benaiah and Zadok were rising in influence. Choosing sides out of self-protective calculation cost both men dearly in the next chapter.

**The spring of Gihon and the spring of En-rogel lay right next to each other.** Both stood in the valley along the southeastern flank of Jerusalem, separated by only a few hundred meters, so the trumpets and cheers at Gihon carried straight down to Adonijah's feast. Ironically, the messenger who brought the bad news was Jonathan son of Abiathar—the very young man who had once waited at En-rogel to carry intelligence that saved David from Absalom (cf. 2 Samuel 17:17).

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, a fourth- and fifth-century biblical scholar, opens his famous letter to the clergyman Nepotian with the story of Abishag warming King David. He reads the scene allegorically: when the body grows old and every earthly pleasure turns cold, only the Wisdom that comes from God remains forever young and pure, warming the heart of the one who fears Him to the very end of life. *(Letter 52 to Nepotian)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jerome's allegorical reading of Abishag as Wisdom at the opening of Letter 52 to Nepotian is a very well-known passage -->

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, explains that bringing Abishag into David's chamber was recommended by the physicians of the day to preserve the king's body heat. He also highlights the resemblance between Adonijah and Absalom: the same arrogant temperament, rushing to build a faction and seize the crown while their father was still alive. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Josephus explicitly mentions the physicians' advice regarding Abishag and compares Adonijah to Absalom near the end of Book 7 -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, pauses over 1:6 to warn parents that David's blind indulgence nurtured Adonijah's self-exaltation. By contrast, he praises the moment David bows in worship on his bed at the end of the chapter, holding him up as a model of one who places God's will and the people's peace above his own hold on power. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Kings 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Matthew Henry's standard observations on verse 6 and David's closing thanksgiving, though not checked line by line against the original text -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, reads the opening two chapters of 1 Kings as a sharply political narrative of power transition. In his view, the picture of an aged king unable to warm himself serves as a vivid literary symbol of fading royal power, setting off a clash between the old establishment around Adonijah and the rising coalition around Nathan, Bathsheba, and Solomon. *(1 & 2 Kings, Smyth & Helwys Bible Commentary, 2000)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading the Abishag scene as a symbol of waning royal potency and factional struggle is characteristic of Brueggemann's commentary; page numbers not verified -->

**Dale Ralph Davis**, an American pastor and Old Testament professor, stresses that amid palace maneuvers that look purely human in chapter 1, what stands out most is the quiet faithfulness of God.
