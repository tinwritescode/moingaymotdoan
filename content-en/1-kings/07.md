# 1 Kings 7 — Solomon's Palace and the Furnishings of the Temple

## Story

Having built the temple in seven years, Solomon turned to constructing his own palace complex, taking a full thirteen years to complete it. The grandest hall was the Palace of the Forest of Lebanon, a hundred cubits long, fifty cubits wide, and thirty cubits high, with dense rows of cedar pillars and beams resembling a forest supporting three tiers of chambers with framed windows facing one another. In front stood a Hall of Pillars, followed by a Hall of Justice paneled in cedar from floor to ceiling where the king's judgment throne stood. Behind an inner courtyard lay Solomon's private residence along with a matching palace built for his wife, Pharaoh's daughter (7:1–8).

The entire palace complex was built from costly stones cut to exact measurements, trimmed smoothly with saws on both the inside and outside. Its foundation rested on massive blocks eight to ten cubits long. The enclosing wall of the great courtyard followed the same pattern as the temple's inner courtyard: three courses of dressed stone alternating with one course of cedar beams (7:9–12).

To craft the bronze furnishings for the temple, Solomon sent to Tyre for a master artisan named Hiram. His mother was a widow from the tribe of Naphtali, and his father was a bronze worker from Tyre. Scripture describes Hiram as "filled with wisdom, understanding, and skill for doing any work in bronze." He accepted the king's invitation and took charge of all the temple's metalwork (7:13–14).

Hiram's first work was a pair of monumental bronze pillars, each eighteen cubits high and twelve cubits in circumference, crowned with five-cubit capitals decorated with latticework, braided chains, two rows of pomegranates, and lily-shaped tops. He set the two pillars at the portico of the temple, naming the pillar on the south Jachin and the one on the north Boaz (7:15–22).

Next, he cast a gigantic circular basin called the "Sea of cast metal," ten cubits from rim to rim, thirty cubits in circumference, and five cubits high, its wall a handbreadth thick with a rim flaring outward like the lip of a cup and the petals of a lily. Beneath the rim ran two rows of gourds cast in one piece with the basin. Holding two thousand baths of water, the entire Sea rested on the backs of twelve bronze oxen facing outward in groups of three toward north, west, south, and east, their hindquarters tucked inward (7:23–26).

Alongside the Sea, Hiram constructed ten bronze movable stands fitted with chariot-like wheels and richly engraved with lions, oxen, cherubim, palm trees, and wreaths. On each stand sat a bronze basin holding forty baths of water; five were stationed on the south side of the temple and five on the north, while the Sea itself stood near the southeast corner (7:27–39).

All of these burnished bronze furnishings—the two pillars, four hundred pomegranates, ten stands, ten basins, the Sea with its twelve oxen, as well as pots, shovels, and sprinkling bowls—were cast in the clay ground of the Jordan plain between Succoth and Zarethan. So vast was the quantity of bronze used that Solomon never weighed it all (7:40–47).

Finally came the pure gold furnishings placed inside the temple itself: the golden altar of incense, the golden table for the Bread of the Presence, ten golden lampstands arrayed five on each side before the inner sanctuary, along with floral ornaments, lamps, tongs, basins, wick trimmers, bowls, dishes, censers, and even gold door sockets. When all the work for the house of the LORD was completed, Solomon brought in all the silver, gold, and vessels his father David had consecrated earlier and placed them in the temple treasuries (7:48–51).

## Key Takeaways

**The sprawling palace complex sitting beside the temple raises a quiet question.** The narrator does not utter a single word of rebuke, yet deliberately inserts the palace construction right into the middle of the temple account and lets the numbers speak for themselves: seven years for the temple (6:38), thirteen years for the palace (7:1); the Palace of the Forest of Lebanon dwarfed the temple in floor space. Right at the peak of prosperity, an attentive reader glimpses royal grandeur beginning to overshadow the house of God.

**God uses the craftsmanship of ordinary people, including those of mixed heritage.** The artist who shaped the bronze masterpieces for God's house did not come from purebred Israelite nobility; he was the son of a widow from Naphtali and a foreign craftsman from Tyre (7:14). Artistic mastery and skilled hands are called "wisdom, understanding, and skill"—the very endowment once bestowed on the builder of the wilderness Tabernacle.

**Sacred furnishings were not mere decoration; they taught truths about God.** Entering the temple courts, worshippers first met two towering bronze pillars named Jachin ("He will establish") and Boaz ("In Him is strength"), followed immediately by the huge bronze Sea and ten washing basins. Before anyone drew near the Holy One, every bronze emblem preached two truths: lasting stability comes from God alone, and human beings need cleansing to approach Him.

**One generation's achievement always rests on the devotion of the generation before.** Even though Solomon's bronze and gold defied weighing (7:47), the chapter closes with him reverently placing the treasures David had dedicated into the temple storehouses (7:51). The temple was never Solomon's solo triumph; it was the fulfillment of his father's lifelong longing.

## Deeper Meaning

**The two pillars Jachin and Boaz tie straight back to God's covenant with David.** In Hebrew, Jachin means "He will establish," and Boaz is commonly understood as "in Him is strength." Towering right at the temple threshold, those two names echoed God's pledge to David that He Himself would establish his royal line and make his kingdom endure (2 Samuel 7:12–16). At the close of Scripture, Jesus picks up that very picture of an enduring pillar, promising the faithful that He will make them "a pillar in the temple of My God," never again to leave it (Revelation 3:12).

**The Sea and ten basins expand the Tabernacle laver and point toward the heavenly sea of glass.** In Moses's day, the Tabernacle courtyard held a single bronze basin where priests washed hands and feet before serving lest they die (Exodus 30:18–21). The book of 2 Chronicles explains the expanded roles here: the ten smaller basins were used to rinse offerings for the burnt sacrifice, while the great Sea served for the priests to wash in (2 Chronicles 4:6). Many interpreters also see the massive basin resting on twelve oxen as symbolizing both the twelve tribes and the unruly waters of chaos tamed beneath God's rule—an image that later reappears before heaven's throne as "a sea of glass, as clear as crystal" (Revelation 4:6).

**Hiram the craftsman echoes Bezalel from the Exodus.** Verse 7:14's praise of Hiram as "filled with wisdom, understanding, and skill for doing any work in bronze" repeats almost verbatim God's description of Bezalel, the chief artisan of the wilderness Tabernacle (Exodus 31:2–5). The echo links Solomon's temple directly to Moses's Tabernacle while opening the doors wide for Gentile skill to help build the house of God.

**The palace for Pharaoh's daughter foreshadows the dynasty's fracture.** Solomon's marriage to Pharaoh's daughter was already noted in chapter 3 (3:1), and now she receives a dedicated palace woven right into the royal complex (7:8). By the time readers reach chapter 11, those dazzling, seemingly savvy political marriages become the doorway that pulls Israel's wisest king after foreign gods.

**The "unweighed" bronze foreshadows the final catastrophe of Kings.** The narrator inserts a detail that sounds like sheer celebration of abundance: Solomon left the bronze of the two pillars, the Sea, and the stands unweighed because there was too much of it (7:47). Yet more than three centuries later, on the day Babylon torched Jerusalem, 2 Kings lists those exact items—the two pillars, the bronze Sea, and the stands—smashed to pieces and hauled away, repeating the very same phrase: "the bronze from all these articles was beyond weighing" (2 Kings 25:13–17; cf. Jeremiah 52:17). Material splendor, no matter how immense, vanishes when a nation loses its faithfulness.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does a ten-cubit diameter with a thirty-cubit circumference in 7:23 mean Scripture miscalculated pi?** Critics sometimes cite this verse to claim the Bible did not know the ratio of circumference to diameter is roughly 3.14. Two down-to-earth explanations resolve the puzzle. First, ancient descriptions routinely rounded measurements to clean whole numbers (ten cubits and thirty cubits). Second, verse 7:26 explicitly notes that the basin's wall was a handbreadth thick (about 3 inches, or 7–8 centimeters) and its rim flared outward like a lily: measuring ten cubits across from outer rim to outer rim while stretching a thirty-cubit cord around the inner rim (or the body beneath the flare) yields a ratio strikingly close to pi.

**Minor differences between 1 Kings 7 and 2 Chronicles 2–4.** Verse 7:14 identifies Hiram's mother as belonging to the tribe of Naphtali, while 2 Chronicles calls her a woman of Dan (2 Chronicles 2:14); many commentators explain that she was born into the tribe of Dan and married into Naphtali, or lived along the northern border shared by both tribes. Similarly, 7:26 says the Sea held two thousand baths while 2 Chronicles says three thousand (2 Chronicles 4:5); common explanations distinguish its normal working fill (two thousand baths) from its brim-full capacity (three thousand baths), alongside the possibility of an ancient copyist's slip in numerals.

**Did the twelve bronze oxen and engraved lions break the commandment against graven images?** The Second Commandment forbade crafting idols to bow down to and worship, not every form of representational art—after all, Moses's own Ark of the Covenant bore two golden cherubim. Here the oxen merely served as structural supports under a water basin, never as objects of worship. Even so, in a region where neighboring nations revered the bull as the emblem of Baal's power, such imagery walked a delicate line—and indeed, within a single generation Jeroboam would erect two golden calves and drag the entire northern kingdom into idolatry.

**Hiram the bronze worker in chapter 7 is not King Hiram of Tyre from chapter 5.** Sharing both the same name and the same Phoenician port city makes them easy to confuse. King Hiram was David's longtime ally who supplied cedar timber and loggers; Hiram in chapter 7 was a master metalworker brought in to direct the bronze casting for the temple.

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 7's layout with a sharper critical eye. In his view, inserting twelve verses about an opulent palace complex—requiring nearly twice as long to build and dwarfing the temple in size—right inside the description of God's house acts as a subtle warning.
