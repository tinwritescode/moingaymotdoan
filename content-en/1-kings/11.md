# 1 Kings 11 — Solomon's Heart Turns Away and the Kingdom Is Torn

## Story

No sooner has the most radiant chapter of Solomon's reign closed than the narrative plunges into sudden shadow. Alongside Pharaoh's daughter, King Solomon came to love many foreign women—Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites, Sidonians, and Hittites. They belonged to the very nations about which the LORD had warned Israel never to intermarry, because they would surely turn Israel's hearts after their own gods. Nevertheless, Solomon clung to them in love. He had seven hundred wives of royal birth and three hundred concubines, and his wives turned his heart astray (11:1–3).

As Solomon grew old, his foreign wives led his heart after other gods. His heart was no longer wholly devoted to the LORD his God as his father David's heart had been. He went after Ashtoreth, goddess of the Sidonians, and Milcom, the detestable idol of the Ammonites. Worse still, on the hill directly east of Jerusalem—in full view of the LORD's own temple—he built high places for Chemosh, the detestable god of Moab, and for Molech, the detestable god of the Ammonites. He did the same for all his foreign wives, so they could freely burn incense and sacrifice to their gods (11:4–8).

The LORD grew angry with Solomon because his heart had turned away from the God who had appeared to him twice and explicitly commanded him not to follow other gods. God delivered a sobering verdict: because Solomon had failed to keep His covenant and statutes, the LORD would surely tear the kingdom away from him and give it to one of his servants. Yet for the sake of his father David, God would not do it during Solomon's lifetime, but would tear it from the hand of his son. And for David's sake, as well as Jerusalem which He had chosen, God would not tear away the entire kingdom, but would leave one tribe for his son (11:9–13).

From that moment on, the long peace of Solomon's reign began to fracture as the LORD raised up adversaries on every side. The first was Hadad, of the royal blood of Edom. The narrative flashes back to David's campaign in Edom, when Commander Joab stayed behind for six months to bury the dead and cut down every male in Edom. Still a young boy at the time, Hadad fled with several of his father's Edomite servants through Midian and Paran into Egypt. Pharaoh welcomed him warmly, granting him a house, food, and land, and even marrying him to the sister of Queen Tahpenes. She bore him a son named Genubath, who grew up right inside Pharaoh's palace among the Egyptian princes. Yet the instant Hadad heard that David and Joab were dead, he asked Pharaoh for permission to return home. When Pharaoh pressed him on what he possibly lacked in Egypt, Hadad answered, "Nothing, yet please let me go"—and from then on he became a relentless thorn on Israel's southern flank (11:14–22).

A second adversary arose in the north: Rezon son of Eliada. Formerly an officer under King Hadadezer of Zobah, Rezon escaped when David routed Zobah's forces, gathered a band of followers, seized Damascus, and established himself there. Throughout Solomon's remaining years, alongside Hadad's hostility, Rezon ruled Aram and nursed bitter animosity toward Israel (11:23–25).

The greatest threat, however, sprang up inside Solomon's own administration: Jeroboam son of Nebat, an Ephraimite from Zeredah whose widowed mother was named Zeruah. Jeroboam was an energetic, capable young man; while Solomon was building the Millo and closing the gap in the wall of the City of David, the king noticed his talent and placed him in charge of the entire labor force of the house of Joseph. One day as Jeroboam walked out of Jerusalem, the prophet Ahijah of Shiloh met him on the open road. Ahijah was wearing a brand-new cloak; alone with Jeroboam in the countryside, the prophet took hold of his new cloak and tore it into twelve pieces (11:26–30).

Ahijah told Jeroboam to take ten pieces for himself and announced the word of the LORD: because Solomon and the people had forsaken Him to worship Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom, God would tear the kingdom from the hand of Solomon's son and give ten tribes to Jeroboam. Even so, for the sake of David and Jerusalem where He had placed His name, God would keep Solomon as ruler all his days and leave one tribe for his son so that David's house might always have "a lamp before Me" in Jerusalem. Then Ahijah offered Jeroboam a stunningly generous promise: if he would obey God's commands and walk in His ways as David had done, God would be with him and build him "an enduring dynasty" just like David's—while humbling David's descendants for this sin, "yet not forever" (11:31–39).

Somehow word leaked out, and Solomon tried to have Jeroboam killed. Jeroboam fled to Egypt, taking refuge with King Shishak until Solomon died. The text closes the life of Israel's wisest king in brisk, cool sentences: the rest of his deeds and wisdom are written in the Book of the Acts of Solomon; he reigned over all Israel in Jerusalem for forty years, rested with his fathers in the City of David, and was succeeded by his son Rehoboam (11:40–43).

## Key Takeaways

**Past wisdom and spiritual experiences never guarantee future faithfulness.** Solomon was no stranger to God: he had built the temple, offered one of the finest prayers in the Old Testament, and received two direct appearances from the LORD (11:9). Yet in his final years he erected pagan shrines right opposite God's house. The New Testament warning fits this scene hand in glove: let the one who thinks he stands take care lest he fall (1 Corinthians 10:12).

**Human hearts usually drift off course slowly through what they choose to love.** Scripture does not say Solomon woke up one morning hating the LORD and cast Him aside. The opening verses note that he "loved" and "clung to" many foreign women (11:1–2). What likely started as diplomatic alliances and personal affection gradually reshaped his loyalties over decades until, in old age, those unchecked attachments pulled his entire heart away from God.

**God's patience does not erase the consequences of sin.** Honoring His promise to David, God delayed the kingdom's split until Solomon's son took the throne (11:12). Yet the seeds of breakup were already sprouting while Solomon lived: northern and southern borders grew turbulent, and harsh forced labor at home bred simmering unrest under Jeroboam's watch. Those who sow compromise sometimes do not reap the full harvest themselves—their children inherit the storm.

**God's grace is both firm in judgment and open to fresh beginnings.** Even while pronouncing the tearing of the kingdom, God preserved "a lamp" for David's house in Jerusalem and promised their humbling would be "not forever" (11:36; 11:39). At the same time, He did not treat Jeroboam merely as a disposable rod to punish Solomon's line: He offered him a real opportunity to build "an enduring dynasty" if he walked in obedience (11:38). God stays true to old promises while opening the future to anyone willing to walk uprightly before Him.

## Deeper Meaning

**Solomon has now broken all three royal commands in the Law of Moses.** Chapter 10 closed with Solomon piling up mountains of gold and sending merchants back to Egypt to acquire horses (10:26–29). In 11:1–3, the final piece clicks into place: a thousand wives and concubines. Centuries earlier, the Law of Moses laid down exactly three prohibitions for Israel's king—do not multiply horses from Egypt, do not take many wives lest your heart turn astray, and do not accumulate excessive silver and gold (Deuteronomy 17:17; 7:3–4). The collapse in chapter 11 is no freak accident; it is the exact outcome Moses predicted long before.

**The torn cloak replays the tragedy of King Saul.** Seeing the prophet Ahijah rip a brand-new cloak into twelve pieces before Jeroboam (11:30–31) immediately revives an unforgettable scene from 1 Samuel: when King Saul disobeyed, Samuel's robe tore, and the prophet declared that the LORD had torn the kingdom of Israel from Saul that very day to give it to another (1 Samuel 15:27–28). The parallel cuts even deeper in 11:40: hearing that his throne will pass to another, Solomon tries to murder Jeroboam—acting just like Saul once hunted David. The son born to build a temple of peace ends his days walking straight down the dark path of Israel's rejected first king.

**Egypt reverses its role with sharp irony.** Early in his reign, Solomon married Pharaoh's daughter (3:1) and traded war horses with Egypt (10:28), assuming the mighty southern empire anchored his throne. By chapter 11, that very Egypt shelters and nurtures his two deadliest rivals: Hadad from the south and Jeroboam from the north (11:18; 11:40). The earthly political ally Solomon trusted turned out to be the nursery of his dynasty's undoing.

**The shrines east of Jerusalem leave a scar lasting more than three centuries.** The high places Solomon erected for Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Molech (11:7) did not disappear when he died. They stood blatantly on the southeastern ridge of the Mount of Olives for over three hundred years across dozens of Judean reigns, earning that hillside the grim name "the Mount of Corruption." Not until King Josiah—Judah's great reforming monarch near the close of the kingdom—were Solomon's pagan shrines finally smashed and defiled (2 Kings 23:13). One old man's indulgence left a spiritual stain spanning generations.

**The promised "lamp" and the words "not forever" keep messianic hope alive.** Even under the verdict of national division, God's covenant with David stayed intact: God had pledged that if David's sons sinned He would discipline them with human rods, yet never withdraw His steadfast love as He had from Saul (2 Samuel 7:12–16). That promise of "a lamp" in Jerusalem (11:36) and the assurance that David's line would not be humbled "forever" (11:39) form an unbroken thread stretching into the New Testament. In Matthew's opening genealogy, the names of Solomon and Rehoboam still stand in the lineage leading straight to Jesus (Matthew 1:6–7)—the one King whose heart never divides and who brings everlasting unity to God's people.

**Nehemiah holds up Solomon as a sober warning after the exile.** Centuries later, after Jewish exiles returned from Babylon to rebuild Jerusalem and drifted once more into faith-eroding marriages, Governor Nehemiah pointed straight back to chapter 11: among many nations there was no king like Solomon, loved by his God, "yet even he was led into sin by foreign women!" (Nehemiah 13:26). Solomon's downfall became an enduring cautionary mirror for the entire people.

## Notes & Nuances

**Joab's six-month slaughter of Edom's males (11:15–16) is a grim passage to encounter.** These verses look back to a ferocious military campaign from David's reign (2 Samuel 8:13–14), when Joab lingered in Edom six months wiping out every male. Scripture neither glamorizes nor celebrates this brutality; instead, the narrator records it here to explain the deep roots of Hadad's bitter vendetta. That massacre left behind a surviving child who escaped to Egypt, grew up nursing vengeance, and returned to harass David's son. Wartime cruelty in Scripture is always narrated candidly alongside its long, bitter aftermath.

**How should we understand seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (11:3)?** Even by ancient Near Eastern royal standards—where marrying neighboring princesses sealed treaties and signaled imperial reach—a harem of one thousand women was staggering. Some scholars view the figures as rounded totals spanning forty years of diplomatic marriages rather than ordinary domestic life, while others read them literally to highlight Solomon's extreme excess. Either way, Scripture's verdict is unmistakable: royal polygamy flaunting power broke the law for kings (Deuteronomy 17:17) and dragged Solomon directly into idolatry.

**Why does Scripture call Ashtoreth, Chemosh, Milcom, and Molech "detestable"?** Such sharp language does not spring from xenophobia, but from the practices tied to those cults. Ashtoreth was the Sidonian goddess of fertility and war, closely associated with ritualized temple sexuality. Chemosh was Moab's national deity, while Milcom and Molech (which many scholars view as closely related titles for the chief Ammonite god) were tied to child sacrifice by fire—an atrocity fiercely forbidden by the Law of Moses (Leviticus 18:21). Erecting shrines to such deities right across from the LORD's temple marked a breathtaking collapse in both faith and morals.

**Why does God grant ten tribes to Jeroboam and "one tribe" to Solomon's son—what happened to the twelfth tribe?** Readers often notice the arithmetic right away in 11:31–32. Two common explanations clarify the count. First, the Levites held no tribal land allotment of their own, and Simeon had long been absorbed inside southern Judah's territory, leaving ten northern tribes splitting away while Judah anchored the south (12:20). Second, when the split actually unfolded in the next chapter, tiny Benjamin bordering Jerusalem stood alongside Judah (12:21); tightly bound around the capital, the southern loyalist bloc is sometimes referred to concisely as "one tribe" over against the ten northern tribes.

**Did Solomon ever repent before he died?** First Kings remains starkly silent on the question—a heavy silence beside the warm praise consistently given to David. A longstanding tradition views the book of Ecclesiastes as an older Solomon's searching reflection after tasting every earthly pleasure, realizing all is vanity, and concluding with a call to fear God and keep His commands. First Kings itself, however, deliberately leaves the somber ending untouched so readers feel the full weight of its warning.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, narrates Solomon's downfall in stern tones. He stresses that a king who had surpassed every ruler in intellect and piety ruined his closing years by breaking Moses's law against marrying foreign women, indulging them until he bowed to alien gods. **The Babylonian Talmud**, the ancient rabbinic commentary, preserves a famous debate over 11:4–7. Rabbi Samuel bar Nahmani quotes Rabbi Jonathan making the startling claim that anyone who thinks Solomon personally worshipped idols is mistaken: rather than bowing to them himself, because he was king and tolerated his wives sacrificing without stopping them, Scripture counts the guilt against him as though he had built and worshipped them personally. Although other rabbis in the same discussion point to the plain gravity of the text, this tradition shows how seriously Jewish ethics views a leader's passive tolerance of evil—counting it equal to committing the evil directly. *(Babylonian Talmud, tractate Shabbat 56b)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with the famous discussion in Shabbat 56b defending Solomon while holding him accountable for tolerating his wives' idolatry, though not checked verbatim -->

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, views Solomon's tragedy through the lens of God's covenant with David. He observes that Solomon's glittering start followed by his collapse through infatuation with foreign wives proves that the promises of an eternal kingdom in 2 Samuel could never find their final resting place in the flesh-and-blood Solomon. God's discipline in tearing the kingdom while never withdrawing mercy from David's line shows that earthly kings are only temporary shadows pointing hearts forward to Christ, the sole flawless King. *(The City of God, Book 17)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Augustine's discussion of Solomon's fall and the Davidic promise looking beyond him in Book 17, though exact chapter numbers were not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, calls chapter 11 one of the saddest pages in Scripture—like a blazing morning sun sinking beneath dark storm clouds. No one commits huge apostasy in a single day, he observes: Solomon's slackness in his marriages steadily wore down his early wholehearted devotion until the wisest man on earth acted like the greatest fool in his declining years. Henry also treats the Bible's silence regarding Solomon's repentance as a solemn caution urging anyone standing firm never to grow overconfident. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 1 Kings 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Matthew Henry's standard reflections on Solomon's gradual slide and the chapter's closing silence, though not checked verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, highlights the deep irony behind the adversaries emerging in chapter 11. The Hebrew word translated "adversary" is *satan*—an opponent deliberately raised up by God to check a monarchy that has cut itself loose from its covenant roots. Most ironically of all, Brueggemann notes, the harder Solomon worked to construct an invincible Egyptian-style empire—built on forced labor and a vast royal harem—the more he turned himself into a new Pharaoh, until out of that very labor system rose Jeroboam to spark the kingdom's breakup. *(1 & 2 Kings, Smyth & Helwys Bible Commentary, 2000)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Brueggemann's analysis of the term *satan* for Solomon's adversaries and the irony of Solomon becoming a new Pharaoh in 1 & 2 Kings; exact page numbers not verified -->
