# James 2 — No Favoritism for the Rich, and Faith Joined with Works

## Story

James opens chapter 2 with a vivid scene straight out of a church gathering. Since believers hold faith in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ, they must never show favoritism based on outward appearances (2:1). He paints a picture everyone can recognize: a man wearing a gold ring and fine clothing walks into their assembly, and at the same time a poor man in shabby clothes walks in as well. If they rush to welcome the well-dressed visitor and offer him the best seat, while telling the poor man to stand over there or sit on the floor by their feet—have they not created class divisions among themselves and become judges with evil motives (2:2–4)?

He reminds them that God's way of viewing people flips the world's standards upside down: has not God chosen those who are poor in the eyes of the world to be rich in faith and to inherit the kingdom He promised to those who love Him? Yet they have dishonored the poor! Is it not the wealthy and powerful who exploit them, drag them into court, and blaspheme the noble name of Christ that they bear (2:5–7)?

If they genuinely fulfill the royal law of Christ's kingdom found in Scripture, "Love your neighbor as yourself," they are doing well. But the moment they show partiality, they commit sin and stand convicted by the law as transgressors. For the law is an indivisible whole: whoever keeps the entire law yet stumbles at one point is guilty of breaking all of it. The very same God who forbade adultery also forbade murder; you cannot boast that you avoid adultery while committing murder. So speak and act as those who will be judged by the law that gives freedom—remembering that judgment without mercy will be shown to anyone who has not been merciful, yet mercy triumphs over judgment (2:8–13).

From partiality that lacks compassion, James moves into the most famous and most debated section of the entire letter: the relationship between faith and works. What good is it if someone claims to have faith, yet has no deeds to show for it? Can that kind of faith save him (2:14)? He gives a piercing example: suppose a brother or sister is without clothes and daily food, and someone in the church offers a polite blessing—"Go in peace; keep warm and well fed"—without giving anything for their physical needs. What good are empty words? Faith is just like that: by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, it is dead (2:15–17).

James then engages an imaginary objector who tries to split faith and works into separate gifts: one person has faith, another has works. James issues an immediate challenge: show me your faith without deeds—an impossible task—and I will show you my faith by what I do (2:18). You pride yourself on believing that there is one God? Good—even the demons believe that exact truth, and shudder! Belief that stops at mental agreement saves no one (2:19–20).

To prove that genuine faith always comes to expression in action, James calls two witnesses from opposite ends of the Old Testament world. First is the patriarch Abraham, the revered father of Israel: when he obediently offered his son Isaac on the altar, was he not shown to be righteous by what he did? His faith and his actions worked together, and his faith was brought to completion by what he did—fulfilling the Scripture that says Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness, and he was called God's friend (2:21–23; Genesis 15:6; 22:9–12). From this James draws his conclusion: a person is justified by works and not by a bare, solitary faith (2:24).

The second witness stands at the far opposite margin of society: Rahab the prostitute of Jericho—a Gentile woman with a tarnished reputation. When she risked her life to welcome the Israelite spies and send them off by another route, was her faith not vindicated by that concrete act of obedience (2:25; Joshua 2:1–21)? James clinches his case with an unmistakable comparison: just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead (2:26).

## Key Takeaways

**Favoritism toward the rich in the church betrays the very gospel of Jesus.** If God does not look at a gold ring or fine clothes when choosing His children, the church has no right to import society's class hierarchy into worship. Loving our neighbor as ourselves demands equal dignity and honor for the poorest person who walks through the door (2:1–9).

**Mercy is the clearest proof that a person has genuinely received mercy.** People love to excuse themselves by pointing to the commands they keep well while brushing aside sins they consider minor, such as slighting the poor. James reminds us that God's law is a single whole, and on the day of judgment, a merciful life reveals a heart that truly knows God's grace (2:10–13).

**Faith that never becomes loving action is hollow talk—no better than the belief of demons.** James is not attacking faith; he is exposing counterfeit "faith" that lives only on the lips or in theory, offering polite wishes to a hungry brother without lifting a hand. Living faith naturally bears the fruit of deeds, just as a living body must breathe (2:14–20; 2:26).

**From the patriarch Abraham to the Gentile prostitute Rahab, true faith is always confirmed by obedience.** Worlds apart in heritage, gender, and social standing, both share one decisive trait: they did not merely claim to believe in God, they staked their lives on obeying Him (2:21–25).

## Deeper Meaning

**Reading James 2 alongside Romans 3–4 and Galatians 2 reveals two complementary perspectives rather than a contradiction.** In Romans and Galatians, Paul insists that a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law (Romans 3:28; Galatians 2:16), and he cites the very same verse about Abraham in Genesis 15:6 to show that Abraham was counted righteous long before he was circumcised (Romans 4:2–5; 4:9–10). Why, then, does James quote that exact verse from Genesis 15:6 (2:23) and yet write that "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (2:24)? Because the two authors are confronting different opponents and using key words with different nuances. Paul is fighting legalism that tries to keep Mosaic rituals in order to earn standing before God (Galatians 2:15–21); James is fighting complacency that hides behind the label of "faith" while living coldly without a single act of love (2:14–17). Paul speaks of the root of salvation—genuine faith apart from merit—while James speaks of the fruit that proves that faith is real, just as Paul himself speaks of "faith expressing itself through love" (Galatians 5:6).

**The royal law, "Love your neighbor as yourself" (2:8), reaches straight back to Leviticus and Jesus' own summary.** That command stands in Leviticus 19:18, right beside the prohibition against showing favoritism to either the poor or the powerful in judgment (Leviticus 19:15). Jesus called it the second greatest commandment summarizing all the Law and the Prophets (Matthew 22:39–40), and James calls it the "royal law"—the law of Christ the King.

**Calling Abraham "God's friend" (2:23) bridges Old Testament promise and gospel friendship.** That deeply intimate title appears in the Old Testament when King Jehoshaphat prays and when God speaks through Isaiah calling Abraham His friend (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8). In the New Testament, Jesus Himself tells His disciples that they are His friends if they do what He commands (John 15:14–15). Obeying God's word is not the drudgery of a slave, but the mark of friendship with God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Recognizing how James 2 uses key terms differently from Romans 3–4 and Galatians 2 clarifies the entire debate.** Comparing these three passages side by side reveals three core distinctions. First, regarding *faith*: in Romans 3–4 and Galatians 2, Paul speaks of living, wholehearted trust in Christ; in James 2:14–19, James is unmasking verbal "faith"—mere intellectual agreement that one God exists, the same assent demons possess (2:19). Second, regarding *works*: Paul rejects "works of the law" such as circumcision and ceremonial observance used to claim credit before God (Romans 3:20; 3:28; Galatians 2:16); James speaks of works of mercy and obedience—clothing the needy, obeying like Abraham and Rahab—that prove faith is alive. Third, regarding *justified*: Paul uses the term forensically—God declares the sinner righteous the moment they trust Him (Romans 4:5); James uses it in the sense of being vindicated or demonstrated as genuine—Abraham's offering of Isaac in Genesis 22, years after Genesis 15:6, proved that the earlier declaration of his righteousness was real (2:21–23). In the famous Reformation maxim: we are saved by faith alone, yet saving faith is never alone.

**Why did Martin Luther once call James an "epistle of straw"?** In his 1522 preface to the New Testament, amid a fierce battle against medieval doctrines of merit, Luther felt that James spoke less directly about the cross and sounded jarring in verse 2:24 next to Romans 3:28, so he called it "straw" in comparison to John's Gospel or Romans. Yet it is worth noting that Luther still kept James in his German Bible, praised it for containing many fine teachings about God's law, and dropped the "epistle of straw" remark from later editions.

**Does James hate the rich (2:5–7)?** Not at all. Back in chapter 1 he calls the wealthy believer a "brother" within the church (1:9–10). In verses 2:5–7 he is describing the concrete social reality in Palestine at the time, where most Jewish believers were poor laborers oppressed and dragged into court by wealthy landowners. He does not condemn wealth itself, but rather fawning over money and despising the poor inside the house of God.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo in the fourth and fifth centuries, was one of the first to harmonize Paul and James clearly. He explained that Paul speaks of works done *before* faith (no deed can earn our salvation), whereas James speaks of works that *follow* faith (the indispensable evidence of a saved life). By distinguishing the timing, the two apostles do not clash but complete each other. *(On Faith and Works, chapter 14; Eighty-Three Different Questions, question 76)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's distinction between works before faith in Paul and works following faith in James is a classic argument in his writings -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, unlike Luther's early hesitation, embraced the letter of James wholeheartedly and explained verses 2:21–24 with clarity. Paul addresses how a person is justified before God, whereas James addresses how a person's faith is demonstrated to be genuine before human eyes. Thus verse 2:24 in no way undermines justification by faith in Romans. *(Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, on James chapter 2 verses 14–26)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's defense of harmony between James 2 and Romans 3–4 through two senses of justification is a famous hallmark of his commentary -->

**Douglas J. Moo**, American New Testament scholar, points out that in verse 2:14 the Greek text places a definite article before "faith" in the second question—best understood as "Can *that* faith save him?" **Scot McKnight**, American New Testament scholar, emphasizes that reading James 2:1–13 seamlessly with 2:14–26 shows the issue is not an abstract theological dispute with Paul, but an urgent pastoral crisis inside the church: believers claiming to have faith while looking down on the poor and ignoring hungry neighbors right in their midst.
