# James 4 — The Root of Conflict, Humility Before God, and Tomorrow in His Hands

## Story

James opens chapter 4 with a question that cuts straight to the heart of church quarrels: where do all these conflicts and disputes among you come from? Do they not spring from your own selfish passions waging war within your members? You crave something and cannot get it, so you kill and covet; you fight and quarrel, yet still come up empty. You do not have because you do not ask God; and when you do ask, you do not receive because you ask with wrong motives, only to spend it on your own pleasures (4:1–3).

James's voice suddenly sharpens into the cadence of the Old Testament prophets: he addresses them as "adulteresses"—people committing spiritual infidelity against God. Do you not know that friendship with the world means enmity with God? Whoever chooses to side with the world's way of life makes himself an enemy of God. Do not imagine Scripture speaks in vain: the Spirit God caused to dwell in us yearns for us with deep, jealous love—and yet He gives us even more grace. That is why Scripture says, "God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble" (4:4–6; Proverbs 3:34).

From that promise of greater grace flows a rapid-fire series of concrete calls to repentance: submit yourselves to God, resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw near to God, and He will draw near to you. Wash your hands, you sinners, and purify your hearts, you double-minded! Grieve, mourn, and wail; change your careless laughter to weeping and your shallow joy to genuine sorrow. Humble yourselves before the Lord, and He Himself will lift you up (4:7–10).

Turning back to relationships inside the church, James warns against slandering and judging fellow believers. Anyone who speaks against a brother or judges him speaks against and judges God's law of love—and once you sit in judgment on the law, you are no longer keeping it. There is only one Lawgiver and Judge, the One who is able to save and to destroy; who are you to climb into His seat and pass judgment on your neighbor (4:11–12)?

At the end of the chapter, James addresses overconfident merchants within the congregation—people who map out their ventures with breezy certainty: today or tomorrow we will go to this or that city, spend a year there, carry on business, and make a handsome profit. James confronts them with a simple fact everyone easily forgets: you do not even know what tomorrow will bring! What is your life? You are merely a morning mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes. Instead of planning as though you own time itself, you ought to say, "If the Lord is willing, we will live and do this or that." Boasting in arrogant self-assurance is evil. And James closes with a razor-sharp ethical principle: anyone who knows the right thing to do, yet fails to do it, is guilty of sin (4:13–17).

## Key Takeaways

**Outer wars between people begin with an inner war of selfish cravings.** The moment pleasure, status, or personal gain takes center stage, other people turn into obstacles to resent and fight—and even prayer gets twisted into a tool for feeding our own ego (4:1–3).

**God's grace is always greater than our sin, yet it pours out only upon humble hearts.** The road back to God is not self-justification, but submission to Him, clean hands and hearts, honest grief over sin, and humility in His sight. Whenever we take a step toward God, He stands ready to draw near and lift us up (4:6–10).

**Slandering and judging a fellow believer usurps a throne reserved for God alone.** When we tear others down with gossip or harsh condemnation, we place ourselves above both God's law of love and our brother, forgetting that there is only one Judge over us all (4:11–12).

**Planning for tomorrow while leaving God out is a practical form of arrogance.** James does not forbid business enterprise or careful planning; he calls us to hold our plans with humble awareness that tomorrow's breath lies in God's hands, and never to put off doing good when we already know what needs to be done (4:13–17).

## Deeper Meaning

**Calling "friendship with the world" spiritual "adultery" (4:4) uses the exact covenant-marriage imagery of the Old Testament prophets.** In Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Hosea, God pictures His relationship with Israel as a faithful marriage; so when His people chase idols and pagan lifestyles, the prophets call it spiritual adultery (Jeremiah 3:20; Hosea 2:2; Ezekiel 16:32). God's jealousy in verse 4:5 is not petty insecurity, but the fierce devotion of a loving husband who refuses to share covenant love with rivals (cf. Exodus 20:5).

**The quotation "God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble" (4:6) comes straight from Proverbs.** Drawn from Proverbs 3:34 (following the Greek Septuagint), this exact line is also quoted verbatim by Peter in 1 Peter 5:5, and it echoes Jesus' words in the Gospels that whoever humbles himself will be exalted (Matthew 23:12; Luke 14:11). Across both Old and New Testaments, humility is the sole doorway into grace.

**The picture of human life vanishing like "a mist" (4:14) continues the reflections of Job, the Psalms, and Ecclesiastes.** The Old Testament repeatedly compares human life to smoke, a fleeting breath, or a lengthening evening shadow (Job 7:7; Psalms 39:5; 144:4), while Proverbs warns against boasting about tomorrow since we do not know what a day may bring (Proverbs 27:1). Jesus likewise told the parable of the rich fool who planned bigger barns for many years ahead without knowing his soul would be demanded that very night (Luke 12:16–21).

## Notes & Nuances

**When verse 4:2 says "you kill," does James mean literal murder was happening inside the church?** Most commentators understand James to be using forceful language in the spirit of Jesus' Sermon on the Mount: anyone who harbors hatred, bitterness, and malice aimed at tearing down a brother already carries murder in his heart (Matthew 5:21–22; 1 John 3:15). Some scholars also note that amid the volatile social unrest of first-century Judea, extremist violence was real, so James may be speaking both metaphorically and as a sober warning of where unchecked greed can ultimately lead.

**Verse 4:5 is one of the most challenging verses in the New Testament to translate.** The original Greek can be read in two main ways. The first (followed by the BSB and several other translations) takes the Holy Spirit as the subject: the Spirit whom God caused to dwell in us longs for us with jealous devotion, refusing to share our hearts with the world. The second takes the human spirit as the subject: the spirit God placed within us tends toward envy, to which verse 4:6 replies that God "gives us more grace" to overcome that bent. Both readings fit the flow of the passage: human hearts easily drift, yet God's grace is strong enough to win us back completely.

**"If the Lord is willing" (4:15) is not meant to be a mechanical catchphrase.** James is not requiring believers to tack those words onto every sentence like a magic formula. He is calling for an inward posture of the heart: making thoughtful plans while keeping open hands ready to welcome God's guidance and sovereignty (cf. Acts 18:21; 1 Corinthians 4:19).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, commented on verses 4:7–8 that successfully resisting the devil requires submitting to God first. Anyone who tries to battle temptation while remaining rebellious toward God is guaranteed to fail; once a person yields to God, His power makes the enemy flee. *(Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, on James chapter 4 verses 7–8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's emphasis on submitting to God before resisting the devil in James 4:7 is a clear point in his commentary -->

**Matthew Henry**, the early eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, paused thoughtfully over verse 4:17 ("Anyone, then, who knows the right thing to do, yet fails to do it, is guilty of sin"). He highlighted it as the sin of omission: many people count themselves innocent simply because they do not murder or steal, yet in God's sight, seeing good that needs doing and ignoring it or putting it off until tomorrow is genuine sin. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on James chapter 4 verse 17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Henry's treatment of sins of omission from James 4:17 is a familiar hallmark of his commentary -->

**Douglas J. Moo**, American New Testament scholar, regards verse 4:6 ("But He gives us more grace") as the theological high point of the entire letter of James. Although the epistle is packed with searching moral demands that expose our weakness, verse 4:6 assures us that God never issues commands and leaves us to fend for ourselves—He supplies abundant grace to everyone who humbles himself and asks. *(The Letter of James, PNTC, 2000, on chapter 4 verse 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: viewing James 4:6 as the grace-filled center sustaining the letter's ethical demands is a prominent observation in Moo's PNTC commentary -->

**Alec Motyer**, twentieth-century Irish commentator, pointed out that verses 4:13–16 expose a widespread form of practical atheism: these merchants never claimed to disbelieve in God; they simply went about planning and running their business affairs every day as though God did not exist. *(The Message of James, BST, 1985, on chapter 4 verses 13–17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: characterizing planning without reference to God in James 4:13–16 as practical atheism is a well-known remark in Motyer's BST volume -->
