# James 5 — Warning the Unjust Rich, Patient Waiting, and the Power of Prayer

## Story

The final chapter opens with a thunderous oracle straight out of the Old Testament prophets, aimed directly at unjust wealthy oppressors exploiting the poor. James tells them to weep and wail over the miseries coming upon them. Their hoarded wealth has rotted, moths have eaten their luxury clothes, and their gold and silver have corroded—and that very corrosion will stand as a witness against them and eat their flesh like fire. In the very last days they have gone on stockpiling treasure! The wages they fraudulently held back from the workers who mowed their fields are crying out, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of Hosts. They have lived in luxury and self-indulgence on earth, fattening their hearts for the day of slaughter; they have condemned and murdered the righteous person, who offered no resistance (5:1–6).

Immediately after denouncing the oppressors, James's tone softens warmly as he turns to comfort suffering believers: be patient, brothers, until the coming of the Lord. Look at the farmer, who waits patiently for the precious crop from the earth, enduring until it receives both the autumn and spring rains. They too must be patient and stand firm, because the Lord's coming is near. Do not grumble against one another, lest you be judged—look, the Judge is standing right at the door (5:7–9).

To strengthen their resolve, James holds up familiar biblical examples: take the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord as models of suffering and patience. We count as blessed those who have persevered. You have heard of Job's perseverance and have seen what the Lord finally brought about—for the Lord is full of compassion and mercy (5:10–11). Along the way he adds a crisp charge echoing Jesus: above all, do not swear—not by heaven, not by earth, not by anything else; let your "Yes" simply mean yes and your "No" mean no, so that you will not fall under judgment (5:12).

The closing section weaves prayer into every season of church life. Is anyone in trouble? Let him pray. Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praises. Is anyone seriously sick? Let him call the elders of the church to pray over him and anoint him with oil in the name of the Lord. The prayer offered in faith will restore the sick person, and the Lord will raise him up; if he has sinned, he will be forgiven (5:13–15).

From there James widens the call to the whole congregation: confess your sins to one another and pray for one another so that you may be healed, for the earnest prayer of a righteous person has great power and produces wonderful results. To prove that effective prayer is not the preserve of distant spiritual superhumans, he points to the prophet Elijah: he was a human being with a nature just like ours, yet when he prayed fervently that it would not rain, not a drop fell on the land for three and a half years; then he prayed again, and the heavens gave rain and the earth yielded its crops (5:16–18; 1 Kings 17:1; 18:42–45).

The letter ends abruptly yet tenderly—with no formal closing greeting, but a call to watch over one another in faith: my brothers, if anyone among you wanders from the truth and someone brings him back, let him know that whoever turns a sinner from the error of his way will save his soul from death and cover over a multitude of sins (5:19–20).

## Key Takeaways

**God hears the cry of exploited workers and will judge every injustice.** Wealth piled up by withholding fair pay from the poor never buys security; it becomes condemning evidence against its owners on the day of judgment (5:1–6).

**Patience in suffering is active expectation anchored in God's mercy and the Lord's return.** Like the farmer trusting the seasonal rains and like Job tasting the Lord's compassion beyond hardship, believers refuse to turn bitter against one another in distress and instead steady their hearts as they wait for Christ (5:7–11).

**Prayer is the natural heartbeat of the believing community in trouble, joy, and sickness alike.** The church is never a place to hide our fragility; it is a family where the sick invite elders to intercede and where brothers and sisters honestly confess their faults, uphold one another, and pray together (5:13–18).

**Bringing a wandering believer back to the truth is one of the greatest acts of love in the church.** The letter opened with faith refined through trials and closes with mutual care to keep one another from straying: no one walks the road of faith alone (5:19–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**The outcry of withheld wages (5:4) goes straight back to Old Testament laws protecting hired laborers.** The law of Moses strictly forbade holding a poor laborer's wages overnight, warning that the worker would cry out to the LORD and it would be counted as sin against the employer (Deuteronomy 24:14–15; Leviticus 19:13). James uses the majestic title "the Lord of Hosts" to declare that the Defender of exploited harvesters is the Sovereign Commander of all heaven's armies.

**The prohibition against oaths in verse 5:12 echoes Jesus' Sermon on the Mount almost word for word.** In Matthew 5:34–37, Jesus taught His disciples not to swear by heaven, earth, or Jerusalem, but simply to let their yes be yes and their no be no. Both Jesus and James aim at total integrity: a person who consistently tells the truth has no need to prop up their word with oaths.

**The examples of Job (5:11) and Elijah (5:17–18) connect ordinary church life to the sweeping narratives of the Old Testament.** Job demonstrates that the Lord is full of compassion and works a gracious purpose behind suffering (Job 42:10–17). And Elijah—who collapsed in exhaustion and fear wishing to die beneath a broom tree right after his triumph on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 19:3–4)—proves that the power of prayer does not rest in the superhuman perfection of the one praying, but in the God who listens.

## Notes & Nuances

**Is the denunciation in verses 5:1–6 aimed at wealthy church members or unbelieving landowners outside the church?** Most commentators note a sharp change in tone from how James warmly addresses believers as "brothers" elsewhere: in verses 5:1–6 he issues no call to repentance, only an announcement of impending doom (much like Old Testament prophetic oracles against the nations), and then in verse 5:7 he turns back to comfort oppressed believers ("Be patient, then, brothers"). Many scholars therefore see verses 5:1–6 as directed primarily at unbelieving wealthy landowners exploiting the church—while also serving as a sobering warning to any believer tempted to chase that same greedy lifestyle.

**What is the significance of "anointing him with oil" in verse 5:14?** Commentators explain this detail in two complementary ways. Some note that olive oil was the most common medicine in the ancient world—as when the Good Samaritan poured oil and wine on the wounded man (Luke 10:34)—suggesting a harmonious partnership of practical medical care and prayer. Many others emphasize that oil throughout Scripture symbolizes consecration to God and the presence of the Holy Spirit (cf. Mark 6:13); anointing with oil in the Lord's name alongside the elders' prayer visibly commits the sick person into God's powerful and merciful hands. Crucially, this is not a rite reserved for the dying, but a prayer asking God to heal and raise the sick person up (5:15).

**Does verse 5:15 guarantee that whenever someone prays with enough faith, every sick person will be healed immediately?** This verse should not be read in isolation from the rest of Scripture, where even Paul left Trophimus ill in Miletus (2 Timothy 4:20) and carried his own thorn in the flesh after pleading three times for its removal (2 Corinthians 12:7–9). Verse 5:15 encourages the church to trust God's genuine healing power as we pray in alignment with His will (cf. 1 John 5:14), while the Greek verbs for "save" and "raise up" also embrace ultimate spiritual restoration and resurrection.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, commented on verse 5:16 ("confess your sins to each other") to distinguish mutual confession among Christian brothers and sisters from the medieval requirement of mandatory private confession exclusively to a priest. **Matthew Henry**, the early eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, summed up verse 5:13 neatly: prayer and praise are the two remedies God provides for the two emotional extremes of life—in affliction do not sink into despair, pray; in gladness do not grow careless, sing praise to God.
