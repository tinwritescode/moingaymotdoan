# Titus 3 — Saved by Mercy and Devoted to Good Works

## Story

If chapter 2 addressed how believers behave inside the home and the church, chapter 3 widens the lens to how they live within a pagan society. Paul instructs Titus to remind the believers to be subject to rulers and authorities, to be obedient, and to be ready for every good work that benefits the community—speaking evil of no one, avoiding quarrels, and showing gentleness and complete courtesy toward everyone (3:1–2).

To keep believers from looking down on unbelieving neighbors, Paul turns their gaze back to their own past: once we too were foolish, disobedient, led astray, enslaved to various passions and pleasures, passing our days in malice and envy, hated by others and hating one another (3:3).

Then the words "But when" reverse the entire story. Not because we managed to fix ourselves, but when the kindness of God our Savior and His love for mankind appeared, He saved us—not by the righteous deeds we had done, but solely according to His mercy. He saved us through the washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit, whom He poured out on us generously through Jesus Christ our Savior. Justified by His grace, we have become heirs having the hope of eternal life (3:4–7).

Paul affirms that this is a trustworthy saying and wants Titus to insist on these truths clearly, so that those who have believed in God will devote themselves to good works—for these things are excellent and profitable for everyone (3:8).

Conversely, Titus must steer clear of foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law, because they are unprofitable and worthless. As for a divisive person in the church, after a first and second warning, have nothing more to do with them, knowing that such a person is warped and sinful, standing self-condemned (3:9–11).

The closing verses offer brief, warm logistical instructions. Once Paul sends Artemas or Tychicus to replace him on Crete, Titus is to do his best to join Paul at Nicopolis, where he plans to spend the winter. Paul asks the church to equip Zenas the lawyer and Apollos thoroughly for their journey so that they lack nothing. Seizing that practical occasion, he repeats his refrain one last time: our people must also learn to devote themselves to good works in order to meet urgent needs, so that their lives of faith will not become barren and unfruitful. Paul sends greetings from everyone with him and closes with a blessing of grace for the whole church (3:12–15).

## Key Takeaways

**Remembering that we were saved by mercy cures pride toward outsiders.** When believers remember that they were once just as foolish, selfish, and unlovable as the society around them, self-righteous judgment fades and gives way to patience and gentleness toward all people (3:1–3).

**Salvation is the unified work of the Triune God, never a reward for human merit.** In four concise verses, Paul displays the work of all three Persons: God the Father initiates salvation out of kindness and mercy, Jesus Christ is the Savior through whom it is poured out, and the Holy Spirit accomplishes inward rebirth and renewal (3:4–7).

**True grace never cancels good works; it produces them.** Titus sets two truths side by side: in verse 3:5, God saved us "not by the righteous deeds we had done," and by verse 3:8, those who have believed must "devote themselves to good works." Good deeds are not the root that earns salvation, but the natural fruit of a heart renewed by the Spirit (3:5–8; 3:14).

**The church must act decisively to protect its unity from chronically divisive people.** Paul does not encourage endless debate with troublemakers; after one or two sincere warnings, if a person remains stubbornly disruptive, the church must maintain firm distance to guard the peace of the whole body (3:9–11).

## Deeper Meaning

**"The washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit" (3:5) fulfills the new covenant promise of Ezekiel.** In the Old Testament, God promised a day when He would sprinkle clean water on His people to cleanse them from all impurity, give them a new heart, and put His Spirit within them so they would walk in His statutes (Ezekiel 36:25–27). Verses 3:5–6 show that grand promise coming alive in everyone who trusts in Jesus.

**The image of God pouring out the Spirit "generously" (3:6) recalls Joel's prophecy and Pentecost.** The prophet Joel foretold that God would pour out His Spirit on all flesh (Joel 2:28), and the apostle Peter proclaimed that promise fulfilled at Pentecost when the exalted Jesus poured out the Holy Spirit on the church (Acts 2:17; 2:33). God does not ration His Spirit drop by drop; He pours Him out in abundance.

**The contrast between our old nature (3:3) and new birth (3:5) echoes Jesus' conversation with Nicodemus.** Jesus told that Jewish teacher that unless someone is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God (John 3:5). Paul follows the exact same logic: polishing our exterior through religion is never enough; human beings need a brand-new beginning from the Holy Spirit.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does "the washing of new birth" (3:5) mean that water baptism automatically saves a person?** The word "washing" certainly evokes baptism, the outward sign marking a believer's entry into the church. Yet verse 3:5 immediately clarifies that rebirth and renewal are the work of the Holy Spirit grounded in God's mercy, not a mechanical effect of water itself. Most commentators understand baptism as the visible outward emblem of the inward spiritual cleansing that the Holy Spirit performs in the believer.

**What does the phrase "a divisive person"—traditionally rendered "a heretic"—in verse 3:10 mean?** The Greek word (*hairetikos*) in Paul's day did not yet refer solely to someone holding formal doctrinal heresy in the later church sense; primarily it described a factious person who foments divisions and party spirit, continuing the mention of quarrels and dissensions in the preceding verse (3:9). Paul's procedure is strikingly balanced: do not sever ties impulsively, but patiently warn the person once or twice; yet if they remain obstinate, do not let them keep tearing the church apart.

**What practical shape does the recurring phrase "good works" take in Titus?** Across three short chapters, this phrase appears six times (1:16; 2:7; 2:14; 3:1; 3:8; 3:14). Verse 3:14 gives a concrete picture: good works are not lofty abstractions, but readiness to meet urgent practical needs—just like supplying Zenas and Apollos on their missionary journey in verse 3:13.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, praised Paul for using the pronoun "we" in verse 3:3, including himself in the list of those who were once foolish and disobedient. By doing so, Paul dismantled every trace of smugness among the Cretan believers: if even the apostle remembers himself as a sinner saved by mercy, no one in the church has any right to despise a neighbor. *(Homilies on Titus, Homily 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's observation on Paul including himself in "we" at Titus 3:3 to humble believers is a familiar point in Homily 5 -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, viewed Titus 3:4–7 as a model summary of the Reformed gospel: verse 3:5 sweeps away all notions of human merit ("not by the righteous deeds we had done"), while verse 3:8 blocks any misunderstanding that trusting in grace makes believers lazy about doing good. *(Commentary on Titus, on chapter 3 verses 4–8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's reading of Titus 3:5 and 3:8 as twin pillars of saving grace and fruitful good works is a clear theme of his commentary -->

**John Stott**, the twentieth-century Anglican pastor and commentator, noted that verses 3:1–2 sketch an exemplary posture for Christians as citizens in a pluralistic society: respecting lawful authority while actively contributing to the common good with a gracious, non-combative spirit. The secret to sustaining that posture lies right in verses 3:3–7: never forgetting that we were saved solely by grace. *(The Message of 1 Timothy and Titus, BST, 1996, on Titus chapter 3 verses 1–8)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking gracious Christian citizenship in 3:1–2 with remembering saving grace in 3:3–7 is a clear argument in Stott's BST commentary -->
