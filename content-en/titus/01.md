# Titus 1 — Appointing Elders and Confronting False Teachers in Crete

## Story

Paul opens his letter to Titus with a stately, theologically rich greeting. He introduces himself as a servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ, sent for the faith of God's elect and their knowledge of the truth—the truth that leads to godliness. All of this rests on the hope of eternal life, which God, who cannot lie, promised before the beginning of time, and at the proper season brought to light through the preaching entrusted to Paul by the command of God our Savior. He writes to Titus, his true child in a common faith, blessing him with grace and peace (1:1–4).

He recalls why he left Titus on the island of Crete: to set right what remained unfinished and appoint elders in every town as Paul had directed him (1:5). The qualifications for elders closely parallel those in 1 Timothy. An elder must be blameless, faithful as the husband of one wife, with believing children who are not open to the charge of wildness or insubordination (1:6). In the very next verse, Paul refers to the same office as an overseer—God's steward—who must therefore be blameless: not arrogant, not quick-tempered, not given to drunkenness, not violent, not greedy for dishonest gain; instead he must be hospitable, a lover of good, sensible, upright, holy, and self-controlled (1:7–8).

The final qualification connects directly to the pressing crisis in Crete: he must hold firmly to the trustworthy message as it has been taught, so that he can both encourage the church with sound doctrine and refute those who oppose it (1:9).

Paul then describes those opponents: there are many rebellious people, especially among the circumcision party, full of empty talk and deception. They must be silenced, because for the sake of shameful profit they are teaching things they ought not to teach and upsetting entire households (1:10–11).

To depict a deep-seated vice on the island, Paul quotes a famous satirical line from one of Crete's own ancient poets (commonly identified as Epimenides of the sixth century BC): "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons." Paul agrees that this testimony is true, so he tells Titus to rebuke them sharply so that they may be sound in the faith, paying no attention to Jewish myths or to human commands invented by people who reject the truth (1:12–14).

Against the external taboos pushed by these teachers, Paul states a probing principle: to the pure, all things are pure; but to the defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure, because their very minds and consciences have been corrupted. They profess to know God with their lips, yet deny Him by their daily actions—detestable, disobedient, and unfit for any good work (1:15–16).

## Key Takeaways

**Gospel truth always goes hand in hand with a godly life.** Right in his opening verse, Paul defines truth as "the truth that leads to godliness" (1:1), and at the end of the chapter he exposes the false teachers because they claim to know God while denying Him by their works (1:16). Believing rightly while living corruptly is never genuine faith.

**A strong church begins with appointing leaders of proven character and solid doctrine.** Paul does not want the Cretan churches to depend permanently on Titus alone; he instructs him to appoint elders in every town. Such leaders must be models in home and everyday life while knowing the gospel thoroughly enough to nourish the flock and guard it against error (1:5–9).

**Sometimes loving the church requires firmness and plain speaking.** When people slip into homes for money and tear Christian families apart, softness is not gentleness—it is irresponsibility. Paul tells Titus to rebuke them sharply, yet the goal remains restorative: "so that they will be sound in the faith" (1:10–14).

**True purity lies in the heart and conscience, not in outward rules and taboos.** The false teachers piled up dietary restrictions and traditional regulations to present themselves as holy. Paul cuts straight to the root: once the gospel cleanses the heart, everything God created is pure; conversely, when the heart is corrupt, keeping endless external rules achieves nothing (1:15–16).

## Deeper Meaning

**The God "who cannot lie" (1:2) stands in sharp contrast to Crete's notorious reputation for lying.** In the Old Testament, Balaam had to confess that God is not a man that He should lie (Numbers 23:19), and Samuel declared that the Glory of Israel does not lie (1 Samuel 15:29). Paul sets God's truthful character in 1:2 directly opposite the Cretan habit of deceit in 1:12: the people of the truthful God must live differently from the culture around them.

**The principle "To the pure, all things are pure" (1:15) echoes Jesus' own teaching on cleanness.** When Pharisees faulted His disciples for eating without ceremonial handwashing, Jesus declared that nothing entering from outside defiles a person, but what comes out of an evil heart—and thereby declared all foods clean (Mark 7:15–19; compare Acts 10:15). Paul applies that very principle to sweep away man-made taboos in Crete.

**Using "elder" and "overseer" side by side for the same office (1:5–7) reflects the simple leadership structure of the early church.** In verse 5 Paul tells Titus to appoint "elders," and in verse 7 explains immediately, "Since an overseer is entrusted with God's work" (literally God's household steward). That picture of a trusted household manager echoes Jesus' parable of the faithful and wise steward set over the master's household (Luke 12:42).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does "having believing children" in verse 6 mean?** The Greek adjective here (*pista*) can mean either "believing" (Christian) or "faithful, trustworthy." Most translations (including the BSB) render it "believing," indicating that children living under an elder's roof are raised in the faith and are not living in wild rebellion. Some commentators prefer "faithful" or "obedient," noting that no parent can coerce inward saving faith in a child's heart, though parents are responsible for guiding the order and character of their home (compare 1 Timothy 3:4).

**Is Paul indulging in ethnic stereotyping when he quotes the criticism of Cretans in verse 12?** No. This was a famous ancient proverb written by a Cretan himself—the poet Epimenides—and ancient Greek even coined a verb meaning "to play the Cretan" for lying (partly because Cretan legend boasted of possessing the tomb of Zeus, whom Greeks believed to be immortal). Paul borrows a well-known bit of their own cultural self-criticism to address a real local pattern exploited by the false teachers, aiming to heal and strengthen them in faith (1:13), not to demean the worth of Cretan people.

**How does the false teaching in Crete compare with that in Ephesus?** Both places saw myths, genealogical debates, and ascetic taboos (compare 1 Timothy 1:4; 4:3; Titus 1:14; 3:9). In Crete, however, the conservative Jewish element stands out even more explicitly: verse 10 singles out "those of the circumcision group," and verse 14 warns against "Jewish myths."

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, calls attention to how seamlessly Paul moves from "elders" (1:5) to "overseer" (1:7) with no explanation. In his view, this shows that in the apostolic era both titles referred to the same local church office, before later centuries distinguished them into separate ranks. *(Homilies on Titus, Homily 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom explicitly notes the interchangeability of elder and overseer in Titus 1:5–7 in his second homily on Titus -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, remarks on 1:9 that a pastor needs two voices: a gentle voice to gather and feed the sheep with sound teaching, and a firm voice to drive away wolves by refuting false doctrine. He also observes that Paul's citation of a pagan poet in 1:12 proves that all useful truth—even when spoken by secular authors—ultimately stems from God's common grace. *(Commentary on Titus, on chapter 1 verses 9 and 12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the pastor's two voices in Titus 1:9 and Calvin's remark on truth in pagan authors at Titus 1:12 are two very well-known points in his commentary -->

**John Stott**, the twentieth-century Anglican pastor and commentator, observes that 1:1–4 is an unusually long greeting for such a brief letter because Paul wants to lay the cornerstone from the very first sentence: truth and morality can never be separated. Everything Titus is about to do in Crete—from appointing elders to rebuking false teachers—aims to reunite faith with godly living. *(The Message of 1 Timothy and Titus, BST, 1996, on Titus chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking truth and godliness in Titus 1:1 as the key to the whole letter is a clear thesis in Stott's BST commentary -->

**Philip H. Towner**, an American New Testament scholar, points out that 1:16 unmasks the fatal contradiction of nominal religion: claiming to know God with the mouth while denying Him with one's life.
