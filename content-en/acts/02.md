# Acts 2 — Pentecost

## Story

When the day of Pentecost arrived, the disciples were all gathered together in one place. Suddenly a sound came from heaven like the roar of a mighty rushing wind, filling the entire house where they were sitting. Tongues that looked like fire appeared to them, separating and resting on each one of them. Every one of them was filled with the Holy Spirit and began speaking in other languages, just as the Spirit enabled them to speak (2:1–4). Pentecost was a harvest festival celebrated fifty days after Passover. Both wind and fire were classic Old Testament signs of God's presence.

At that time devout Jews from every nation under heaven were staying in Jerusalem. Hearing the sound, a crowd gathered and was bewildered because each person heard the disciples speaking in their own native language. Amazed and perplexed, they asked, "Aren't all these people who are speaking Galileans? Then how is it that each of us hears them in the language of the land where we were born?" There were Parthians, Medes, and Elamites; residents of Mesopotamia, Judea, Cappadocia, Pontus, and Asia; people from Phrygia, Pamphylia, Egypt, and the regions of Libya near Cyrene; visitors from Rome, both Jews and converts to Judaism; Cretans and Arabs—all heard them declaring the mighty deeds of God in their own tongues (2:5–11). At the Tower of Babel, speech was scrambled and humanity was scattered. Here, many languages declare one gospel and draw people together.

Everyone was astonished and puzzled, asking one another what this could mean. Some, however, scoffed, "They are drunk on sweet new wine." Peter stood up with the eleven, raised his voice, and addressed the crowd: these people are not drunk as you suppose, since it is only the third hour—nine in the morning. Rather, this is what the prophet Joel spoke of: in the last days God will pour out his Spirit upon all people; sons and daughters will prophesy, young men will see visions, old men will dream dreams; even on his male and female servants he will pour out his Spirit; wonders will appear in the sky above and signs on the earth below before the great day of the Lord arrives; "And everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord will be saved" (2:12–21). Peter, who had denied his Master before a servant girl, now stands before an entire multitude and preaches.

Men of Israel, listen: Jesus of Nazareth was a man attested to you by God through mighty works, wonders, and signs performed through him in your midst, as you yourselves know. Handed over according to God's deliberate plan and foreknowledge, he was nailed to a cross and killed by you through the hands of lawless men. Yet God raised him up, loosing the agonizing cords of death, because it was impossible for death to keep its hold on him (2:22–24).

David spoke about him: I have kept the Lord continually before me; because he is at my right hand, I will not be shaken; therefore my heart rejoices, and my body too will rest in hope, "Because you will not abandon my soul to Hades, nor let your Holy One undergo decay." Brothers, the patriarch David died and was buried, and his tomb stands right here among us to this day. Being a prophet, however, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to seat one of his descendants on his throne, David foresaw and spoke of the resurrection of the Christ: he was not abandoned to Hades, nor did his body see decay (2:25–31).

This very Jesus God has raised to life, and we are all witnesses of it. Exalted to the right hand of God and having received the promised Holy Spirit from the Father, he has poured out what you now see and hear. For David did not ascend into heaven, yet he himself says: the Lord said to my Lord, sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet. "Therefore let the whole house of Israel know for certain that God has made this very Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ" (2:32–36).

Hearing this, they were pierced to the heart and asked Peter and the other apostles, "Brothers, what must we do?" Peter answered, "Repent, and let each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit." For the promise belongs to you, to your children, and to all who are far off—everyone whom the Lord our God will call. With many other words he bore witness and urged them: save yourselves from this crooked generation! All who accepted his message were baptized, and about three thousand people were added to their number that day (2:37–41).

They devoted themselves steadfastly to the apostles' teaching, to fellowship, to the breaking of bread, and to prayer. A deep awe came over everyone, for many wonders and signs were taking place through the apostles. All the believers stayed together and shared everything in common. They sold their land and possessions and distributed the proceeds to all, according to each person's need. Day after day they met together with one heart in the temple courts, broke bread in their homes, and ate together with glad and sincere hearts, praising God and enjoying the goodwill of all the people. And each day the Lord added to the church those who were being saved (2:42–47). Here is the very first portrait of the church: learning, sharing meals, praying, giving generously, and rejoicing.

## Key Takeaways

**The Holy Spirit descends and gives birth to the church.** On the exact day of Pentecost, the Father's promise comes true with the roar of wind, tongues like fire, and disciples speaking other languages to proclaim the mighty works of God (2:1–11). That power is not meant for spectacle, but to carry the gospel across linguistic barriers to every nation.

**Peter opens the Scriptures to demonstrate that Jesus is Lord and Christ.** Facing a crowd torn between amazement and mockery, the fisherman who once denied Jesus now steps forward boldly and interprets the event through Joel's prophecy and David's psalms: God has raised Jesus from the dead, exalted him to his right hand, and poured out the Spirit (2:14–36).

**Repentance opens the door into a new community.** When the cut-to-the-heart crowd asks what they must do, the answer is unmistakable: repent, be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and receive the Holy Spirit (2:37–39). Three thousand people respond in a single day, launching the early church with remarkable vitality (2:41).

**The early church's life rests on four steady pillars.** The new believers do not live as isolated individuals; they commit themselves to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer. That shared faith overflows into voluntary material generosity and a joy that wins the respect of the wider public (2:42–47).

## Deeper Meaning

**Pentecost reverses the division of the Tower of Babel.** In Genesis, human pride erected a tower to make a name for itself, and God confused human speech so that people scattered across the earth (Genesis 11:1–9). In Jerusalem, the Holy Spirit allows people from diverse nations to hear one shared message about God in their own mother tongues, reuniting humanity into a single people without erasing each group's distinct identity (2:5–11).

**Mount Sinai and the upper room of Pentecost mirror one another.** In Jewish tradition, Pentecost commemorated the giving of the law at Mount Sinai amid thunder, lightning, and fire; after the people sinned with the golden calf at the foot of the mountain, roughly three thousand people died (Exodus 19:16–19; 32:28). Now on that very feast day, the fire of the Spirit comes down and roughly three thousand people are brought to life, signaling the new covenant in which God's law is written on human hearts rather than tablets of stone (2:3; 2:41; Jeremiah 31:33).

**Moses' ancient longing comes to pass.** In the wilderness, when someone grew jealous because two elders prophesied, Moses wished that all the Lord's people were prophets and that God would place his Spirit on all of them (Numbers 11:29). The prophecy of Joel that Peter quotes—God pouring out his Spirit on all flesh, sons and daughters, old and young, male and female servants alike—shows that the Spirit is no longer reserved for a few select kings or prophets, but given to God's entire people (2:17–18; Joel 2:28–29).

**David's throne finds its ultimate fulfillment in the risen Christ.** God had sworn to David that one of his offspring would sit upon his throne forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Peter points out that David died and his tomb is still right there, proving that the psalm's words about the Holy One escaping decay and sitting at God's right hand point directly to the risen Jesus (2:25–35; Psalms 16:8–11; 110:1).

## Notes & Nuances

**The tongues in chapter 2 are real human languages.** Pilgrims from at least fifteen distinct regions around the Mediterranean world are stunned to hear Galileans speaking the exact dialects of their homelands (2:6–11). Many commentators note this distinction between the immediately intelligible miracle of languages at Pentecost and the spiritual gift of tongues requiring interpretation that Paul later addresses in Corinth (1 Corinthians 14:2–19).

**The phrase "in the last days" does not refer only to a distant apocalypse.** Quoting Joel, Peter applies this expression to the very era inaugurated by Jesus' resurrection and the outpouring of the Spirit (2:17). Throughout the New Testament, "the last days" span the entire period between Christ's first coming and his return (Hebrews 1:2).

**Holding everything in common in Jerusalem was voluntary, not the abolition of private property.** Verses 2:44–45 describe generous hearts willing to sell possessions whenever fellow believers faced need, rather than a compulsory system requiring everyone to surrender all ownership. Verse 2:46 notes that they still broke bread in private homes, and in chapter 5 Peter explicitly tells Ananias that his land and its sale price remained completely under his own control (5:4).

**The relationship between repentance, baptism, and receiving the Spirit in 2:38.** Christian traditions have discussed this verse closely. Some emphasize that baptism is directly linked with the forgiveness of sins, while others stress that repentance and faith are the essential core and baptism is their outward public expression. Reading further in Acts also shows that the Holy Spirit refuses to be boxed into a rigid sequence: in Samaria the Spirit falls after the laying on of hands (8:15–17), whereas in Cornelius' house the Spirit falls on Gentile listeners even before they are baptized (10:44–48).

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo, views Pentecost as the direct counterpart to Babel: pride multiplied languages to fracture humanity, while humility and the Holy Spirit use many languages to unite the church. He also explains that the miracle of speaking all tongues on that day was a prophetic sign that the church would spread worldwide and praise God in every language on earth. *(Sermons on Pentecost — Sermons 267 and 271)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the imagery of Pentecost reversing Babel and the church speaking every tongue is a familiar motif in Augustine's Pentecost sermons -->

**John Calvin**, expounding 2:23, insists on holding both truths together at once: Jesus' death belonged to God's foreordained plan, yet that fact in no way diminishes the guilt of those who crucified him. In Calvin's view, God exercises sovereign rule even over wicked human deeds to accomplish redemption, while people remain fully accountable for their own malicious intent. *(Commentary upon the Acts of the Apostles, on chapter 2 verse 23)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this pairing of divine sovereignty and human responsibility at 2:23 is a hallmark argument of Calvin's Acts commentary -->

Howard Marshall**, Scottish New Testament scholar, observes that Peter's sermon in chapter 2 preserves the core structure of early Christian preaching: presenting Jesus' earthly ministry, noting his crucifixion, proclaiming his resurrection and exaltation from the Old Testament Scriptures, and closing with a call to repent.
