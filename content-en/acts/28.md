# Acts 28 — Arrival in Rome

## Story

Once safely ashore, we discovered the island was called Malta. The local residents showed us unusual kindness: they kindled a blazing fire and welcomed all of us because it was raining and bitterly cold. Paul gathered an armful of brushwood and tossed it onto the fire, when a viper driven out by the heat fastened straight onto his hand. Seeing the snake dangling from his hand, the islanders remarked to one another, "This man must be a murderer! Though he escaped the sea, Justice has not allowed him to live." Yet Paul simply shook the snake off into the fire and suffered no harm at all. They stood watching for him to swell up or drop dead on the spot. After waiting a long time and seeing nothing unusual happen to him, they flipped completely and decided he must be a god (28:1–6). In Lystra people hailed him as a god and then stoned him; on Malta they brand him a murderer and then hail him as a god. Both times he is simply a human being kept safe by God.

Near that spot lay estates belonging to the island's chief official, named Publius, who welcomed us warmly and entertained us hospitably for three days. Publius' father was lying in bed suffering from fever and dysentery. Paul went in to see him, prayed, laid his hands on him, and healed him. After that, the rest of the sick people on the island came and were healed as well. They honored us in many ways, and when the time came to set sail, they stocked our ship with every supply we needed (28:7–10).

Three months later we put out to sea aboard an Alexandrian vessel that had wintered at the island, bearing the figurehead of the Twin Gods. Putting in at Syracuse, we stayed three days, then sailed around to Rhegium. The next day a south wind sprang up, carrying us in just two days to Puteoli. There we found believers who invited us to spend seven days with them. And so we came to Rome (28:11–14). Paul enters Italy in chains, yet even at his very first Italian port, the church is already waiting ahead of him.

The believers in Rome heard news of our arrival and traveled all the way out to the Forum of Appius and the Three Taverns to meet us. "At the sight of them, Paul thanked God and took fresh courage." Upon arriving in Rome, Paul was permitted to live by himself with a soldier assigned to guard him (28:15–16). Years earlier he had written to this very church longing to see them (Romans 1:11–12); now they walk a full day's journey just to welcome him.

Three days later Paul invited the local Jewish leaders to meet with him. Once gathered, he explained: brothers, although I have done nothing against our people or the customs of our ancestors, I was arrested in Jerusalem and handed over to the Romans. They examined me and wanted to release me because I was guilty of no offense deserving death. Yet when the Jews objected, I was forced to appeal to Caesar—not that I had any charge to bring against my own nation. That is why I asked to see and speak with you, for it is because of the hope of Israel that I wear this chain (28:17–20). Right to the very end, Paul goes to his Jewish brothers first.

They replied: we have received no letters from Judea concerning you, nor have any brothers arriving from there reported or spoken anything bad about you. Still, we want to hear what your views are, since we know people everywhere are speaking against this sect. Setting a date, they came in even larger numbers to the place where he was staying. From morning until evening he explained and testified about the kingdom of God, striving from Moses' law and the Prophets to persuade them about Jesus. Some were convinced by what he said, while others refused to believe (28:21–24).

Disagreeing among themselves, they began to leave after Paul delivered one closing word: the Holy Spirit spoke accurately to your ancestors through the prophet Isaiah when he said, "Go to this people and say: you will keep hearing yet never understand, and keep seeing yet never perceive; for this people's heart has grown calloused, their ears barely hear, and they have shut their eyes, lest they see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their hearts, and turn for me to heal them." "Therefore let it be known to you that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles, and they will listen!" Verse 29, noting that the Jews departed arguing heatedly among themselves, does not appear in the earliest Greek manuscripts (28:25–29; Isaiah 6:9–10). Jesus himself had quoted these exact lines from Isaiah when explaining why he spoke in parables (Matthew 13:14–15).

For two full years Paul remained in his own rented house, welcoming everyone who came to visit him, boldly proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ without hindrance (28:30–31). The book closes without telling whether Paul was acquitted or executed. The story stays open because the Spirit's work has never closed. Starting in Jerusalem with a small circle waiting in an upstairs room, the gospel now rings out right inside the world's capital from a prisoner's lips—completely unhindered.

## Key Takeaways

**Simple hospitality and God's healing power open doors for the gospel even on an unfamiliar shore.** Stepping drenched and shivering onto Malta, the shipwreck survivors meet extraordinary kindness from islanders who light a welcoming bonfire (28:1–2). Paul pitches straight in gathering firewood; surviving a venomous snakebite unharmed and praying to heal Publius' father and numerous islanders leaves an indelible witness of God's power and care across their three-month winter stay (28:3–10).

**Christian fellowship supplies encouragement that even the mightiest apostle needs.** Arriving on Italian soil as an escorted prisoner, Paul is refreshed by a week with believers in Puteoli, and then Rome's Christians walk dozens of kilometers out to the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns to greet him (28:13–15). Seeing their faces moves Paul to thank God and regain fresh heart—proving even spiritual giants lean deeply on warm brotherhood.

**Love for one's own people perseveres patiently even when responses stay divided.** Just three days after reaching Rome, Paul's first move is summoning local Jewish leaders, assuring them he bears no accusation against his own nation and wears chains solely for Israel's hope (28:17–20). From dawn to dusk he patiently opens Moses and the Prophets pointing to Jesus; some believe and others refuse, repeating a pattern seen across his whole journey (28:23–27).

**Messengers can be chained, yet nothing can shackle the word of God.** The closing two verses of Acts paint an unforgettable finale: across two entire years under house arrest in a rented Roman dwelling, Paul welcomes all visitors, announcing God's kingdom and teaching about Jesus Christ boldly and completely unhindered (28:30–31). The very final word in the Greek text of the whole book means "unhindered."

## Deeper Meaning

**The opening promise of Acts 1:8 completes its arc from Jerusalem to Rome.** Beginning in a small Jerusalem upper room with roughly a hundred and twenty waiting disciples (1:15), the gospel moves across Judea, Samaria, Antioch, Asia Minor, and Greece until it resounds freely right at the heart of the Roman Empire (28:30–31). Though Rome is not the geographic edge of the planet, from the crossroads of the world's roads the message radiates out to every nation.

**Isaiah 6:9–10 closes the book with both sorrow over hardened hearts and wide-open hope for the nations.** Paul cites the exact passage Jesus quoted in the Gospels (Matthew 13:14–15; Luke 8:10)—not declaring that God has permanently written off Israel, since verse 24 expressly notes that some Jews believed, but warning against spiritual numbness while announcing that God's salvation now streams freely to Gentiles (28:24–28; Romans 11:11–14).

**The open-ended finale of Acts invites every generation of the church to keep writing the story.** Luke deliberately leaves Paul's trial verdict untold because the true protagonist of Acts is neither Peter nor Paul, but the Holy Spirit and the gospel of Jesus Christ. Individual apostles finish their race, yet unhindered proclamation of God's kingdom keeps rolling forward through every era of believers.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Luke stop abruptly after two years of house arrest without recording Paul's trial or death?** Scholars highlight two main explanations. First, timing: many conclude Luke finished writing Acts right around AD 62, as Paul's two years of custody concluded and before Nero had issued any verdict. Second, literary design: Luke's stated goal from 1:8 was tracing the gospel's unhindered arrival in Rome; once 28:31 reaches that triumphant milestone, the narrative achieves its exact climax, making Paul's later personal fate beside the point.

**Which letters did Paul compose during these two years of Roman house arrest (28:30)?** Church tradition and most scholars place the four Prison Epistles—Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, and Philemon—during this two-year period in Paul's rented Roman quarters (Philippians 1:12–14; Ephesians 6:20). Those two seemingly restricted years ended up bearing spiritual fruit that has nourished the church for two millennia.

**Verse 29 is absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts.** That line adds that after Paul's closing words, the Jews left arguing vigorously among themselves; many modern translations place it in a footnote because the oldest manuscripts move directly from verse 28 to verse 30.

**The islanders' whiplash reaction on Malta (28:4–6) mirrors timeless human fickleness.** Seeing a viper clamped onto Paul's hand, they instantly brand him a murderer hunted down by divine Justice; minutes later, when he neither swells nor collapses, they swing straight over to calling him a god (28:4–6). Echoing Lystra's swing from worship to stoning (14:11–19), it offers a vivid reminder never to build our identity on the shifting applause or blame of crowds.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, treats Paul's citation of Isaiah 6:9–10 in 28:26–27 as a sober warning to anyone who hears God's word repeatedly yet keeps brushing it aside. **F. F. Bruce**, twentieth-century Scottish New Testament scholar, highlights the deep resonance of the very last word of Acts in Greek at 28:31—*akōlytōs*, meaning "unhindered." Ending the entire volume on that single triumphant adverb declares Luke's overarching message: from Jerusalem to Rome, through mobs, courts, and shipwrecks, nothing stops the gospel of Jesus. *(The Book of the Acts, NICNT, on chapter 28)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: highlighting the closing Greek adverb akōlytōs in 28:31 is one of the most celebrated insights in Bruce's commentary -->

**John Stott**, twentieth-century Anglican pastor and scholar, views the open-ended finish of Acts as a direct summons reminding readers that the story never actually ended.
