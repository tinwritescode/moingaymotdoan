# Acts 13 — The First Missionary Journey

## Story

The church at Antioch had prophets and teachers: Barnabas, Simeon called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen—who had been brought up with Herod the tetrarch—and Saul. It was a remarkably diverse circle, spanning Africa and royal palace courts. While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them." After fasting and praying, they placed their hands on them and sent them off (13:1–3).

Sent out by the Holy Spirit, the two went down to Seleucia and sailed from there to Cyprus, Barnabas' homeland. Arriving at Salamis, they proclaimed God's word in the Jewish synagogues, with John Mark along as their helper. Traveling across the entire island to Paphos, they encountered a Jewish sorcerer and false prophet named Bar-Jesus. He was an attendant of the proconsul Sergius Paulus, an intelligent man who summoned Barnabas and Saul because he wanted to hear God's word. Yet Elymas—meaning sorcerer—opposed them and tried to turn the proconsul away from the faith (13:4–8).

Then Saul, who was also called Paul, filled with the Holy Spirit, looked straight at him and declared: you son of the devil, full of every kind of deceit and villainy, enemy of all righteousness, will you never stop twisting the straight paths of the Lord? Now look—the hand of the Lord is against you; you will go blind and will not see the sun for a time. Immediately mist and darkness fell over him, and he groped around looking for someone to lead him by the hand. Seeing what had happened, the proconsul believed, astonished at the teaching about the Lord (13:9–12). From here on the book calls him Paul, and his name begins taking the lead before Barnabas. The man struck blind on the road to Damascus now pronounces temporary blindness on an opponent of the gospel.

Paul and his companions sailed from Paphos to Perga in Pamphylia, where John Mark left them and went back to Jerusalem. Pushing inland, they came to Pisidian Antioch. On the Sabbath they entered the synagogue and took seats. Following the reading of the Law and the Prophets, the synagogue rulers sent word to them: brothers, if you have any word of encouragement for the people, please speak (13:13–15). Mark's departure here will later spark a sharp split between Paul and Barnabas (15:37–39).

Standing up and motioning with his hand, Paul addressed the Israelites and God-fearing Gentiles alike. He retraced Israel's story: God chose our ancestors, multiplied them in Egypt, brought them out with an uplifted arm, put up with their ways for roughly forty years in the desert, displaced seven nations in Canaan to give them the land, and appointed judges until the time of Samuel. When the people demanded a king, God gave them Saul son of Kish; afterward God removed Saul and raised up David, of whom he testified, "I have found David son of Jesse, a man after my own heart." From David's descendants, just as he promised, God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus. Ahead of his arrival, John preached a baptism of repentance and declared himself unworthy even to untie the sandals of the One coming after him (13:16–25).

Brothers, this message of salvation has been sent to us. The residents of Jerusalem and their rulers failed to recognize Jesus, and in condemning him they actually fulfilled the very words of the prophets read aloud every Sabbath. Although finding no grounds for a death sentence, they pressed Pilate to execute him. After completing everything written about him, they took him down from the tree and laid him in a tomb. "Yet God raised him from the dead." For many days he appeared to those who had traveled up with him from Galilee to Jerusalem, who are now his witnesses before the people (13:26–31).

We bring you good news: the promise made to our ancestors God has fulfilled for us, their children, by raising Jesus, just as it stands written in the second psalm, "You are my Son; today I have become your Father." God raised him never again to return to decay, echoing the promise of steadfast blessings to David and the psalm's words: you will not allow your Holy One to see decay. David, after serving God's purpose in his own generation, fell asleep, was buried with his ancestors, and underwent decay; yet the One God raised never experienced decay (13:32–37; Psalms 2:7; Isaiah 55:3; Psalms 16:10).

Therefore, brothers, let it be known to you that through this Jesus forgiveness of sins is proclaimed to you, and through him everyone who believes is justified from everything from which Moses' law could never justify you. Watch out, then, lest what the prophets spoke overtake you: look, you scoffers, marvel and perish, for in your days I am doing a work you would never believe even if someone told you (13:38–41; Habakkuk 1:5). This marks the first time Acts records the word "justified" on Paul's lips—the exact concept he will unfold across Romans and Galatians.

As they walked out, the people begged them to speak more on these things the next Sabbath. Once the synagogue dismissed, many Jews and devout converts followed Paul and Barnabas, who urged them to remain grounded in God's grace. The following Sabbath nearly the entire city turned out to hear the word of the Lord. Seeing the massive crowds, the Jewish leaders were consumed with jealousy, contradicting Paul's words and hurling abuse at him (13:42–45).

Paul and Barnabas spoke out boldly: God's word had to be proclaimed to you first; yet since you push it aside and judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life, watch—we now turn to the Gentiles. For so the Lord has commanded us: "I have appointed you as a light for the Gentiles, that you may bring salvation to the ends of the earth." Hearing that, the Gentiles rejoiced and honored the Lord's word, and all who were appointed for eternal life believed. The word of the Lord spread across the whole region (13:46–49; Isaiah 49:6).

Yet the Jewish leaders incited prominent God-fearing women and leading men of the city, stirring up persecution against Paul and Barnabas and driving them out of their district. Shaking the dust off their feet as a protest against them, the two moved on to Iconium. And the disciples left behind were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit (13:50–52; Luke 9:5). The messengers are driven out, yet the joy stays behind.

## Key Takeaways

**Global mission springs from a worshiping, fasting congregation.** While Antioch's wonderfully diverse leadership team waits on God in worship and fasting, the Holy Spirit personally singles out Barnabas and Saul for the work ahead (13:1–3). From this point onward, the gospel enters a new phase: no longer propelled merely by refugees fleeing persecution, but intentionally sent into major centers of the Roman Empire.

**Jesus accomplishes what the law of Moses could never accomplish.** In Paul's first full recorded sermon at the synagogue of Pisidian Antioch, he sweeps from the Exodus through King David to the risen Messiah: through trusting Jesus, people receive forgiveness of sins and are justified from everything Moses' law could never justify them from (13:16–39).

**Rejection by some swings the doorway wider for Gentiles.** When jealous synagogue leaders attack the message, Paul and Barnabas turn to Isaiah's prophecy and announce their pivot toward the Gentiles—who embrace the good news with overflowing joy (13:45–49). Even after both missionaries are expelled from the city, the young disciples stay filled with joy and the Holy Spirit (13:50–52).

## Deeper Meaning

**Isaiah's Servant mission becomes the church's own calling.** In 13:47, Paul quotes God's charge to the Servant in Isaiah—to shine as a light for the nations and carry salvation to the ends of the earth (Isaiah 49:6). What was first spoken of the Messiah now extends through his messengers to the Gentile world.

**Three Old Testament passages harmonize to verify Christ's resurrection.** To show that Jesus' resurrection was never an unexpected plot twist but the core of Israel's promises, Paul weaves together three key Scriptures: Psalm 2 declaring the enthroned Son of God (13:33; Psalms 2:7), Isaiah's pledge of sure mercies guaranteed to David (13:34; Isaiah 55:3), and Psalm 16 promising that God's Holy One would never see decay (13:35; Psalms 16:10).

**Habakkuk's warning cautions against despising God's surprising work.** Closing his synagogue sermon, Paul cites Habakkuk's sober warning to ancient Judah when they refused to believe God was doing something beyond their imagination (13:40–41; Habakkuk 1:5). Salvation through a crucified Savior offered freely to Gentiles is that astonishing work; whoever scoffs forfeits the very blessing held out to them.

**The theological seed of Romans and Galatians is already fully present on the first journey.** Verses 13:38–39—justification by faith where the law of Moses falls short—prove that Paul's central doctrine was no late theological invention, but the beating heart of his preaching from his very first visit into the Galatian region (Galatians 2:16; Romans 3:20–24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Luke start calling Saul "Paul" at 13:9?** Many readers assume God renamed Saul to Paul at his conversion on the Damascus road, yet Luke waits all the way until chapter 13 to note "Saul, who was also called Paul." As a Roman citizen of Jewish birth, he carried both names from childhood: his Hebrew name Saul within Jewish circles and his Roman cognomen Paulus across the Greco-Roman world. Once he steps into his Gentile mission and stands before a Roman proconsul named Sergius Paulus, Luke naturally adopts his Roman name.

**Was striking Elymas the sorcerer blind (13:9–11) overly harsh?** Compared with the healing miracles filling Acts, an apostle pronouncing physical affliction is rare indeed. Three points help keep it in perspective: Elymas was actively trying to block the proconsul from hearing the truth, Paul spoke under the direct impulse of the Holy Spirit (13:9), and the blindness was only temporary (13:11)—much like the three days of blindness that once brought Saul himself to his senses outside Damascus (9:8–9).

**How is 13:48—"all who were appointed for eternal life believed"—understood?** This verse often stands at the center of conversations about predestination and human freedom. Reformed commentators emphasize that "appointed" affirms God's sovereign initiative in saving sinners. Meanwhile, Wesleyan and Arminian scholars observe that the Greek verb form can also bear the sense "disposed toward" or "aligned with" eternal life, forming a direct contrast with 13:46 where the opponents "judge themselves unworthy of eternal life."

**What did shaking dust off one's feet (13:51) signify?** Observant Jews returning home from foreign lands traditionally shook Gentile dust off their sandals to avoid bringing ritual impurity onto Israel's soil. Jesus himself instructed his disciples to shake the dust from their feet when leaving towns that refused the message of peace (Luke 9:5; 10:11). Performing that gesture against opposing Jewish leaders in Pisidian Antioch declared that Paul and Barnabas had discharged their duty faithfully and no longer bore responsibility for the leaders' rejection.
