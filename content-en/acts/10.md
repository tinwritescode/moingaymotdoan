# Acts 10 — Peter and Cornelius

## Story

In Caesarea lived a man named Cornelius, a centurion in what was called the Italian Regiment. Though a Gentile, he and his whole household revered God; he gave generously to people in need and prayed constantly. One afternoon around three o'clock, he clearly saw in a vision an angel of God walking in and calling him by name. Staring at him in alarm, Cornelius asked, "What is it, Lord?" The angel answered, "Your prayers and your gifts to the poor have risen as a memorial offering before God." Send men to Joppa to summon Simon, who is called Peter; he is staying with Simon the tanner, whose house sits by the sea. Once the angel departed, Cornelius called two of his household servants and a devout soldier from his staff, explained everything to them, and dispatched them to Joppa (10:1–8).

The next day around noon, while those messengers were nearing the city, Peter climbed onto the rooftop to pray. Feeling hungry, he wanted something to eat, and while the meal was being prepared, he fell into a trance. He saw heaven opened and something resembling a large sheet lowered to the ground by its four corners, filled with every kind of four-footed animal, reptile, and bird of the air. A voice told him, "Get up, Peter, kill and eat." Peter protested, "Never, Lord! I have never eaten anything impure or unclean." The voice spoke a second time: "Do not treat as unclean what God has made clean." This happened three times, and immediately the sheet was drawn back up into heaven (10:9–16). Three times—echoing Peter's three denials and Jesus' three questions asking whether Peter loved him.

While Peter was still puzzling over what the vision meant, the messengers sent by Cornelius located Simon's house and stood outside the gate asking whether Peter was lodging there. The Spirit spoke to Peter: three men are looking for you; get up, go downstairs, and accompany them without hesitation, for I myself have sent them. Going down to meet them, Peter said, "I am the man you are looking for; why have you come?" They answered: Centurion Cornelius—an upright, God-fearing man respected by the entire Jewish nation—was instructed by a holy angel to invite you to his home so he can hear what you have to say. Peter invited them inside and put them up for the night (10:17–23). A Jew welcoming Gentiles under his own roof: the lesson has already begun.

The following morning Peter set out with them, accompanied by several believers from Joppa. The next day they arrived in Caesarea, where Cornelius was waiting with his relatives and close friends gathered together. As Peter stepped inside, Cornelius met him, dropped to his feet, and bowed low in reverence. Peter lifted him up, saying, "Stand up—I am only a human being myself." Chatting with him, Peter walked into the room and found a large crowd assembled (10:23–27).

Peter spoke to them: you know very well that it is unlawful for a Jew to associate closely with a Gentile or enter a Gentile home; yet God has shown me that I must not call any human being impure or unclean. That is why I came without raising any objection when you sent for me. May I ask why you sent for me? Cornelius recounted his vision from four days earlier and concluded: so here we all stand in God's presence right now, eager to hear everything the Lord has commanded you to tell us (10:28–33). Peter has grasped the point: the vision was never only about food—it was about people.

Opening his mouth, Peter declared, "Truly I now understand that God shows no partiality," but in every nation whoever fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him. He shared the message of peace through Jesus Christ, who is Lord of all: beginning in Galilee after the baptism John proclaimed, God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and power, and he went around doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, because God was with him (10:34–38).

We are witnesses of everything he did across Judea and in Jerusalem. They killed him by hanging him on a tree, yet on the third day God raised him up and caused him to appear—not to all the people, but to witnesses God had chosen beforehand, namely us, who ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead. He commanded us to preach to the people and testify that he is the One God appointed as judge of the living and the dead. All the prophets bear witness about him that everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name (10:39–43).

While Peter was still speaking those words, the Holy Spirit fell upon everyone listening to the message. The circumcised Jewish believers who had accompanied Peter were astonished that the gift of the Holy Spirit was poured out even upon Gentiles, for they heard them speaking in tongues and praising God. Peter asked, "Can anyone withhold water from baptizing these people, who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?" So he ordered them to be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ. Afterward they urged him to stay with them for several days (10:44–48). It is nothing less than a Gentile Pentecost. God waits neither for Peter to finish his sermon nor for anyone to lay on hands: he swings the door open himself.

## Key Takeaways

**God prepares both messenger and listener to tear down dividing walls.** Cornelius is not the only one who needs to hear the gospel; Peter himself needs a deep shift of heart to step past a lifetime of barriers separating Jews from Gentiles (10:1–23). The rooftop vision of the sheet filled with unclean creatures—repeated three times—teaches him never to label unclean anyone whom God has cleansed (10:15; 10:28).

**God plays no ethnic favorites.** Peter's concise sermon in Cornelius' home sums up the entire gospel: Jesus is Lord of all, crucified and risen, appointed judge of the living and the dead, and everyone who puts faith in him receives forgiveness through his name (10:34–43).

**The Gentile Pentecost breaks out mid-sermon.** Before Peter even finishes talking, lays hands on anyone, or calls for baptism, the Holy Spirit descends straight onto Cornelius' household just as he had on the apostles at Pentecost (10:44–47). God puts his own unmistakable stamp of acceptance on Gentiles without requiring them to be circumcised or adopt Jewish identity first.

## Deeper Meaning

**The clean-and-unclean food laws of Moses have fulfilled their historical purpose.** Under the Old Testament, dietary distinctions separated Israel from surrounding idol-worshiping nations (Leviticus 11:1–47; 20:24–26). Now that the Messiah has arrived and Jesus has already declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19), God dismantles that ceremonial barrier so Jews and Gentiles can share one table inside one family (10:15; Ephesians 2:14–16).

**The mission from Joppa reverses the story of Jonah.** Strikingly, Peter receives his summons to go to Gentiles right in the port city of Joppa—the exact harbor where the prophet Jonah once boarded a ship fleeing God's command to preach to Gentiles in Nineveh (10:5–6; Jonah 1:3). Jonah dreaded seeing God show mercy to outsiders; Peter, after a moment's hesitation, obeys and walks straight into a Roman officer's house proclaiming forgiveness.

**God's impartiality runs straight through Scripture from the Old Testament onward.** When Peter exclaims that God shows no partiality (10:34), he echoes Moses' declaration about the Lord in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 10:17). Paul will later build the argument of Romans on that exact foundation: every human being needs grace equally and is welcomed through faith equally (Romans 2:11; 3:22–24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Does 10:35 mean anyone who lives decently in any religion is automatically saved?** Some readers lift "in every nation whoever fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him" out of context to suggest all religions lead to the same place. Yet reading the full account makes the opposite plain: Cornelius—already deeply devout, generous, and prayerful (10:2)—is still told by an angel to send for Peter so he can hear words by which he and his household will be saved (11:14), and Peter still preaches Christ's death and resurrection for forgiveness of sins through faith in his name (10:39–43). Verse 10:35 means God never shuts anyone out on ethnic grounds, welcoming every seeker from every nation into salvation in Christ.

**Who were "God-fearers" like Cornelius?** Across the first-century Greco-Roman world, many Gentiles grew disillusioned with pagan polytheism and were drawn to Judaism's worship of one God and high moral standards. They attended synagogues, prayed to Israel's God, and gave generously to the poor, yet stopped short of circumcision and full conversion to Judaism. This circle later proved the most receptive audience for the gospel across Paul's missionary journeys.

**Cornelius bowing down and Peter's refusal (10:25–26).** When Cornelius collapses at Peter's feet in homage, the apostle immediately pulls him up, insisting he is merely human. That response contrasts sharply with Jesus, who repeatedly accepted worship without correcting those who bowed before him (Matthew 14:33; John 9:38), highlighting the unmistakable boundary between mere humans and God.

**John Chrysostom** observes that in Acts 10 God patiently instructs Peter through a vision repeated three times because uprooting deeply ingrained habits and prejudices in devout hearts is never easy. **John Calvin** examines 10:35 closely to prevent readers from concluding that good deeds earn salvation: Cornelius' reverence and charity were not merits purchasing salvation, but evidence that God had already begun drawing his heart by grace in order to lead him to Christ through Peter's preaching. *(Commentary upon the Acts of the Apostles, on chapter 10 verses 34–35)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's reading of Cornelius' piety in 10:34–35 as preparatory grace rather than works-salvation is well known -->

Bruce** considers chapter 10 the most revolutionary turning point in early church history: without the breakthrough in Cornelius' house, Christianity might easily have remained an inward-looking sect inside Judaism.
