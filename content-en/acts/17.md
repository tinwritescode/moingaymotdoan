# Acts 17 — Thessalonica, Berea, and Athens

## Story

Passing through Amphipolis and Apollonia, Paul and Silas arrived in Thessalonica, where there was a Jewish synagogue. Following his custom, Paul went inside and for three Sabbaths reasoned with them from the Scriptures, explaining and proving that the Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead—and that this Jesus whom he was proclaiming is the Christ. Some of the Jews were persuaded and joined Paul and Silas, along with a large number of God-fearing Greeks and quite a few prominent women (17:1–4).

Jealous over this, some Jews rounded up bad characters from the marketplace, formed a mob, and threw the city into an uproar. They rushed Jason's house searching for Paul and Silas in order to drag them out before the crowd. Failing to find them, they hauled Jason and several believers before the city officials shouting, "These men who have turned the world upside down have come here too, and Jason has welcomed them into his home!" All of them defy Caesar's decrees, claiming there is another king named Jesus. Hearing this, both the crowd and the city officials grew alarmed. They took security money from Jason and the others and released them (17:5–9). Their enemies spoke truer than they knew: the gospel really is turning the world upside down, and there truly is another King.

As soon as night fell, the believers sent Paul and Silas away to Berea. Arriving there, they went straight into the Jewish synagogue. Now the Bereans were more noble-minded than those in Thessalonica: they welcomed the message with great eagerness and "examined the Scriptures every day to see whether these things were true." As a result, many of them believed, including a fair number of prominent Greek women and men (17:10–12). The Bereans are praised not for gullibly nodding along, but for combining an open heart with diligent verification.

When the Jews in Thessalonica learned that Paul was preaching God's word in Berea too, they went there as well, agitating and stirring up the crowds. Immediately the brothers sent Paul off toward the coast, while Silas and Timothy remained behind. Those escorting Paul took him all the way to Athens, returning with instructions for Silas and Timothy to join him as soon as possible (17:13–15).

While waiting for them in Athens, Paul's spirit was deeply distressed to see the city full of idols. He reasoned in the synagogue with the Jews and God-fearing Gentiles, and day after day in the marketplace with anyone who happened to be there. Some Epicurean and Stoic philosophers began debating with him. Some sneered, "What is this babbler trying to say?" Others remarked, "He seems to be advocating foreign deities," because he was preaching the good news about Jesus and the resurrection. They brought him to the Areopagus and asked, "May we learn what this new teaching is that you are presenting? You bring strange ideas to our ears, and we want to know what they mean." For all the Athenians and the foreigners living there spent their time doing nothing else but talking about and listening to the latest novelty (17:16–21).

Standing in the middle of the Areopagus, Paul spoke: Men of Athens, I see that in every way you are extremely religious. Walking around and looking carefully at your objects of worship, I even discovered an altar inscribed, "To an Unknown God." Therefore, the One you worship without knowing him is the very One I proclaim to you. The God who made the world and everything in it is Lord of heaven and earth; he does not dwell in temples built by human hands, nor is he served by human hands as though he needed anything, since he himself gives all people life and breath and everything else (17:22–25). He begins right at their own altar, rather than quoting Scriptures they do not yet know.

From one man he made every nation of humanity to inhabit the whole face of the earth, determining their appointed eras and the boundaries of where they live, so that they might seek God and perhaps grope for him and find him—though he is not far from any one of us. "For in him we live and move and have our being," just as some of your own poets have written, "We are indeed his offspring." Since we are God's offspring, then, we ought not to imagine that the divine nature resembles gold, silver, or stone shaped by human artistry and imagination (17:26–29). Paul turns Greek poetry itself to demonstrate how absurd idols really are.

In the past God overlooked such ages of ignorance, yet now he commands all people everywhere to repent, because he has fixed a day on which he will judge the world with justice through a Man he has appointed—and he has given proof of this to everyone by raising him from the dead. The moment they heard about the resurrection of the dead, some mocked, while others said, "We will hear you again on this subject." So Paul left the council. Still, a few people joined him and believed, among them Dionysius, a member of the Areopagus, a woman named Damaris, and several others (17:30–34). The Athenians gladly listened to talk about an unknown God, yet stumbled over the resurrection. That remains the stumbling block for many today.

## Key Takeaways

**The gospel never asks for blind credulity; it welcomes careful inquiry and verification.** In Thessalonica, Paul steps into the synagogue and reasons straight from Scripture about a suffering, risen Christ (17:2–3). In Berea, the listeners earn praise not for unthinking agreement, but for pairing warm openness with daily examination of the Scriptures to verify whether Paul's message holds up (17:11). Mature faith never fears honest questions; it grows deeper through serious study.

**Gospel communication adapts to its audience without ever altering its core.** Speaking to Jewish congregations in Thessalonica and Berea, Paul starts from the Old Testament Scriptures they already know (17:2; 17:11). Standing before Greek philosophers in Athens, he does not open with Old Testament citations, but starts from an Athenian altar and quotes Greek poets to point them toward the Creator (17:22–28). Though his starting bridges shift, his destination stays identical: repentance before coming judgment and the resurrection of Jesus (17:30–31).

**God is the giver of life, not a deity needing human upkeep or trapped inside a shrine.** Facing a city packed with statues of gold, silver, and stone, Paul flips conventional religion on its head: humans do not fabricate gods to feed or bargain with; rather, God made everything, supplies breath to every creature, and stands close to each of us (17:24–27). Every nation traces back to one origin and shares the same deep longing to find its Maker (17:26–27).

**The resurrection of Jesus draws the sharpest dividing line in every audience.** As long as Paul speaks of a Creator and divine nearness, the Athenian thinkers listen attentively. Yet the instant he mentions resurrection from the dead, responses divide along three timeless paths: some scoff, some postpone the conversation for another day, and a humble remnant steps forward in faith (17:32–34).

## Deeper Meaning

**The Areopagus speech bridges creation's witness with Christ's revelation.** Already in the Old Testament, Scripture affirmed that the heavens declare God's glory and that no human-built temple can contain him (Psalms 19:1; 1 Kings 8:27; Isaiah 66:1–2). Paul stands on that exact biblical ground to engage Greek philosophy, showing that humanity's deepest quest for truth finds its final answer in the risen Man appointed by God (17:24–31).

**The accusation of "another king" unwittingly names the gospel's true claim.** Opponents in Thessalonica try to frame Paul's group for treason against Caesar by accusing them of proclaiming another king named Jesus (17:7). Politically they misread him—Jesus leads no army to topple Rome—yet spiritually they stumble onto a profound reality: Jesus truly is King of kings, and every believer's ultimate allegiance belongs to him above every earthly empire (Revelation 19:16; Philippians 2:9–11).

**The equality of all peoples springs from humanity's single shared origin.** Addressing Athenians proud of their supposed superiority over "barbarians," Paul's statement that God created every nation from one single man shatters ethnic arrogance (17:26; Genesis 1:27; 3:20). Because all share one Creator, no nation may look down on another, and the gospel belongs to no single culture alone.

## Notes & Nuances

**Was Paul's address in Athens a failure?** Because only a few converts are named in Athens (17:34) and Paul later writes to Corinth that he resolved to know nothing except Jesus Christ crucified (1 Corinthians 2:2), some readers guess Paul regretted engaging philosophy in Athens. Most scholars reject that theory: in Athens, Paul openly proclaimed repentance, final judgment, and resurrection without any compromise—and winning a sitting councilor of the Areopagus like Dionysius was a notable breakthrough rather than a defeat (17:30–34).

**What did the Epicureans and Stoics teach?** Epicureans believed any gods lived far away indifferent to human affairs, so life's aim was avoiding pain and pursuing tranquil pleasure, with no afterlife. Stoics held that a divine rational principle pervaded the cosmos, prized self-control and living in harmony with nature, yet likewise rejected bodily resurrection. Paul's speech addresses both the insights and blind spots of each school: God is neither distant and indifferent as Epicureans supposed, nor an impersonal cosmic force immune to judgment as Stoics assumed (17:24–31).

**Quoting Greek poets in 17:28 does not endorse polytheism.** Paul cites lines originally composed about Zeus by the poet Aratus—and possibly Epimenides—using phrases familiar to his audience to point toward the true Creator (17:28). He gathers a glimmer of preserved truth inside their culture as a bridge, then immediately corrects their idolatrous imagery in the very next verse (17:29).

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, sees in 17:27–28 a deep insight into human nature: every person carries an implanted sense of the divine, because we live and exist in God. **John Stott**, twentieth-century Anglican pastor and scholar, treats the Areopagus address as a timeless blueprint for engaging contemporary culture with the gospel. In Stott's view, Paul combines genuine grief over the city's idolatry (17:16) with attentive observation of local literature and customs to locate common ground, before courageously challenging their worldview with the Creator and the resurrection. *(The Message of Acts, BST, 1990, on chapter 17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: presenting the Areopagus speech as a paradigm for cultural engagement—both empathetic and confrontational—is a prominent theme of Stott on chapter 17 -->
