# Acts 14 — From Worshiped as Gods to Stoned

## Story

In Iconium, Paul and Barnabas again entered the Jewish synagogue and spoke so effectively that a great number of both Jews and Greeks believed. Yet the Jews who refused to believe stirred up the Gentiles and poisoned their minds against the believers. Even so, the two stayed on for quite some time, speaking fearlessly in reliance on the Lord, who confirmed the message of his grace by enabling them to perform signs and wonders. The city split into two camps—some siding with the Jewish leaders, others with the apostles. When a plot arose among both Gentiles and Jews, together with their officials, to mistreat and stone them, the two learned of it, fled to the Lycaonian cities of Lystra and Derbe and the surrounding countryside, and kept preaching the good news there (14:1–7).

In Lystra sat a man crippled in his feet, lame from birth, who had never taken a step. Listening as Paul preached, he caught Paul's steady gaze; seeing that the man had faith to be healed, Paul called out loudly, "Stand straight up on your feet!" The man jumped up and began walking around (14:8–10). This healing closely mirrors Peter healing the lame beggar at the Beautiful Gate (3:2–8). Luke wants readers to see the exact same Lord working through both apostles.

Seeing what Paul had done, the crowd shouted in the Lycaonian language, "The gods have come down to us in human form!" They named Barnabas Zeus, and called Paul Hermes because he was the chief speaker. The priest of the temple of Zeus just outside the city gate brought bulls and garlands to the gates, wanting to join the crowd in offering sacrifices to them (14:11–13). Not understanding the local dialect, the two apostles only realized what was unfolding when bulls and garlands showed up.

Once they grasped what was happening, Barnabas and Paul tore their clothes and rushed into the middle of the crowd shouting: friends, why are you doing this? We too are only human beings, just like you! We bring you good news so that you will turn away from these worthless things to the living God, who created heaven, earth, sea, and everything in them. In past generations he let all the nations walk their own paths, yet he never left himself without witness: he has shown kindness by giving you rain from heaven and fruitful harvests, filling your stomachs with food and your hearts with joy. Even with these words, they barely kept the crowd from offering sacrifices to them (14:14–18). Speaking to listeners unfamiliar with Scripture, Paul begins not with Abraham and David, but with rain and harvests.

Then Jewish opponents arrived from Antioch and Iconium and won over the crowd. They stoned Paul, dragged him outside the city assuming he was dead, and left him there. Yet as the disciples gathered around him, he got back up and walked right back into the city. The next day he and Barnabas left for Derbe (14:19–20). The exact same crowd that wanted to worship him as a god one day stones him the next. Years later Paul would look back and note simply, "once I was stoned" (2 Corinthians 11:25).

After preaching the gospel in Derbe and making a substantial number of disciples, the two turned around and retraced their steps straight back through Lystra, Iconium, and Antioch—the very cities that had just persecuted them. They strengthened the hearts of the disciples, urged them to remain steadfast in the faith, and reminded them that "we must enter the kingdom of God through many hardships." Appointing elders for them in every church, with prayer and fasting they committed them to the Lord in whom they had put their trust (14:21–23). They refuse to leave fledgling congregations without shepherds.

Traveling back through Pisidia into Pamphylia, they preached the word in Perga, went down to Attalia, and sailed home to Antioch—the very place where they had been entrusted to God's grace for the mission now completed. Gathering the church together, they reported everything God had accomplished through them and how he had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles. There they stayed with the disciples for a long time (14:24–28). They do not boast about what they achieved; they recount what God has done through them.

## Key Takeaways

**Popular applause turns on a dime: hailed as gods one moment, stoned nearly to death the next.** In Lystra, watching a man lame from birth leap to his feet prompts the crowd to rush forward with bulls and garlands to worship Barnabas and Paul (14:8–13). The two apostles tear their clothes in horror to redirect their eyes toward the living God, yet shortly afterward outside agitators sway that exact same crowd into stoning Paul and dumping him outside the city for dead (14:14–19).

**The gospel adapts its starting point without ever altering its core.** Addressing Bible-literate Jews in chapter 13, Paul starts from Abraham, David, and the Old Testament Scriptures; speaking to biblically unfamiliar Gentiles in Lystra, he starts from the Creator, rain from heaven, fruitful seasons, and everyday joy (14:15–17).

**Nurturing young churches matters just as much as breaking fresh ground.** Instead of taking the quick overland road home to Antioch from Derbe, Paul and Barnabas courageously loop back through the very cities that had driven them out and stoned them—Lystra, Iconium, and Pisidian Antioch—to steady the believers, warn them of coming hardship, and appoint local elders in every church (14:21–23).

**Every fruit belongs to God rather than to his messengers.** Returning to their sending church in Antioch, the missionaries give a clear account of what God had done with them and how God himself had swung open a door of faith for the Gentiles (14:26–28).

## Deeper Meaning

**Creation itself serves as the Creator's quiet witness.** Paul's reminder at Lystra that God never left himself without testimony—sending rain from the sky and abundant crops (14:17)—echoes the psalms declaring that the heavens proclaim God's glory and that his open hand satisfies every living creature (Psalms 19:1; 145:15–16). Paul will later unfold this same theme in Romans: God's eternal power and divine nature shine plainly through the things he has made (Romans 1:20).

**The road into God's kingdom passes through hardship.** The apostles' candid reminder to brand-new believers—"we must enter the kingdom of God through many hardships" (14:22)—carries forward Jesus' own call to take up the cross and expect opposition (Luke 9:23; John 16:33). Paul refuses to paint a rosy picture promising an easy life, preparing young disciples early to stand firm under pressure.

**Two pillars, Peter and Paul, perform the identical work of the risen Lord.** Luke's description of Paul locking eyes with the man lame from birth in Lystra, commanding him to stand upright, and watching him leap up and walk (14:8–10) deliberately parallels Peter healing the man lame from birth at the Beautiful Gate (3:2–8). Readers see unmistakably that Paul carries the exact same apostolic authority and resurrection power as Peter.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Lystra's crowd call Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes (14:12)?** Greco-Roman mythology—famously recorded by the Roman poet Ovid in his *Metamorphoses* and set in this very region—told an old tale in which Zeus (Jupiter in Roman myth) and Hermes (Mercury, spokesman of the gods) once visited the area disguised as ordinary travelers; the whole neighborhood refused them hospitality except an impoverished elderly couple named Philemon and Baucis, so the region was flooded in punishment while the couple received blessings. Terrified of repeating that legendary blunder, Lystra's citizens rushed out with sacrificial bulls the moment they saw a miracle, assuming the calmer, more dignified Barnabas was Zeus and the outspoken Paul was Hermes.

**What did tearing their clothes (14:14) signify?** In Jewish culture, ripping one's garments was the starkest possible response upon hearing blasphemy against God (Matthew 26:65). That instinctive gesture reveals how deeply appalled both apostles were at human beings being treated as deities—forming a vivid contrast with King Herod in chapter 12, who basked gladly in chants calling him a god (12:22–23).

**Lystra was Timothy's hometown.** Although chapter 14 never mentions him by name, chapter 16 introduces young Timothy living right in Lystra (16:1–2), and in Paul's final letter he reminds Timothy of the sufferings he endured at Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra as events Timothy knew firsthand (2 Timothy 3:10–11). Timothy's family very likely came to faith during this very blood-and-tears visit.
