# Acts 8 — The Gospel Reaches Samaria and Ethiopia

## Story

Saul gave his approval to Stephen's execution. That very day a fierce persecution broke out against the church in Jerusalem. Apart from the apostles, all the believers were scattered throughout Judea and Samaria. Devout men buried Stephen and mourned deeply for him. Meanwhile Saul began ravaging the church, barging from house to house and dragging off both men and women to throw them into prison (8:1–3). Jesus' word back in chapter 1 about Judea and Samaria now starts coming true—driven by persecution itself.

Wherever the scattered believers went, they preached the good news. Philip, one of the seven helpers, went down to a city in Samaria and proclaimed the Christ to them. The crowds paid unified attention to what Philip said when they heard him and saw the signs he performed: impure spirits came out of many people with loud shrieks, and numerous paralyzed and lame people were healed. Great joy filled that city (8:4–8). Jews and Samaritans had long despised one another, yet the gospel breaks straight through that wall.

In that city lived a man named Simon who practiced sorcery, amazing the Samaritan people and boasting that he was someone great. From the youngest to the oldest, everyone followed him closely, calling him "the Great Power" of God. Yet when they believed Philip as he proclaimed the good news of God's kingdom and the name of Jesus Christ, both men and women were baptized. Simon himself believed as well, was baptized, and stayed right by Philip's side, astounded by the great signs and miracles he saw taking place (8:9–13).

When the apostles back in Jerusalem heard that Samaria had accepted God's word, they sent Peter and John to them. On arriving, the two prayed for the new believers to receive the Holy Spirit, because the Spirit had not yet come upon any of them; they had simply been baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus. Then Peter and John laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit (8:14–17). John, who once wanted to call down fire from heaven to consume a Samaritan village, now prays for the Holy Spirit to bless that very people (Luke 9:52–55).

Seeing that the Spirit was given through the laying on of the apostles' hands, Simon offered them money and asked, "Give me this ability too, so that anyone I lay my hands on will receive the Holy Spirit." Peter replied, "May your silver perish along with you, because you thought you could buy God's gift with money!" You have no share or part in this matter, because your heart is not right before God. Repent of this wickedness and plead with the Lord, in case the intent of your heart may be forgiven you, for I see you are trapped in bitter poison and bound by sin. Simon answered, "Pray to the Lord for me yourselves, so that none of the things you said will happen to me" (8:18–24). From his name came the English word *simony*—the sin of buying or selling sacred church offices.

After testifying and proclaiming the Lord's word, Peter and John headed back to Jerusalem, preaching the gospel in many Samaritan villages along the way (8:25).

Then an angel of the Lord told Philip to go south down the desert road leading from Jerusalem to Gaza. He got up and went. Along the way he encountered an Ethiopian eunuch, a high-ranking official in charge of the entire treasury of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians. Having gone up to Jerusalem to worship, he was now returning home, seated in his chariot and reading the prophet Isaiah. The Spirit told Philip to walk over and stay near that chariot. Running up beside it, Philip heard him reading Isaiah aloud and asked, "Do you understand what you are reading?" The eunuch replied, "How can I understand unless someone guides me?" and invited Philip to climb up and sit beside him (8:26–31).

The Scripture passage he was reading spoke of one led like a sheep to slaughter, silent as a lamb before its shearer, never opening his mouth; humiliated and deprived of justice, his life taken away from the earth. The eunuch asked, "Tell me, who is the prophet talking about here—himself, or someone else?" Starting right from that passage of Scripture, Philip shared the good news about Jesus with him (8:32–35; Isaiah 53:7–8). The traveler asks the question; Philip points out the answer.

As they traveled on, they came to some water. The eunuch said, "Look, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" Verse 37, which records the eunuch's confession of faith, is absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts. He ordered the chariot to stop; both men stepped down into the water, and Philip baptized him. When they came up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord suddenly carried Philip away; the eunuch saw him no more, yet went on his way rejoicing. Philip appeared at Azotus and traveled onward, preaching the gospel in every town until he reached Caesarea (8:36–40). A eunuch, barred from the assembly under Moses' law, is now welcomed completely (Deuteronomy 23:1; Isaiah 56:3–5).

## Key Takeaways

**Persecution never extinguishes the gospel; it scatters the seed wider.** When Saul ravages the Jerusalem church, ordinary believers scatter across Judea and Samaria, preaching the word wherever their feet carry them (8:1–4). The storm itself pushes the church outside its comfort zone to carry out the missionary roadmap of 1:8.

**God's gifts cannot be purchased or harnessed for personal ego.** In Samaria, Simon the magician—once the marvel of the whole crowd—also believes and receives baptism, yet still treats the Holy Spirit's power as a superior trick he can buy with cash (8:9–24). Peter's blunt rebuke draws an unmistakable line between genuine faith and exploiting religion for status.

**The gospel crosses every racial, geographical, and legal barrier.** From Samaria, long scorned by Jewish neighbors, the Holy Spirit leads Philip down a desert road to meet an Ethiopian eunuch—a foreigner excluded from the assembly under the old law—explaining Isaiah and baptizing him right by the roadside (8:26–39).

## Deeper Meaning

**The Ethiopian eunuch fulfills a tender promise in Isaiah.** Mosaic law barred eunuchs from entering the assembly of the Lord (Deuteronomy 23:1). Yet through the prophet Isaiah, God promised a brighter day: let neither the foreigner nor the eunuch say, "I am only a dry tree," because God will give them inside his house a name better than sons and daughters (Isaiah 56:3–5). When Philip opens Isaiah's Suffering Servant song (Isaiah 53:7–8) and baptizes the eunuch, that ancient promise springs to life on a desert highway (8:32–38).

**The first wave of God's promise reaching Cush.** In the Old Testament, Ethiopia—commonly called Cush, the Nubian region south of Egypt—stood as an emblem of distant lands at the edges of the earth; the psalmist had foretold a day when Cush would stretch out its hands eagerly toward God (Psalms 68:31). Sending Queen Candace's treasurer home rejoicing with the gospel marks the first dawn of Christ's light moving into Africa (8:27; 8:39).

**The Samaritan church is united with the Jerusalem church.** Bitterness between Jews and Samaritans had simmered for centuries since the divided kingdom and the rival temple built on Mount Gerizim (John 4:9; 4:20). By having Peter and John travel down from Jerusalem to lay hands on the Samaritans so they receive the exact same Holy Spirit (8:14–17), God prevents the early church from splitting into two separate ethnic churches.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did the Samaritans believe and receive baptism without immediately receiving the Holy Spirit (8:14–17)?** Christian traditions interpret this episode quite differently. Some Pentecostal streams view it as a pattern demonstrating that baptism in the Spirit is a distinct second experience following conversion. Most commentators, however, point to the unique historical setting of Acts: given the centuries-old rift between Jews and Samaritans, God temporarily delayed the visible outpouring of the Spirit until apostles came down from Jerusalem to lay hands on them, proving unmistakably to both groups that Samaritan believers belong to the exact same single church as Jewish believers.

**Was Simon the magician genuinely converted?** Verse 8:13 states that Simon believed and was baptized, yet by 8:20–23 Peter tells him he has no share in this ministry and remains bound in bitter poison and sin. This reveals that a shallow belief swept along by outward miracles can exist without genuine inward repentance. The narrative leaves Simon's outcome open-ended, as he only asks the apostles to pray on his behalf so he escapes punishment (8:24).

**Why is 8:37 missing from many modern Bible translations?** Older traditional translations include verse 37, where Philip asks a condition and the eunuch confesses faith in Jesus Christ as the Son of God, yet that verse does not appear in almost any of the oldest and most reliable Greek manuscripts. Textual scholars conclude it originated as a standard early-church baptismal confession jotted in the margin by a scribe and later folded into the main text.
