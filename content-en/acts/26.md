# Acts 26 — Before King Agrippa

## Story

King Agrippa told Paul, "You have permission to speak for yourself." Stretching out his hand, Paul began his defense: King Agrippa, I consider myself fortunate to stand before you today making my defense against all the Jewish accusations, especially since you are thoroughly familiar with every Jewish custom and point of controversy. I therefore beg you to listen to me patiently (26:1–3).

Every Jew knows how I have lived since my youth, both in my own country and in Jerusalem. They have known me for a long time and could testify, if they were willing, that I lived as a Pharisee according to the strictest party of our religion. And now I stand on trial because of my hope in the promise God made to our ancestors—the very promise our twelve tribes earnestly hope to see fulfilled as they worship God night and day. It is for this hope, Your Majesty, that I am accused by the Jews. "Why should any of you consider it unbelievable that God raises the dead?" (26:4–8).

I myself was once convinced that I ought to do everything possible to oppose the name of Jesus of Nazareth. That is exactly what I did in Jerusalem: backed by authority from the chief priests, I locked many of the saints in prison, and when they were condemned to death, I cast my vote against them. Frequently I went from synagogue to synagogue punishing them and trying to force them to blaspheme; in raging fury I chased them even to foreign cities (26:9–11).

On one such mission I was heading to Damascus with the authority and commission of the chief priests. About noon along the road, Your Majesty, I saw a light from heaven—brighter than the sun—blazing around me and my companions. All of us fell to the ground, and I heard a voice addressing me in the Hebrew dialect: "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads." I asked, "Who are you, Lord?" The Lord answered, "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting" (26:12–15). A goad was a pointed stick used to prod oxen; an ox kicking back against it only wounds itself worse. Saul may have been fighting something stirring inside his own heart for quite some time.

Now rise and stand on your feet, for I have appeared to you to appoint you as a servant and witness both of what you have seen and of what I will show you. I will rescue you from your own people and from the Gentiles to whom I am sending you, to open their eyes so they may turn from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God, receiving forgiveness of sins and an inheritance among those sanctified by faith in me (26:16–18). This is the third time Acts recounts Paul's encounter with Christ, and here his commission appears in its fullest form.

So then, King Agrippa, I was not disobedient to that heavenly vision. First to those in Damascus, then in Jerusalem and throughout Judea, and also to the Gentiles, I proclaimed that they must repent, turn to God, and live out deeds consistent with repentance. That is why the Jews seized me in the temple courts and tried to kill me. Yet with God's help I stand here to this very day, testifying to small and great alike—saying nothing beyond what the prophets and Moses foretold would happen: that the Christ would suffer, and as the first to rise from the dead, would proclaim light both to our own people and to the Gentiles (26:19–23).

Right at this point in Paul's defense, Festus interrupted at the top of his voice, "You are out of your mind, Paul! Your great learning is driving you insane!" Paul answered calmly, "I am not insane, Most Excellent Festus; what I am speaking is true and reasonable." The king knows all about these matters, and I can speak freely before him; I am convinced none of this has escaped his notice, because it was not done in some hidden corner. King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know you do (26:24–27). Paul stops addressing a crowded hall and speaks straight to one man's conscience.

Agrippa replied to Paul, "In so short a time would you persuade me to become a Christian?" Paul answered, "Short time or long—I pray to God that not only you, but everyone listening to me today may become just as I am, except for these chains!" (26:28–29). Many commentators read the king's reply as polite irony: do you imagine a brief speech can turn me into a Christian? Either way, Paul responds with a prayer. The man wearing iron chains sees himself as richer than the king.

The king stood up, followed by the governor, Bernice, and those seated with them. Leaving the hall, they talked among themselves: this man has done nothing deserving death or imprisonment. Agrippa remarked to Festus, "This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar" (26:30–32). Once again, ruling authorities affirm Paul's innocence—just as Pilate and Herod declared regarding Jesus (Luke 23:14–15).

## Key Takeaways

**The prisoner in chains stands as the freest person in the entire courtroom.** Facing King Agrippa, Bernice, Governor Festus, and resplendent dignitaries, Paul never cowers or begs for sympathy; lifting his chained wrists, he wishes that both king and audience might become just like him—minus the iron (26:29). Deep peace and unshakable hope in Christ liberate the soul from fear and status alike.

**The gospel's mission is opening blind eyes to move from darkness into light.** In this third and fullest account of the Damascus road, Paul recalls Jesus sending him to open Jewish and Gentile eyes alike, turning people from Satan's grip to God for forgiveness and an eternal inheritance (26:16–18). Good news is never mere self-improvement tips; it is total rescue that changes one's master and destiny.

**Christian faith rests on public historical events, never secret myths tucked away in corners.** When Festus shouts that too much study has driven Paul mad, Paul replies steadily and courteously: his words are truthful and sane, because the events surrounding Jesus unfolded openly before the public eye, not in some hidden corner (26:24–26). Christianity never hides from honest historical inspection.

**Genuine testimony speaks lovingly straight to the heart, not merely winning arguments.** Rather than settling for clearing his own legal record, Paul pivots directly to Agrippa and asks whether he believes the prophets (26:27). Even when the king sidesteps with wry irony, Paul answers with a heartfelt prayer longing for everyone in the hall to know Christ (26:28–29).

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul's commission in 26:16–18 resonates deeply with the calls of Israel's great Old Testament prophets.** Being told to rise to his feet and promised deliverance from both his own people and the nations echoes God's commissioning of Ezekiel and Jeremiah (Ezekiel 2:1; Jeremiah 1:7–8). Opening blind eyes and carrying light to the nations directly mirrors Isaiah's Servant Songs (Isaiah 42:6–7; 49:6). Paul extends the Messiah's own mission of bringing God's light to the ends of the earth.

**The suffering and resurrection of Christ unlock Moses and all the Prophets.** Paul insists he preaches nothing novel beyond what Moses and the prophets foretold: a Messiah who suffers, rises first from the dead, and announces light to Israel and the nations alike (26:22–23; Isaiah 53:5–11; Luke 24:25–27). Every strand of Old Testament Scripture converges right here.

**The hearing before Agrippa crowns Paul's sequence of courtroom defenses before sailing to Rome.** Moving from the barracks stairs (chapter 22) to the Sanhedrin (chapter 23), Governor Felix (chapter 24), Governor Festus (chapter 25), and now King Agrippa (chapter 26), the rank of each audience rises step by step—yet Paul's testimony stays unchanging: innocent before Roman law and utterly loyal to the risen Christ (26:31–32).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was King Agrippa's remark in 26:28 sincere emotion or polite sarcasm?** Older traditional translations render it "Almost you persuade me to become a Christian," making Agrippa sound nearly won over. Yet the concise Greek phrase readily reads as wry deflection: "In such a brief time you think you can make me a Christian?" Most modern scholars favor the ironic reading, since Agrippa immediately rises to end the session without asking another question (26:30). Whether joking or quietly unsettled, Paul's gracious reply in 26:29 stays completely unfazed: quick or slow, his deepest wish is that every hearer would find what he has found.

**What does "kicking against the goads" in 26:14 mean?** This vivid proverb circulated widely across Greek literature and Mediterranean farming life: a plowman carried a sharp-tipped pole to guide oxen straight down the furrow, and a stubborn ox kicking back against the spike only hurt its own legs harder. The saying reveals that even at the height of Saul's furious crusade against the church, he was injuring himself fighting what God was doing—perhaps his conscience had already been pricked watching Stephen die in peace (7:58–60).

**Why does Acts tell the Damascus road story three separate times (chapters 9, 22, and 26)?** In ancient literature, triple repetition signals the central pillar of a work. Chapter 9 gives Luke's narrator account; chapter 22 presents Paul speaking to Jerusalem Jews, highlighting continuity with the law; chapter 26 presents Paul addressing King Agrippa and a Roman governor, folding his Gentile commission directly into the initial encounter so royal listeners grasp the full sweep of his calling.
