# Acts 6 — The Seven Helpers and Stephen

## Story

As the number of disciples kept growing, complaints began to surface. Greek-speaking Jewish believers, called Hellenists, raised a grievance against the native Hebrew-speaking Jewish believers because their widows were being overlooked in the daily food distribution (6:1). Having just weathered deceit from within, the church now faces the threat of internal division.

The Twelve summoned the entire body of disciples and said, "It would not be right for us to neglect teaching the word of God in order to wait on tables." Therefore, brothers, select from among yourselves seven men of good reputation, full of the Spirit and wisdom, and we will put them in charge of this responsibility. As for us, we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word (6:2–4). The apostles do not look down on serving food—they insist that the people doing it must be filled with the Holy Spirit.

The proposal pleased the whole gathering. They chose Stephen, a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit, along with Philip, Procorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas, and Nicolas, a convert to Judaism from Antioch. All seven bear Greek names, as though the church deliberately placed the care of the community into the hands of the very minority that felt slighted. They presented these seven men to the apostles, who prayed and laid their hands on them (6:5–6).

So the word of God continued to spread. The number of disciples in Jerusalem multiplied rapidly, and even a great many priests became obedient to the faith (6:7). Once the internal tension is healed, the gospel surges forward again.

Now Stephen, full of grace and power, performed great wonders and signs among the people. Opposition arose, however, from members of the synagogue known as the Synagogue of the Freedmen, together with Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia, and Asia, who began debating with Stephen. Yet they could not stand up against the wisdom and the Spirit by which he spoke (6:8–10).

Unable to beat him in honest debate, they secretly prompted men to claim they had heard Stephen speaking blasphemous words against Moses and against God. They stirred up the crowd, the elders, and the scribes, seized him, and hauled him before the Sanhedrin. There they produced false witnesses who testified that this man never stops speaking against this holy place and the law: we have heard him say that this Jesus of Nazareth will destroy this place and change the customs Moses handed down to us (6:11–14). It was the very same strategy used to frame Jesus.

Everyone seated in the Sanhedrin stared intently at Stephen, and they saw that his face looked "like the face of an angel" (6:15). Surrounded by slander, he radiates the calm of a messenger from heaven.

## Key Takeaways

**Growth brings friction, and wisdom turns friction into strength.** As the church expands quickly, Greek-speaking widows slip through the cracks of the daily food relief, risking a split along cultural and linguistic lines (6:1). Instead of ignoring the complaint or trying to handle everything themselves, the apostles organize clear delegation so the needy are cared for well while prayer and Scripture teaching stay central (6:2–4).

**Practical service requires Spirit-filled character.** The church does not assign its least gifted members to logistical relief; it selects seven men of proven integrity, full of the Spirit and wisdom—and graciously picks seven Greek-named leaders to reassure the aggrieved minority (6:3; 6:5–6). Because the issue is handled fairly, the word spreads even faster and many priests come to faith (6:7).

**Stephen opens a turning point amid false accusations.** Far from confining himself to tables, Stephen works great signs through grace and power and debates with wisdom his opponents cannot answer; defeated in argument, they recruit false witnesses to drag him before the Sanhedrin (6:8–15).

## Deeper Meaning

**Following Moses' pattern of shared leadership in the wilderness.** When Israel's numbers grew too heavy for Moses to shoulder alone, Jethro advised him to appoint trustworthy helpers for everyday disputes while Moses focused on standing before God and teaching the law (Exodus 18:17–23; Numbers 11:16–17). The apostles in chapter 6 apply that exact wisdom, keeping both bodily care and spiritual nourishment healthy side by side (6:2–4).

**Stephen's angelic face recalls Moses descending from Sinai.** While false witnesses accuse Stephen of blaspheming Moses and the temple, his own face shines "like the face of an angel" right before the council (6:11–15). The image calls to mind Moses stepping down from Mount Sinai with a radiant face after being in God's presence (Exodus 34:29–35): the very man charged with opposing Moses bears the same glow of God's presence that Moses bore.

**The servant walks the same trial path as his Master.** Every tactic deployed against Stephen—bribed false witnesses, charges of speaking against the temple, threats to tear down the sanctuary, accusations of altering Moses' customs—mirrors Jesus' own trial only a short time before (6:11–14; Mark 14:55–59). Jesus' warning that his disciples would share his fate is already unfolding (Luke 21:12–15).

## Notes & Nuances

**Were the Seven in chapter 6 the first deacons?** Church tradition commonly refers to these seven men as the first deacons, since the Greek verb for waiting on tables (6:2) shares its root with the later office title "deacon" (1 Timothy 3:8). Still, the Greek text of Acts never actually applies that title to them, calling them simply "the Seven" (21:8)—even though some traditional translations describe them as deacons—and both Stephen and Philip immediately preach, perform signs, and baptize like evangelists (6:8; 8:5–13). This episode is therefore best seen as the historical forerunner of the deacon's office rather than a rigid wall separating preachers from social caregivers.

**Who were the Hellenists and the Hebrews in 6:1?** Both groups were Jewish followers of Jesus, not yet Gentiles. The "Hebrews" were Palestinian Jews whose native language was Aramaic, whereas the "Hellenists" were Greek-speaking Jews from diaspora communities around the Mediterranean who had settled in Jerusalem and absorbed Greco-Roman culture. Those cultural and language differences inadvertently left the Hellenist widows underserved.

**What did the laying on of hands in 6:6 signify?** This gesture continued the Old Testament custom in which Moses laid hands on Joshua to commission him publicly before the congregation (Numbers 27:18–23). It was not an automatic transfer of magical power, but a public sign that the church recognized, prayed over, and entrusted a specific ministry to them.
