# Acts 7 — Stephen's Speech and Martyrdom

## Story

The high priest asked Stephen, "Are these charges true?" Stephen did not answer the accusations directly. Instead, he retold the story of Israel, starting with Abraham. The God of glory appeared to Abraham while he was still living in Mesopotamia and told him to leave his country and go to a land God would show him. Abraham moved to Haran and, after his father died, settled in this land. Yet God gave him no inheritance in it—not even enough ground to set his foot on—only promising to give it to his descendants while he was still childless. God also foretold that his offspring would be enslaved in a foreign country for four hundred years, after which God would judge the nation that enslaved them and bring them out to worship him. God gave Abraham the covenant of circumcision, passed down through Isaac, Jacob, and the twelve patriarchs (7:1–8). Right from the beginning, God met his people outside the holy land.

Out of jealousy, the patriarchs sold Joseph into Egypt, yet God stayed with him. God rescued him from all his troubles and granted him favor and wisdom before Pharaoh, who appointed him governor over the whole country. When famine struck, Jacob sent his sons down to Egypt for grain. On their second trip Joseph revealed himself to his brothers, and then sent for his father and entire household—seventy-five people in all. Jacob and the patriarchs died in Egypt, and their bodies were later brought back to be buried at Shechem (7:9–16). The very brother they rejected turned out to be the one God used to save them.

As the time approached for God's promise to be fulfilled, the people grew numerous in Egypt until another king rose to power who knew nothing of Joseph. That ruler dealt treacherously with our people, forcing them to expose their newborn infants. At that time Moses was born, a child of extraordinary beauty. For three months he was cared for in his father's house, then placed outside, where Pharaoh's daughter took him in and raised him as her own son. Moses was educated in all the wisdom of Egypt and became powerful in both speech and action (7:17–22).

When Moses turned forty, he went to visit his fellow Israelites. Seeing one of them mistreated, he stepped in to defend the man and killed the Egyptian. He assumed his brothers would realize that God was using his hand to deliver them, yet they failed to understand. The next day he came upon two Israelites fighting and tried to reconcile them, but the man who was wronging his neighbor pushed Moses aside: who made you ruler and judge over us? Are you planning to kill me the way you killed the Egyptian yesterday? Moses fled to Midian, where he fathered two sons (7:23–29).

Forty years later, in the desert near Mount Sinai, an angel appeared to him in the flames of a burning bush. As Moses stepped closer to look, the Lord's voice spoke: I am the God of your fathers—the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Trembling with fear, Moses did not dare look. The Lord told him to take off his sandals, because the ground where he stood was holy ground, and sent him back to Egypt to liberate his people (7:30–34). That holy ground lay out in a foreign desert, a thousand years before any temple stood.

Stephen drives home the point: this very Moses, whom they had rejected, was the one God sent as ruler and deliverer. He led them out of Egypt, performing wonders and signs in Egypt, at the Red Sea, and for forty years in the desert. This same Moses declared that God would raise up for them a prophet like himself from among their own brothers. He received living words to pass on to us. Yet our ancestors refused to obey him, turned back in their hearts toward Egypt, told Aaron to make gods to march ahead of them, forged a calf, and rejoiced in the work of their own hands. So God turned away and gave them over to worship the starry host of heaven, just as the prophet Amos spoke of Moloch and Rephan and of exile beyond Babylon (7:35–43; Amos 5:25–27).

Our ancestors had the tabernacle of testimony in the wilderness, built according to the exact pattern Moses had seen. Joshua brought it into the land, where it remained until the days of David, who found favor with God and asked to provide a dwelling place for him. Yet it was Solomon who built a house for him. "Still, the Most High does not live in houses made by human hands," as the prophet declared: heaven is my throne and the earth is my footstool; what kind of house will you build for me? (7:44–50; Isaiah 66:1–2). That is Stephen's answer to the charge that he spoke against the holy place.

Then Stephen turns straight toward the court itself: "You stiff-necked people, with uncircumcised hearts and ears!" You always resist the Holy Spirit—just as your ancestors did, so do you. Which of the prophets did your ancestors fail to persecute? They killed those who foretold the coming of the Righteous One, and now you yourselves have betrayed and murdered him. You received the law delivered through angels, yet you have not obeyed it (7:51–53).

Hearing this, they were furious and gnashed their teeth at him. Yet Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, gazed up into heaven and saw the glory of God, and Jesus standing at God's right hand. He exclaimed, "Look, I see the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God!" Screaming at the top of their voices, they clapped their hands over their ears, rushed at him together, dragged him outside the city, and stoned him. The witnesses laid their cloaks at the feet of a young man named Saul (7:54–58).

While stones rained down on him, Stephen prayed, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit." Then he dropped to his knees and cried out loudly, "Lord, do not hold this sin against them!" Having said this, he fell asleep (7:59–60). He died just as his Master had died: entrusting his spirit and forgiving his killers. Meanwhile, the young man guarding coats nearby is about to become the central figure of the second half of the book.

## Key Takeaways

**God can never be boxed inside a building or a single territory.** Answering the charge that he spoke against the temple and Moses' law, Stephen traces Israel's story to show that God appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia, stayed with Joseph in Egypt, met Moses at a burning bush in Midian, and traveled with his people in a portable tent long before Solomon's temple ever existed (7:2–4; 7:9; 7:30–33; 7:44–50).

**Rejecting God's messengers is a recurring pattern across history.** The brothers jealously sold Joseph, and the people shoved Moses aside—even though those exact men were the rescuers God had sent to save them (7:9; 7:27; 7:35). Now the Sanhedrin repeats that same tragic habit by betraying and murdering the Righteous One, Jesus (7:51–53).

**The church's first martyr dies with the exact spirit of Jesus.** Surrounded by rage and flying stones, Stephen sees Jesus standing at God's right hand to welcome him, and spends his final breath on two prayers: entrusting his spirit to the Lord and asking forgiveness for the very men executing him (7:55–60).

## Deeper Meaning

**Holy ground is wherever God is present.** When God told Moses to slip off his sandals because the ground beneath him was holy, he was standing in a foreign desert far from Jerusalem (7:33; Exodus 3:5). Joined with Isaiah's word that heaven is God's throne and earth his footstool (7:49–50; Isaiah 66:1–2), Stephen's speech opens the theological doorway for the gospel to move past the temple walls out to all nations.

**Joseph and Moses foreshadow the rejected Messiah.** Both Joseph and Moses were first rejected by their own brothers, only to return later as the rescuers of the entire nation (Genesis 37:28; 45:5–7; Exodus 2:14; 3:10). Stephen's retelling shows that the cross does not prove Jesus failed; rather, it places him squarely in the Old Testament line of rejected deliverers (7:9–14; 7:35–37).

**The Son of Man standing at God's right hand.** In Daniel's vision, the Son of Man comes with the clouds of heaven to receive everlasting dominion (Daniel 7:13–14), and throughout the New Testament Jesus is normally pictured seated at God's right hand (2:34; Psalms 110:1). Yet in the instant Stephen faces stoning, he sees the Son of Man standing at God's right hand (7:55–56)—like the heavenly Judge and Advocate rising to welcome and vindicate his faithful witness.

**Dying in peace as though falling asleep.** Stephen's final two prayers—committing his spirit to the Lord and begging pardon for his killers—directly echo Jesus' own words from the cross (7:59–60; Luke 23:34; 23:46). Luke closes the brutal scene with a remarkably gentle verb: amid a storm of stones, Stephen simply fell asleep (7:60).

## Notes & Nuances

**Minor historical differences between Stephen's speech and the Old Testament text.** Careful readers notice several details where Stephen's summary differs slightly from the Hebrew text of Genesis and Exodus: for instance, seventy-five people going down to Egypt (7:14) follows the Greek Septuagint rather than the Hebrew figure of seventy (Genesis 46:27); Abraham's burial cave purchase in Hebron gets compressed together with Jacob's land purchase at Shechem (7:16; Genesis 23:16–19; 33:19); and the quotation from Amos 5:27 speaks of exile beyond Babylon instead of Damascus (7:43). Scholars note that Stephen is delivering an extended spoken defense from the Greek Bible familiar to Hellenist Jews, compressing episodes to serve his main argument rather than reading out footnotes.

**Was Stephen's stoning a legal execution or mob violence?** Under Roman rule, the Jewish Sanhedrin did not possess legal authority to carry out death sentences on its own (John 18:31). Still, Stephen's killing blends features of Mosaic blasphemy execution—dragging the accused outside the city and letting witnesses cast the first stones (7:58; Leviticus 24:14; Deuteronomy 17:7)—with an uncontrolled burst of crowd fury, possibly during a moment when Roman oversight was slack or between governors.

**Why is Jesus standing rather than seated in 7:55–56?** This is the only passage in the New Testament that pictures Jesus standing at God's right hand instead of sitting. Commentators suggest three lovely, compatible explanations: he rises to welcome the church's first martyr home; he stands as witness and advocate acknowledging Stephen before the heavenly court just as he promised (Luke 12:8); or he rises ready to judge and act on his church's behalf.
