# Acts 18 — Corinth

## Story

Leaving Athens, Paul traveled on to Corinth. There he met a Jew named Aquila, originally from Pontus, along with his wife Priscilla; they had recently arrived from Italy because Emperor Claudius had ordered all Jews to leave Rome. Paul went to see them, and because he shared their trade as a tentmaker, he stayed and worked alongside them. Every Sabbath he reasoned in the synagogue, seeking to persuade both Jews and Greeks (18:1–4). The church's foremost apostle works with his hands to support himself, and a tentmaking couple become his lifelong partners in ministry.

Once Silas and Timothy arrived from Macedonia, Paul devoted himself exclusively to preaching, testifying to the Jews that Jesus is the Christ. When they opposed him and hurled abuse, he shook out his garments and declared, "May your blood be on your own heads! I am innocent of it; from now on I will go to the Gentiles." Leaving the synagogue, he moved right next door to the home of a God-fearing man named Titius Justus. Crispus, the synagogue ruler, believed in the Lord along with his entire household, and many of the Corinthians who heard Paul believed and were baptized (18:5–8). He walks out of the synagogue—only to set up right next door, with the synagogue ruler himself following him.

One night the Lord spoke to Paul in a vision: "Do not be afraid; keep on speaking and do not stay silent," for I am with you, and no one will lay a hand on you to harm you, because I have many people in this city. So Paul stayed there a year and a half, teaching God's word among them (18:9–11). Even a man who faced stoning crowds has moments of fear, and the Lord sees and cares (1 Corinthians 2:3).

While Gallio was proconsul of Achaia, the Jews made a united attack on Paul and brought him before the tribunal, charging: this man is persuading people to worship God in ways contrary to the law. Just as Paul was about to speak, Gallio cut in and told the Jews: if this involved a real crime or serious misdemeanor, I would reasonably listen to your complaint; since it is merely a dispute over words, names, and your own law, settle it yourselves—I refuse to act as judge over such matters. And he drove them away from the judgment seat. Then they all grabbed Sosthenes, the synagogue ruler, and beat him right in front of the tribunal, while Gallio paid no attention whatsoever (18:12–17). Gallio's indifferent dismissal ends up shielding the gospel: Roman officials classify the movement as an internal Jewish debate, not an illegal threat.

Paul stayed on in Corinth for some time longer before bidding the believers farewell and sailing for Syria accompanied by Priscilla and Aquila. Before boarding at Cenchreae, he had his hair cut short because of a vow he had taken. Reaching Ephesus, he left Priscilla and Aquila there while he entered the synagogue and reasoned with the Jews. When they urged him to stay longer, he declined, promising as he left: if God wills, I will come back to you. Then he set sail from Ephesus (18:18–21).

Landing at Caesarea, he went up to greet the church and then traveled down to Antioch. After spending some time there, he set out again, traveling systematically through Galatia and Phrygia and strengthening all the disciples (18:22–23). The second missionary journey ends and the third begins within the span of just two verses.

Meanwhile a Jew named Apollos, native to Alexandria, arrived in Ephesus—an eloquent speaker thoroughly grounded in the Scriptures. Instructed in the way of the Lord and fervent in spirit, he spoke and taught accurately about Jesus, though he knew only John's baptism. He began speaking boldly in the synagogue. Hearing him, Priscilla and Aquila invited him to their home and explained the way of God to him more accurately (18:24–26). A gifted orator humbly sits down to learn from two tentmakers—with the wife's name listed first.

When Apollos wanted to cross over to Achaia, the believers encouraged him and wrote to the disciples there asking them to welcome him. Arriving, he greatly aided those who through grace had believed, vigorously refuting Jewish opponents in public debate and proving from the Scriptures that Jesus is the Christ (18:27–28).

## Key Takeaways

**Even the bravest apostle experiences fear, and the Lord meets him right on time.** In Corinth—a bustling, wealthy port city notorious for immorality and hostility—Paul runs into fierce opposition and insults inside the synagogue (18:6). Right then the Lord appears at night telling him not to be afraid, to keep speaking without going silent, because God is with him and has many people in that city (18:9–10). God never promises an easy road free of conflict, yet he guarantees his presence and sure fruit from faithful labor.

**Gospel mission is never a solo showcase, but teamwork built by ordinary believers.** In a strange city, God brings Paul together with Aquila and Priscilla, tentmakers recently uprooted from Rome, who work beside him at the bench and grow into lifelong ministry coworkers (18:2–3). Later this humble couple gently invites Apollos—a brilliant public speaker—into their home to clarify the gospel more fully (18:24–26). Inside the church, no gifted preacher is ever too accomplished to learn, and working-class believers can play a pivotal role in teaching truth.

**God can use even a Roman magistrate's indifference to protect the gospel.** When opponents haul Paul before Proconsul Gallio, Paul does not even have to open his mouth before Gallio throws the case out as an internal Jewish argument (18:12–16). That cool legal dismissal sets a crucial precedent for years, allowing the gospel to travel across the Roman Empire without being branded an outlaw religion.

**Humility to keep learning multiplies a gifted person's usefulness.** Apollos possesses eloquence and deep biblical knowledge yet knows only John's baptism (18:24–25). Rather than bristling when two tentmakers offer private instruction, he welcomes their help gladly, and goes on to strengthen the Corinthian church enormously (18:26–28).

## Deeper Meaning

**God always has people waiting in places humans write off as pitch-dark.** The Lord's assurance to Paul in 18:10 echoes God's encouragement to Elijah when the prophet felt utterly alone amid rebellion: God had still reserved seven thousand who had never bowed to Baal (1 Kings 19:18). Just as God once told Jeremiah not to be afraid because he was with him to rescue him (Jeremiah 1:8), Christ goes ahead of Paul in Corinth, preparing receptive hearts in advance.

**Political upheaval that looks like disaster becomes God's tool to scatter gospel seed.** Emperor Claudius' edict expelling Jews from Rome dealt a jarring blow to Aquila and Priscilla's livelihood (18:2). Yet that forced relocation brought them to Corinth at the exact moment Paul arrived, forging one of the most enduring ministry partnerships in the New Testament (Romans 16:3–4; 1 Corinthians 16:19).

**The Corinthian church proves that grace can bloom in the hardest soil.** Ancient Corinth was so famous for vice that Greeks turned the city's name into a verb for debauchery. Yet Paul invests eighteen months there, planting a thriving congregation whom he later reminds that despite stained pasts, they have been washed, sanctified, and justified in Jesus' name (18:11; 1 Corinthians 6:9–11).

## Notes & Nuances

**What was the vow and hair-cutting at Cenchreae (18:18)?** Under Jewish practice, someone taking a temporary Nazirite vow of thanksgiving or petition abstained from wine and let their hair grow, then shaved their head at the end of the term and presented an offering in Jerusalem (Numbers 6:1–21). While the Greek wording leaves slight room to wonder whether Aquila or Paul took the vow, most scholars read it as Paul. This confirms that although Paul fiercely opposed forcing Gentiles to keep Moses' law for salvation, he personally felt free to observe meaningful Jewish expressions of gratitude whenever they did not compromise the gospel.

**A fascinating hint about Sosthenes after his beating before Gallio (18:17).** In 18:17, the crowd—many manuscripts specify the Greeks—seizes Sosthenes the synagogue leader and beats him right in front of Gallio, who shrugs it off. At the opening of 1 Corinthians, Paul sends greetings alongside a Christian coworker named "Sosthenes our brother" (1 Corinthians 1:1). While we cannot prove with absolute certainty they are the same man, many commentators suspect that the very synagogue official who led the lawsuit against Paul eventually came to faith just like his predecessor Crispus (18:8).

**Proconsul Gallio's term anchors Paul's timeline with rare historical precision.** An ancient stone inscription discovered at Delphi in Greece dates Gallio's proconsulship over Achaia specifically to AD 51–52. That single artifact provides one of the sturdiest chronological pillars for reconstructing Paul's travels and the dates of his letters.
