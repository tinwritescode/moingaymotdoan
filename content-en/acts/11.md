# Acts 11 — The Church at Antioch

## Story

The apostles and believers throughout Judea heard that the Gentiles had also received God's word. When Peter returned to Jerusalem, the circumcised believers criticized him: "You went into the home of uncircumcised men and ate with them!" (11:1–3). What disturbed them was not that Gentiles believed, but that Peter sat down at the same dinner table with them.

Peter calmly recounted everything step by step. While praying in Joppa he fell into a trance, saw something like a large sheet lowered from heaven containing animals, reptiles, and birds, and heard a voice telling him to kill and eat. He replied that nothing impure or unclean had ever entered his mouth. The voice from heaven answered, "Do not call impure what God has made clean." That happened three times (11:4–10).

Right at that moment, three messengers arrived from Caesarea at the house where he stayed. The Spirit directed him to go with them without making any distinction. These six brothers also came along, and together we entered the man's home. He told us how an angel had instructed him to send for Peter, who would bring a message through which he and his entire household would be saved. As soon as I started speaking, the Holy Spirit came upon them just as he had come upon us at the beginning. Then I remembered the Lord's word: John baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit. So if God gave them the very same gift he gave us when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ, "who was I to stand in God's way?" (11:11–17; 1:5).

Hearing this, they raised no further objection and praised God: "Then God has granted even to the Gentiles repentance that leads to life!" (11:18). Peter offers no abstract theological debate. He simply tells what God did and lets God's own deed settle the matter.

Meanwhile, believers scattered by the persecution following Stephen's death traveled as far as Phoenicia, Cyprus, and Antioch, sharing the word only with Jews. Yet some men from Cyprus and Cyrene came to Antioch and began speaking to Greeks as well, proclaiming the good news about the Lord Jesus. The Lord's hand was with them, and a great number believed and turned to the Lord (11:19–21). Unnamed everyday believers launch the first major Gentile-mixed church—without any apostle initiating it.

News of this reached the church in Jerusalem, and they sent Barnabas down to Antioch. Arriving and seeing evidence of God's grace, he was overjoyed and encouraged all of them to remain true to the Lord with devoted hearts, for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and faith. A large crowd was brought to the Lord. Then Barnabas traveled to Tarsus to look for Saul, and finding him, brought him back to Antioch. For an entire year the two met with the church and taught large numbers of people. It was in Antioch that the disciples were first called Christians (11:22–26). Once again, Barnabas is the one who pulls Saul right into ministry.

During those days some prophets came down from Jerusalem to Antioch. One of them, named Agabus, stood up and predicted through the Spirit that a severe famine would sweep across the entire Roman world—a famine that indeed took place during the reign of Emperor Claudius. Each disciple decided, according to their ability, to send financial relief to the believers living in Judea. They carried this out, delivering their gift to the elders through the hands of Barnabas and Saul (11:27–30). The young Gentile church already knows how to care for the mother church back in Jerusalem.

## Key Takeaways

**Prejudice melts before clear testimony of what God has done.** Rather than losing his temper or pulling apostolic rank when challenged by the circumcision faction in Jerusalem, Peter walks quietly through every step of God's leading and lands on an unanswerable question: if God gave them the exact same Holy Spirit, who am I to block God? (11:4–17). That plain account turns criticism into praise (11:18).

**Unnamed believers plant a new missionary hub in Antioch.** While the apostles remain in Jerusalem, nameless refugees from Cyprus and Cyrene boldly share the gospel with Greeks in Antioch—the third-largest city in the Roman Empire—giving birth to a vibrant multiethnic congregation (11:19–21).

**Cross-border brotherhood proves itself through encouragement and relief.** Barnabas arrives in Antioch not to police the new church, but to celebrate God's grace and recruit Saul from Tarsus for a full year of teaching (11:22–26). In turn, when Agabus foretells famine, the Antioch believers immediately collect aid for struggling brothers and sisters in Judea (11:27–30).

## Deeper Meaning

**Repentance itself is a gift granted by God.** The Jewish believers' amazed exclamation in 11:18—that God has granted even Gentiles repentance leading to life—reveals that turning to God is never purely a human achievement, but begins with God's gracious initiative opening the heart (5:31; 2 Timothy 2:25).

**The Antioch church fulfills the promise of a house of prayer for all nations.** Old Testament prophets had long envisioned a day when Gentile nations would seek the Lord and worship alongside Israel (Isaiah 49:6; 56:7; Zechariah 2:11). In Antioch, Jews and Greeks worship side by side in such numbers that outsiders must coin a distinct name for this brand-new community (11:26).

**Mutual care binds Jerusalem and Antioch into one unified church.** Jerusalem sends Barnabas south to nurture Antioch spiritually, and Antioch sends famine relief back north to sustain Judean believers physically (11:22; 11:29–30). Later in Romans, Paul explains this very principle of reciprocity: Gentiles who have shared in Jerusalem's spiritual blessings rightly serve them in material things as well (Romans 15:26–27).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Luke retell Cornelius' story all over again right here in chapter 11?** Modern readers can find the repetition surprising, yet in ancient storytelling—and especially in Luke's writing—repeating a major scene (Saul's conversion three times, Cornelius twice) signals its supreme historical weight. The official recognition of Gentile inclusion by the whole Jerusalem church in 11:18 matters just as much as Cornelius' conversion itself.

**Where did the name "Christians" (11:26) come from?** The word blends a Greek root with a Latin ending, meaning "Christ's people" or "partisans of Christ." Many historians think residents of Antioch—notorious for inventing witty nicknames—originally coined it for this mixed group of Jews and Greeks who could no longer be classified simply as a Jewish sect. The label appears only three times in the entire New Testament (11:26; 26:28; 1 Peter 4:16), and believers gradually embraced it with honor.

**The elders of Jerusalem (11:30) make their first appearance.** Without ceremony or explanation, the closing verse of chapter 11 introduces "the elders" of the Jerusalem church receiving the relief offering brought by Barnabas and Saul. This leadership model grew naturally out of the council of elders in Jewish synagogues, and Paul and Barnabas will later appoint elders in every newly planted congregation (14:23).

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**—himself born, raised, and a preacher for many years right in Antioch—speaks with special pride of 11:26 in his sermons to the city's believers. He urges the Antioch Christians not merely to boast that their city was the first to wear the name "Christian," but to live worthy of that title through genuine love and humility. *(Homilies on the Statues, Homily 17; and Homilies on the Acts of the Apostles, Homily 25)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's civic pride and moral exhortation to Antioch over the name "Christian" in Acts 11:26 are very well known -->
