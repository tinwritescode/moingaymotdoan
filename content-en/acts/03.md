# Acts 3 — The Lame Man at the Beautiful Gate

## Story

At the ninth hour of prayer—three in the afternoon—Peter and John were walking up to the temple together. A man who had been crippled from birth was carried every day and placed at the temple entrance called the Beautiful Gate, so he could beg for alms from those entering the temple courts. Seeing Peter and John about to go in, he asked them for money. Both men fixed their gaze on him, and Peter said, "Look at us." The man gave them his full attention, hoping to receive something from them (3:1–5).

Peter said, "Gold and silver I do not have, yet what I possess I give to you: in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk!" Taking him by the right hand, Peter pulled him up. Immediately the man's feet and ankles grew strong. He sprang to his feet, stood upright, and began walking. Then he entered the temple with them, walking and leaping and praising God (3:6–8). He had asked for a few coins; he received a working pair of legs.

All the people saw him walking around and praising God. Recognizing him as the beggar who always sat at the Beautiful Gate, they were filled with wonder and astonishment at what had happened to him. While the man clung tightly to Peter and John, the whole astonished crowd ran up to them in Solomon's Colonnade (3:9–11).

Seeing this, Peter addressed the people: Men of Israel, why does this amaze you? Why do you stare at us as though we had made this man walk by our own power or godliness? The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—the God of our fathers—has glorified his Servant Jesus, whom you handed over and disowned before Pilate when he had decided to release him. You disowned the Holy and Righteous One and asked for a murderer to be granted to you instead; you killed "the Lord of life," yet God raised him from the dead, and we are witnesses to that fact. It is through faith in his name that this man, whom you see and know, has been made strong; the faith that comes through Jesus has given him complete healing right in front of you all (3:12–16). Peter refuses to take even a shred of credit for himself.

Now, brothers, I know that you and your leaders acted out of ignorance. Yet in this way God fulfilled what he had foretold through the mouths of all the prophets, that his Christ would suffer. Repent, therefore, and turn back, so that your sins may be wiped clean, that seasons of refreshing may come from the Lord, and that he may send the Christ appointed for you, namely Jesus. Heaven must hold him until the time when all things are restored, as God spoke long ago through his holy prophets (3:17–21). A sharp indictment concludes with an open call to repent.

Moses said: the Lord God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your own brothers; listen to everything he tells you. Anyone who refuses to listen to that prophet will be completely cut off from the people. Indeed, all the prophets from Samuel onward have also announced these days. You are heirs of the prophets and of the covenant God made with your fathers when he promised Abraham, "All the families of the earth will be blessed through your offspring." When God raised up his Servant, he sent him to you first to bless you by turning each of you away from your wicked ways (3:22–26).

## Key Takeaways

**Power comes from the name of Jesus, not from wealth or apostolic virtue.** The beggar crippled from birth hoped only for spare change, yet Peter and John offer him far more: complete restoration in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth (3:6–8). When an amazed crowd rushes over, Peter immediately deflects all praise away from himself and directs their eyes to the risen Lord (3:12–16).

**Even the gravest sin still leaves a door open for repentance.** Peter does not soften the truth: they disowned the Holy One, asked for a murderer's release, and killed the Lord of life (3:14–15). Yet right after that blunt charge, he holds open the road home: repent so that your sins may be wiped away and refreshing may come from the Lord (3:19–20).

**Jesus is the focal point of the entire Old Testament.** From Moses' promise of a coming prophet to the testimony of every prophet from Samuel onward and God's covenant oath to Abraham, every thread of promise in Israel's story converges in Jesus (3:22–26).

## Deeper Meaning

**A leaping lame man announces that the messianic era has arrived.** Isaiah had foretold that when God comes to rescue his people, "the lame will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:5–6). Seeing a man lame from birth walk and leap into the temple praising God is not merely an isolated cure; it is a living signal that the promised restoration has already begun (3:8).

**Isaiah's suffering Servant is now glorified.** Twice in his speech Peter calls Jesus God's "Servant," declaring that God has glorified him while also naming him the Righteous One who had to suffer (3:13–14; 3:18; 3:26). This language ties directly back to Isaiah's Servant Song, where the righteous Servant bears wounds for the people and is then lifted high by God (Isaiah 52:13; 53:11).

**The prophet like Moses has stepped onto the stage.** Before his death, Moses promised that God would raise up a prophet like him from among their own people, warning that whoever refused to heed him would be called to account and cut off (Deuteronomy 18:15–19). Peter declares that Jesus is that prophet, meaning a person's response to him decides whether they remain part of God's covenant people (3:22–23).

**The blessing of Abraham's covenant is turning away from evil.** God promised Abraham that all peoples on earth would be blessed through his offspring (Genesis 12:3; 22:18). In the chapter's closing verse, Peter defines the heart of that blessing: not material riches or political dominance first of all, but God turning each individual away from wickedness (3:25–26).

## Notes & Nuances

**The apostles still kept the rhythm of temple prayer.** Peter and John heading to the temple at the ninth hour shows that the earliest believers did not view themselves as breaking away to form an alien religion disconnected from the Old Testament Scriptures (3:1). To them, trusting Jesus meant stepping into the true fulfillment of their ancestors' faith.

**Saying they acted in ignorance does not mean they were innocent.** When Peter tells the crowd and their leaders that they acted out of ignorance (3:17), his tone echoes Jesus' prayer from the cross (Luke 23:34). Under Old Testament law, sins committed in ignorance still required atonement, yet they differed from defiant, high-handed sin that could not be pardoned (Numbers 15:27–31); thus the door to repentance remained wide open for them (3:19).

**Does "the time of restoring all things" (3:21) mean everyone will be saved?** Some thinkers throughout history have appealed to this phrase to teach universalism—the idea that every soul, and even the devil, will eventually be restored. Yet just two verses later Peter explicitly warns that anyone who will not listen to that prophet will be cut off from the people (3:23). Most commentators therefore understand the phrase to describe the new heaven and new earth when all creation is freed from decay, just as Paul and Revelation envision (Romans 8:19–22; Revelation 21:1–5).

**Where was the Beautiful Gate located?** Historians and archaeologists still debate two main candidates: the gleaming Corinthian-bronze Nicanor Gate linking the Court of the Gentiles with the Court of the Women, or the eastern Shushan Gate. Whichever gate it was, the detail that a crippled man previously excluded outside now walks straight into the temple courts leaping and praising God paints a striking contrast.

## What Commentators Say

**Thomas Aquinas**, thirteenth-century Catholic theologian, is linked with a famous anecdote tied to 3:6: visiting Pope Innocent IV while money was being counted, the pope remarked playfully that the church no longer had to say "silver and gold I do not have," and Aquinas replied that neither could the church any longer say "rise and walk." **Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor and commentator, observes on 3:6 that God regularly gives far better things than people dare to ask: the lame man begged only for coins to survive in his disability, whereas God gave him health to stand up, walk, and worship. He also praises Peter's reaction in 3:12 for immediately disclaiming glory that belongs to God alone, like a faithful steward refusing to pocket his master's honor. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Acts 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident of Matthew Henry's core points on 3:6 and 3:12, though uncertain of his exact phrasing -->

**Darrell L. Bock**, American New Testament scholar, highlights the striking title "Lord of life" in 3:15—a Greek term that also conveys the sense of "Author" or "Originator" of life. He observes the sharp irony Peter draws: human beings executed the very source of their own life, yet that life could not stay buried and instead became the healing power restoring a crippled man right before their eyes. *(Acts, BECNT, 2007, on chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Bock discusses archegos and the contrast in 3:14–15 in BECNT, though it is a standard scholarly observation and exact wording is uncertain -->

**John Calvin** appeals to 3:21 to argue that Christ's resurrected physical body truly resides in heaven until his return, and therefore cannot be physically present in many places on earth at the same time.
