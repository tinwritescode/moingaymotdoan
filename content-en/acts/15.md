# Acts 15 — The Jerusalem Council

## Story

Certain men came down from Judea to Antioch and began teaching the believers, "Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved." Paul and Barnabas got into a sharp dispute and debate with them. So the church decided to send Paul, Barnabas, and several others up to Jerusalem to consult the apostles and elders about this question (15:1–2). Here stands the defining crisis of the early church: must Gentiles become Jews first before they can become Christians?

Sent on their way by the church, they traveled through Phoenicia and Samaria, sharing the news of how Gentiles were turning to God and bringing great joy to all the believers there. Arriving in Jerusalem, they were welcomed by the church, the apostles, and the elders, and reported everything God had accomplished through them. Yet some believers belonging to the party of the Pharisees stood up and insisted: Gentiles must be circumcised and required to obey the law of Moses (15:3–5).

The apostles and elders gathered to examine the question. After lengthy debate, Peter rose to speak: brothers, you know that in the early days God made a choice among you that through my mouth the Gentiles would hear the message of the gospel and believe. God, who knows the human heart, testified in their favor by giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us. He made no distinction between us and them, for he cleansed their hearts by faith. Now then, why do you put God to the test by placing on the necks of the disciples a yoke that neither our ancestors nor we ourselves have been able to bear? "On the contrary, we believe it is through the grace of the Lord Jesus that we are saved, just as they are" (15:6–11). Peter does not say Gentiles are saved like Jews; he says Jews are saved like Gentiles—in the exact same way, by grace.

The whole assembly fell silent as they listened to Barnabas and Paul describe the signs and wonders God had worked through them among the Gentiles. When they finished, James spoke up: brothers, listen to me. Simon has recounted how God first intervened to take from the Gentiles a people for his own name. The words of the prophets agree with this, just as it is written: after this I will return and rebuild David's fallen tent, so that the rest of humanity and all the Gentiles who bear my name may seek the Lord (15:12–18; Amos 9:11–12).

James continued: therefore my judgment is that we should not create unnecessary obstacles for Gentiles who are turning to God, but write instructing them to abstain from food polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from the meat of strangled animals, and from blood. For Moses has been preached in every city from ancient times and is read aloud in the synagogues every Sabbath (15:19–21). Gentiles do not keep the law to earn salvation, yet they are asked to avoid practices that would make shared table fellowship painful for Jewish believers.

The apostles and elders, together with the whole church, agreed to choose men from among themselves—Judas called Barsabbas and Silas, respected leaders among the brothers—to accompany Paul and Barnabas down to Antioch carrying an official letter. Addressed to the Gentile believers in Antioch, Syria, and Cilicia, the letter noted that unauthorized men had gone out from among them and unsettled their minds; now the council unanimously sends delegates alongside beloved Barnabas and Paul, men who have risked their lives for the name of the Lord Jesus Christ. "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us not to burden you with anything beyond these essential requirements": abstain from food offered to idols, from blood, from the meat of strangled animals, and from sexual immorality. "Farewell!" (15:22–29).

Dispatched to Antioch, the delegates gathered the congregation and handed over the letter. Reading it, the people rejoiced at its encouraging message. Judas and Silas, being prophets themselves, spoke at length to encourage and strengthen the believers. After spending some time there, they were sent back in peace to those who had sent them. Verse 34, stating that Silas decided to remain behind, does not appear in the earliest Greek manuscripts. Meanwhile Paul and Barnabas stayed on in Antioch, teaching and proclaiming the word of the Lord alongside many others (15:30–35).

Some time later Paul proposed to Barnabas: let us go back and visit the believers in every town where we preached the word of the Lord to see how they are doing. Barnabas wanted to take John, also called Mark, along with them. Paul, however, thought it unwise to take the man who had deserted them in Pamphylia and had not continued with them in the work. Their disagreement became so sharp that they parted company. Barnabas took Mark and sailed for Cyprus, while Paul chose Silas, was commended by the believers to the Lord's grace, and set out through Syria and Cilicia strengthening the churches (15:36–41). Scripture never hides the fact that two godly men quarreled fiercely. Yet years later Paul would write: bring Mark with you, "for he is very useful to me in ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11).

## Key Takeaways

**Salvation rests completely on grace through faith, with no ritual add-ons required.** When a group of Jewish believers insists Gentiles must be circumcised to be saved, the early church faces a threat of splitting straight down the middle (15:1). Peter settles the argument with a statement that overturns all religious pride: Gentiles are not saved on Jewish terms—rather, Jews themselves are saved through the grace of Jesus in the exact same way as Gentiles (15:11). Tacking any extra requirement onto faith turns good news into an unbearable yoke (15:10).

**Freedom in grace walks hand in hand with loving sensitivity in shared life.** While refusing to impose Moses' law on Gentiles as a condition for salvation, the council asks them to refrain from four things: food sacrificed to idols, sexual immorality, strangled meat, and blood (15:20; 15:29). James' reasoning is warmly practical: Jewish communities read Moses' law every Sabbath in cities everywhere, so casual Gentile eating and worship habits would offend them and wreck shared fellowship (15:21). True freedom never ignores a brother's conscience; love gladly limits itself to preserve church unity.

**The church resolves conflict by listening, testing experience against Scripture, and pursuing consensus.** Instead of trading accusations from afar in Antioch, the leaders sit down together in Jerusalem, hear firsthand reports from Peter, Barnabas, and Paul, and weigh their experience under the light of the prophets (15:6–18). The final verdict is no solo edict, but a unified, warm letter carried by trusted envoys who bring real encouragement to the churches (15:22–31).

**Even devoted servants clash sharply at times, yet God still works through both sides.** The split between Paul and Barnabas over John Mark proves Scripture never airbrushes its heroes (15:36–39). Paul prioritizes mission dependability, while Barnabas champions giving a stumbling young worker a second chance. In God's providence, one mission team becomes two heading in different directions, and years afterward Paul himself commends Mark as deeply useful (15:39–41; 2 Timothy 4:11).

## Deeper Meaning

**The promises to Abraham and David expand to all nations without canceling the old covenant.** James quotes Amos' prophecy that God will rebuild David's house so that every nation bearing God's name may seek the Lord (15:16–17; Amos 9:11–12). From the very start of God's plan, choosing Israel was never an exclusive dead end, but the channel through which every family on earth would be blessed (Genesis 12:3). As Gentiles trust Jesus and receive the Spirit, the old covenant is not discarded—it reaches its intended flowering.

**The four abstentions echo ancient guidelines for resident foreigners living among Israel.** Leviticus already contained specific rules regarding idol sacrifices, consuming blood, unbled meat, and unlawful sexual relations that applied equally to native Israelites and foreign residents living in their midst (Leviticus 17:8–14; 18:26). By drawing on those exact four points, James affirms that Gentiles join God's people as welcomed Gentiles, without having to convert into Jews.

**Chapter 15 serves as the hinge of the entire book of Acts.** Before this chapter, the story centers on Jerusalem and Peter; after it, the focus shifts decisively toward the Greek and Roman world with Paul, driving steadily toward Rome (1:8). Had the Jerusalem Council ruled the other way, Christianity might easily have shrunk into a minor Jewish sect instead of shining as good news for all humanity.

## Notes & Nuances

**How the Jerusalem Council in chapter 15 relates to Paul's visit in Galatians 2 remains debated.** Paul writes of going up to Jerusalem to meet the church's pillars and defend Gentile freedom (Galatians 2:1–10). Most scholars identify that trip with the council in chapter 15 seen from Paul's personal perspective; others place Galatians 2 earlier, during the famine-relief visit of chapter 11 (11:27–30). Either way, both accounts stand in complete agreement on the core point: Gentiles do not require circumcision to be saved.

**The four requests are not a brand-new legal code for salvation.** Readers sometimes wonder whether Christians today remain strictly bound by rules on blood and strangled meat, especially since Paul later addresses food offered to idols using principles of conscience and love (1 Corinthians 8:1–13; 10:25–29). Seen in context, sexual purity belongs to timeless moral law, while the food guidelines were crafted primarily to protect table harmony between Jewish and Gentile believers worshiping under one roof.

**Verse 34, reporting that Silas chose to stay in Antioch, is absent from the earliest manuscripts.** Many modern translations move that line to a footnote because the oldest Greek copies move directly from verse 33 to verse 35; a later scribe likely added it to clarify how Silas was back in Antioch ready to travel with Paul in verse 40. The small scribal variant changes nothing in the narrative's meaning.
