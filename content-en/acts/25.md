# Acts 25 — Appealing to Caesar

## Story

Three days after arriving in the province to take office, Festus went up from Caesarea to Jerusalem. The chief priests and leading Jewish figures presented their charges against Paul and pressed Festus, as a special favor to them, to have Paul transferred back to Jerusalem—secretly planning an ambush to kill him along the road. Festus replied that Paul was being held securely at Caesarea and that he himself would be heading back there shortly. He told them: let your leaders travel down with me, and if the man has done anything wrong, they can press charges against him there (25:1–5). Two full years have passed, yet their hatred has not cooled one bit; they dust off their old ambush plot for a brand-new governor.

After spending roughly eight or ten days with them, Festus went down to Caesarea. The next morning he took his seat on the tribunal and ordered Paul brought in. As soon as Paul stepped forward, the Jews who had come down from Jerusalem surrounded him, piling up numerous grave accusations that they could not substantiate. Paul made his defense: "I have done nothing wrong against the Jewish law, against the temple, or against Caesar" (25:6–8).

Eager to win favor with the Jews, Festus asked Paul, "Are you willing to go up to Jerusalem and stand trial before me there on these charges?" Paul answered: I am standing right now before Caesar's tribunal, where I ought to be tried. I have done no wrong to the Jews, as you yourself know very well. If I am guilty of any crime deserving death, I do not shrink from dying; yet if there is no truth in the accusations these men bring against me, no one has the right to hand me over to them. "I appeal to Caesar." After conferring with his council, Festus pronounced, "You have appealed to Caesar; to Caesar you shall go" (25:9–12). Paul knows going back to Jerusalem means certain death. Exercising his right as a Roman citizen sets in motion the Lord's promise from chapter 23: he will testify in Rome (23:11).

A few days later King Agrippa and Bernice arrived in Caesarea to pay their respects to Festus. Since they were staying for several days, Festus laid Paul's case before the king: there is a prisoner here left behind by Felix. When I visited Jerusalem, the chief priests and Jewish elders presented charges against him and demanded a conviction. I answered that Roman custom does not allow handing anyone over before the accused meets his accusers face to face and gets an opportunity to defend himself. So when they came here with me, I did not delay; the very next day I convened court and ordered the man brought in (25:13–17). Agrippa II was the great-grandson of Herod the Great, and Bernice was his sister.

Festus continued: when his accusers rose to speak, they brought none of the crimes I had anticipated. Instead, they merely argued with him about points of their own religion and about "a certain dead man named Jesus whom Paul insisted was alive." Uncertain how to investigate matters like these, I asked if he would like to go to Jerusalem and be tried there. Yet Paul appealed to be kept in custody for the Emperor's decision, so I ordered him held until I can send him to Caesar. Agrippa told Festus, "I would like to hear this man myself." Festus replied, "Tomorrow you shall hear him" (25:18–22). A pagan Roman official summarizes the gospel more accurately than he realizes: everything turns on a dead man who is alive.

The next day Agrippa and Bernice entered the audience hall with great pomp, accompanied by high-ranking military officers and prominent citizens of the city. At Festus' command, Paul was brought in. Festus declared: King Agrippa and all who are gathered with us, look at this man! The whole Jewish community both in Jerusalem and here has petitioned me, shouting that he ought not to live any longer. Yet I found he has done nothing deserving death; and since he himself appealed to the Emperor, I decided to send him. The trouble is, I have nothing definite to write to His Majesty about him. Therefore I have brought him before all of you, and especially before you, King Agrippa, so that after this examination I may have something to write. After all, it seems absurd to send off a prisoner without specifying the charges against him (25:23–27). A whole hall packed with earthly power gathers simply because nobody can find a crime in one single prisoner.

## Key Takeaways

**Politicians easily trade justice for popularity, yet believers stand firm on what is right.** Barely sworn into office, the new governor Festus feels heavy pressure from Jewish leaders demanding Paul's return to Jerusalem (25:1–3). Though knowing the charges lack evidence, Festus hopes to appease local elites by floating a transfer to Jerusalem—straight into an awaiting ambush (25:7–9). Faced with that compromise, Paul refuses to walk needlessly into a trap and calmly invokes his Roman right to appeal to Caesar (25:10–11).

**Amid political maneuvering, God's quiet hand guides Paul straight toward his promised goal.** Festus wants local goodwill, Jewish leaders want an assassination, and Paul simply wants a fair hearing (25:3; 25:9–11). Yet when Festus pronounces "to Caesar you shall go," he unwittingly signs the official travel order fulfilling the Lord's promise two years earlier that Paul would witness in Rome (25:12; 23:11).

**The heart of the gospel comes down to one decisive event: Jesus died, yet now lives.** Recounting the trial to King Agrippa with the bemused tone of an outsider Roman bureaucrat, Festus inadvertently captures all of Christian faith sharper than many long treatises: the entire controversy hinges on a man named Jesus who died and whom Paul insists is alive (25:19).

**Worldly splendor looks dazzling on the surface yet stands helpless before plain truth.** King Agrippa and Bernice march into the hall in gleaming ceremony beside military tribunes and civic dignitaries (25:23). Yet all that pomp assembles for an ironic embarrassment: Governor Festus cannot scrape together a single valid legal charge to put in an official dossier for Rome (25:25–27).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus' prophecy that Paul would bear his name before kings comes true word for word.** On the very day Saul believed in Damascus, the Lord told Ananias he had chosen this man to carry his name before Gentiles, kings, and the people of Israel (9:15). For well over a decade Paul preached across Israel and the Gentile provinces; now in chapter 25 palace doors swing open for him to testify before King Agrippa and head straight toward Caesar in Rome (25:12; 25:23).

**Repeated Roman declarations of innocence mirror the acquittal of Jesus himself.** Just as Pilate three times announced he found no basis for a charge against Jesus yet yielded to crowd pressure (Luke 23:4; 23:14; 23:22), now Lysias, Felix, and Festus each find nothing worthy of death in Paul (23:29; 25:25). Luke makes crystal clear to his readers that Christian faith poses no threat to public order or Roman law.

**God governs history even through seemingly routine legal and political decisions.** Nobody could have predicted that the church's foremost missionary would travel to the imperial capital not as a free traveler, but as a prisoner escorted at Rome's own public expense (25:12).

## Notes & Nuances

**Which Emperor Caesar did Paul appeal to (25:11)?** At this juncture (around AD 59–60), the sitting Roman emperor was Nero. Modern readers instantly associate Nero with the ferocious persecution of Christians in AD 64, yet during his first five years on the throne (roughly AD 54–59), guided by capable advisors such as the philosopher Seneca, Nero's administration was widely viewed as reasonably fair in judicial matters. Appealing to Caesar at this date was therefore a prudent and completely sound legal move.

**Who were King Agrippa and Bernice?** Herod Agrippa II was the son of King Herod Agrippa I—who had executed the apostle James (12:2)—and full brother of Drusilla, Governor Felix's wife. Ruling several northern territories in Palestine, he held Roman authority over the Jerusalem temple treasury and the appointment of high priests, making him deeply versed in Jewish affairs. Bernice was his full sister; contemporary Roman writers buzzed with scandalous rumors about an improper relationship between the two.

**Festus' description of Jesus in 25:19 captures how first-century Romans saw the new faith.** To an imperial administrator viewing life through bureaucratic eyes, Jewish disputes sounded like odd local superstition, and claims of a dead man being alive seemed baffling. Yet that detached pagan summary verifies that from the earliest days, Paul's central proclamation was always the literal resurrection of Jesus rather than vague moral philosophy.
