# Acts 4 — We Cannot Stop Speaking

## Story

While Peter and John were speaking to the people, the priests, the captain of the temple guard, and the Sadducees came up to them. They were deeply annoyed because the two were teaching the crowd and proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection of the dead. They arrested both men and held them in custody until the next day, since evening had already fallen. Even so, many who heard the message believed, and the number of men who believed grew to about five thousand (4:1–4). The Sadducees denied that any resurrection existed, so this message struck them right at their core.

The next day, the rulers, elders, and scribes assembled in Jerusalem, joined by Annas the high priest, Caiaphas, John, Alexander, and all members of the high priestly family. They brought Peter and John into the center and demanded, "By what power or in whose name did you do this?" Filled with the Holy Spirit, Peter replied: if we are being examined today over an act of kindness done to a disabled man and asked how he was healed, then let it be known to all of you and to the entire people of Israel that it is by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth—whom you crucified, and whom God raised from the dead—that this man stands completely well right in front of you (4:5–10). Only weeks earlier, these very same men had put Jesus on trial.

"This Jesus is the stone rejected by you builders, which has become the cornerstone." "Salvation is found in no one else, for under heaven there is no other name given among humanity by which we must be saved" (4:11–12).

Seeing the boldness of Peter and John and realizing they were ordinary, unlettered men, the council members marveled, and they recognized them as companions who had been with Jesus. Yet with the healed man standing right beside them, they had nothing they could say in reply. Ordering the two apostles to step outside the Sanhedrin, they conferred together: what are we going to do with these men? Everyone in Jerusalem knows an unmistakable miracle has happened through them, and we cannot deny it. Still, to keep this from spreading any further among the people, let us threaten them never again to speak to anyone in this name (4:13–17). Their wisdom did not come from elite academies, but from having walked with Jesus.

Calling them back in, they strictly forbade them to speak or teach at all in the name of Jesus. Peter and John answered, "Judge for yourselves whether it is right in God's sight to listen to you rather than to God." "As for us, we simply cannot stop talking about what we have seen and heard." After issuing further threats, the council released them, finding no safe way to punish them because of the public, for everyone was glorifying God for what had happened. The man healed through this miracle was over forty years old (4:18–22).

Once released, Peter and John went back to their own community and reported everything the chief priests and elders had said. Hearing the news, the whole group lifted their voices together to God in prayer: Sovereign Lord, Maker of heaven, earth, sea, and everything in them, you spoke by the Holy Spirit through the mouth of our ancestor David, your servant: why do the nations rage and the peoples plot futile schemes? The kings of the earth take their stand, and the rulers band together against the Lord and against his Anointed One. Indeed, Herod and Pontius Pilate, together with the Gentiles and the people of Israel, gathered in this very city against your holy Servant Jesus, doing whatever your hand and your purpose had decided beforehand would happen (4:23–28).

Now, Lord, take note of their threats and grant your servants boldness to proclaim your word without fear; stretch out your hand to heal and perform signs and wonders through the name of your holy Servant Jesus. When they finished praying, the place where they were meeting shook; all of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and kept speaking God's word with courage (4:29–31). They did not ask God to eliminate their enemies; they asked him to make them brave.

The whole body of believers was united in heart and mind. Nobody claimed any of their possessions as strictly their own, but they held everything in common. With tremendous power the apostles kept bearing witness to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and abundant grace rested upon them all. Not a single needy person remained among them, because owners of fields or houses sold them, brought the proceeds, and placed the money at the apostles' feet, and it was distributed to each person according to need (4:32–35).

Among them was Joseph, a Levite native to Cyprus, whom the apostles nicknamed Barnabas, which means Son of Encouragement. He owned a field, sold it, and brought the money to lay at the apostles' feet (4:36–37). Barnabas will reappear many times in this story, always lifting someone else up.

## Key Takeaways

**The gospel advances right through its first wave of opposition.** Healing the lame man triggers the church's first arrest, yet imprisonment cannot stop the number of believing men from climbing to about five thousand (4:1–4). Standing before the very council that condemned Jesus, Peter and John show no fear and proclaim clearly that salvation belongs to Jesus' name alone (4:8–12).

**True boldness grows out of being with Jesus, not from academic credentials.** The religious leaders are stunned that two fishermen without formal rabbinic training speak with such clarity and poise; all they can recognize is the unmistakable mark of having spent time with Jesus (4:13). Ordered into silence, the apostles answer plainly that they cannot obey human authorities over God (4:18–20).

**The church answers threats by praying for greater courage.** Rather than asking God to strike down their opponents or remove the danger, the believers place the persecution under the sovereign rule of the Creator and pray simply for boldness to speak his word (4:24–31). God answers at once with a fresh filling of the Holy Spirit.

**Spiritual unity proves itself through tangible generosity.** This community of one heart and mind cares for one another so thoroughly that no one goes without, illustrated by the sincere example of Barnabas selling his field and laying the money at the apostles' feet (4:32–37).

## Deeper Meaning

**The stone discarded by the builders becomes the cornerstone.** Peter cites Psalm 118 to interpret the paradox of the cross: the religious authorities who saw themselves as the builders of God's house threw Jesus aside, yet God himself installed him as the decisive foundation stone holding the entire structure together (4:11; Psalms 118:22). Shortly before his death, Jesus himself had applied this exact verse to his own rejection before the chief priests (Luke 20:17).

**Scripture transforms fear of earthly power into steady trust.** Under threat, the church immediately prays through Psalm 2 and recognizes that persecution is no accident spinning out of control: earthly kings and rulers have always conspired against God's Anointed, yet even in their rebellion they unwittingly carry out what God's hand and plan had already determined (4:25–28; Psalms 2:1–2).

**A community with no needy members fulfills the vision of Moses' law.** In Deuteronomy, God promised that there would be no poor among his people if they wholeheartedly listened to him and opened their hands generously to brothers and sisters in need (Deuteronomy 15:4; 15:7–11). When Luke notes that not a needy person remained among the believers (4:34), he shows the Spirit's grace bringing to life the ideal the law had long pointed toward.

**One unique Savior in a world crowded with gods.** The declaration that no other name under heaven has been given by which humanity must be saved (4:12) continues the exclusive claim of the Lord in Isaiah, who declares that apart from him there is no Savior (Isaiah 43:11; 45:21).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does it mean that Peter and John were "uneducated" in 4:13?** The Greek wording does not imply that the two men were illiterate; rather, it means they had never received formal training in the rabbinic schools of Jerusalem. What baffled the Sanhedrin was hearing two ordinary Galileans quote and interpret Scripture with the precision of trained scholars.

**Understanding the exclusive claim of 4:12 in a pluralistic world.** To many modern ears, stating that salvation exists in no one else can sound narrow-minded. Yet in context, Peter is not speaking from religious arrogance, but testifying as an eyewitness who just watched resurrection power heal a forty-year-old cripple. The doorway of salvation is narrow in having only one Savior, yet wide open to every person under heaven regardless of background or ethnicity.

**Civil disobedience in 4:19–20 has clear boundaries.** Scripture generally instructs believers to honor and submit to governing authorities (Romans 13:1–7; 1 Peter 2:13–17). Only when human power directly forbids what God commands or forces what God forbids must believers choose to obey God rather than people, accepting whatever penalty follows without resorting to violent retaliation.

**Why does Luke introduce Barnabas right at the end of chapter 4?** The snapshot of Barnabas selling his field and presenting the money does more than bring chapter 4 to an inspiring close; it sets up a direct contrast with Ananias and Sapphira at the very start of chapter 5 (5:1–11). Both sell land and lay money at the apostles' feet, yet one acts out of transparent generosity and the others out of calculated deceit.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century church father of Constantinople, marvels at Peter's transformation—from a man terrified into denying Jesus before a servant girl in the high priest's courtyard to a bold witness standing straight before that very council to proclaim the resurrection. For Chrysostom, nothing can account for such a turnaround except that Jesus truly rose from the dead and the Holy Spirit genuinely dwelt within them. *(Homilies on the Acts of the Apostles, Homily 10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the contrast between Peter before and after Pentecost is a well-known theme in Chrysostom's homilies on Acts -->

**John Calvin** treats 4:28 as one of Scripture's clearest statements on divine providence: Herod, Pilate, and the crowds acted out of their own wicked motives, yet everything remained bounded by what God's hand and purpose had ordained beforehand. Anchored in that conviction, the church in 4:29 refuses to panic when threatened. *(Commentary upon the Acts of the Apostles, on chapter 4 verse 28)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin cites Acts 4:28 in both his commentary and the Institutes to explain God's providence over human wickedness -->

Bruce** notes that a Levite like Barnabas owning land in 4:36–37 might puzzle readers of the Old Testament, since the ancient law stated that Levi had no territorial inheritance (Numbers 18:20).
