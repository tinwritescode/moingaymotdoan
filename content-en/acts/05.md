# Acts 5 — Obeying God Rather Than People

## Story

A man named Ananias, together with his wife Sapphira, sold a piece of property. With his wife's full knowledge, he held back part of the proceeds and brought the remainder to lay at the apostles' feet as if it were the entire sum. Peter asked, "Ananias, why has Satan so filled your heart that you lied to the Holy Spirit and kept back part of the money from the land? Before it was sold, didn't it belong to you? And after it was sold, wasn't the money still at your disposal? Why did you conceive this scheme in your heart? You have not lied to human beings, but to God" (5:1–4). Their sin was not keeping some money, but pretending they had given it all.

Hearing these words, Ananias collapsed and breathed his last. Great fear seized everyone who heard what had happened. Some young men stepped forward, wrapped his body, and carried him out for burial. About three hours later his wife walked in, unaware of what had taken place. Peter asked her, "Tell me, was this the exact price you got for the land?" "Yes," she replied, "that was the price." Peter said to her, "How could you agree together to test the Spirit of the Lord? Listen—the footsteps of the men who buried your husband are right outside the door, and they will carry you out too." Immediately she fell down at his feet and died. The young men came in, found her dead, and carried her out to bury her beside her husband. Great awe gripped the whole church and everyone who heard about these events (5:5–11). In the earliest Greek manuscripts, this is the very first time Acts uses the word "church," and it arrives in a moment of sobering reverence.

Many signs and wonders were continually performed among the people through the apostles' hands. The believers regularly met together in unity at Solomon's Colonnade. No outsider dared join their ranks casually, yet the general public held them in high honor. More and more men and women believed in the Lord and were added to their number. People even carried the sick out into the streets and laid them on cots and mats, hoping that as Peter walked by, at least his shadow might fall across some of them. Crowds also gathered from the towns surrounding Jerusalem, bringing the sick and those tormented by impure spirits, and every single one of them was healed (5:12–16).

Then the high priest and all his associates—members of the Sadducee party—were filled with jealousy, arrested the apostles, and put them in the public jail. During the night, however, an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors, led them out, and told them, "Go, take your stand in the temple courts, and proclaim to the people the full message of this life." Obeying that word, they entered the temple at daybreak and began teaching (5:17–21).

Meanwhile the high priest and his party convened the Sanhedrin along with the entire senate of Israel and sent officers to the jail to fetch the prisoners. When the guards reached the prison, they could not find them, so they came back and reported: we found the jail securely bolted with the guards standing at the doors, yet when we opened them, nobody was inside! Hearing this, the temple captain and the chief priests were utterly perplexed, wondering what would come of all this. Just then someone arrived with news: look, the men you locked in jail are standing right in the temple teaching the people! The captain went with his officers and brought the apostles back—without using force, because they feared the crowd would stone them (5:21–26).

They brought the apostles before the Sanhedrin, and the high priest interrogated them: we gave you strict orders never to teach in this name, yet you have filled Jerusalem with your teaching and are determined to pin this man's blood on us! Peter and the other apostles answered, "We must obey God rather than human beings." The God of our ancestors raised up Jesus, whom you killed by hanging him on a tree. God exalted him to his own right hand as Prince and Savior, to grant Israel repentance and forgiveness of sins. We are witnesses to these facts, and so is the Holy Spirit, whom God has given to those who obey him (5:27–32).

Furious at what they heard, the council members wanted to put them to death. Yet a Pharisee named Gamaliel, a teacher of the law honored by all the people, stood up in the council and ordered the men to be put outside for a moment. He then addressed the assembly: Men of Israel, think carefully about what you plan to do to these men. Some time ago Theudas rose up, claiming to be somebody, and about four hundred men rallied around him; he was killed, all his followers dispersed, and the whole movement came to nothing. After him, during the census, Judas the Galilean rose up and drew a crowd after himself; he too perished, and all his followers were scattered (5:33–37).

So in this present case I advise you: leave these men alone and let them go. If this plan or movement is of human origin, it will collapse on its own; but if it comes from God, you will not be able to overthrow them, and you may even find yourselves fighting against God. They took his counsel. Calling the apostles back in, they had them flogged, ordered them never to speak in Jesus' name, and released them. The apostles left the Sanhedrin rejoicing because they had been counted worthy of suffering disgrace for the Name. Day after day, both in the temple courts and from house to house, they never stopped teaching and proclaiming the good news that Jesus is the Christ (5:38–42). Beaten yet joyful, forbidden yet still preaching.

## Key Takeaways

**Hypocrisy inside the church threatens more than hostility outside it.** The Sanhedrin could not break the apostles' stride, yet right inside the fellowship Ananias and Sapphira tried to buy a reputation for generosity through deceit (5:1–4). Their sudden deaths remind the community that the Holy Spirit truly dwells among God's people and cannot be fooled (5:5–11).

**No prison wall or whip can lock up the message of life.** Even when jealous Sadducees throw the apostles in jail, an angel opens the doors and sends them straight back to the temple to keep preaching (5:17–21). Back before the council, they hold an unwavering line: we must obey God rather than people (5:29).

**Suffering disgrace for Christ's name becomes a reason for joy.** Following Gamaliel's cautious advice, the council spares the apostles' lives yet still has them flogged and ordered into silence; remarkably, they walk out glad to be counted worthy of suffering shame for Jesus, and by the next day they are right back to teaching without pause (5:38–42).

## Deeper Meaning

**Ananias and Sapphira repeat the sin of Achan at the threshold of a new era.** Right as Israel entered Canaan, Achan secretly kept back plunder devoted to God, and swift judgment at the dawn of that era safeguarded the holiness of the entire nation (Joshua 7:1–26). The Greek verb used for Ananias holding back part of the money is the exact term the Greek Old Testament uses for Achan's theft (5:2). Similarly, right after the tabernacle was consecrated, Nadab and Abihu faced immediate judgment for offering unauthorized fire before the Lord (Leviticus 10:1–3).

**The Holy Spirit is fully God.** Peter's back-to-back sentences in 5:3–4—first saying Ananias lied to the Holy Spirit, then immediately concluding he lied to God—provide one of the earliest and clearest New Testament witnesses that the Holy Spirit is not an impersonal force, but the personal God who can be lied to and put to the test (5:3–4; 5:9).

**The cursed tree becomes the fountain of repentance and forgiveness.** Peter deliberately reminds the council that they killed Jesus by hanging him on a tree (5:30), invoking Moses' law that anyone hung on a tree stands under God's curse (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). Yet the very One treated as accursed has been lifted to God's right hand as Prince and Savior to extend mercy to the very people who rejected him (5:31).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why was the punishment of Ananias and Sapphira so severe?** Many modern readers feel jolted by the sudden deaths of this couple. The key is seeing what their sin actually was: not keeping their property—Peter states plainly that both the land and the money were theirs to dispose of freely (5:4)—but conspiring together to deceive both the church and the Holy Spirit to win spiritual prestige. Just as with Achan or Nadab and Abihu, God delivered a decisive warning at the start of a new redemptive era to halt hypocrisy before it took root; and it was God who executed judgment, not Peter lifting a hand against them.

**Was expecting healing from Peter's shadow in 5:15 mere superstition?** Luke records the crowd's actual practice of laying the sick along the streets so Peter's shadow might pass over them, without ever claiming that a shadow holds magic power of its own—much like the bleeding woman who touched the fringe of Jesus' cloak or the handkerchiefs carried from Paul to the sick (Luke 8:43–48; 19:11–12). Healing power always belongs to God, who in compassion sometimes meets sincere faith even when it is mixed with naive assumptions.

**The chronological puzzle around Theudas and Judas the Galilean in Gamaliel's speech (5:36–37).** The Jewish historian Josephus records a rebel named Theudas who rose up around AD 44–46, more than a decade after this trial and well after Judas the Galilean (around AD 6). Scholars propose two main explanations: either an earlier rebel also bearing the fairly common name Theudas led an uprising prior to AD 6 that Josephus omitted, or Luke slipped on the chronological order when reconstructing Gamaliel's address.

**Does Gamaliel's principle hold true in every situation?** His pragmatic rule—if a movement is merely human it will fizzle out, whereas if it is from God nothing can stop it (5:38–39)—proved shrewd in the moment and saved the apostles' lives. Still, it is not an infallible test of truth in every case: history shows plenty of false movements enduring for centuries while genuinely good efforts sometimes get crushed in the short term.
