# Acts 20 — Farewell at Miletus

## Story

Once the uproar subsided, Paul summoned the disciples, encouraged them, said farewell, and set out for Macedonia. Traveling across that region, he spoke many encouraging words to the believers before coming to Greece, where he stayed three months. Just as he was about to sail for Syria, he uncovered a Jewish plot against him, so he decided to return overland through Macedonia. Accompanying him were Sopater son of Pyrrhus from Berea, Aristarchus and Secundus from Thessalonica, Gaius of Derbe, Timothy, and Tychicus and Trophimus from the province of Asia. These men went on ahead and waited for us at Troas. After the Feast of Unleavened Bread, we sailed from Philippi, caught up with them five days later in Troas, and stayed there seven days (20:1–6). This delegation brings together representatives from multiple Gentile churches, likely carrying the relief collection up to Jerusalem (1 Corinthians 16:1–4).

On the first day of the week we gathered to break bread. Since Paul planned to leave the next morning, he kept speaking to the believers right up to midnight. Many lamps were burning in the upstairs room where we met. Seated on a windowsill, a young man named Eutychus sank into a deep sleep as Paul talked on and on; completely overcome by sleep, he tumbled out from the third story and was picked up dead. Paul rushed downstairs, threw himself over the young man, embraced him, and said, "Do not be alarmed—his life is still in him." Then he went back upstairs, broke bread and ate, and talked at length until daybreak before leaving. The believers took the young man home alive and were immensely comforted (20:7–12). Here stands one of the earliest glimpses of Christians worshiping on Sunday—along with a gentle warning against sermons that run far too long.

We boarded the ship ahead of time and sailed for Assos, planning to pick Paul up there since he preferred to walk overland. Meeting him at Assos, we took him aboard and went on to Mitylene. Sailing from there, we passed opposite Chios the next day, crossed over to Samos the day after, and reached Miletus the following day. Paul had decided to sail past Ephesus to avoid losing time in Asia, because he was hurrying to reach Jerusalem in time for Pentecost if possible (20:13–16).

From Miletus he sent word to Ephesus asking the elders of the church to meet him. Once they arrived, he spoke to them: you know how I lived among you from the very first day I set foot in Asia. I served the Lord with complete humility, with many tears, and amid severe trials caused by Jewish plots. I never held back anything helpful to you, preaching and teaching you publicly and from house to house, testifying to both Jews and Greeks about repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus (20:17–21).

Now, compelled by the Spirit, I am heading to Jerusalem without knowing what awaits me there, except that in city after city the Holy Spirit testifies to me that chains and afflictions are waiting for me. "Yet I consider my life worth nothing to myself, if only I may finish my race and complete the ministry I received from the Lord Jesus—to testify to the gospel of God's grace." Now I know that none of you among whom I have gone about proclaiming the kingdom will ever see my face again. Therefore I declare to you today that I am innocent of the blood of everyone, for I did not shrink back from declaring to you the whole counsel of God (20:22–27).

Keep watch over yourselves and over the entire flock of which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to shepherd the church of God, which he purchased with his own blood. I know that after my departure fierce wolves will break in among you and will not spare the flock; even from your own ranks men will rise up speaking twisted things to draw disciples away after themselves. Stay alert, then, remembering that for three years, night and day, I never stopped warning each of you with tears (20:28–31). The gravest threat facing the church comes not only from outside foes, but from leaders arising within.

Now I entrust you to God and to the word of his grace, which has power to build you up and grant you an inheritance among all who are sanctified. I have coveted no one's silver, gold, or clothing. You yourselves know that these very hands worked to meet my own needs and the needs of my companions. In everything I showed you that by working hard like this we must support the weak, remembering the words the Lord Jesus himself spoke: "It is more blessed to give than to receive" (20:32–35). This saying of Jesus appears nowhere in the four Gospels; we have it solely because Paul preserved it here.

Having finished speaking, Paul knelt down and prayed with them all. Everyone wept openly, throwing their arms around his neck and kissing him. What grieved them most was his statement that they would never see his face again. Then they escorted him down to the ship (20:36–38).

## Key Takeaways

**A true shepherd serves with tears, candor, and readiness to lay down life itself.** In his deeply moving farewell at Miletus—the only sermon in Acts addressed exclusively to church leaders—Paul boasts of no resume highlights, recalling instead three years spent among them in humility, tears, and unflinching honesty in sharing God's full truth (20:18–20; 20:27; 20:31). Knowing chains and suffering wait in Jerusalem, he values his own survival less than finishing the mission Christ placed in his hands (20:22–24).

**The church belongs to God, not to its leaders, and was bought at an infinite price.** Paul tells the Ephesian elders first to watch over themselves, and then over the entire flock entrusted to them by the Holy Spirit (20:28). What makes shepherding so sacred is its price tag: God bought this church with his own blood (20:28). Remembering how dearly the flock was purchased keeps leaders from carelessness or treating a congregation like personal property.

**The church's sharpest danger often emerges right from inside its own circle.** Paul warns that after he leaves, not only will savage wolves attack from outside, but men from among the elders themselves will distort truth to build personal followings (20:29–30). Shepherds must stay vigilant, anchoring both themselves and their flocks to the word of God's grace rather than relying on any single charismatic personality (20:31–32).

**Authentic servants of Christ look to give rather than take.** Paul can look these leaders in the eye and say he never coveted anyone's silver, gold, or fine garments; his own calloused hands earned enough for himself and his team (20:33–35). Jesus' saying—"it is more blessed to give than to receive"—draws a clear line separating genuine shepherds from self-serving profiteers (20:35).

## Deeper Meaning

**Shepherd imagery at Miletus reaches straight back to Ezekiel and the Good Shepherd.** In Ezekiel, God sharply condemns Israel's false shepherds for feeding themselves while abandoning weak sheep to predators, promising that he himself will shepherd his flock (Ezekiel 34:2–12). Paul invokes Ezekiel's watchman metaphor when declaring himself innocent of anyone's blood because he faithfully warned them of God's full word (20:26–27; Ezekiel 33:7–9), entrusting the elders to care for sheep belonging to the Great Shepherd, Jesus (John 10:11; 1 Peter 5:2–4).

**Gathering on the first day of the week in Troas reveals the church's new weekly rhythm.** Rather than meeting exclusively on the seventh-day Jewish Sabbath, believers in Troas gather on the first day of the week—the day of Jesus' resurrection—for the Lord's Supper and Scripture teaching (20:7; 1 Corinthians 16:2). Paul bending down and embracing young Eutychus after his fall from the third floor echoes Elijah and Elisha stretching themselves over lifeless children in the Old Testament (20:9–10; 1 Kings 17:21; 2 Kings 4:34), confirming that God's resurrection life remains active inside his church.

**The tearful parting on the Miletus shore shows the church is a real family, not a cold institution.** Seasoned elders kneel together on the beach, throw their arms around Paul's neck, and weep freely at the thought of never seeing his face again (20:36–38). Faithful teaching always grows out of deeply affectionate, shared life.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 20:28 holds a rich textual nuance pointing toward Christ's deity.** Most modern translations—including the Berean Standard Bible—render the phrase "the church of God, which he purchased with his own blood" (20:28). Among ancient Greek manuscripts, some read "the church of the Lord," while the oldest and strongest manuscripts read "the church of God"; additionally, the Greek phrase translated "his own blood" can also be construed as "the blood of his own [Son]." Either way, this line stands among the earliest and strongest New Testament affirmations binding God's very identity to Jesus' sacrificial cross.

**In 20:17 and 20:28, "elders" and "overseers" (bishops) refer to the exact same leaders.** Verse 17 summons the "elders" of Ephesus, and verse 28 tells those exact same men that the Holy Spirit has appointed them "overseers" (Greek *episkopos*, often translated "bishop"). This shows that in the first-century church the two titles had not yet divided into separate hierarchical tiers as they did in later centuries, and local congregations were normally guided by a team of elders rather than one lone figure (Philippians 1:1; Titus 1:5–7).

**Paul's warning about wolves in Ephesus came true remarkably quickly.** Only a few years later, writing 1 Timothy to Timothy stationed right in Ephesus, Paul had to address false teachers spreading distortions inside this exact church (1 Timothy 1:3–7; 1:19–20). By the time of Revelation, Jesus commends the Ephesian congregation specifically for testing and exposing false apostles (Revelation 2:2).

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, treats Paul's Miletus address as the gold standard for every pastor and bishop. He highlights that Paul never confined his teaching to public halls, but moved from house to house admonishing individuals with tears (20:20; 20:31)—reminding shepherds that caring for souls can never be done casually from a distance. *(Homilies on the Acts of the Apostles, Homily 44)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Chrysostom holding up Miletus as the pastoral benchmark of tearful house-to-house care, though unsure of the exact homily number -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, points out from 20:28 that "elder" and "overseer" name one single office, arguing that original church governance was shared collaboratively rather than concentrated in one sole figure. On the phrase that God bought the church with his own blood, Calvin explains it through the unity of divine and human natures in the one person of Christ. *(Commentary upon the Acts of the Apostles, on chapter 20, verse 28)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's exposition of 20:28 on the equivalence of elders/overseers and the communication of idioms in Christ's two natures is very well known -->

**Richard Baxter**, seventeenth-century English Puritan pastor, built his classic handbook on pastoral ministry directly upon 20:28. He unpacks Paul's sequence—watch over yourself first, then over the entire flock: if a preacher lets his own spiritual life grow cold and careless, he can never faithfully guard the flock Christ has entrusted to him. *(The Reformed Pastor, 1656, opening exposition on chapter 20, verse 28)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Richard Baxter's entire classic work The Reformed Pastor is explicitly structured around Acts 20:28 -->

**F. F. Bruce**, twentieth-century Scottish New Testament scholar, observes that the Miletus speech reflects Paul's distinctive vocabulary and tone more closely than any other discourse in Acts, echoing his epistles because Luke stood right there listening firsthand.
