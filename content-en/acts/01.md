# Acts 1 — Waiting for the Father's Promise

## Story

The book opens with an address to Theophilus, the same recipient as the Gospel of Luke. The author recalls that in his first book he recounted all that Jesus began to do and teach until the day he was taken up into heaven. After his suffering, he presented himself alive to the apostles with many convincing proofs, appearing to them over forty days and speaking about the kingdom of God. He instructed them not to leave Jerusalem, but to wait for what the Father had promised: John baptized with water, whereas in a few days they would be baptized with the Holy Spirit (1:1–5). Luke's Gospel tells what Jesus "began to do and teach"; this book tells what he continues to do through his disciples.

Those gathered together asked him, "Lord, is this the time when you are restoring the kingdom to Israel?" He answered, "It is not for you to know the times or seasons that the Father has fixed by his own authority." Yet when the Holy Spirit comes upon them, they will receive power and serve as his witnesses "in Jerusalem, throughout all Judea and Samaria, and to the farthest reaches of the earth" (1:6–8). This verse provides the roadmap for the entire book: the narrative will move outward from Jerusalem to Judea, Samaria, and all the way to Rome.

After saying this, he was lifted up before their eyes, and a cloud hid him from sight. While they were still gazing up into the sky, two men dressed in white stood beside them and asked, "Men of Galilee, why are you standing here staring up into heaven? This same Jesus, who has been taken up from you into heaven, will return in the exact same way you saw him go into heaven" (1:9–11). Do not stand around staring at the sky; he will come back, and in the meantime there is work to be done.

They returned to Jerusalem from the Mount of Olives, a journey of about a kilometer, and went up to the upstairs room where they were staying. Present were Peter, John, James, Andrew, Philip, Thomas, Bartholomew, Matthew, James son of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot, and Judas son of James. All of them joined together constantly and with one mind in prayer, along with several women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers (1:12–14). Jesus' own brothers, who had previously not believed in him, are now part of the praying community.

In those days Peter stood up among the believers, a gathering of about one hundred and twenty people, and spoke: the Scripture that the Holy Spirit spoke beforehand through David's mouth concerning Judas, who served as guide to those who arrested Jesus, had to be fulfilled. Judas had been numbered among us and shared in this ministry. With the wages of his wickedness he acquired a field; then he fell headlong, his body burst open, and all his intestines spilled out. Everyone living in Jerusalem heard about this, so they called that field Akeldama, meaning Field of Blood (1:15–19).

Peter continued: in the book of Psalms it is written, "May his dwelling become desolate, with no one living in it," and again, "May another take his place of leadership." Therefore, from among the men who accompanied us throughout the entire time the Lord Jesus moved in and out among us, beginning from John's baptism until the day Jesus was taken up from us, one of them must join us as a witness to his resurrection (1:20–22).

They put forward two men: Joseph, called Barsabbas and also known as Justus, and Matthias. Then they prayed, "Lord, you know every human heart; show us which of these two you have chosen" to assume the apostolic ministry that Judas abandoned to go to his own place. They cast lots, the lot fell to Matthias, and he was numbered together with the eleven apostles (1:23–26). The twelve apostles are complete once more, like the twelve tribes of Israel, ready for what is about to come.

## Key Takeaways

**Jesus' work did not stop when he ascended to heaven.** Luke's opening phrase noting what Jesus "began to do and teach" signals that the first Gospel was only the opening chapter; the risen Jesus now keeps working through the Holy Spirit and the apostles (1:1–2).

**The disciples' calling is not to calculate dates, but to bear witness.** When the disciples still ask about the timing of Israel's national restoration, Jesus turns them away from curiosity about schedules reserved to the Father and hands them a clear map instead: receive the Spirit's power and go from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth (1:6–8).

**Biblical waiting does not mean sitting idle.** After watching Jesus ascend, the disciples do not linger staring into the clouds once the two men in white remind them of their calling; they head back to Jerusalem, devote themselves together to persistent prayer, and restore the ranks of the twelve apostles (1:10–14; 1:21–26).

## Deeper Meaning

**Forty days of preparation for a new era.** The forty days during which the risen Jesus appears and teaches about the kingdom of God echo Moses' forty days on the mountain receiving the law and Israel's forty years in the wilderness before entering the promised land (1:3; Exodus 24:18; Deuteronomy 8:2). A renewed people of God is being prepared to step out into the world.

**The departing Master leaves his Spirit to his successors.** The scene of Jesus being taken up while the disciples watch recalls the prophet Elijah ascending into heaven before Elisha's eyes, after which Elisha received his master's spirit to carry on an even broader ministry (1:9; 2 Kings 2:9–11). The cloud concealing Jesus also evokes the glory of God's presence and the promise of the Son of Man coming with the clouds of heaven (1:9; Daniel 7:13).

**Carrying light to the ends of the earth.** Verse 1:8 is not an improvised missionary strategy; it echoes God's commission to the Servant in Isaiah—not merely to restore the tribes of Israel, but to serve as a light to the Gentiles all the way to the ends of the earth (1:8; Isaiah 49:6).

**Restoring the full twelve apostles like the twelve tribes.** The urgency of replacing Judas before Pentecost shows that the number twelve was never incidental. The apostles represent the restored Israel that God is rebuilding around Jesus, reflecting his promise that they would sit judging the twelve tribes (1:15–26; Luke 22:30).

## Notes & Nuances

**How do the accounts of Judas' death in Acts and Matthew differ?** Matthew reports that Judas threw the silver back into the temple and went out and hanged himself, and that the priests used the money to buy the potter's field (Matthew 27:3–8); Acts says Judas acquired a field and fell headlong so that his body burst open (1:18). Many scholars view the two accounts as complementary: the field was bought with Judas' blood money, so it is attributed to him, while the grotesque fall could describe what happened afterward if the rope snapped or his body dropped onto rocks below. Others recognize them as two independent traditions preserved from separate sources.

**Apostolic office in chapter 1 carried strict qualifications.** When choosing Judas' replacement, Peter laid down two clear requirements: the candidate had to have walked with Jesus from John's baptism until the ascension, and had to be a firsthand witness of the resurrection (1:21–22). Because of this, the Twelve hold a unique foundational role in church history, even though later on the word "apostle" is occasionally applied more broadly to figures like Paul or Barnabas.

**Was casting lots for Matthias a mistake?** Some readers have argued that the disciples acted rashly in choosing Matthias instead of waiting for God to appoint Paul. Yet Luke's narrative offers no hint of disapproval: they prayed first, followed an established Old Testament practice trusting God's sovereignty over the lot (Proverbs 16:33), and Luke later refers naturally to "the Twelve" (6:2). This is also the final time casting lots appears in the New Testament, right before the Holy Spirit is poured out.

**Jesus' brothers had undergone a dramatic change.** During Jesus' earthly ministry, his brothers did not believe in him and even thought he was out of his mind (Mark 3:21; John 7:5). Yet after the resurrection, they appear in the upper room praying alongside the apostles (1:14); later, James the Lord's brother became one of the foremost leaders of the Jerusalem church (15:13; 1 Corinthians 15:7).
