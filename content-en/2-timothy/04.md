# 2 Timothy 4 — Preach the Word and Paul's Final Farewell

## Story

Entering the closing chapter of his final letter, Paul places Timothy before an awe-inspiring tribunal: in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus, who will judge the living and the dead, in view of His appearing and His kingdom. Then he delivers his supreme charge: preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and encourage with complete patience and careful teaching (4:1–2).

The reason for such steadfastness is clear: a time is coming when people will no longer tolerate sound doctrine, but following their own desires will gather around themselves teachers to scratch their itching ears. They will turn their ears away from the truth and wander off into myths. Amid that drift, Timothy must stay sober-minded in everything, endure hardship, do the work of an evangelist, and discharge all the duties of his ministry (4:3–5).

Paul then speaks of himself in some of the most moving words he ever wrote: his life is already being poured out like a drink offering, and the time of his departure is near. Looking back over the whole road behind him, he says calmly, "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith." From now on, the crown of righteousness is laid up for him; the Lord, the righteous Judge, will award it to him on that Day—and not to him alone, but also to all who have longed for His appearing (4:6–8).

From that solemn summit, Paul's tone turns remarkably intimate, down-to-earth, and tinged with loneliness. He urges Timothy to do his best to come to him soon. Demas, once a fellow worker, has deserted him out of love for this present world and gone to Thessalonica. Crescens has gone to Galatia (some ancient manuscripts read Galilee), and Titus to Dalmatia. Only Luke the physician remains at his side. Paul asks Timothy to pick up Mark and bring him along, because Mark is very useful to him for ministry—a deeply heartwarming note, since years earlier Paul had parted ways sharply with Barnabas over refusing to take Mark along (4:9–11; Acts 15:37–39). As for Tychicus, Paul has sent him to Ephesus to take Timothy's place (4:12).

From a damp cell with winter approaching, Paul asks for a few simple personal belongings: when Timothy comes, bring the cloak Paul left with Carpus at Troas, along with the scrolls, and especially the parchments (4:13). He also warns Timothy to watch out for Alexander the coppersmith, who did Paul great harm and fiercely opposed their message; Paul leaves repayment in the Lord's hands (4:14–15).

He recounts his first defense in court: no one stood up to support him; everyone deserted him. Yet he holds no grudge, praying that it may not be charged against them. Even when people abandoned him, the Lord stood by him and strengthened him, so that through him the message might be fully proclaimed and all the Gentiles might hear, and he was rescued from the lion's mouth. Confident that the Lord will rescue him from every evil deed and bring him safely into His heavenly kingdom, Paul lifts up eternal praise to God (4:16–18).

The letter closes with warm greetings to Priscilla and Aquila and the household of Onesiphorus; brief updates that Erastus stayed in Corinth and Trophimus was left sick in Miletus; one more plea to come before winter (when Mediterranean sea travel shut down); greetings from the believers in Rome; and a final benediction: the Lord be with your spirit, and grace be with you all (4:19–22).

## Key Takeaways

**The unchanging task of the preacher is to proclaim God's Word faithfully even when listeners would rather hear something else.** There will always be seasons when crowds prefer soothing flattery over sharp truth. Standing firm in Scripture is not about winning a contest with the culture, but answering to God and to the One who will judge the living and the dead (4:1–5).

**Those who have lived wholeheartedly for Christ can face death with peace and hope.** Paul does not view his looming execution as a tragic failure, but as a final drink offering poured on the altar, a race run all the way to the tape, and the threshold of receiving a crown from the righteous Judge (4:6–8).

**Even the greatest apostle needed friendship, forgiveness, a warm cloak, and Scripture in his final days.** Deserted by Demas and left alone at his trial, Paul still forgives those who failed him, reconciles with and prizes Mark, and longs to see Timothy and his parchments before winter sets in. Strong faith never erases deeply human needs (4:9–16).

**When everyone else walks away, the Lord Himself stands near and brings His people safely home.** Paul's testimony in 4:17–18 shows that ultimate deliverance does not necessarily mean escaping a death sentence, but being strengthened to finish one's mission and brought safely into the Lord's heavenly kingdom (4:16–18).

## Deeper Meaning

**The image of being "poured out like a drink offering" (4:6) links Paul's life to Old Testament sacrifice.** Under the Law of Moses, when a burnt offering was placed on the altar, the priest poured out wine alongside it as a drink offering fully devoted to God (Numbers 15:1–10; 28:7). Paul had used this image earlier when writing to the Philippians (Philippians 2:17); now at the end of his life, he views his own soon-to-be-shed blood as the final drops completing a lifetime of offering.

**The prayer "May it not be charged against them" (4:16) echoes the prayers of Jesus and Stephen.** While nailed to the cross, Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34); while being stoned to death right in front of the young Saul years earlier, Stephen knelt and prayed that the Lord would not hold that sin against them (Acts 7:60). Now, abandoned in court himself, Paul reflects the very forgiving spirit of his Lord and of the first martyr he once watched die.

**Being "rescued from the lion's mouth" (4:17) recalls both David's prayer and Daniel's deliverance.** In Psalm 22—the very psalm Jesus quoted on the cross—the righteous sufferer cries out to be saved from the lion's mouth (Psalms 22:21), and in the book of Daniel, God shut the lions' mouths to spare His servant (Daniel 6:22). For Paul, even though execution still lay ahead, surviving his first hearing so the gospel could be proclaimed to the Gentiles was already a remarkable divine rescue.

## Notes & Nuances

**What were "the parchments" (4:13) Paul asked Timothy to bring?** Two main writing materials existed in the ancient world: papyrus (cheaper and more fragile) and parchment made from treated animal skins (far more durable and costly). Most scholars think the papyrus "scrolls" may have been notebooks or copies of letters, while the prized "parchments" Paul longed to hold in prison before dying were copies of the Old Testament Scriptures (and perhaps early written collections of Jesus' sayings). The detail shows that even an apostle standing at death's door never stopped studying God's Word.

**What does Trophimus being left sick at Miletus (4:20) tell us about the gift of healing?** Just as with Epaphroditus's near-fatal illness (Philippians 2:25–27) and Timothy's stomach ailments (1 Timothy 5:23), Paul—who had performed remarkable healing miracles—could not simply heal his coworker Trophimus at will, and had to leave him behind to recover. This reminds believers that miraculous healing always remains subject to God's sovereign will, not a power humans can switch on whenever they choose.

**Mark's return to usefulness (4:11) is a deeply encouraging story of restoration.** Years earlier, during the first missionary journey, Mark turned back midway, leaving Paul so disappointed that he firmly refused to bring Mark on the second trip—triggering a sharp parting with Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39). Over time, however, Mark matured and returned to serve beside Paul (compare Colossians 4:10), to the point that in his final hours Paul specifically asks for him because "he is useful to me for ministry." Past failure is not the final word for someone who serves Christ.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, comments tenderly on 4:7–8: Paul says "I have fought the good fight" not to boast of his record before others, but to console a grieving spiritual son about to lose his father. He also notices that the crown of righteousness is not reserved for apostles alone, but open to "all who have longed for His appearing," so that every ordinary believer may share the same hope. *(Homilies on 2 Timothy, Homily 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: observing that Paul cites his faithfulness to console Timothy and extend hope to all who long for Christ's appearing is a familiar point in Chrysostom's ninth homily -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the renowned nineteenth-century London preacher, preached one of his most famous sermons from verse 13 alone: the cloak, the books, and the parchments. He observed that this seemingly minor personal request reveals a beautiful truth about God's servants: though inspired by the Holy Spirit, Paul still felt the chill of winter and still needed to read, study, and feed on Scripture down to his final breath. *(Sermon "Paul: His Cloak and His Books," 1863, on 2 Timothy chapter 4 verse 13)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's 1863 sermon "Paul: His Cloak and His Books" on 2 Timothy 4:13 is one of his most famous sermons -->

**John Stott**, the twentieth-century Anglican pastor and commentator, notes that 4:1–5 sets an ageless standard for Christian preaching: the measure of a faithful sermon is never the applause of crowds eager for flattering words, but faithfulness to God's Word before the Judge of the living and the dead. On 4:11, he calls Paul's praise of Mark one of the most moving examples of restoring grace in the New Testament. *(The Message of 2 Timothy, BST, 1973, on chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Stott's reflections on faithful preaching in 4:1–5 and Mark's restoration in 4:11 are clear points in his BST commentary -->

**N. T. Wright**, a British New Testament scholar, draws attention to the pointed contrast in 4:8 between "the Lord, the righteous Judge" and the unjust Roman imperial court trying Paul. Just as an earthly tribunal prepares to pronounce a sentence of death, Paul lifts his eyes to the true Judge of all the earth—who awards not condemnation, but a crown of righteousness to the faithful. *(Paul for Everyone: The Pastoral Letters, 2003, on 2 Timothy chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: contrasting the unjust Roman tribunal with Christ the righteous Judge in 2 Timothy 4:8 is a prominent insight in Wright's commentary -->
