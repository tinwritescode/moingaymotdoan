# 2 Timothy 3 — Difficult Times and All Scripture God-Breathed

## Story

Paul warns Timothy that in the last days times of real difficulty and peril will come. He then paints a blunt portrait of moral collapse when human hearts turn away from God: people will be lovers of self, lovers of money, boastful, arrogant, abusive, disobedient to parents, ungrateful, unholy, unloving, unforgiving, slanderous, without self-control, brutal, hostile to good, treacherous, reckless, conceited, lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God (3:1–4). Most dangerous of all, they keep up an outward appearance of godliness while denying its actual transforming power. Paul gives a crisp directive: avoid people like this (3:5).

He exposes how these hypocrites creep into the church: among them are men who slip into households and captivate vulnerable women burdened with sins and swayed by all kinds of desires—always eager to learn new ideas yet never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth. Just as Jannes and Jambres opposed Moses before Pharaoh, these men oppose the truth, corrupted in mind and disqualified regarding the faith. Yet they will not get very far, for their folly will eventually become plain to everyone, just as it did with those two ancient magicians (3:6–9).

Paul then turns to a striking contrast: "You, however, have followed my teaching." Timothy has closely observed and imitated Paul's teaching, conduct, purpose, faith, patience, love, and endurance—including the brutal persecutions and sufferings that struck him in Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra, Timothy's own home region. Paul endured them all, and the Lord rescued him out of every single one (3:10–11; Acts 13:50; 14:5; 14:19). From that lived experience he states an unvarnished reality: everyone who desires to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted. Meanwhile, evil people and impostors will go from bad to worse, deceiving others and being deceived themselves (3:12–13).

In a world thick with deceit and hostility, what can Timothy hold onto? Paul points to two firm anchors: continue in what you have learned and firmly believed, knowing those from whom you learned it—his mother, grandmother, and Paul himself—and recalling that from infancy he has known the Sacred Scriptures, which are able to make him wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus (3:14–15).

The chapter concludes with one of the most foundational statements about Scripture in the entire New Testament: all Scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching truth, rebuking error, correcting what is crooked, and training in righteousness—so that the servant of God may be complete, thoroughly equipped for every good work (3:16–17).

## Key Takeaways

**At the root of moral decay lies disordered love.** The long catalog in 3:2–4 begins with lovers of self and lovers of money, and closes with lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God. Once God is pushed off the throne of the heart, people may still wear a religious shell on the outside while lacking any real power to change within (3:1–5).

**Genuine godliness cannot avoid opposition, yet the Lord always stands by His own.** Paul never promises Timothy an easy road; recalling the stoning at Lystra, he states plainly that all who want to live godly lives in Christ will face persecution. Still, the Lord rescued Paul through it all, and He will sustain Timothy too (3:10–12).

**Scripture is the unshakable anchor in an age of confusion.** Amid attractive yet deceptive teachings, believers stand firm through Scripture—first because it leads them to wisdom for salvation through faith in Christ, and second because it comes directly from the breath of God (3:14–16).

**The purpose of Scripture is not merely accumulating information, but transforming life and equipping for service.** The four progressive uses in verse 16—teaching, rebuking, correcting, and training in righteousness—all converge on a single destination in verse 17: making the person of God complete and ready for every good work (3:16–17).

## Deeper Meaning

**The reference to Jannes and Jambres (3:8) recalls Moses' confrontation with Egypt's magicians.** Exodus recounts how Pharaoh's sorcerers used secret arts to mimic Moses' initial signs, until they hit a wall and had to admit, "This is the finger of God" (Exodus 7:11–12; 8:18–19). Ancient Jewish tradition preserved the names of the two leading magicians as Jannes and Jambres. Paul invokes them to assure Timothy that counterfeits can imitate truth on the surface for a while, yet in the end their emptiness before God's truth always comes to light.

**The term "God-breathed" (3:16) ties Scripture directly to God's creative breath.** The Greek compound word literally means breathed out by God. In Genesis, God breathed the breath of life into humanity's nostrils and man became a living being (Genesis 2:7); in the Psalms, the heavens were made by the word of the LORD and their starry host by the breath of His mouth (Psalms 33:6). Applying that image to Scripture means the Bible is not merely human religious reflection endorsed by God after the fact, but springs from the living breath of God Himself.

**The Old Testament Scriptures point straight to faith in Christ Jesus.** When Paul says the Sacred Scriptures Timothy learned from infancy—the Old Testament—can make him wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus (3:15), he echoes what Jesus Himself taught the two disciples on the road to Emmaus: the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms all bear witness to Him (Luke 24:27; 24:44; John 5:39).

## Notes & Nuances

**When are "the last days" (3:1)?** In the New Testament, this phrase does not refer only to a few years right before the end of history, but to the whole era stretching from Christ's first coming until His return (compare Acts 2:17; Hebrews 1:2). The proof sits right in verse 5, where Paul tells Timothy himself to avoid such people—showing that those difficult last days had already begun in the first century and continue to our own day.

**Should verse 16 be translated "All Scripture is God-breathed and useful" or "Every God-breathed Scripture is useful"?** Because the Greek sentence omits the linking verb, grammar alone allows two possible placements of "is." Yet the vast majority of modern scholars and trusted translations (including the BSB) adopt the first rendering, because Paul is not dividing Scripture into inspired and uninspired parts; having just named "the Sacred Scriptures" in verse 15, he now affirms that all of Scripture is breathed out by God and therefore wholly profitable.

**Verse 16 referred directly to the Old Testament when Paul wrote it, so does it apply to the New Testament as well?** When Timothy was a child, the only Scriptures in existence were the Old Testament. Even during the apostolic era, however, New Testament writings were already beginning to be grouped as "Scripture"—Paul himself cites a saying of Jesus recorded in Luke alongside Deuteronomy as "Scripture" (1 Timothy 5:18), and the apostle Peter places Paul's letters alongside "the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:16). Accordingly, the church across the ages has rightly received 3:16 as true of all Scripture, Old and New Testaments alike.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, applies 3:12 to comfort believers facing hostility: do not be surprised if living uprightly draws resentment, for a bright lamp in a dark room always troubles thieves. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, treats 3:16–17 as a core foundation for the sufficiency of Scripture (*Sola Scriptura*). He reasons that if Scripture alone suffices to make the person of God "complete, equipped for every good work," then human traditions added outside Scripture as binding requirements for faith are superfluous. *(Commentaries on the Epistles to Timothy, on 2 Timothy chapter 3 verses 16–17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: arguing for the sufficiency of Scripture from 2 Timothy 3:16–17 is a very famous point in Calvin's commentary -->

**B. B. Warfield**, the late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American theologian, wrote a classic philological study of the Greek word *theopneustos* in 3:16. He demonstrated that the term does not mean Scripture "breathes out God" or is merely filled with human inspiration, but carries a clear passive force: breathed out *by* God, originating directly from God's creative act. *(The Inspiration and Authority of the Bible, chapter "God-Inspired Scripture")* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Warfield's classic study on the passive "God-breathed" meaning of theopneustos in 2 Timothy 3:16 is widely cited worldwide -->

**John Stott**, the twentieth-century Anglican pastor and commentator, highlights the practical four-step design of 3:16: the first pair deals with doctrine ("teaching" what is true and "rebuking" what is false), while the second pair deals with life ("correcting" what is crooked and "training in righteousness" to walk straight). Thus Scripture both safeguards the mind from error and trains the hands for good works. *(The Message of 2 Timothy, BST, 1973, on chapter 3 verses 16–17)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: dividing the four uses of Scripture in 3:16 into two pairs covering doctrine and ethics is a well-known insight in Stott's BST volume -->
