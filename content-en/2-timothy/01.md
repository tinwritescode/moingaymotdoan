# 2 Timothy 1 — Fan Into Flame Your Gift and Do Not Be Ashamed of the Gospel

## Story

Paul writes this second letter from a Roman prison not long before his execution. He opens by identifying himself as an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, in keeping with the promise of life that is in Christ Jesus, addressing Timothy as his beloved child with a blessing of grace, mercy, and peace (1:1–2). In contrast to the more formal tone of his first letter, this entire epistle breathes the warmth and urgency of an aging father sending final words to his trusted son.

He thanks God—whom he serves with a clear conscience as his ancestors did—and says that night and day he remembers Timothy constantly in his prayers. Recalling Timothy's tears when they parted, he longs to see him again so that he may be filled with joy. He also remembers Timothy's sincere faith, a faith that first lived in his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice, and Paul is convinced now lives in him as well (1:3–5).

For this reason Paul urges Timothy to fan into flame the gift of God that is in him through the laying on of Paul's hands. After all, God has not given believers a spirit of fear and timidity, but of power, love, and self-control. Therefore Timothy must not be ashamed to testify about the Lord, nor ashamed of Paul, who is a prisoner for Christ's sake; instead, relying on the power of God, he is to share in suffering for the gospel (1:6–8).

Paul reminds him why this gospel is worth suffering for: God Himself has saved us and called us to a holy calling—not because of our own works, but because of His own purpose and the grace He gave us in Christ Jesus before the beginning of time. That grace has now been openly revealed through the appearing of our Savior Christ Jesus, who abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel (1:9–10). It is for this gospel that Paul was appointed a preacher, an apostle, and a teacher, and for this reason he is suffering. Yet he is not ashamed, for he knows whom he has believed, and he is convinced that God is able to guard what has been entrusted until that final Day (1:11–12).

He charges Timothy to hold fast to the pattern of sound teaching he heard from Paul, in the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus. By the Holy Spirit who dwells within, Timothy must guard the good treasure entrusted to him (1:13–14).

The chapter closes with a stark, realistic contrast in human loyalty during hardship. Paul reports that everyone in the province of Asia has deserted him, including Phygelus and Hermogenes. By contrast, he prays for the Lord's mercy on the household of Onesiphorus: that loyal friend refreshed Paul many times and was not ashamed of his chains; as soon as he arrived in Rome, he searched diligently everywhere until he found Paul in prison. Paul prays that the Lord will grant Onesiphorus mercy on the day of judgment, reminding Timothy that he knows better than anyone how devotedly Onesiphorus served Paul in Ephesus (1:15–18).

## Key Takeaways

**God's gift requires active renewal by the one who receives it.** The verb "fan into flame" in verse 6 pictures blowing glowing embers back into a bright fire. Under the pressure of persecution and exhaustion, even someone as faithful as Timothy can hesitate; yet the Spirit God gives is not one of fear, but of power, love, and self-control (1:6–7).

**Salvation rests wholly on God's eternal purpose and grace.** Paul insists that God saved us "not because of our works," but out of grace purposed in Christ before time began. Because Christ has shattered the power of death, suffering for the gospel is not defeat—it is sharing in His victory (1:8–10).

**Steadiness in hardship comes from knowing whom we trust.** Paul does not say he knows *what* he has believed, but "I know whom I have believed" (1:12). When abandoned and in chains, a believer's ultimate anchor is not an abstract system of ideas, but a living relationship with a faithful Lord.

**Loyal friendship in trouble is worth far more than endless praise in easy times.** When Paul was imprisoned in Rome, many acquaintances from Asia kept their distance out of fear of association. In that loneliness, a friend like Onesiphorus who went out of his way to track Paul down in prison became an unforgettable source of comfort (1:15–18).

## Deeper Meaning

**Three generations of faith in Timothy's family fulfill the Old Testament command to pass faith down to one's children.** When Paul recalls how Lois and Eunice passed sincere faith on to Timothy (1:5), we see a living embodiment of Moses' charge to Israel to teach God's words diligently to their children at home (Deuteronomy 6:6–7; Psalms 78:4–7). Even though Timothy's father was a Greek unbeliever (Acts 16:1), the steadfast faith of two women in that household laid the groundwork for one of the early church's most important leaders.

**Christ having "abolished death" (1:10) fulfills the ancient promises of the prophets.** In the Old Testament, Isaiah and Hosea foretold the day when God would swallow up death forever and break the power of the grave (Isaiah 25:8; Hosea 13:14). Through the resurrection of Jesus, that once-distant hope has now been brought into clear daylight through the gospel.

**The call "do not be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord" (1:8) echoes Jesus' own warning.** In the Gospels, Jesus told His disciples that whoever is ashamed of Him and His words in a sinful generation, of that person the Son of Man will also be ashamed when He comes in glory (Mark 8:38; Luke 9:26). Facing a death sentence, Paul proves with his own life that the gospel is the power of salvation and never a cause for shame (Romans 1:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was Lois Timothy's maternal or paternal grandmother (1:5)?** The Greek word used here simply means "grandmother," without specifying the maternal or paternal side. Because Acts records that Timothy's mother was a Jewish believer while his father was Greek (Acts 16:1), most commentators understand Lois to be his maternal grandmother—Eunice's mother—who raised Eunice and joined her in teaching Timothy the Scriptures from infancy (3:15).

**What is "what has been entrusted" in verse 12?** In the Greek, the phrase can be read in two directions: either what Paul has entrusted to God—his soul, life, and ministry—or what God has entrusted to Paul—the treasure of the gospel, using the very same Greek term repeated two verses later when Paul tells Timothy to guard "the good deposit entrusted to you" (1:14). Both senses are deeply true: God guards His servant and guards the gospel to which His servant gave his life.

**Is Paul praying for the dead in verse 18?** Many scholars notice that in 1:16 and 4:19 Paul blesses and greets "the household of Onesiphorus" without naming Onesiphorus directly, while in 1:18 he prays that the Lord may grant him mercy "on that Day," leading some to surmise that Onesiphorus had already died. However, it is equally possible that he was away traveling or still making his way back from Rome, so Paul sends blessings to his family at home in Ephesus. Since the text never states whether he was alive or deceased, commentators caution against building a doctrine of prayer for the dead on an uncertain inference.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, explains the call to "fan into flame the gift of God" (1:6) through the picture of a fire that needs fuel and airflow. In his view, a God-given gift can grow dim through sluggishness, fear, or neglect, yet blaze up again through courage, earnest prayer, and wholehearted service. *(Homilies on 2 Timothy, Homily 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the imagery of feeding a fire with wood and zeal in explaining 2 Timothy 1:6 is a familiar point in Chrysostom's first homily -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, points out how sharply 1:9 cuts against every notion of human merit: God did not foresee any good works in us as the basis for choosing us, since grace was already given in Christ "before time began." He also observes that 1:7 is medicine for servants of God who are naturally timid: when courage runs low, we do not force ourselves into recklessness, but ask the Holy Spirit for a spirit of power and sound judgment. *(Commentaries on the Epistles to Timothy, on 2 Timothy chapter 1 verses 7 and 9)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the argument for eternal grace against human merit in 1:9 is a foundational theological point in Calvin's commentary on 2 Timothy 1 -->

**John Stott**, the twentieth-century Anglican pastor and commentator, reads 1:8–14 as a fourfold charge to every generation of Christians facing social pressure: do not be ashamed of the gospel, be ready to suffer for the gospel, hold fast to the pattern of sound doctrine, and guard that treasure by the power of the Holy Spirit. In his view, the secret of such boldness lies in 1:12—knowing personally whom we trust. *(The Message of 2 Timothy, BST, 1973, on chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the structure of unashamed witness, suffering, and guarding the gospel deposit is Stott's main outline in his BST volume on 2 Timothy -->

**Gordon D. Fee**, an American New Testament scholar, observes that 2 Timothy reads like Paul's deeply personal final testament written in isolation in Rome. He argues that mentioning Phygelus, Hermogenes, and Onesiphorus right at the end of chapter 1 is not incidental closing chatter, but two contrasting examples placed directly before Timothy so he must choose which path he will walk. *(1 and 2 Timothy, Titus, NIBC, 1988, on 2 Timothy chapter 1 verses 15–18)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading the contrast between the believers from Asia and Onesiphorus as a direct challenge to Timothy is a clear point in Fee's NIBC commentary -->
