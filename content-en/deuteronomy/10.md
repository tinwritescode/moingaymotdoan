# Deuteronomy 10 — New Tablets of Stone and What the LORD Requires

## Story

Continuing the story of the golden calf and his intercession from chapter 9, Moses tells how God restored the broken covenant. The LORD instructed him to chisel out two new stone tablets like the first ones he had shattered, build a wooden ark, and come back up the mountain. Moses made an ark of acacia wood, chiseled two stone tablets, and climbed the mountain. The LORD Himself wrote on the new tablets the exact same Ten Commandments He had spoken from the fire and handed them to Moses. Moses carried them down and placed them inside the ark—and there they have remained ever since (10:1–5).

A brief narrative note recalls Israel's journey to Moserah, where Aaron died and his son Eleazar succeeded him as priest, and onward to Gudgodah and Jotbathah. At that time the LORD also set apart the tribe of Levi to carry the ark of the covenant, to stand before the LORD to minister to Him, and to pronounce blessings in His name. For that reason the Levites have no land inheritance among their brothers: the LORD Himself is their inheritance (10:6–9).

Moses closes his memory of Horeb: he stayed on the mountain forty days and nights as before, the LORD listened to his plea not to destroy the people, and God commanded him to rise and lead Israel onward to possess the land (10:10–11).

Flowing straight out of that mercy, Moses asks a question that sums up the entire life of faith: "And now, O Israel, what does the LORD your God ask of you?" He answers with five cascading verbs: fear the LORD your God, walk in all His ways, love Him, serve Him with all your heart and soul, and keep His commandments and statutes "for your own good" (10:12–13).

To help them see how lofty this calling is, Moses lifts their eyes to the grandeur of the One who chose them: heaven, the highest heavens, the earth, and everything in it all belong to Him; yet He set His affection on their ancestors and chose their descendants above all peoples. Therefore, "Circumcise your hearts, therefore, and stiffen your necks no more" (10:14–16).

Chapter 10 culminates in a magnificent portrait of God. He is "God of gods and Lord of lords," great, mighty, and awesome—yet His greatness bends directly toward society's most vulnerable: He "shows no partiality and accepts no bribe," executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the foreigner, giving them food and clothing. Therefore Israel must love the foreigner as well, because they themselves were foreigners in Egypt. They are to fear, serve, hold fast to the LORD, and swear only by His name—He who is their praise, the God who turned the seventy people who went down to Egypt into a nation as numerous as the stars in the sky today (10:17–22).

## Key Takeaways

**God willingly rewrites His covenant on new tablets after human failure shatters the old.** God's forgiveness does not stop at withholding punishment (10:10); He fully restores the relationship, rewrites the Ten Commandments with His own hand, and sends His people forward again (10:1–4; 10:11). Grace always gives God's people a new beginning.

**What God requires is genuinely for His people's good.** The fivefold call of 10:12–13—fear, walk, love, serve, obey—is not the selfish demand of a tyrant. Moses ends with its purpose stated outright: "for your own good." God owns heaven and earth (10:14) and needs nothing from us; every command exists to preserve our flourishing.

**God demands inward transformation of the heart, not merely external ritual.** Verse 10:16 uses the striking image of *circumcising the heart*—stripping away what is stubborn and calloused inside. Outward signs on the body mean nothing if the heart within remains obstinate and stiff-necked.

**The supreme God of the universe champions the smallest and most vulnerable.** Astonishingly, right after proclaiming Him "God of gods and Lord of lords" (10:17), Moses does not mention royal courts or war chariots; he immediately points to the orphan, the widow, and the foreigner (10:18). Anyone who truly worships this God must share His active compassion for the powerless (10:19).

## Deeper Meaning

**The question *What does the LORD ask of you?* (10:12) forms a golden thread linking Old and New Testaments.** Centuries later, the prophet Micah echoes this very question with his celebrated answer: act justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with your God (Micah 6:8). And when Jesus summarizes the entire Law into loving God and loving one's neighbor (Matthew 22:37–40), He brings together the exact two halves of Deuteronomy 10:12–19.

**The image of *circumcision of the heart* (10:16) becomes Paul's definition of God's true people.** The prophet Jeremiah repeated this appeal (Jeremiah 4:4), and Deuteronomy 30:6 later promises that God Himself will ultimately perform this surgery on His people's hearts. In the New Testament, Paul declares that true circumcision is circumcision of the heart, by the Spirit rather than by the written code (Romans 2:29; Colossians 2:11).

**The title *Lord of lords* and God's impartiality carry straight into Christ and the church.** Revelation hails the Lamb as Lord of lords and King of kings (Revelation 17:14; 19:16). Meanwhile Paul and James echo 10:17–18 directly, affirming that God shows no partiality (Romans 2:11; Ephesians 6:9) and that pure, undefiled religion before God means caring for orphans and widows in their distress (James 1:27).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 10:6–9 shift to the third person to mention Aaron's death and the tribe of Levi?** Reading straight from 10:5 to 10:10, Moses is speaking in the first person (*I*), when suddenly 10:6–9 shifts to the third person to note Aaron's death at Moserah and the setting apart of the Levites. Commentators commonly understand these verses as an explanatory note inserted right after mentioning the ark of the covenant (10:5), clarifying who was appointed to care for the ark and confirming that the priesthood continued intact after Aaron's death.

**Where does the number *seventy* who went down to Egypt (10:22) come from?** This figure matches Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5, counting Jacob's entire household when they migrated to Egypt during the famine. Moses sets that tiny number alongside the vast multitude standing before him to spotlight God's faithfulness to His promise to Abraham (Genesis 15:5).

**The command to *love the foreigner* in 10:19 carried deep social force.** In ancient Israel, *foreigners* (resident aliens) were non-Israelites living among them without inherited land or clan protection, leaving them acutely vulnerable to exploitation. Mosaic law did not merely forbid mistreating them; it required Israel to *love* them practically, just as God provides them food and clothing (10:18), grounded in Israel's own bitter memory of being outsiders in Egypt.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, lingers over 10:12–13 and notes how reasonable and gracious all of God's requirements are. He traces four motives Moses offers right in this chapter to inspire obedience: God's majesty as Owner of heaven and earth (10:14), His elective love for the ancestors (10:15), His justice and compassion (10:17–18), and the fact that He Himself is Israel's praise (10:21). *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Deuteronomy 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: summarizing the motives for obedience across 10:12–21 is characteristic of Matthew Henry's exposition of chapter 10; exact text not checked -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, explains in discussing *circumcision of the heart* (10:16) that every external rite God ordained was meant to point toward inward renewal. When Moses commands Israel to circumcise their own hearts, Calvin argues, he does not imply human beings can accomplish this without grace; rather, he sets out what God requires so they will humbly ask Him to grant the new heart He later promises to work Himself (30:6). *(Harmony of the Law, on Deuteronomy 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's theological interplay between the command to circumcise the heart in 10:16 and God's promise to do so in 30:6 is highly characteristic of his exposition; exact wording not checked -->

**Christopher J. H. Wright**, British Old Testament scholar, considers 10:17–19 one of the finest statements of social ethics in the Old Testament. Moses places two seemingly opposite truths side by side—God's cosmic transcendence over all powers (10:17) and His concrete care for food and clothing for the unprotected poor (10:18)—showing that true faith never separates worship from social justice. *(Deuteronomy, Understanding the Bible Commentary Series, 1996, on chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: analyzing the pairing of divine transcendence with care for the vulnerable in 10:17–19 is a highlight of Christopher Wright's commentary; exact pages not checked -->

Craigie**, twentieth-century Old Testament scholar, notes the seamless pivot between the two halves of chapter 10: the first half (10:1–11) recounts the gracious restoration of the covenant tablets, and the second half (10:12–22) draws out the response of love and obedience.
