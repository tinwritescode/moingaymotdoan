# Deuteronomy 5 — The Ten Commandments Restated

## Story

Moses summons all Israel to open his second great address: restating the heart of the law before they enter the Promised Land. He begins with a striking declaration to this new generation: the covenant at Horeb was not made only with their ancestors, but "with all of us who are alive here today." On that day the LORD spoke face to face with the people out of the fire, while Moses stood between them and God to relay His words, because the people were terrified of the fire and did not dare go up the mountain (5:1–5).

Moses then recites the Ten Commandments in full. The preface grounds everything in God's identity and His act of deliverance: He is the LORD their God, who brought them out of Egypt, "out of the house of slavery" (5:6).

The first four commandments shape Israel's relationship with God. First, they are to have no other gods before Him. Second, they must not make or bow down to any carved idol, for the LORD is a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generations of those who hate Him, but "showing loving devotion to a thousand generations of those who love Me and keep My commandments." Third, they must not misuse the name of the LORD. Fourth, they must observe the Sabbath day to keep it holy: six days they are to work, and on the seventh day everyone rests—not only householders, but children, male and female servants, livestock, and resident foreigners. Moses gives a vivid reason: remember that you yourselves were slaves in Egypt, and the LORD rescued you with a mighty hand (5:7–15).

The remaining six commandments govern family and community life. Fifth, honor your father and mother, that your days may be long and that it may go well with you in the land God is giving. Sixth, do not murder. Seventh, do not commit adultery. Eighth, do not steal. Ninth, do not give false testimony against your neighbor. Tenth, do not covet your neighbor's wife, house, field, servant, livestock, or anything belonging to your neighbor (5:16–21).

Moses reminds them that the LORD proclaimed these commandments in a thunderous voice from the fire, cloud, and deep darkness—"and He added nothing more"—before writing them on two stone tablets and handing them to Moses. Overwhelmed with fear, the tribal leaders and elders hurried to Moses. They marveled that mortals could hear the voice of the living God speaking from the fire and still live, yet they feared they would die if they heard it directly again. They asked Moses to go near, listen to everything the LORD says, and tell them, promising to hear and obey (5:22–27).

The LORD approved their request as well spoken. Then He uttered a deeply moving longing: "Oh, that their hearts would be inclined to fear Me and keep all My commandments always, so that it might go well with them and their children forever!" God sent the people back to their tents while Moses stayed to receive the detailed statutes and ordinances to teach them. From this foundation, Moses urges Israel to walk carefully in the whole way God commanded, turning neither right nor left, so they may live and prosper long in the land (5:28–33).

## Key Takeaways

**God's covenant is always addressed to today's generation, never merely an heirloom from the past.** Most of Moses' listeners had been small children—or not yet born—at Horeb. Yet Moses insists that God made His covenant "with all of us who are alive here today" (5:3). Every generation must answer God personally rather than coasting on the faith of its parents.

**The Sabbath is a day of freedom and compassion.** Whereas Exodus 20:11 anchors the Sabbath in creation—God creating in six days and resting on the seventh—Moses here anchors it in Israel's liberation from Egyptian slavery (5:14–15). People who know what it feels like to be exploited must never reduce their workers, or even their animals, to tools of endless labor.

**The Ten Commandments reach all the way into the human heart.** Moving past murder, adultery, theft, and false witness, the commandments strike at the hidden root in the tenth command: "Do not covet" (5:21). God does not merely police outward behavior; He cares about the desires inside the heart.

**God longs for His people's obedience for their own good.** The tender sigh of 5:29 opens a window into God's heart: He does not give laws to burden people, but because He genuinely wants life to go well for them and their children forever.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus reads the Ten Commandments from outward actions down into the heart.** In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus takes up these very commands from chapter 5—against murder, adultery, and false oaths—and shows that contemptuous anger already carries the seed of murder and a lustful gaze is already adultery in the heart (Matthew 5:21–37). This carries forward the spirit of the tenth commandment (5:21) and God's longing for a fearing, willing heart (5:29).

**Israel's request for a mediator (5:27) prepares the way for Christ the Mediator.** Israel recognized that sinful people cannot stand unmediated before the blazing holiness of God and needed Moses to stand between them. In the New Testament, Paul declares that there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus (1 Timothy 2:5), and Hebrews contrasts the terror of Mount Sinai with the gracious approach to Mount Zion through the blood of Jesus (Hebrews 12:18–24).

**The New Testament sums up our neighbor-directed commandments in love.** Paul lists the commands of 5:17–21—do not commit adultery, do not murder, do not steal, do not covet—and concludes that all of them are summed up in a single word: love your neighbor as yourself (Romans 13:9). He also singles out the fifth commandment to honor parents as the first commandment with a promise (Ephesians 6:2–3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Deuteronomy 5 differ slightly from Exodus 20?** Placing the two texts side by side reveals small but meaningful variations. For the Sabbath, Exodus 20:11 cites creation, whereas Deuteronomy 5:15 cites deliverance from slavery in Egypt. In the fifth commandment, 5:16 adds "and that it may go well with you." In the tenth commandment, 5:21 places "wife" ahead of "house" and uses a distinct verb to set a wife apart from property. These adjustments fit naturally into Moses' spoken address to a generation about to settle in the land, stressing humane concern and the memory of redemption.

**Is 5:9 unjust when it speaks of visiting fathers' iniquity on children to the third and fourth generation?** A hasty reading might suggest God punishes innocent children for their ancestors' sins. Yet the verse itself states a clear qualifier—"of those who hate Me"—referring to successive generations that continue walking in their ancestors' hostility toward God, alongside the sobering reality that sin's family and social damage ripples across several generations living under one roof. By contrast, the very next verse stresses that God's steadfast love extends "to a thousand generations" (5:10), and this very book explicitly forbids putting children to death for their fathers' sins or fathers for their children's (24:16).

**Different traditions number the Ten Commandments slightly differently.** Jewish tradition treats 5:6 (the declaration of deliverance) as the first word and combines 5:7–10 as the second. Protestant and Eastern Orthodox traditions treat 5:7 (no other gods) as the first and 5:8–10 (no idols) as the second. Catholic and Lutheran traditions combine 5:7–10 as the first and split 5:21 (coveting a neighbor's wife and coveting goods) into the ninth and tenth. Despite the different numbering, all three preserve the exact same tenfold content.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, views the first commandment (5:6–7) as the root from which the remaining nine grow. Once a person truly fears, loves, and trusts God above all things, honoring His name, keeping the Sabbath, respecting parents, and refusing to harm one's neighbor follow naturally; conversely, every sin against a neighbor begins by putting some idol in God's place. *(Large Catechism, on the Ten Commandments)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: treating the first commandment as the foundation from which all the other commandments flow is a famous central theme of Luther's Large Catechism -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, draws attention to the new reason given for the Sabbath in 5:14–15.
