# Deuteronomy 15 — The Year of Release and Freeing Servants

## Story

Chapter 15 opens with a remarkably compassionate economic law: at the end of every seven years, Israel must observe a release of debts. Anyone who has lent to a fellow Israelite must cancel the debt and stop demanding repayment, because the LORD's release has been proclaimed. Payment may still be collected from a foreigner, but what is owed by a fellow countryman must be released. Moses holds up a noble ideal: if Israel diligently obeys the LORD, there should be no poor among them, for God will bless them so richly that they will lend to many nations and borrow from none (15:1–6).

Yet Moses stays deeply realistic. Whenever a fellow Israelite in any of their towns falls into poverty, they must not harden their hearts or clench their fists, but open their hands wide and lend generously whatever is needed. Moses exposes a selfish calculation that easily creeps into the human heart: noticing that the seventh year of release is approaching and refusing to lend to the poor for fear of losing the loan. Moses names that attitude "a wicked thought": if the neglected poor person cries out to the LORD, the tightfisted neighbor stands guilty of sin. They must give freely without a grudging heart, and the LORD will bless all their work. Then Moses speaks a famous line: "There will always be poor people in the land," so Israel must always open its hand wide to needy brothers and sisters (15:7–11).

A second law protects fellow Israelites—whether men or women—forced by poverty to sell themselves into indentured service to pay off debt. They serve for six years; in the seventh year, the master must set them free. Even more, "do not send him away empty-handed": the master must equip them generously from the flock, threshing floor, and winepress, passing along the very blessing God has given him. The deepest reason is rooted in memory: Israel must remember they themselves were slaves in Egypt and the LORD redeemed them. If a servant genuinely loves his master's household and chooses freely to stay permanently, the master pierces his ear with an awl against the doorpost as a lifelong pledge—and the exact same rule applies to female servants. Nor should the master resent letting the servant go, since six years of service have already been worth twice the cost of a hired worker (15:12–18).

At the chapter's close, Moses reviews the law of firstborn males from cattle and sheep. They must be set apart as holy to the LORD—never working the firstborn ox or shearing the firstborn sheep—and eaten annually with one's household before God at His chosen sanctuary. If an animal has any defect such as lameness or blindness, it must not be sacrificed to the LORD, but may be eaten locally at home like gazelle or deer, always pouring the blood onto the ground like water (15:19–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Remembering past slavery teaches compassion for the desperate.** The deepest motive behind canceling debts, lending without calculation, and provisioning freed servants sits in 15:15: Israel once knew slavery in Egypt and was redeemed by the LORD. Memory of grace must translate into generosity toward the vulnerable (15:12–15).

**God's law reaches into hidden calculations of the heart.** Civil codes normally police only outward actions, yet here the unspoken thought—*the seventh year is near, so I will not lend*—is exposed as wickedness and sin. God sees not only whether our hand is open or closed, but whether our heart gives begrudgingly (15:9–10).

**True liberation requires a sustainable fresh start.** Releasing an indentured worker empty-handed would almost guarantee they fell straight back into debt and bondage. So the law requires masters to supply sheep, grain, and wine, giving newly freed people real capital to rebuild their lives (15:13–14).

**Give God and the needy what is whole and ungrudging.** From debt release to offering unblemished firstborn animals, the whole chapter teaches one consistent posture: never keep the best for yourself while handing leftovers or defective gifts to God or to the poor (15:10; 15:21).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus quotes 15:11 at Bethany.** When a woman pours costly perfume over Jesus' head and some disciples object that it could have been sold for the poor, Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 15:11 directly: "The poor you will always have with you" (Matthew 26:11; Mark 14:7). Far from dismissing the poor, He invokes the very verse commanding ongoing openhanded generosity toward them, while highlighting the unrepeatable moment just before His death for the world.

**The early church lived out the vision of no needy person among them.** Verse 15:4 sets the covenant goal that "there should be no poor among you" when Israel obeys God. In Acts, Luke echoes that very phrasing to describe the first Jerusalem believers: those with property shared so freely that there was not a single needy person among them (Acts 4:34–35).

**Jesus is both the Year of Release and the unblemished Lamb.** When Jesus launched His ministry in the Nazareth synagogue proclaiming the year of the Lord's favor and freedom for the oppressed, He brought the liberating spirit of the seventh year and Jubilee to spiritual fulfillment (Luke 4:18–19). Meanwhile, the unblemished firstborn of 15:19–21 foreshadows Christ—the spotless Lamb offered to redeem the world (1 Peter 1:18–19).

## Notes & Nuances

**Do 15:4 and 15:11 contradict each other?** Verse 15:4 says "there should be no poor among you," while 15:11 says "there will always be poor people in the land." Far from clashing, the two verses complement each other: 15:4 holds up the ideal goal an obedient community strives toward—ensuring no one is abandoned in destitution—while 15:11 faces realistic human conditions in a broken world so Israel never stops keeping its hand open.

**What does the *year of release* (15:1–2; 15:9) mean?** The Hebrew term *shemittah* literally means a *letting drop*, *unbinding*, or *canceling*. Rather than merely pausing interest temporarily, the command calls creditors genuinely to let go of their claim on fellow Israelites so that crushing debt cannot trap families across generations.

**How did servitude in Deuteronomy 15 differ from modern chattel slavery?** The situation in 15:12–18 involves an impoverished citizen contracting labor to settle debts for a maximum of six years—far removed from kidnapping, human trafficking, or lifelong ownership of people as property. Rather than endorsing slavery, this law sets clear guardrails protecting human dignity: capping service length, extending equal rights to female servants—more progressive than other ancient codes—and requiring generous severance gifts upon release.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, admires the sharp insight of 15:9 in exposing the hidden reasoning of wealthy hearts. Greed is remarkably clever at inventing respectable excuses to shut the door of mercy, he observes; only people who truly trust that blessing comes from God rather than from hoarding money will dare open their hands without regret. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Deuteronomy 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: analyzing the psychology of greed around 15:9 is characteristic of Matthew Henry's notes; exact text not checked -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, explains that 15:11 states the poor will always remain in the land precisely to test the charity of God's people in every generation. He adds that 15:4 does not promise automatic wealth without sharing; rather, God's abundance becomes a true blessing only as it flows through the hands of those who have plenty to those in need. *(Harmony of the Law, on Deuteronomy 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: harmonizing verses 4 and 11 around testing believers' charity matches Calvin's consistent exposition; exact text not verified -->

**Christopher J. H. Wright**, British Old Testament scholar, calls chapter 15 a charter of economic compassion. Highlighting the requirement that masters supply freed servants generously (15:13–14), he notes that biblical justice never stops at declaring freedom on paper; it provides concrete material resources so vulnerable people can stand securely on their own feet. *(Deuteronomy, NIBC, 1996)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Wright regularly uses Deuteronomy 15 to expound economic ethics and restorative justice; specific page numbers not checked -->
