# Deuteronomy 16 — Three Great Festivals and Righteous Judges

## Story

Chapter 16 outlines the three great annual pilgrimage festivals, weaving Israel's agricultural calendar together with the story of their redemption. The first is Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread in the month of Abib, the month when the LORD brought them out of Egypt by night. For seven days no leaven may be found anywhere in their territory; they eat unleavened bread—called "the bread of affliction"—to remember all their lives how hastily they fled Egypt. Unlike that first night in Egypt, the Passover animal is no longer slaughtered privately in hometowns, but sacrificed, roasted, and eaten at sunset solely at the LORD's chosen place. On the seventh day they hold a solemn assembly and rest from all work (16:1–8).

The second is the Feast of Weeks (later called Pentecost). Beginning from the day the sickle first enters the standing grain, they count seven full weeks and celebrate before the LORD. They bring a freewill offering in proportion to how the LORD has blessed them, rejoicing together at the chosen sanctuary—sons and daughters, male and female servants, Levites, resident foreigners, orphans, and widows alike. Moses adds a familiar reminder: remember that you were slaves in Egypt, and carefully follow these decrees (16:9–12).

The third is the Feast of Tabernacles, celebrated for seven days after gathering in the produce of both the threshing floor and the winepress. Once more, entire households along with Levites, foreigners, orphans, and widows gather at the chosen place to be "altogether joyful" because God has blessed their harvest and all the work of their hands. In summary, three times each year—at Unleavened Bread, Weeks, and Tabernacles—every Israelite man must appear before the LORD at His chosen place, never coming empty-handed, each giving in proportion to the blessing God has bestowed (16:13–17).

At verse 18, the law shifts to a major new section continuing through the next chapters: organizing public leadership across society. First comes the justice system. Israel must appoint judges and officials in every town throughout their tribes to administer righteous judgment. The standard is unmistakable: never pervert justice, never show partiality, and never accept a bribe, "for a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise and twists the words of the righteous." Moses presses home the charge: "Follow justice and justice alone," so that they may live and hold fast to the land the LORD is giving them (16:18–20).

The final two verses guard the sanctuary courtyard from pagan contamination: Israel must never plant a wooden Asherah pole or erect a sacred stone pillar beside the altar of the LORD, for God detests such things (16:21–22).

## Key Takeaways

**The rhythm of God's people revolves around redemptive memory and gratitude.** The three festivals do not merely track barley, wheat, and autumn harvests; they keep reminding Israel where they came from and who feeds them. Time itself stays anchored in God rather than drifting aimlessly by (16:1; 16:10; 16:15).

**Giving matches blessing received, never pressured comparison.** Moses instructs Israel not to appear empty-handed before God, then immediately sets a gracious standard: "Every man must appear with a gift as he is able, according to the blessing the LORD your God has given you." God looks for proportionate gratitude rather than expecting the poor to match the wealthy (16:16–17).

**Festival joy always leaves room for the vulnerable.** Both the Feast of Weeks and the Feast of Tabernacles carefully name those easiest to overlook: servants, Levites, resident foreigners, orphans, and widows. A celebration before God is incomplete unless the poor rejoice too (16:11; 16:14).

**Impartial justice is the bedrock keeping a nation standing.** Moving straight from three festivals to appointing judges within the same chapter shows how tightly worship and social justice belong together. Bribery is perilous precisely because it blinds even genuinely wise people (16:18–20).

## Deeper Meaning

**All three great festivals find rich fulfillment in Christ and the Spirit.** Passover reaches fulfillment when Jesus dies as our Passover Lamb sacrificed for humanity, and Paul urges the church to keep the festival free from the yeast of malice and evil (1 Corinthians 5:7–8). The Feast of Weeks—Pentecost—became the exact day the Holy Spirit was poured out, launching the church's first spiritual harvest of three thousand new believers (Acts 2:1–4; 2:41).

**Jesus at the climax of the Feast of Tabernacles.** In John's Gospel, on the last and greatest day of the Feast of Tabernacles in Jerusalem, Jesus stands up and cries out, inviting anyone who is thirsty to come to Him and drink living water (John 7:2; 7:37–38). Tabernacles also looks ahead to the final day when God pitches His dwelling among all nations in the new creation (Revelation 7:9; 21:3).

**The cry for pure justice echoes through the prophets and the New Testament.** Verse 16:20 literally doubles the Hebrew word for *justice* ("justice, justice you shall pursue"). Later prophets like Isaiah and Amos relentlessly confronted worshipers who observed grand festivals while taking bribes and crushing the poor at the city gate (Isaiah 1:11–17; Amos 5:21–24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Changes in Passover observance between Exodus 12 and Deuteronomy 16.** On the original night in Egypt, each family slaughtered its lamb at home and roasted it over fire (Exodus 12:3–9). By Deuteronomy 16:5–7, looking ahead to settled life in Canaan, Passover is centralized at the one chosen sanctuary to prevent it from turning into local paganized rituals. The detail in 16:7 commanding them to *roast* or *cook* the meat—using a Hebrew verb that can broadly mean cooking by fire or boiling—has been discussed and harmonized in several ways by Jewish and Christian interpreters (cf. 2 Chronicles 35:13).

**Why does 16:16 require only *all your men* to appear three times a year?** Assigning mandatory pilgrimage to adult men as family representatives spared pregnant women, nursing mothers, and the frail from grueling long-distance travel. Even so, verses 16:11 and 16:14 warmly welcome daughters, wives, and female servants to join the festival rejoicing, just as Elkanah and Hannah or Joseph and Mary traveled up to the sanctuary together each year (1 Samuel 1:3–7; Luke 2:41–42).

**Flowing straight into chapter 17.** The final two verses (16:21–22), banning Asherah poles and sacred pillars beside the altar, actually introduce the judicial procedures against idolatry that open chapter 17. Chapter divisions were added many centuries later and occasionally cut across a single continuous train of thought.

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, delights in 16:15 ("and you will be altogether joyful"). **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, comments incisively on the ban against bribery in 16:19. Scripture warns that bribes *blind the eyes of the wise* specifically to humble those confident in their own intellect and integrity: nobody is sharp enough to take gifts from litigants and remain genuinely impartial in judgment. *(Harmony of the Law, on Deuteronomy 16)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting how bribes corrupt even wise judges aligns closely with Calvin's commentary style; exact text not verified -->

**Peter Craigie**, twentieth-century Canadian Old Testament scholar, highlights the major turning point at 16:18: from here through the end of chapter 18, Moses outlines the four institutional pillars of Israelite society—judges, kings, priests, and prophets. Placing judges first signals that the covenant nation rests on righteous rule of law rather than arbitrary personal power. *(The Book of Deuteronomy, NICOT, 1976)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: identifying the fourfold leadership structure from 16:18 through chapter 18 is a standard feature of Craigie's commentary; exact page numbers not verified -->
