# Deuteronomy 33 — Moses Blesses the Tribes of Israel

## Story

Before climbing Mount Nebo to die, Moses—honored with the solemn title *the man of God*—leaves a final poetic blessing over Israel. He opens with a majestic vision of the LORD dawning like sunrise from Sinai, Seir, and Mount Paran amid myriads of holy angels, fiery law streaming from His right hand. Truly God loves His people, holding all His holy ones in His hand while they sit at His feet receiving His word, reigning as King over the assembled tribes of Jeshurun (33:1–5).

Next, Moses blesses each tribe in turn. He prays that Reuben may live and not die out, though his numbers be few; and that the LORD hear Judah's cry, restore him to his people, and stand beside him against his foes. Reserving special solemnity for Levi, Moses recalls the Urim and Thummim entrusted to God's faithful ones, praises their uncompromising loyalty above even parents, brothers, and children to guard God's covenant, and affirms their calling to teach Jacob God's judgments and offer incense and burnt offerings on His altar (33:6–11).

Turning to Benjamin, Moses calls him "the beloved of the LORD," resting securely beside Him and sheltered safely all day long. Moses pours out his richest blessing on Joseph—encompassing both Ephraim and Manasseh—praying for the choicest gifts of heaven above, morning dew, deep springs below, sunlit harvests, and the favor of Him who dwelt in the burning bush; in might Joseph stands like a firstborn bull whose wild-ox horns gore opposing nations (33:12–17).

The remaining tribes each receive bright promises. Zebulun rejoices in his seafaring trade and Issachar in his tents, both summoning peoples to the mountain for righteous sacrifices and drawing abundance from the seas. Gad crouches fierce as a lioness, having chosen the choicest eastern portion yet marching ahead with Israel's leaders to carry out God's justice. Dan leaps like a lion cub from Bashan; Naphtali brims with favor and blessing around the Sea of Galilee; and Asher enjoys the favor of his brothers, dipping his foot in abundant olive oil, with iron and bronze bolts securing his gates and strength matching all his days (33:18–25).

The poem closes in a soaring hymn of praise. There is no one like the God of Jeshurun, who rides across the heavens and clouds to rescue His people. "The eternal God is your dwelling place, and underneath are the everlasting arms." He drives out enemies before them so Israel dwells securely in a land overflowing with grain, new wine, and dew from heaven. Moses bursts out in a final exclamation: how blessed is Israel, saved by the LORD—their protecting shield and glorious sword (33:26–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Moses' parting words to Israel are blessing, not bitterness.** After the tearful warning song of chapter 32, Moses does not leave his people in resentment. Knowing full well how often they will stumble, he still looks at them through the lens of God's grace and speaks life over every tribe (33:1; 33:29).

**God's law is not cold chains, but a gift of love.** Right at the opening, Moses joins two truths together: God's right hand delivers fiery law, and "Indeed, You love the people" (33:2–3). In Scripture's eyes, God gives commandments to Israel as a loving father hands a safe map to his cherished child.

**Every tribe carries a distinct calling under one shared King.** Levi tends the altar and teaches Scripture, Judah leads in struggle, Joseph harvests abundance, Zebulun sails the seas, and Benjamin rests securely beside God (33:7–19). Diversity within God's people does not divide them—it weaves them together under the LORD's protection.

**Humanity's deepest refuge rests in God's *everlasting arms*.** Verse 27 gives us an unforgettable picture of comfort: above us, the eternal God is our home; beneath us, His everlasting arms hold us up (33:27). No matter how steep the road ahead or how dearly we miss departing leaders, those who belong to God can never fall into emptiness.

## Deeper Meaning

**Moses' blessing builds on Jacob's earlier blessing and reveals the power of redemption.** On his deathbed, Jacob had sternly rebuked Simeon and Levi for their violence at Shechem and foretold they would be scattered throughout Israel (Genesis 49:5–7). Yet by Deuteronomy 33:8–11, Levi's story has completely turned around: because they stood with the LORD during the golden calf crisis (Exodus 32:25–29), that old scattering became a blessing—dispersed across every tribe as priests teaching God's word.

**Later temple history and Christ Himself fulfill bright strands of this poem.** Benjamin was promised peaceful dwelling beside the LORD between His shoulders (33:12)—and Jerusalem with its temple later rose right along the Judah-Benjamin border. Jesus grew up and carried out much of His ministry across the Galilean territories of Zebulun and Naphtali (33:18–19; 33:23; Matthew 4:13–16); He Himself is the One who appeared in the burning bush (Exodus 3:2–6; John 8:58), now become the eternal refuge for all who trust Him.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why is the tribe of Simeon missing from chapter 33's blessing?** Attentive readers notice only eleven tribes named—Joseph is split into Ephraim and Manasseh, yet Simeon is absent. The most common explanation is that Simeon's allotted towns lay entirely inside Judah's sprawling territory (Joshua 19:1–9), so over time Simeon merged into Judah and shared in Judah's blessing in verse 7. Some also note Simeon's sharp population drop following the plague at Baal-peor (Numbers 25:14; 26:14).

**What were the Urim and Thummim in verse 8?** They were two sacred objects kept inside the high priest's breastpiece used to seek the LORD's decision in critical national matters (Exodus 28:30; 1 Samuel 28:6). Scripture never details their physical shape or exact procedure; their Hebrew names are associated with *lights* and *perfections*.

**Saying Levi *did not recognize his brothers or acknowledge his own children* (33:9) does not endorse neglecting family.** This alludes directly to the golden calf incident (Exodus 32:26–29), where Levites put loyalty to God above even blood ties to halt rampant idolatry. Jesus later used similarly bold language to teach that disciples must love Him above father, mother, wife, and children (Luke 14:26).

## What Commentators Say

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, preached some of his most beloved sermons on the closing promises of this chapter—above all "As your days, so shall your strength be" (33:25) and "Underneath are the everlasting arms" (33:27). He comforted exhausted believers that God does not stockpile tomorrow's strength today lest we grow proud, but grants ample strength for every day's exact burden—and no matter how low we fall, His everlasting arms always catch us underneath. *(Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit, sermons on Deuteronomy chapter 33 verses 25 and 27)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's sermons on "As thy days so shall thy strength be" and "Underneath are the everlasting arms" are among his most famous and widely quoted discourses -->
