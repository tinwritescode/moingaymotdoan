# Deuteronomy 32 — The Song of Moses and the Summons to Mount Nebo

## Story

Moses opens his song by calling heaven and earth to listen, praying that his teaching fall gently like rain and morning dew upon tender grass. He praises the LORD as *the Rock*—perfect, just, faithful, and upright. Standing in stark contrast is a crooked, foolish generation that repaid their own Father, the One who redeemed and shaped them, with base ingratitude (32:1–6).

The song looks back to ancient days to recall amazing grace. When the Most High assigned boundaries to all nations, He claimed Jacob as His own inheritance. He found them in a barren, howling wasteland, shielded and guarded them "as the apple of His eye," and carried them like an eagle spreading its wings to bear its young aloft. The LORD alone guided them to the heights of the land and fed them lavishly with honey, oil, curds, rich meat, finest wheat, and red grape wine. Yet when Jeshurun—an affectionate name for Israel—grew fat and sleek, he kicked back, abandoned the Rock of his salvation, chased after newly arrived pagan gods, and sacrificed to demons (32:7–18).

The LORD saw it all and burned with anger. He declared He would hide His face: because they provoked His jealousy with what are not gods, He would provoke their envy with a people who are no nation. Fire from His wrath blazes to the depths of Sheol; disasters, famine, plague, wild beasts, and sword strike young and old alike. God stays His hand from erasing their memory entirely only lest arrogant enemies boast that their own might—not the LORD—won the victory. If only Israel were wise enough to realize that unless their own Rock had handed them over, how could one enemy soldier ever chase a thousand? (32:19–33).

Then the song pivots toward judgment on Israel's foes and mercy for God's people. The LORD declares: "Vengeance is Mine; I will repay." When He sees His people's strength completely spent with no one left to help, He will have compassion, mock the useless idols asking where they are now, and assert His unmatched sovereignty: besides Him there is no god; He puts to death and brings to life, wounds and heals. Whetting His flashing sword to repay His adversaries, He invites all nations to rejoice with His people, for He will avenge the blood of His servants and make atonement for His land and His people (32:34–43).

Moses and Joshua recite the entire song aloud before all the people. Afterward, Moses pleads with them to take every word to heart and pass it to their children, because these are not empty, idle words—"they are your life." That very day the LORD tells Moses to climb Mount Nebo in the Abarim range of Moab, across from Jericho, view the land of Canaan from afar, and die on that mountain just as his brother Aaron died on Mount Hor. God recalls why: because both brothers failed to uphold His holiness among Israel at the waters of Meribah-kadesh, Moses may gaze upon the Promised Land from a distance, yet never step inside (32:44–52).

## Key Takeaways

**God stands as an unshakable *Rock* amid fickle human hearts.** The image of *the Rock* rings through the song like a steady refrain (32:4; 32:15; 32:18; 32:30–31). While human hearts swing wildly—grumbling in hardship, forgetting in comfort—God remains faithful, righteous, and completely unchanging.

**Comfort tempts people to forget the Hand that fed them.** Verse 15 sketches a painfully recognizable picture: a well-fed animal kicking the very master who raised it. Israel drifted from God far less in wilderness scarcity than once they grew sleek and comfortable in the Promised Land (32:13–18). Gratitude is often tested more severely by abundance than by want.

**God's word is not an extra burden bolted onto life—it is life itself.** Verse 47 distills the purpose of all Deuteronomy: Scripture is not pointless trivia to hear passively, but the true lifeline sustaining parents and children across every generation (32:46–47).

**God's justice and mercy walk together to the end of history.** He disciplines covenant unfaithfulness severely, yet when His people reach utter helplessness, He refuses to abandon them forever—returning with compassion, vindication, and atonement (32:36; 32:43). Ultimate hope always rests on God's faithfulness rather than human merit.

## Deeper Meaning

**The Song of Moses is one of the Old Testament passages quoted most often in the New Testament.** Paul quotes 32:21 to explain how God uses Gentile salvation to stir healthy jealousy in Israel (Romans 10:19), and quotes 32:43 summoning all nations to rejoice alongside God's people (Romans 15:10). Teaching believers never to take personal revenge but leave room for God's righteous judgment, Paul quotes 32:35 directly (Romans 12:19). Hebrews likewise quotes 32:35–36 to warn of God's sober judgment (Hebrews 10:30), and cites the command for all God's angels to worship Christ in Hebrews 1:6 from the ancient Greek (Septuagint) reading of 32:43.

**The *Rock* and the *Song of Moses* echo all the way into Revelation.** Recalling the wilderness trek, Paul writes that Israel drank from the spiritual Rock following them, "and that rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4); he also echoes 32:17's warning that sacrifices offered to idols are in reality offered to demons (1 Corinthians 10:20). At the Bible's climax, victorious saints standing beside the sea of glass sing "the song of Moses the servant of God and the song of the Lamb," celebrating the just and true ways of the King of the nations (Revelation 15:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does the name *Jeshurun* in verse 15 mean?** It is a poetic pet name for Israel meaning *the upright one* or *beloved* (33:5; 33:26; Isaiah 44:2). Placing that tender nickname directly alongside the picture of a pampered beast kicking back creates aching irony: the nation called *the upright one* behaved most crookedly.

**Why does Hebrews 1:6 quote Deuteronomy 32:43 as mentioning angels, while some translations omit that line?** The standard Hebrew Masoretic Text of verse 43 reads simply, *Rejoice, O nations, with His people.* However, the ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) and a Dead Sea Scrolls fragment discovered at Qumran preserve an additional line: *Let all God's angels worship Him.* Because the author of Hebrews regularly quoted the Septuagint, he cited that exact line in Hebrews 1:6.

**Verse 8 (*according to the number of the sons of Israel*) has a famous ancient textual variant.** The traditional Hebrew Masoretic Text reads *sons of Israel*, whereas the Dead Sea Scrolls and Septuagint read *sons of God* (or *angels of God*). Under either reading, the core truth of verses 8–9 remains identical: the Most High exercises supreme sovereignty over all humanity, yet chose Israel as His own special inheritance.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth-century English pastor, calls the eagle training its young to fly in verse 11 one of Scripture's tenderest pictures of divine care: God stirs up the nest so His people do not grow complacent, yet spreads broad wings right underneath whenever they falter. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, draws attention to 32:39 declaring that God both puts to death and makes alive, both wounds and heals. This single sentence dismantles every dualistic claim that good comes from God while disaster comes from an equal rival power; every event rests under God's sovereign hand so that when disciplined, His people run straight back to the very One able to heal them. *(Harmony of the Law, on Deuteronomy 32)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: underscoring God's undivided sovereignty over both discipline and healing in 32:39 matches Calvin's theology consistently; exact pages not checked -->

**Jonathan Edwards**, eighteenth-century American theologian and preacher, took the phrase "their foot will slip" from Deuteronomy 32:35 as the text for the most famous sermon in American history, *Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God*.
