# Deuteronomy 2 — Bypassing Kindred Nations and Defeating King Sihon

## Story

After the failure at Kadesh, Israel turned back into the wilderness toward the Red Sea and wandered around the hill country of Seir for many years. At last the LORD declared that they had circled that hill country long enough and told them to turn north (2:1–3).

Their new route passed through the territory of nations related to them by blood. First came the descendants of Esau, Jacob's brother, living in Seir. The LORD warned Israel to be very careful and not to provoke them to war, for He would not give Israel "even a foot's breadth" of their land—God Himself had given Mount Seir to Esau. Israel was to pay with silver for the food they ate and the water they drank, for throughout forty years the LORD had blessed all the work of their hands, watched over them, and seen to it that "you have lacked nothing" (2:4–7).

Leaving Seir, they moved along the wilderness road of Moab. Once again the LORD forbade them to attack Moab, because He had given Ar to the descendants of Lot. The text inserts a historical note: formerly the Emim, a people as tall as the Anakim, lived there, just as the Horites once lived in Seir before Esau's descendants drove them out (2:8–12).

Then the LORD told them to cross the Zered Valley. Moses carefully marks the timeline: from the time they left Kadesh-barnea until they crossed the Zered Valley was exactly thirty-eight years—long enough for the entire generation of unbelieving fighting men to perish from the camp, just as the LORD had sworn (2:13–16).

Once the old generation had passed away, the LORD spoke again. They skirted the border of Moab and approached the land of the Ammonites, who were also descendants of Lot. Again the LORD forbade Israel from encroaching on their territory. Another historical note explains that earlier the LORD Himself had destroyed the giant Rephaim—whom the Ammonites called Zamzummim—so the Ammonites could settle there, just as He had done for Esau's descendants against the Horites, and just as the Caphtorites had replaced the Avvim near Gaza (2:17–23).

At the Arnon Valley, everything changed. The LORD announced that He had handed over Sihon the Amorite, king of Heshbon, along with his land, and that from that very day He would begin to put the dread and fear of Israel upon all the peoples under heaven (2:24–25). Even so, from the Wilderness of Kedemoth, Moses sent messengers to Sihon with words of peace: asking only to pass along the main highway without turning left or right, offering to pay in silver for food and water just as they had done with Esau's descendants and Moab, until they crossed the Jordan (2:26–29).

Sihon refused. Moses explains that the LORD had made Sihon's spirit stubborn and his heart obstinate in order to deliver him into Israel's hands. Sihon marched out with his entire army to fight at Jahaz. The LORD handed them over to Israel; the Israelites struck down Sihon, captured every city from the Arnon Valley up to Gilead, and devoted men, women, and children to destruction, taking only the livestock and plunder. Not a single fortified town was too strong for them. Yet Israel strictly obeyed God's word and never went near the land of the Ammonites along the Jabbok River (2:30–37).

## Key Takeaways

**God is Lord over the history of every nation, not Israel alone.** The parenthetical notes about Esau, Moab, Ammon, and the Caphtorites (2:10–12; 2:20–23) are not digressions; they carry a sweeping theological claim. The LORD Himself assigns borders and grants land even to non-Israelite peoples. Israel has no license to seize anyone's territory outside the bounds God appoints.

**Even during years of discipline, God faithfully sustains His people.** Thirty-eight years of wandering were the consequence of unbelief at Kadesh (2:14–15). Yet Moses can still testify that across all forty years the LORD was with them and "you have lacked nothing" (2:7). God's discipline never cancels His care.

**Giants cannot block God's purpose.** At Kadesh, Israel had been terrified of the towering Anakim (1:28). Now Moses reminds them that even Edom, Moab, and Ammon had driven out giant peoples just like them because the LORD cleared the way before them (2:10–12; 2:20–22). Israel therefore had no reason to tremble before Canaan.

**The promise of victory begins coming true even before crossing the Jordan.** Triumphing over Sihon and his walled cities served as concrete proof to the new generation that when they follow God in obedience, no fortress can stand against them (2:36).

## Deeper Meaning

**God's sovereignty over the boundaries of nations echoes throughout Scripture.** The perspective in chapter 2 that God guided even the Caphtorites—ancestors of the Philistines—into their land (2:23) is boldly echoed by the prophet Amos, when the LORD asks Israel whether He did not also bring the Philistines from Caphtor and the Arameans from Kir (Amos 9:7). In the New Testament, the apostle Paul preaches in Athens that God has determined the appointed times and boundaries of the lands where every nation lives (Acts 17:26).

**Crossing the Zered marks the boundary between dying in unbelief and stepping into new grace.** The old generation had to come to a complete end before Israel moved into its season of victory (2:14–16). In the New Testament, Paul speaks in parallel terms of the old self dying so that the believer may walk in newness of life in Christ (Romans 6:4–6).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does it mean that the LORD hardened King Sihon's heart (2:30)?** The language immediately recalls Pharaoh in Egypt (Exodus 4:21; 9:12). In biblical thought, God holds ultimate sovereignty over history while human beings remain genuinely responsible for their choices. Here Sihon received a courteous offer of peace (2:26–29) yet actively rejected it and marched out to attack at Jahaz (2:32). His hardened heart was part of God's judgment on an Amorite ruler whose people's iniquity had reached its full measure (cf. Genesis 15:16).

**The command to *completely destroy* or *devote to destruction* in 2:34 sounds deeply unsettling.** The Hebrew term (*herem*) reflects an ancient Near Eastern concept of setting enemies or spoils completely apart to God as an act of divine judgment, holding nothing back for private gain. This raises a major ethical question in Deuteronomy and Joshua, which will be examined more thoroughly and candidly in chapter 7.

**Where do the parenthetical notes (2:10–12; 2:20–23) come from?** Careful readers notice that these geographic and ethnographic notes shift to the distant past tense and include the phrase "to this day" (2:22). Many scholars regard them as explanatory comments added by the author or a later editor so subsequent generations of readers could understand the background of ancient peoples who had long since disappeared.

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, draws a practical lesson in neighborly ethics from 2:4–7: even when God's people are strong and bound for their inheritance, they must not bully their neighbors, but respect others' property and pay fairly for what they use. **J. G. McConville**, British Old Testament scholar, places special weight on the historical notes in 2:10–12 and 2:20–23.
