# Deuteronomy 11 — A Land Watered by Rain from Heaven and Two Paths

## Story

Chapter 11 concludes the broad exhortation stretching from chapter 5 before Moses moves into specific statutes in chapter 12. He opens with a concise charge: love the LORD your God and always keep His statutes and commands. Then he speaks directly to the adults standing before him—not their young children who never saw these things—reminding them of the mighty acts their own eyes witnessed: what God did in Egypt to Pharaoh, how the Red Sea swallowed the Egyptian army and chariots, what God did for them in the wilderness, and how the earth opened its mouth to swallow Dathan and Abiram with their tents and households when they rebelled in the camp (11:1–7).

Because they witnessed these mighty deeds firsthand, they must keep every command so they will have strength to enter, conquer, and live long in "a land flowing with milk and honey." Moses draws a vivid contrast between old Egypt and new Canaan. In Egypt, flat land beside the Nile required farmers to sow seed and toil by watering it "by foot like a vegetable garden." Canaan, by contrast, is a land of hills and valleys that "drinks rain from heaven." It is a land the LORD personally cares for—"the eyes of the LORD your God are always upon it, from the beginning of the year to the very end" (11:8–12).

Because the land's life depends directly on rain from the sky, the bond between faithfulness and harvest is unmistakable. If they listen carefully, loving and serving the LORD with all their heart and soul, He will send both the autumn (early) rain and the spring (late) rain in season, yielding abundant grain, new wine, oil, and lush grass for their livestock. Conversely, if their hearts are enticed away to serve other gods, the LORD's anger will burn against them: He will shut up the heavens so no rain falls, the land will yield nothing, and they will quickly perish from the good land He is giving them (11:13–17).

Moses repeats the heartfelt charge from chapter 6: lay these words upon your heart and soul, bind them as signs on your hands and foreheads, teach them to your children when sitting at home, walking along the road, lying down, and getting up, and write them on your doorframes and gates, so that your days and your children's days may endure as long as the heavens are above the earth. If they faithfully love and cling to the LORD, He will drive out greater, stronger nations before them; every place where the soles of their feet tread—from the wilderness to Lebanon, from the Euphrates River to the Mediterranean Sea—will be theirs (11:18–25).

Finally, Moses sets Israel squarely at a crossroads: "See, today I am setting before you a blessing and a curse"—blessing if they obey the LORD's commands, curse if they turn aside to follow unfamiliar gods. He adds instructions for a solemn ceremony upon entering Canaan: pronounce the blessing on Mount Gerizim and the curse on Mount Ebal, twin mountains facing each other near the oaks of Moreh. They are about to cross the Jordan to possess the land, so they must be careful to follow every statute Moses sets before them today (11:26–32).

## Key Takeaways

**Firsthand experience of God brings corresponding responsibility.** Moses speaks straight to those who saw the Red Sea and the wilderness with their own eyes (11:2–7): knowing much and witnessing abundant grace is never grounds for pride, but a compelling call to deeper loyalty.

**Life in the Promised Land is a life of daily dependence on God.** Unlike Egypt's Nile irrigation canals, which could tempt farmers to think their own labor controlled the water supply, Canaan depended on rain from heaven (11:10–12). God placed His people in a land where every harvest taught them to look upward in trust.

**Obedience is never a half-measure, but a choice between life and death.** Moses leaves no gray middle ground: before them lie two stark paths, blessing or curse (11:26–28). Authentic faith involves a clear choice every single day.

**Keeping God's word alive in the home protects a nation's future.** Repeating nearly verbatim his charge to parents from chapter 6 (11:18–21) reveals Moses' deepest concern: a people stands firm in God's promise only when His word passes vividly from one generation to the next.

## Deeper Meaning

**The warning of *shutting up the heavens* came painfully true in the days of Elijah.** Centuries later, when King Ahab and Israel chased after Baal—whom Canaanites revered as the storm god who controlled rain—the prophet Elijah prayed in direct keeping with 11:17, bringing a three-and-a-half-year drought to demonstrate that the LORD alone commands the rain (1 Kings 17:1; James 5:17).

**The promise of *autumn and spring rains* (11:14) echoes into New Testament patience and provision.** James draws on this exact picture of the farmer waiting patiently for the early and late rains to urge believers to persevere until the Lord's return (James 5:7), while Paul reminds the people of Lystra that the living God gives rain from heaven and fruitful seasons as a witness to His kindness (Acts 14:17).

**The crossroads of blessing and curse (11:26–28) prepares for Jesus' call to choose the narrow way.** Setting two contrasting paths before the hearer echoes in Psalms 1 and reaches its summit when Jesus urges disciples to enter the narrow gate leading to life rather than the wide gate leading to destruction, building on rock by hearing and obeying His words (Matthew 7:13–14; 7:24–27).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does *watering by foot* in 11:10 mean?** In ancient Egypt, farmers operated wooden waterwheels with their feet or used their feet to open and close small earthen channels guiding Nile water into garden plots. Moses uses this familiar contrast to juxtapose two ways of living: exhausting reliance on human effort and artificial channels versus living by direct rain from heaven.

**Why does 11:6 mention only Dathan and Abiram without Korah?** In Numbers 16, Korah (a Levite) joined Dathan and Abiram (Reubenites) in rebelling against Moses. The ground split open and swallowed Dathan and Abiram's tents, while fire from the LORD consumed Korah's two hundred fifty incense-bearers (Numbers 16:31–35; Psalms 106:17–18). Here Moses cites the earth swallowing Dathan and Abiram briefly as a vivid example of divine judgment right inside the camp.

**When was the ceremony on Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal (11:29–30) carried out?** These two mountains face each other across the valley of Shechem in the very heart of Canaan, near where Abraham built his first altar upon entering the land (Genesis 12:6–7). Moses gives detailed instructions for this rite in chapter 27, and Joshua faithfully carried it out right after the capture of Ai (Joshua 8:30–35).

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, treasures 11:12 ("the eyes of the LORD your God are always upon it, from the beginning of the year to the very end"). Canaan's dependence on rain was a blessing rather than a disadvantage, he observes: it kept God's people close to their Creator, looking heavenward in every season and remembering that every good gift rests under the watchful eye of their heavenly Father. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Deuteronomy 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing dependence on God's continual watchfulness from year's beginning to end in 11:10–12 is Matthew Henry's familiar exposition; exact text not checked -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, interprets the contrast between Egypt and Canaan in 11:10–12 as a school of divine providence. People living beside the Nile easily imagine fertility flows automatically from the river without looking to heaven, whereas placing Israel in a rain-fed land trained them to recognize every timely drop of rain as a direct gift from God's hand. *(Harmony of the Law, on Deuteronomy 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin regularly stresses that God used Canaan's geography to train faith in His providence in Harmony of the Law; exact wording not checked -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English preacher, frequently preached from 11:12 as a New Year's encouragement for his congregation. No one knows what hills or valleys a new year may bring (11:11), he told them, yet those who belong to God step forward with one unchanging assurance: the eyes of the LORD watch over them every single day from the beginning of the year to the very end. *(New Year's Sermon on Deuteronomy chapter 11 verse 12, Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon is famous for New Year sermons on Deuteronomy 11:12 celebrating God's watchful care throughout the year; exact title and volume not checked -->
