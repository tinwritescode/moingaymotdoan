# Deuteronomy 6 — Loving God with All Your Heart

## Story

Following the Ten Commandments, Moses explains the core spirit of all the statutes so that Israel will fear the LORD all their days and prosper in "a land flowing with milk and honey" (6:1–3).

Immediately comes the most famous confession of faith in the entire Old Testament, known in Jewish tradition as the *Shema* (meaning *Hear*): "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One. And you shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your strength." These words are not merely to pass across the ears; they must rest deeply on the heart. Parents are to impress them diligently on their children in the rhythm of everyday life—when sitting at home, when walking along the road, when lying down, and when getting up. They are to tie them as symbols on their hands, bind them on their foreheads, and write them on the doorframes of their houses and on their gates (6:4–9).

Moses then warns of a subtle trap awaiting them in the Promised Land. When the LORD brings them into the land and gives them large, flourishing cities they did not build, houses filled with good things they did not accumulate, wells they did not dig, and vineyards and olive groves they did not plant—right in the middle of eating and being satisfied, they must watch themselves carefully lest they forget the LORD who rescued them from slavery in Egypt (6:10–12).

They must fear, serve, and swear by the name of the LORD alone, never chasing after the gods of the surrounding peoples, for the LORD in their midst is "a jealous God." Nor may they test the LORD as their ancestors tested Him at Massah when they lacked water. If they carefully do what is right and good in the LORD's sight, He will bless them and drive out every enemy before them just as He promised (6:13–19).

Chapter 6 closes with a warm family scene. In days to come, when a child curiously asks his father what all these decrees, statutes, and ordinances mean, the father does not reply with a dry lecture, but tells a story of rescue: "We were slaves of Pharaoh in Egypt," and the LORD brought us out with a mighty hand and awesome signs to bring us into the land He swore to our ancestors. God gave these commands not to restrict us, but for our own lasting good and survival to this day. Faithfully obeying them is what it means to live rightly before Him (6:20–25).

## Key Takeaways

**The root of obedience is not terror, but wholehearted love.** Before unpacking hundreds of detailed statutes, Moses gathers the whole way of life into one command: love the one true God with all your heart, soul, and strength (6:4–5). God does not want grudging outward compliance; He seeks the whole person—mind, affections, and active energy alike.

**Passing on faith happens inside the ordinary rhythm of family life.** God's word is never meant to be locked inside temples or reserved for solemn festivals. Moses tells parents to talk about it while sitting at home, walking down the road, going to sleep, and waking up (6:7). Faith endures best when it weaves naturally into daily meals and conversations between parents and children.

**Prosperity tempts people to forget God even more easily than hardship does.** In the barren wilderness, Israel had to look up to God for bread every single day. Moses worries most about the moment they step into finished houses and full vineyards and eat until they are full (6:10–12): unearned comfort easily breeds pride and forgetfulness of the Giver.

**Commandments only make sense when anchored in the story of grace.** When a child asks why they keep these laws (6:20), the answer starts with "We were slaves" and God rescued us (6:21). The father even says *we*, though the child was born long after the Exodus, inviting the child to step personally into that story of deliverance.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus chooses the *Shema* of 6:4–5 as the greatest commandment in all of Scripture.** When a scribe asks which commandment is the most important of all, Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:4–5 directly and immediately pairs it with Leviticus 19:18 on loving one's neighbor (Mark 12:28–31; Matthew 22:37–40). The whole Old and New Testaments converge at one point: love God wholeheartedly and love your neighbor as yourself.

**Jesus uses chapter 6 to defeat two of Satan's three wilderness temptations.** When the devil urges Him to throw Himself from the pinnacle of the temple to test God's power, Jesus answers with 6:16: do not put the Lord your God to the test (Matthew 4:7; Luke 4:12). When the devil offers all the kingdoms of the world in exchange for worship, Jesus answers with 6:13: worship the Lord your God and serve Him only (Matthew 4:10; Luke 4:8). On the very wilderness ground where ancient Israel stumbled, Jesus stood completely victorious through the words of Deuteronomy.

**The confession *the LORD is One* is expanded by Paul in the light of Christ.** Writing to the Corinthian church in a Greco-Roman world crowded with idols, Paul echoes the *Shema* of 6:4 directly: for us there is but one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ (1 Corinthians 8:4–6).

## Notes & Nuances

**What place does the *Shema* (6:4–9) hold in Jewish life?** For millennia, observant Jews have recited the *Shema* twice daily, morning and evening. Verses 6:8–9—binding the words on the hand, wearing them on the forehead, and writing them on doorposts—are also practiced literally through small leather boxes containing Scripture scrolls strapped to the arm and forehead during prayer (*tefillin*) and a small case affixed to the doorframe of the home (*mezuzah*). Whether practiced literally or symbolically, the shared goal is to keep God's word constantly before one's eyes and guiding everything one's hands do.

**Verse 6:4 can be translated with slightly different nuances.** In the original Hebrew, the sentence is remarkably concise. It can be understood as *The LORD our God, the LORD is One*—stressing that there is only one true God—or as *The LORD is our God, the LORD alone*—stressing exclusive devotion shared with no rival deity. Both nuances belong together in Israel's confession of faith.

**When did the testing at Massah (6:16) take place?** Shortly after leaving Egypt, Israel camped at Rephidim with no water to drink, quarreled with Moses, and skeptically demanded to know whether the LORD was truly among them or not (Exodus 17:1–7). Testing God here means demanding that He prove His presence on our doubtful terms instead of trusting Him.

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo, reflects deeply on the call to love God with all heart, soul, and strength in 6:5. He points out Moses' deliberate sequence: God's words must first be "upon your heart" before they can be taught to children; parents who speak of God only with their lips while their own hearts are cold will rarely pass on a living faith to the next generation. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Deuteronomy 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: noting that God's word must live in parents' hearts before being taught to children is Matthew Henry's familiar emphasis on chapter 6; exact wording not checked -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, sounds a strong warning from 6:10–12 about the spiritual dangers of prosperity. Adversity often humbles people and drives them to call upon God, he observes, whereas abundance acts like strong wine that intoxicates the heart and makes people proudly forget the Giver.
