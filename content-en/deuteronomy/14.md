# Deuteronomy 14 — God's Holy Children

## Story

Moses opens chapter 14 with a title brimming with warmth: "You are sons of the LORD your God." Bearing that identity as a holy people chosen from all nations on earth as His treasured possession, they must not imitate pagan mourning customs such as slashing their flesh or shaving the front of their heads for the dead (14:1–2).

From mourning practices, the law turns to the everyday table: "Do not eat any detestable thing." Among land animals, Israel may eat ten clean kinds—familiar livestock like cattle, sheep, and goats, as well as wild game like deer, gazelle, and ibex. The general rule is simple: any animal that both has a split hoof and chews the cud may be eaten; creatures possessing only one of those traits—such as the camel, hare, rock badger, or pig—are unclean, and Israel may neither eat their meat nor touch their carcasses (14:3–8).

Among water creatures, anything with fins and scales may be eaten, while anything lacking them is unclean. Among birds, clean birds are permitted, whereas a long list of birds of prey, scavengers, night birds, and the bat are forbidden. Winged swarming insects in general are also off limits. In addition, Israel may not eat any animal found already dead—they may give it to a resident foreigner or sell it to an outsider—and a brief, intriguing command follows: "You must not boil a young goat in its mother's milk" (14:9–21).

The rest of chapter 14 outlines the law of the tithe. Each year, Israel is to set aside a tenth of all that their fields produce, bringing grain, new wine, oil, and the firstborn of herds and flocks to the LORD's chosen place to eat there in His presence. The goal is not drudgery, but learning always to revere the LORD. If the journey to the chosen sanctuary is too far to haul bulky crops, they may exchange the tithe for silver, carry the silver to the chosen place, and spend it on whatever their heart desires—cattle, sheep, wine, or celebratory drink—to feast joyfully with their households before the LORD. Amid their celebration, they must never neglect the Levites living in their towns (14:22–27).

Finally, at the end of every third year, that year's entire tithe stays right inside their own towns instead of traveling to the sanctuary. It becomes a local public storehouse so that Levites, resident foreigners, orphans, and widows may come and eat until they are satisfied—and in turn the LORD will bless all the work of their hands (14:28–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Identity shapes lifestyle.** Before forbidding a single mourning custom or food item, Moses reminds Israel who they are: children of the LORD and His treasured, holy people. Daily distinctions were never about being eccentric; they were outward signs of belonging to God (14:1–2; 14:21).

**Holiness reaches down into something as ordinary as a meal.** The food laws reminded Israel three times a day that faith does not live only inside a sanctuary, but sits right at the dinner table. Choosing clean over unclean trained daily habits of discernment and obedience (14:3–20).

**Giving is a school of reverence and joy.** Chapter 14's tithe law surprises many readers because the givers themselves feast joyfully with their families before God—even converting produce into silver to make the journey easier. God understands real-life logistics and wants gratitude expressed with gladness, not resentment (14:23–26).

**True worship always provides for the unprotected.** The third-year tithe shows that in God's eyes, honoring Him cannot be separated from feeding four vulnerable groups: landless Levites, resident foreigners, orphans, and widows (14:28–29).

## Deeper Meaning

**From a people marked by dietary laws to a holy people in Christ.** The titles of holy nation and God's treasured possession in 14:2 are taken up by Peter for the entire church of Jewish and Gentile believers in Jesus (1 Peter 2:9; Titus 2:14). The privilege of being called God's children now opens wide to all who receive Him (John 1:12; 1 John 3:1).

**The New Testament declares all foods clean.** As the gospel prepared to embrace the nations, Jesus taught that what defiles a person is not what enters the mouth, but what comes out of the heart—and Mark explicitly notes that in saying this, Jesus declared all foods clean (Mark 7:18–19). Afterward, through the vision of the sheet lowered from heaven, God taught Peter never to call Gentiles unclean, signaling that the dividing wall of dietary laws had fulfilled its historic purpose (Acts 10:11–15; 10:28; Romans 14:14).

**Cheerful, generous giving endures.** Although the New Testament does not impose a rigid percentage like Old Testament tithing, the heartbeat of Deuteronomy 14 lives on in Paul's teaching: give cheerfully, gratefully, and generously to care for those in need within the community (2 Corinthians 9:7–9; James 1:27).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why were certain animals classified as unclean?** Scholars offer several explanations: some emphasize hygiene in warm climates, others note links to pagan cult rituals, and many modern scholars view the food laws as a symbolic ordering system reminding Israel daily of its separation to God. Verse 14:7 groups the hare among animals that "chew the cud" based on ordinary ancient visual observation—hares move their jaws in a cud-chewing motion—rather than modern zoological anatomy.

**What does the ban on boiling a young goat in its mother's milk mean?** Verse 14:21 (cf. Exodus 23:19; 34:26) has intrigued interpreters for centuries. Some scholars connect it to a Canaanite fertility ritual; others see a humane principle honoring the order of life—milk meant to nourish a young creature should not become the medium for cooking it. From this command, Jewish tradition developed the *kosher* practice of keeping meat and dairy completely separate.

**Was selling a naturally dead animal to a foreigner unfair?** Verse 14:21 allows giving such meat to a resident foreigner or selling it to a stranger. This does not refer to spoiled or unsafe meat, but to an animal that died without ritual slaughter and blood drainage, rendering it ceremonially unclean for Israelites. Non-Israelites outside Israel's ritual food laws could still consume it normally.

**How does the tithe here relate to Numbers 18?** In Numbers 18:21–24, the tithe goes to support the Levites. In Deuteronomy 14, the family eats the tithe joyfully before God in years one and two, while year three's tithe supports Levites and the poor locally. Interpreters harmonize this in two main ways: Jewish tradition treats them as distinct tithes (a first tithe for the Levites, and a second tithe for festivals and the poor), while many modern scholars see Deuteronomy adapting tithing practice flexibly for settled life across the land.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, draws special attention to the ban on self-cutting in mourning at the start of chapter 14. Those who know they are children of God may rightly grieve when losing loved ones, he observes, yet they must not grieve in despair like people without hope, because their heavenly Father lives and cares for them still. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Deuteronomy 14)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: connecting sonship to God with restrained grief in mourning is characteristic of Matthew Henry's commentary; exact wording not checked -->

**Mary Douglas**, twentieth-century British anthropologist, proposed an influential interpretation of the clean and unclean animal lists in both Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14. Holiness corresponds to wholeness and proper order, she argued: creatures fully fitting their natural domain—such as fish with both fins and scales—symbolize completeness, whereas boundary-crossing creatures are marked unclean to remind Israel to guard holy boundaries. *(Purity and Danger, 1966, Chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chapter 3 of Purity and Danger directly examines the abominations of Leviticus and Deuteronomy with Douglas's famous thesis on wholeness and classification -->
