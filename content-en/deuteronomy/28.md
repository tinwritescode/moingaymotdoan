# Deuteronomy 28 — Blessings for Obedience and Curses for Disobedience

## Story

Chapter 28 sets before Israel two unmistakable paths whose consequences reach down into every meal, every harvest, and the fate of the entire nation. First come the blessings if they faithfully obey the LORD's voice and keep all His commands. They will be blessed everywhere and always—in the city and in the field, coming in and going out. Their children, crops, herds, baskets, and kneading troughs will overflow with blessing. Enemies marching against them on one road will scatter before them down seven roads. God will open the heavens, His rich storehouse, sending rain in season and prospering them so they lend to many nations and borrow from none; He will establish them as His holy people, making them "the head and not the tail," lifted high above every nation on earth (28:1–14).

Starting at verse 15, however, the tone turns sharply into a warning nearly four times longer than the blessings, spelling out what follows if they abandon God. First, the six opening blessings flip word for word into six matching curses: cursed in city and field, cursed in basket and kneading trough, cursed in children, crops, herds, and every coming and going (28:15–19).

The next block depicts waves of calamity striking from within and without. Plague, fever, drought, scorching heat, and crop blight pursue them; the sky overhead turns bronze and the earth beneath turns iron, raining dust and powder instead of rain. In battle they suffer rout, marching out one way and fleeing seven ways, their corpses left in the open with no one to scare away birds or beasts. Incurable boils, madness, and confusion strike them until they grope at noon like blind people in darkness. Everything they build falls to strangers: betrothed brides violated by others, houses built yet never lived in, vineyards planted yet never enjoyed, sons and daughters dragged away before their helpless eyes until the sights drive them mad (28:20–35).

The warnings escalate to national humiliation and exile. Both people and the king they appoint are marched away to an unfamiliar nation to serve lifeless gods of wood and stone, becoming a horror, proverb, and byword among the nations. Back home, locusts, worms, and swarms strip every crop; foreigners living among them rise higher and higher while Israel sinks into debt at the very tail. Moses names the root cause bluntly: because they did not serve the LORD joyfully and gladly in the midst of abundance, they will serve their enemies in hunger, thirst, nakedness, and dire poverty under an iron yoke (28:36–48).

The chapter's most harrowing section describes a ruthless empire swooping from afar, speaking an incomprehensible tongue, swift as an eagle, showing neither respect to the elderly nor pity to the young. That army devours every crop and flock and besieges every fortified wall Israel trusted in. Siege starvation grows so horrific that even the gentlest man and the most refined, delicate woman turn secretly against spouse and children to eat their own newborn infants (28:49–57).

The final section delivers the heaviest sentence: if they refuse to revere the glorious and awesome name of the LORD, all the old plagues of Egypt along with unnamed diseases will cling to them. A nation once numerous as the stars will dwindle to a tiny remnant, torn from the Promised Land and scattered across the earth from one horizon to the other. In exile they will find no rest: anxious hearts, failing eyes, life hanging by a thread—longing in the morning for evening and at evening for morning. The ending reverses the entire Exodus: the LORD sends them back to Egypt in ships, offering themselves for sale to their enemies as slaves—yet no one will even buy them (28:58–68).

## Key Takeaways

**God desires joyful service, never reluctant drudgery.** Amid dozens of terrifying warnings, verse 47 shines as the key to the whole chapter: bondage falls on them "because you did not serve the LORD your God with joy and gladness of heart in all your abundance" (28:47). God is no harsh taskmaster hunting for excuses to punish; He grieves when people soak up His every blessing yet turn cold and indifferent toward Him.

**Covenant unfaithfulness reverses the entire story of redemption.** Genesis and Exodus moved forward from Egypt to freedom, from a small family to a star-like multitude, into a land flowing with milk and honey; the end of chapter 28 plays that movie backward—dwindling numbers, lost land, scattering, and sailing back to Egypt in bondage (28:62–68). Walking away from God, the source of life, leads straight back to old slavery.

**Chapter 28 states national covenant terms, not a private prosperity formula.** These blessings and curses address Israel's collective life in Canaan under the Sinai covenant. Scripture also gives us Job and the Psalms to remind us that faithful individuals still endure suffering while the wicked temporarily prosper. Twisting chapter 28 into an automatic formula—*faithful people always get rich, sick people must be sinning*—wounds innocent sufferers deeply.

## Deeper Meaning

**Israel's later history fulfilled chapter 28's warnings in agonizing detail.** As kings and people repeatedly abandoned God for idols, the unthinkable siege famine unfolded literally when Syria besieged Samaria (2 Kings 6:24–29) and when Babylon besieged Jerusalem in 586 BC (Lamentations 2:20; 4:10). Assyria, Babylon, and later Rome swooped in like distant eagles, smashing fortified walls and scattering the nation into exile.

**Christ stepped directly into the covenant curse to open blessing to all nations.** Under the old covenant, standing honestly beneath chapters 27–28, no one could earn its blessings by human strength. Paul explains that Christ bore the curse of the law in our place upon the cross (Galatians 3:10–13). All the richest blessings chapter 28 pictured in earthly land terms now reach their eternal, spiritual fullness in Christ (Ephesians 1:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why are the curses nearly four times longer than the blessings?** This never means God prefers punishing over blessing. In ancient Near Eastern vassal treaties—the literary form Deuteronomy borrows to frame the covenant—violation curses were always far longer and more graphic to impress upon signers the deadly seriousness of oath-breaking. Moreover, Moses knew Israel's stubborn bent all too well and sounded the alarm urgently to pull them back before disaster struck.

**What does verse 63 mean by saying the LORD will *delight* in destroying them?** At first glance this jars against passages declaring that God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezekiel 18:23; 33:11). In Deuteronomy 28:63, the parallel phrasing highlights the decisive, unwavering consistency of divine justice: just as God acted wholeheartedly without hesitation to bless them when they obeyed, so He acts with equal resolve to uphold covenant justice, unclouded by weak sentimentality.

**Why does verse 68 say they offer themselves as slaves yet *no one will buy you*?** Two explanations reinforce each other: either captives flooded slave markets in such overwhelming numbers that demand collapsed, or war and famine left them so broken and diseased they held no value even as laborers. Both portray the rock bottom of human degradation apart from God's protection.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth-century English pastor, calls verse 47 one of Scripture's most searching indictments: God seeks not mere external compliance, but a cheerful heart in serving Him. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, attributes the sheer intensity of chapter 28's curses to human stubbornness. Were God to warn gently, people would quickly brush it off once life grew comfortable; hence God paints terrifying scenes like a loud alarm bell meant to wake dull consciences before catastrophe arrives. *(Harmony of the Law, on Deuteronomy 28)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: explaining the severity of the curses as an awakening alarm for hard hearts matches Calvin's commentary on chapter 28; exact pages not checked -->

**Peter C. Craigie**, Canadian Old Testament scholar, highlights the close structural parallels between Deuteronomy 28's blessings and curses and ancient Hittite and Assyrian political treaties. Yet he notes one radical difference: pagan treaties invoked pantheons of different gods to inflict various plagues, whereas chapter 28 places both blessing and judgment exclusively in the hands of the one LORD, affirming His absolute sovereignty over history. *(The Book of Deuteronomy, NICOT, 1976)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: comparing chapter 28's blessing-curse structure with ancient Near Eastern vassal treaties is one of the best-known features of Craigie's NICOT commentary -->
