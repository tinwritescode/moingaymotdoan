# Deuteronomy 26 — Firstfruits, Tithes, and Covenant Commitment

## Story

Having completed the long collection of statutes, Moses teaches two thanksgiving ceremonies to be practiced once Israel settles in the Promised Land. The first is presenting the firstfruits. Worshipers gather the earliest produce from their land into a basket, carry it to the LORD's chosen sanctuary, and declare to the priest that they have indeed entered the land God swore to their ancestors. The priest sets the basket before the altar (26:1–4).

Then the worshiper recites a concise, powerful confession of faith summarizing Israel's entire story of salvation: "My father was a wandering Aramean. He went down to Egypt with a few people and lived there," growing from a handful into a great and mighty nation; when the Egyptians mistreated and oppressed them with hard labor, they cried out, and the LORD heard, bringing them out with a mighty hand and awesome wonders into this land flowing with milk and honey. Therefore they now bring the firstfruits of the soil God gave them, bow down in worship, and rejoice in every good gift together with the Levites and the foreigners living among them (26:5–11).

The second ceremony occurs in the third year—the year of tithing reserved for Levites, foreigners, orphans, and widows in local towns so they may eat and be satisfied. Once that tithe is fully distributed, the householder declares before God that he has cleared the sacred portion completely from his house according to every command—withholding nothing, using none during mourning or ritual impurity, and offering none to the dead. Finally, he prays that the LORD look down from His holy dwelling in heaven and bless Israel and the land flowing with milk and honey He swore to give (26:12–15).

The final four verses bring the whole legal section of Moses' address to a close with a mutual covenant affirmation. Moses calls Israel to keep every statute with all their heart and soul. Today Israel publicly declares the LORD to be their God and pledges to walk in His ways; and the LORD publicly declares Israel to be "His treasured possession," a holy people set high in praise, fame, and honor above all nations (26:16–19).

## Key Takeaways

**True worship always begins by remembering where we came from.** Standing before the altar with a basket of firstfruits, the Israelite farmer does not boast of his own sweat and skill. He recites that his ancestor was a wandering Aramean, his people were suffering slaves, and the LORD rescued them and gave them this soil (26:5–10). Remembering redemption keeps prosperity from turning into pride.

**Gratitude to God must overflow into joyful sharing with those in need.** Both ceremonies in chapter 26 tie worship directly to social care. Offering firstfruits leads straight into feasting alongside Levites and resident foreigners (26:11); the third-year tithe goes directly to landless Levites, foreigners, orphans, and widows (26:12–13). No one can claim to love God while shutting the door on hungry neighbors.

**Covenant is a two-sided relationship of mutual belonging.** The closing verses sound nothing like a cold legal contract; they ring like wedding vows: Israel claims the LORD as their God, and the LORD claims Israel as His treasured people (26:17–18). Keeping His law does not purchase God's love—it responds to the love that already chose and rescued them.

## Deeper Meaning

**The image of *firstfruits* becomes a symbol of the risen Christ and His people.** In the Old Testament, presenting the basket of firstfruits acknowledged that the entire harvest belonged to God. In the New Testament, Paul calls the risen Christ "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep," guaranteeing that all who belong to Him will also rise (1 Corinthians 15:20). James likewise calls believers reborn through the word of truth "a kind of firstfruits of all He created" (James 1:18).

**The title *God's treasured possession* passes onward to the New Testament church.** The solemn declaration of 26:18–19 echoes God's promise at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19:5–6). Later, the apostle Peter applies those very titles to believers in Jesus from every nation: a chosen race, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession, called to proclaim His excellencies (1 Peter 2:9; cf. Titus 2:14).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is *a wandering Aramean* in verse 5?** Most interpreters identify him as Jacob, who fled to Aram to serve Laban for twenty years and later brought his tiny family down to Egypt (Genesis 31:38–41; 46:1–7). Others understand the phrase as encompassing all three patriarchs—Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—who traced their roots to Aram and lived as nomads. Some translations render *wandering* as *perishing* or *fugitive*, highlighting even more vividly how fragile Israel's beginnings were before God raised them up.

**The oath *I have not offered any of it to the dead* in verse 14 reflects ancient customs.** Among several neighboring peoples in Canaan, mourners left food offerings for spirits of the dead or conducted meals invoking underworld deities. Israelites solemnly affirmed before God that their sacred tithe remained completely pure, untainted by pagan mortuary rituals.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth-century English pastor, holds up the confession of verses 5–10 as a timeless pattern for genuine thanksgiving: worshipers look back to their humble origins to stay low in humility, then look up to God's mighty grace to praise Him wholeheartedly. **Gerhard von Rad**, twentieth-century German Old Testament scholar, famously identified Deuteronomy 26:5–9 as Israel's oldest *short historical creed*. In his view, the entire core of Old Testament faith—from the wandering patriarch through the Exodus to receiving the land—was condensed into this liturgical confession and later expanded into the narrative stretching from Genesis through Joshua. *(The Problem of the Hexateuch, 1938; Deuteronomy, OTL)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the "little historical credo" hypothesis regarding 26:5–9 is one of Gerhard von Rad's most famous contributions to Pentateuchal scholarship -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, focuses closely on verses 17–18 portraying the mutual pledge between God and Israel. Although the text mentions Israel's pledge before God's, Calvin notes that in the order of grace God always takes the first step: Israel can choose the LORD only because He first chose and redeemed them. *(Harmony of the Law, on Deuteronomy 26)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: emphasizing God's prior initiative of grace beneath mutual covenant affirmation in 26:17–18 is a theological hallmark of Calvin's exposition; exact pages not checked -->
