# Deuteronomy 31 — Commissioning Joshua, Writing the Law, and Foretelling Rebellion

## Story

Chapter 31 opens the final chapter of Moses' life. Stepping before all Israel, he announces that he is now one hundred twenty years old, no longer able to lead them out and bring them in, and that the LORD has decreed he will not cross the Jordan. Yet Israel need not fear: the LORD Himself will cross ahead of them to destroy the nations just as He did to Sihon and Og, and Joshua will cross at their head. Moses encourages the whole congregation, then summons Joshua publicly before all Israel and charges him with the same unwavering promise: "Be strong and courageous," for "the LORD Himself goes before you; He will be with you. He will never leave you nor forsake you" (31:1–8).

Having handed over leadership, Moses finishes writing down this law and entrusts it to the Levitical priests who carry the ark of the covenant, along with all the elders of Israel. He establishes a lasting ordinance: at the end of every seven years, in the year of debt release during the Feast of Tabernacles when all Israel gathers before God, they must read this entire law aloud before everyone—men, women, little children, and resident foreigners—so successive generations learn to fear the LORD and carefully obey His word (31:9–13).

The LORD tells Moses that the day of his death is near and instructs him to bring Joshua to the tent of meeting so God may commission him directly. The LORD appears in the pillar of cloud standing over the entrance. Yet even in that solemn hour, God discloses a bitter reality to Moses: once Moses rests with his ancestors, this people will enter the land flowing with milk and honey, eat their fill and grow sleek, and then turn to foreign gods, despising the LORD and breaking His covenant. God's anger will flare, He will hide His face, and many disasters will overtake them. Therefore the LORD tells Moses to write down a song and teach it to Israel so that when trouble strikes, the song will stay alive on their descendants' lips as an undeniable witness (31:14–21).

That very day Moses writes the song and teaches it to Israel. The LORD directly commissions Joshua son of Nun: "Be strong and courageous, for you will bring the Israelites into the land that I swore to give them, and I will be with you." Once Moses finishes writing the entire book of the law, he commands the Levites to place it solemnly beside the ark of the covenant as a witness. He speaks bluntly of their stubborn nature—rebellious even while he is alive, and surely worse after his death. Calling together the elders and officials with heaven and earth as witnesses, he recites the entire song from start to finish in the hearing of the whole assembly (31:22–30).

## Key Takeaways

**Human leaders pass away, yet the God who walks with His people never leaves.** At one hundred twenty, Moses stops at the eastern bank of the Jordan, yet God's work never stalls with him (31:2–3). Israel's true security rests neither in Moses' genius nor Joshua's courage, but in God's promise to go ahead and never abandon them (31:6; 31:8; 31:23).

**God's word must be preserved in writing and read aloud across generations.** Moses did not entrust the law merely to oral memory, vulnerable to fading over time. He wrote it down, placed it beside the ark as a fixed standard, and mandated public reading every seven years before whole families including children (31:9–13; 31:24–26). Faith is always only one generation from amnesia unless God's word is intentionally passed on.

**Song engraves truth most deeply into communal memory.** Foreseeing that Israel would neglect the law amid comfort, God instructed Moses to compose a song for everyone to learn by heart (31:19–21). Long statute books easily gather dust on shelves, whereas a song learned in childhood rises spontaneously to the lips when crisis hits.

**God's faithfulness outlasts clear-eyed realism about human failure.** Chapter 31 holds zero illusions about Israel: both God and Moses know full well they will break covenant once comfortable (31:16; 31:20; 31:27–29). Even so, God faithfully leads them in, equips them with a new leader, a written Scripture, and a song—leaving a loving witness to call them home even after they fall.

## Deeper Meaning

**The promise *never to leave or forsake* becomes an anchor for every believer.** Moses' encouragement to Israel and Joshua in 31:6 and 31:8 is echoed straight to Joshua at the opening of his book (Joshua 1:5–9). Centuries later, the author of Hebrews quotes this exact promise to comfort anxious believers: stay free from the love of money and be content, because God Himself has said, "Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5).

**Passing the baton from Moses to Joshua foreshadows moving from Law to Savior.** In Hebrew, the name *Joshua* (meaning *the LORD is salvation*) is the older form of the Greek name *Jesus*. Moses—the lawgiver—brings Israel right up to the border of the Promised Land and stops, while Joshua carries them across the river into their inheritance. Early church fathers and commentators saw a vivid gospel shadow here: the law leads us to the threshold, while Jesus Himself brings God's people into eternal rest (John 1:17; Hebrews 4:8–10).

## Notes & Nuances

**Does 31:2 (*I am no longer able to go out and come in*) contradict 34:7 (*his eyes were not weak nor his strength gone*)?** Not at all. In Hebrew idiom, *going out and coming in* refers to exercising public leadership and leading troops into battle (cf. Numbers 27:17; 1 Kings 3:7). In 31:2 Moses is not complaining of bedridden illness, but stating that his term of active leadership has ended by God's decree prohibiting him from crossing the Jordan.

**The Book of the Law was placed *beside* the ark, not *inside* it.** Verse 31:26 specifies placing the scroll right beside the ark of the covenant. Inside the ark rested only the two stone tablets of the Ten Commandments (1 Kings 8:9), whereas the full scroll of the law sat alongside it so priests could readily retrieve it for public reading every seven years at Tabernacles and keep it as a covenant witness.

**The seven-year public reading fell into neglect and was later revived.** During Israel's monarchy, the written law was nearly forgotten until priests rediscovered the scroll in the temple under King Josiah (2 Kings 22:8–13). After exile, Ezra restored this very ceremony, reading the law aloud with explanation before men, women, and children during the Feast of Tabernacles (Nehemiah 8:1–18).

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth-century English pastor, remarks on Moses commissioning Joshua that the wisest leader never clings jealously to office or fears being eclipsed by a successor, but wholeheartedly uplifts and strengthens the next generation before the entire community. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, draws attention to Moses putting the law into writing in verses 9 and 24. **Peter C. Craigie**, Canadian Old Testament scholar, explains placing the scroll beside the ark (31:26) through ancient Near Eastern treaty practice: copies of solemn treaties were regularly deposited at the feet of a deity's statue inside its temple as sacred witnesses. Israel had no idol statues, so the ark of the covenant—symbolizing the LORD's footstool—served as the supreme repository for the covenant text. *(The Book of Deuteronomy, NICOT, 1976)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: linking the deposit of the law beside the ark to ancient Near Eastern treaty archives in temples is a prominent point in Craigie's NICOT commentary -->
