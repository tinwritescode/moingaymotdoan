# Deuteronomy 7 — God's Treasured People and a Clean Break with Canaanite Idols

## Story

Moses prepares Israel to confront seven stronger nations living in the land of Canaan: the Hittites, Girgashites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites. When the LORD hands these nations over to them, Israel must devote them completely to destruction, making no treaty with them, showing no mercy, and refusing to intermarry with them. The reason is stated plainly: they will turn Israel's children away from following the LORD to serve other gods, provoking God's fierce anger to destroy Israel. Accordingly, Israel must tear down their altars, smash their sacred pillars, cut down their Asherah poles, and burn their carved images (7:1–5).

Behind this uncompromising charge stands Israel's identity: they are "a holy people" chosen by the LORD out of all the peoples on earth to be His treasured possession. Moses immediately crushes any hint of national pride: the LORD did not choose them because they were more numerous than other nations—in fact, they were the fewest of all peoples. Rather, "because the LORD loved you and kept the oath He swore to your fathers," He redeemed them from the house of slavery in Egypt. He is "the faithful God," keeping His covenant of loving devotion to a thousand generations of those who love and obey Him, while repaying to their face those who hate Him (7:6–11).

If Israel listens and faithfully keeps these ordinances, the LORD will honor His oath to their ancestors and shower them with blessing: many children, abundant grain, new wine, and oil, multiplying herds and flocks, freedom from barrenness, and protection from the dreaded diseases they had known in Egypt. In return, they must have nothing to do with the gods of Canaan, for serving them would become "a snare to you" (7:12–16).

Moses anticipates the quiet fear still lingering in their hearts: these nations are stronger than we are—how can we drive them out? He tells them not to be afraid, but to remember what the LORD did to Pharaoh and all Egypt. The LORD will do the exact same to the Canaanite nations, sending terror and the hornet to flush out even those who hide. Still, God will drive them out "little by little" rather than all at once, lest the empty land be overrun by wild beasts. Finally, Moses adds a very specific safeguard: when burning the idols, they must not covet the silver or gold plating on them and bring it home, for it is a detestable thing set apart for destruction that will drag anyone who keeps it into the same doom (7:17–26).

## Key Takeaways

**God chooses His people purely out of love and faithfulness, never merit.** The reason given for God's love sounds almost circular, yet it is wonderfully profound: God loved Israel simply because He loved them and kept His oath to their ancestors (7:7–8). The worth of God's people never rests on being bigger, smarter, or better than others, but on being loved by God.

**Separation from sin is essential to preserving faith.** The gravest danger awaiting Israel in Canaan was not the enemy's sword, but inner assimilation through mixed worship and covenant-breaking marriages (7:3–4). Even the gold and silver stripped from an idol was forbidden in the home (7:25–26), because small compromises with idolatry eventually turn into a trap.

**God moves step by step for His people's good.** He could sweep away every adversary overnight, yet He chooses to clear them out "little by little" so wild beasts will not overrun the land (7:22). Often God's unhurried timing is not weakness or forgetfulness, but wise care for what His children can safely handle.

**Remembering past deliverance cures fear in the present battle.** Whenever Israel's courage falters before a larger foe, Moses prescribes the same remedy: look back, because the God who humbled mighty Egypt stands right in their midst today (7:17–21).

## Deeper Meaning

**The identity of *a holy, treasured people* expands in the New Testament to include the church from every nation.** The apostle Peter draws directly on the language of Deuteronomy 7:6 and Exodus 19:5–6 to call believers in Jesus—Gentiles included—a holy nation and a people for God's own possession, called out of darkness into His marvelous light to proclaim His excellencies (1 Peter 2:9).

**The principle of refusing partnership with idolatry continues in Paul's letters.** Just as 7:3–4 forbids binding family life to Canaanite idol worship in order to guard covenant faithfulness, Paul urges believers not to be unequally yoked with unbelievers, asking what agreement the temple of God can have with idols (2 Corinthians 6:14–16).

**God's sheer grace in choosing Israel (7:7–8) underpins the New Testament theology of grace.** Paul echoes this exact principle when reminding the Corinthians that God often chooses what is weak and lowly in the world so that no one may boast before Him (1 Corinthians 1:26–29), insisting that God's mercy depends not on human merit but on the God who shows mercy (Romans 9:15–16; Ephesians 2:8–9).

## Notes & Nuances

**How should we fairly understand the command to *completely destroy* the seven Canaanite nations in 7:1–2 and 7:16?** This is one of the hardest passages in the Old Testament, and honest readers should not brush past it. Within Scripture itself, the command is neither racial prejudice nor an open license for religious violence. First, Deuteronomy explicitly explains in 9:4–5 and 18:9–12 that this was a unique act of divine judgment after centuries of patience (cf. Genesis 15:16) against brutal Canaanite religious practices, including burning children alive as offerings to gods. Second, many biblical scholars point out that ancient Near Eastern war rhetoric routinely used hyperbole—*destroy utterly, leaving no one*—to describe a decisive military victory and the complete uprooting of a pagan religious order: right here in chapter 7, verse 7:2 says "completely destroy them," yet verse 7:3 immediately forbids "intermarrying with them," showing that Canaanites remained in the land and that the central concern was preventing idolatry. Even so, many readers and scholars candidly recognize the heavy moral weight of this text; what all orthodox Christian traditions agree on is that this command belonged exclusively to one unique historical moment in the settlement of Canaan and is never a model for believers to use violence against others, especially in light of Jesus' command to love our enemies (Matthew 5:43–45).

**What is *the hornet* in 7:20?** The Hebrew word literally refers to a hornet or wasp. Some commentators take it literally as swarms of stinging insects sent by God to throw enemy ranks into chaos; others read it figuratively for the panic and dread God cast over the Canaanites (cf. Joshua 2:9–11), or as a symbol of external forces—such as Egyptian campaigns—that weakened Canaan before Israel's arrival.

**Do the promises of freedom from disease and barrenness in 7:13–15 apply automatically to individual believers today?** No. These were national covenant blessings addressed to Israel as a theocratic nation under the Sinai covenant. Even within the Old Testament, deeply faithful people like Job suffered devastating loss and illness, and the New Testament makes clear that material prosperity and physical health do not automatically track with faithfulness (2 Corinthians 12:7–9).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, pauses deeply over 7:7–8 to expound God's completely unconditional election. He observes that Moses deliberately strips away every conceivable ground for Israelite pride in order to lead them back to the sole fountainhead: God's free love. **Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, lingers on the detail that God drives out the Canaanites *little by little* in 7:22. Applying the picture to spiritual life, he notes that God often frees believers from their corruptions step by step, keeping them humble and watchful in dependence on Him rather than letting them grow proud too soon as though they were already perfected. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Deuteronomy 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: applying the gradual conquest in 7:22 to progressive sanctification is a familiar touch in Matthew Henry's commentary; exact wording not checked -->

**Paul Copan**, American Christian philosopher and scholar, emphasizes the role of conventional ancient Near Eastern military hyperbole in the conquest commands of chapter 7. Pointing out that the command to "completely destroy" sits immediately alongside the ban on intermarriage and treaties (7:2–3), he argues that the real goal was not indiscriminate extermination, but dismantling Canaan's political strongholds and idolatrous religious system in the Promised Land. *(Is God a Moral Monster?, 2011, section on the Canaanite wars)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the argument regarding ancient Near Eastern hyperbole and the juxtaposition of 7:2 and 7:3 is a famous core thesis of Paul Copan's Is God a Moral Monster? -->
