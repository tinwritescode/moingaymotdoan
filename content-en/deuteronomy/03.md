# Deuteronomy 3 — Defeating King Og and Moses' Unanswered Prayer

## Story

Leaving Sihon's territory, Israel turned north along the road toward Bashan. Og, king of Bashan, marched out with his entire army to meet them in battle at Edrei. The LORD reassured Moses not to fear him, for He had delivered Og, his army, and his land into Israel's hands just as He had done with Sihon. Indeed, Israel completely defeated Og's forces, capturing sixty fortified cities with high walls, gates, and bars throughout the region of Argob, along with a great many unwalled villages. They devoted all the inhabitants of those cities to destruction, taking only the livestock and goods as plunder (3:1–7).

Israel thus took possession of the vast territory east of the Jordan River, stretching from the Arnon Valley all the way to Mount Hermon, including the plateau, all of Gilead, and all of Bashan. Moses adds a striking detail: Og was the last survivor of the giant Rephaim; his iron bedstead measured nine cubits long and four cubits wide—about thirteen feet long and six feet wide—and was still on display in Rabbah of the Ammonites (3:8–11).

Moses allotted this newly conquered eastern land to two and a half tribes: Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh, including the clans of Jair and Machir. Yet he attached a clear condition: the fighting men of these two and a half tribes had to cross the Jordan armed at the head of their brothers, leaving their wives, children, and livestock behind in the cities they had just received. Only after the LORD gave the other tribes rest and possession of their land west of the Jordan could they return to their own inheritance in the east (3:12–20).

At that time Moses also turned to encourage Joshua, his successor: Joshua's own eyes had seen everything the LORD did to those two Amorite kings, and the LORD would do the very same to all the kingdoms across the river. "Do not be afraid of them, for the LORD your God Himself will fight for you" (3:21–22).

Moses then shares a deeply personal and painful moment. Seeing that God had begun to display His greatness through these remarkable victories, Moses pleaded earnestly with the LORD to let him cross over and see "the good land beyond the Jordan—that fine hill country and Lebanon." Yet the LORD, angry with him because of the people, refused. God spoke decisively: "That is enough! Do not speak to Me again about this matter." The LORD permitted Moses only to climb to the top of Pisgah and look out toward the west, north, south, and east, for he would never cross the Jordan. Instead, he was to commission, encourage, and strengthen Joshua, who would lead the people into the land. The historical prologue closes right where they stand camped: in the valley opposite Beth-peor (3:23–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Victory over the most intimidating foe guarantees confidence for the future.** King Og, a giant ruling sixty heavily fortified cities (3:5; 3:11), embodied Israel's deepest fears. Watching the LORD bring him down east of the Jordan became living proof for Joshua and the entire nation before they faced Canaan (3:21–22).

**Receiving your portion first never justifies abandoning your brothers behind.** The two and a half tribes already had rich pastureland for their herds, yet Moses refused to let them settle down early. They had to leave their families behind and fight on the front lines with the other tribes until everyone entered rest (3:18–20). The unity of God's people outweighs any single group's convenience.

**Even God's greatest servant sometimes receives the answer *no*.** Moses' prayer was humble, heartfelt, and noble (3:24–25). Yet the LORD answered with a firm refusal (3:26). God's love does not mean He grants every desire of His servants, even their most sincere wishes.

**Passing the torch well is the mark of a mature leader.** Though heartbroken at being barred from the Promised Land, Moses did not turn bitter or give up. He obeyed God, turned to strengthen and encourage Joshua (3:28), and ensured that God's work would move forward.

## Deeper Meaning

**Moses' exclusion from the Promised Land points to the limits of the Law and the role of Christ.** Commentators from ancient times have noticed a profound symbol here: Moses—who represents the Law—could lead the people up to the edge of the Jordan and view the land from afar, yet Joshua was the one who brought them into rest (3:27–28). In the New Testament, John writes that the Law was given through Moses, while grace and truth came through Jesus Christ (John 1:17).

**Moses' disappointment was astonishingly comforted in the New Testament.** Although he could not set foot in the Promised Land during his lifetime, centuries later Moses stood on a high mountain right inside that very land, speaking face to face with Jesus at the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:1–3; Luke 9:28–31). God once said *no* to Moses' earthly request, only to grant him a far more glorious encounter later.

## Notes & Nuances

**What was King Og's iron bedstead (3:11)?** According to the measurements given in verse 11, it was roughly four meters long and nearly two meters wide. Some scholars think it was an actual bed built for an unusually large man, while others suggest it was a *sarcophagus*—a stone burial box carved from dark Bashan basalt, sometimes described as iron because of its color and hardness—kept as a trophy or monument in Rabbah. Either way, the detail highlights the formidable stature of the enemy God enabled Israel to defeat.

**Why does Moses say the LORD was angry with him *because of you* (3:26; cf. 1:37)?** In Numbers 20:7–12, the direct offense was Moses' own: in anger he struck the rock twice with his staff instead of speaking to it as God commanded, failing to uphold God's holiness before the people. Here Moses is not denying his guilt, but reminding Israel that their relentless grumbling and provocation drove him to that moment of lost composure (cf. Psalms 106:32–33). It also underscores the heavy accountability resting on spiritual leaders.

**What does the place name Beth-peor at the end of the chapter (3:29) recall?** Beth-peor lay near the site where Israel had recently fallen into the worship of Baal of Peor, triggering a plague that killed twenty-four thousand people (Numbers 25:1–9). Naming this place at the close of the historical review puts a vivid warning right before the people's eyes as Moses prepares to deliver his urgent call to faithfulness in chapter 4.
