# Deuteronomy 29 — Covenant Renewal in Moab and the Secret Things of God

## Story

Chapter 29 opens the covenant renewal in Moab, extending the covenant the LORD made at Mount Horeb forty years earlier. Summoning all Israel, Moses reminds them of the great wonders their own eyes witnessed: signs in Egypt, forty wilderness years in which neither their clothes nor their sandals wore out and they lived without ordinary bread or wine so they would know the LORD alone sustained them, and victories over Kings Sihon and Og east of the Jordan. Yet Moses speaks a painfully honest truth: "yet to this day the LORD has not given you a mind to understand, eyes to see, or ears to hear" (29:1–9).

Moses then announces that everyone standing before God today—tribal heads, elders, officials, men, women, little children, right down to the resident foreigner who chops wood and carries water in the camp—is entering into the sworn covenant of the LORD. Going further, he declares that this covenant binds not only those physically present today, but also generations not yet born (29:10–15).

Recalling the detestable idols of wood, stone, silver, and gold they saw in Egypt and among nations along the way, Moses delivers a sharp warning: make sure no man, woman, clan, or tribe quietly turns away from the LORD to serve other gods, becoming *a root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit*. Anyone who hears the words of this oath yet privately reassures himself—telling himself he will stay completely safe even while walking in stubbornness—will receive no forgiveness; every curse written in this book will settle on him and blot out his name from the community (29:16–21).

Moses pictures future generations and distant foreigners gazing on a scorched wasteland of sulfur, salt, and burning ruin like Sodom and Gomorrah. When they ask in horror why the LORD burned with such fierce anger, the answer will be unmistakable: because this people abandoned the covenant of the LORD who brought them out of Egypt to worship foreign gods, He uprooted them from their soil and hurled them into another land. Finally, Moses closes with a profound maxim: "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things revealed belong to us and to our children forever, so that we may follow all the words of this law" (29:22–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Seeing miracles with physical eyes is never enough unless the heart is opened.** Israel watched seas split and wore clothes and sandals that never wore out for forty years, yet still remained spiritually dull (29:2–6). Genuine faith is never merely stacking up external evidence; humans need God Himself to open eyes, ears, and hearts from within to truly know Him.

**Before God's covenant, every rank in the community stands on equal footing.** From powerful tribal chiefs to the humblest foreign woodcutter and water-carrier, from adults to little children, no one stands outside the covenant (29:10–12). Nor is faith merely one generation's private affair: parents' choices today ripple deeply into children not yet born (29:14–15).

**The deadliest illusion is believing stubbornness can still end in peace.** Moses unmasks that familiar self-deception: hearing clear warnings yet murmuring inwardly, *I will be fine anyway* (29:19). Secret sin in one life never stays isolated—it spreads like a bitter, poisonous root contaminating the entire community (29:18).

**Accepting the limits of human reason frees us to focus on obedience.** Verse 29:29 draws a healthy boundary for faith: many hidden realities inside God's sovereign plan remain beyond human reach, and we need not pry into them. What belongs to us is what God has plainly revealed in His word—not for endless speculation, but to put into practice day by day (29:29).

## Deeper Meaning

**The warning against a *bitter root* and hardened hearts returns for the New Testament church.** The author of Hebrews borrows directly from 29:18 when urging believers to watch over one another so no one misses God's grace and "no bitter root springs up to cause trouble and defile many" (Hebrews 12:15). Meanwhile, Paul combines the lament of 29:4—unperceiving hearts, blind eyes, deaf ears—with Isaiah 29:10 to explain the spiritual hardening of part of Israel toward the gospel (Romans 11:8).

**The need for a new heart in 29:4 opens straight toward the New Covenant.** If human beings lack on their own a heart able to understand and obey, the only solution is for God Himself to create a new heart. Starting in 30:6, and expanding through Jeremiah and Ezekiel, God promises to remove hearts of stone, give hearts of flesh, and write His law inside them (Jeremiah 31:33; Ezekiel 36:26–27)—fulfilled completely through Jesus and the Holy Spirit.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 29:4 say the LORD *has not given* them a mind to understand?** Taken hastily, this might sound as if God deliberately blocked Israel from understanding and then blamed them. In Old Testament idiom, Moses is not excusing Israel or blaming God, but exposing deep human inability: despite witnessing extraordinary miracles, apart from God's inward transforming grace the human heart stays stubbornly blind. Verse 29:4 prepares directly for God's promise to renew their hearts in 30:6.

**What does *as it is today* in verse 28 mean?** Some scholars see this phrase as reflecting a later editor's voice looking back after the Babylonian exile at the fulfillment of Moses' warning. Many others, however, note that it sits directly inside the imagined speech of future onlookers (continuing verses 24–27): on the future day of devastation, observers gazing at the ruins right before their eyes will speak those very words.

**The phrase *the watered with the dry* in verse 19 is an ancient idiom.** Pairing two opposites—*watered* and *dry*—is a Hebrew merism meaning *everything without exception*. When one stubborn rebel assumes he can defy God's law without consequence, his presumption drags innocent and guilty alike into shared catastrophe.
