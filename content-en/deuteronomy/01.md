# Deuteronomy 1 — Recalling the Journey from Horeb to Kadesh

## Story

Forty years of wandering in the wilderness are nearly over. On the first day of the eleventh month of the fortieth year, the people of Israel are camped on the east side of the Jordan River, in the land of Moab, having just defeated the two Amorite kings Sihon and Og. Before them, across the river, lies the land of Canaan. At this pivotal moment, Moses stands up to expound the law of the LORD to the new generation about to enter the Promised Land (1:1–5).

He begins with the LORD's command at Mount Horeb nearly forty years earlier: they had stayed around that mountain long enough, and it was time to break camp and advance into Canaan—from the hill country of the Amorites all the way to the Euphrates River—to take possession of the land the LORD had sworn to give to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (1:6–8).

Moses recalls that even then the people had grown "as numerous as the stars in the sky," far too heavy a burden for him to carry all their disputes alone. So he told each tribe to choose wise, respected men to be appointed as leaders over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. He charged the judges to judge fairly between fellow Israelites and resident foreigners alike: "Do not show partiality in judging," hear both the small and the great, and be afraid of no one, "for judgment belongs to God." Only cases too difficult for them were to be brought to Moses (1:9–18).

Then Israel set out from Horeb and crossed a vast and dreadful wilderness to Kadesh-barnea, right at the threshold of the Amorite hill country. The trip should have taken only eleven days (1:2). Upon arrival, Moses urged the people to go up and take the land without fear. Instead, the people proposed sending spies ahead to scout the route and the cities. Moses thought the plan seemed good and selected twelve men, one from each tribe. They went up as far as the Valley of Eshcol, brought back some of its fruit, and confirmed that the land was indeed good (1:19–25).

Yet the people refused to go up. They grumbled in their tents that the LORD hated them and had brought them out of Egypt only to hand them over to the Amorites to be destroyed. They panicked at reports of taller, stronger people, cities fortified up to the sky, and the giant descendants of Anak. Moses tried to reassure them: the LORD Himself would go before them and fight for them just as He had done in Egypt, and all along the wilderness road He had "carried you, as a man carries his son," going ahead in fire by night and in a cloud by day to find places for them to camp. Still, they would not trust Him (1:26–33).

The LORD heard their words, grew angry, and swore that not one man of that unbelieving generation would see the Promised Land—except Caleb, because he had followed the LORD wholeheartedly. Even Moses, because of the people, was told he would not enter the land; Joshua would lead Israel across instead. As for the little ones whom the people had feared would become plunder for their enemies, they were the very ones who would inherit the land. The older generation was ordered to turn back into the wilderness toward the Red Sea (1:34–40).

Hearing the sentence, the people panicked, confessed their sin, strapped on their weapons, and insisted on marching up to fight immediately. The LORD told Moses to warn them not to go, because He was not among them and they would be defeated. Defiantly, they marched up into the hill country anyway. The Amorites came out and "chased you like a swarm of bees," routing them all the way to Hormah. The people returned and wept before the LORD, but He would no longer listen. So they remained at Kadesh for a long time (1:41–46).

## Key Takeaways

**An eleven-day journey turned into nearly forty years because of unbelief.** The author places the figure of eleven days right at the start of the speech (1:2) as a painful reminder. God's promise was never far away; fear and unbelief stretched a short walk into an entire lifetime.

**Unbelief always distorts the heart of God.** In their fear, Israel did not merely doubt God's strength—they accused His motives, claiming He hated them and had rescued them only to leave them to die (1:27). Moses answers with the exact opposite picture: God is like a father carrying his own son through dangerous ground (1:31).

**Delayed, self-willed obedience is still disobedience.** When God told them to go, they were too afraid to move; when God told them to stop, they rushed eagerly into battle (1:41–43). Victory never depends on a burst of human enthusiasm, but on whether the LORD is with His people.

**Justice in the community flows from God Himself.** In appointing judges, Moses was not merely delegating administration. He reminded them never to favor the wealthy or fear the powerful, because every verdict they rendered was given on God's behalf (1:17).

## Deeper Meaning

**The image of a father carrying his son opens the way for Scripture's portrait of God's fatherly love.** Moses describes God carrying all Israel through the wilderness as a father carries his son (1:31). Centuries later, the prophet Hosea echoes this picture of God teaching His people to walk and taking them up in His arms (Hosea 11:3–4), and in the New Testament Paul draws on the same thought in his sermon at Antioch, recalling how God patiently bore with them in the wilderness for forty years (Acts 13:18).

**The tragedy at Kadesh-barnea becomes a lasting warning for the New Testament church.** The Letter to the Hebrews holds up this very generation that fell in the wilderness as a lesson for followers of Jesus: they failed to enter God's rest not because they lacked strength, but because of hardened hearts and unbelief (Hebrews 3:16–19). Just as Joshua succeeded Moses to lead Israel into the Promised Land (1:38), Jesus—who bears the same Hebrew name as Joshua—brings His people into true and lasting rest (Hebrews 4:8–10).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who wrote Deuteronomy, and when was it written?** Long-standing Jewish and Christian tradition attributes the bulk of the book to Moses near the end of his life, around the fifteenth to thirteenth centuries BC, with the account of his death in chapter 34 added by a successor such as Joshua. Many modern scholars also highlight how closely the book's structure resembles ancient Near Eastern *suzerain-vassal treaties*—historical prologue, stipulations, blessings and curses, and witnesses—and connect the book with the "Book of the Law" discovered in the temple during King Josiah's reign in 622 BC (2 Kings 22:8–13). From this, one line of scholarship argues that the book was compiled or edited in the seventh century BC from earlier Mosaic traditions, while more conservative scholars note that the treaty structure matches earlier second-millennium forms from the fourteenth to thirteenth centuries BC, supporting a Mosaic origin that was carefully preserved and copied down to Josiah's day. Whichever view one takes, the book's core message remains unchanged: a call to love God wholeheartedly and remain faithful to His covenant.

**Why does Moses say the people asked to send spies (1:22), whereas Numbers says the LORD commanded it (Numbers 13:1–2)?** The two accounts view the same event from different angles rather than contradicting each other. Out of anxiety, the people proposed the idea; Moses agreed and brought it before the LORD, who granted permission and issued the formal command to select twelve men. Retelling the story on the plains of Moab, Moses highlights the people's responsibility from the very first step.

**From whose perspective is the phrase *east of the Jordan* or *beyond the Jordan* in 1:1 written?** To someone standing in the land of Canaan on the west bank, the plains of Moab lie "beyond the Jordan" on the east bank. This phrasing shows that the opening verses serve as an editorial introduction written from within the Promised Land, looking back at Moses' final addresses.

**Matthew Henry**, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English pastor, remarks that before entering the Promised Land, the new generation needed to hear the history of their fathers' failures so they would not repeat the same mistakes.
