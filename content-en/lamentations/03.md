# Lamentations 3 — Faithfulness in the Deepest Pit

## Story

At the very center of Lamentations, the lens narrows abruptly. Gone is the wide view of a ruined city or the weeping widow of the opening two chapters; chapter 3 begins with the voice of a single man standing alone in the dark: "I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of His wrath." Throughout the first seventeen verses, he does not even speak God's name, repeating only the pronoun "He" as though every blow from heaven is aimed straight at his own body (3:1).

He describes that torment in suffocating, relentless images. This One has driven him into darkness, turned His hand against him all day long, worn away his flesh and skin, broken his bones, besieged him with bitterness, and trapped him in gloom like those long dead. He has walled him in so he cannot escape, weighed him down with heavy chains, shut out his prayer, and blocked his path with cut stones to twist his steps. He lies in wait like a bear and a lion to tear him apart, bends His bow, and sets him up as a target for arrows piercing his kidneys. He has become a laughingstock and the subject of mocking songs all day long; He has fed him wormwood, ground his teeth with gravel, trampled him in ashes, stripped his soul of peace, and made him forget what happiness feels like. Hitting absolute rock bottom, he utters a word of surrender: "My strength has perished, along with my hope from the LORD." Remembering his bitterness and wormwood, his soul sinks down within him (3:2–20).

Right at the floor of that abyss, the whole poem—and the entire book of Lamentations—pivots. He intentionally calls something else to mind: "Yet I call this to mind, and therefore I have hope: Because of the loving devotion of the LORD we are not consumed, for His mercies never fail. They are new every morning; great is Your faithfulness!" His soul answers back in firm resolve: "The LORD is my portion; therefore I will hope in Him." From that shaft of light, he reflects on how to endure affliction: the LORD is good to those who wait for Him and seek Him; it is good to wait quietly for His salvation, good to bear the yoke in one's youth, to sit alone in silence, to put one's mouth in the dust, and even to offer one's cheek to the striker and be filled with reproach (3:21–30).

His hope anchors itself in God's own character. The Lord will not cast off forever; even if He causes grief, He will show compassion according to the abundance of His steadfast love, "for He does not willingly afflict or grieve the sons of men." When ruthless people crush prisoners underfoot or pervert justice before the Most High, the Lord does not approve. Yet nothing unfolds outside His sovereign decree—both calamity and good come from the mouth of the Most High—so no living mortal has cause to complain when punished for their own sins (3:31–39).

Here the lone voice unexpectedly widens into the shared voice of the whole community ("us," "we"). He summons the entire nation: "Let us examine and test our ways, and turn back to the LORD. Let us lift up our hearts and hands to God in heaven." Yet their confession immediately hits a freezing wall of cloud: "We have transgressed and rebelled, and You have not forgiven!" God has wrapped Himself in anger and hidden behind a thick cloud so no prayer can pass through; the people have been made "scum and refuse" among the nations, mocked by open-mouthed foes, engulfed in panic, pitfall, ruin, and destruction (3:40–47).

From communal grief the speaker returns to the first person, streams of tears pouring down without ceasing until the LORD looks down from heaven, his heart aching over all the daughters of his city. He recalls how enemies hunted him like a bird for no reason, flung him into a pit, dropped stones over him, and left waters closing over his head until he thought, "I am cut off!" (3:48–54).

From the bottom of that deep pit, he called upon the name of the LORD and remembers how God answered: He heard his sighing, drew near, and spoke, "Do not fear!" God took up the cause of his soul and redeemed his life. Clinging to that memory, he lays all his present wrongs before God's courtroom: the LORD has seen every oppression, plot, taunt, and mocking song his foes aim at him all day long. Chapter 3 closes with a fierce plea for justice: repay them, O LORD, according to the work of their hands, put a veil over their hearts with Your curse upon them, and pursue and destroy them from under Your heavens (3:55–66).

## Key Takeaways

**True hope is not born from ignoring pain, but from remembering God rightly right inside pain.** Lamentations 3 does not begin with upbeat praise. The speaker walks through twenty pitch-black verses—teeth broken on gravel and hope extinguished (3:16–18)—before arriving at the radiant confession of 3:22–23. Mercies new every morning become truly precious only after we have walked through a night that seemed to have no dawn.

**Judgment is never God's delight; compassion is His true heart.** One of the deepest statements in the Old Testament stands in 3:33: "For He does not willingly afflict or grieve the sons of men." When God disciplines sin, He acts because righteousness requires it, never taking pleasure in human misery. When He shows mercy, He acts out of the fullness of His own heart (3:32).

**Having God as our portion is enough to stand when everything else is stripped away.** With land conquered, temple burned, and homes in ruins, the speaker still tells himself, "The LORD is my portion" (3:24). If our deepest treasure consists of earthly things, catastrophe can wipe it all out; if God Himself is our inheritance, no enemy can touch it.

**The journey through suffering is never a smooth upward climb.** Right after the bright summit of hope in 3:21–39, the speaker plunges back into crying "You have not forgiven!" and feeling prayer blocked by thick clouds (3:42–44). Scripture is refreshingly honest about the human heart: believers do not stay soaring forever after one moment of comfort; they keep crying out from the pit again and again, clinging to the God who once drew near and said, "Do not fear!" (3:55–57).

## Deeper Meaning

**Mercies new every morning trace straight back to God's self-revelation at Sinai.** When the speaker declares, "Because of the loving devotion of the LORD we are not consumed, for His mercies never fail" (3:22), he is gripping the very character God proclaimed to Moses after the golden calf crisis: "The LORD, the LORD God, compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and truth" (Exodus 34:6). That echo harmonizes with the psalmist's testimony that God's anger lasts only a moment while His favor lasts a lifetime—weeping may stay the night, but joy comes in the morning (Psalms 30:5).

**"The LORD is my portion" echoes the vow of the tribe of Levi.** When Canaan was divided, the Levites received no territorial allotment like the other tribes, for God told Aaron, "I am your portion and your inheritance" (Numbers 18:20). Now that all Judah has lost land and home, every believer learns to pray like the Levites and the psalmists: "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup" (Psalms 16:5; 73:26).

**The suffering man evokes Job, Jeremiah, and the Messiah.** The first half of chapter 3 directly echoes Job's agony—walled in by darkness and set up as a target for arrows piercing his kidneys (3:7–13; Job 16:12–13; 19:8). Being mocked all day and flung into a deep pit also closely mirrors Jeremiah's own life when dropped into the muddy cistern (3:14; 3:53–55; Jeremiah 20:7; 38:6). Looking further ahead, the sufferer who "offers his cheek to the one who strikes him" and takes his fill of insults (3:30) links directly to Isaiah's Suffering Servant (Isaiah 50:6) and to both the teaching and the passion of Jesus (Matthew 5:39). Likewise, "it is good for a man to bear the yoke while he is young" (3:27) shines in fresh light when Jesus invites the weary to take His easy yoke upon themselves and find rest for their souls (Matthew 11:29–30).

**The apostle Paul borrows Lamentations' picture of "scum and refuse."** In 3:45, the exiles lament that God has made them "scum and refuse among the nations." Centuries later, describing the humiliation the apostles endured for the gospel, Paul writes to the Corinthians using the very same imagery: "We have become the scum of the earth, the refuse of the world" (1 Corinthians 4:13). Following the suffering Christ stands in direct continuity with the sorrows of God's people of old.

**Crying out from the depths and hearing "Do not fear!"** Calling up from the pit and hearing God draw close with the word "Do not fear!" (3:55–57) is a motif running right through Scripture—from the patriarchs to Jesus stepping toward His disciples across stormy waters. God does not always prevent the pit, yet He descends right into the pit to plead our case and redeem our lives (3:58).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is "the man who has seen affliction" in 3:1?** The text leaves the speaker unnamed. Traditional interpretation identifies him directly with Jeremiah, since he endured daily mockery (Jeremiah 20:7) and was lowered into a deep cistern (Jeremiah 38:6), matching 3:14 and 3:53–55 closely. Many modern scholars, however, note that the speaker identifies with the whole nation as "we" (3:22; 3:40–47) and acknowledges punishment for sin (3:39; 3:42), whereas Jeremiah was not guilty of national rebellion. Accordingly, they see this "man" as a representative figure embodying Judah itself, or a model for any sufferer walking through disaster while learning to trust God. Both readings complement one another: one individual's lived agony can become the voice of an entire people.

**Does 3:38—"both adversity and good come from the mouth of the Most High"—mean God creates evil?** This line often troubles readers, much like Isaiah 45:7. In Hebrew, the word translated "adversity" or "calamity" (contrasted with "good") denotes disaster, hardship, or judgment rather than moral evil or sin. Just two verses earlier (3:34–36), the speaker explicitly states that when people crush prisoners or twist justice, "the Lord does not approve." Verse 3:38 affirms that the universe is not split between two equal rival deities—one good, one evil—but that every event great and small unfolds beneath the supreme sovereignty of God.

**Chapter 3 uses a triple alphabet acrostic.** Whereas chapters 1 and 2 have twenty-two verses corresponding to the twenty-two Hebrew letters, chapter 3 expands to sixty-six verses: every group of three consecutive verses begins with the same letter from *Aleph* to *Tav*, keeping the same *Pe*-before-*Ayin* swap seen in chapter 2 (3:46–51). Placing this most intricate poem at the exact center of the five-chapter book is unmistakably deliberate: the beating heart of the whole book of laments is the confession of God's faithfulness in 3:21–33.

**Why does mid-chapter hope sink back into darkness and curses at the end?** Many people quote 3:22–24 as uplifting encouragement while skipping the rest of the chapter. Yet immediately after that luminous confession, the poet still grieves that prayers cannot pierce the cloud (3:44), then ends by asking God to bring hardness of heart, His curse, and destruction upon his foes beneath heaven (3:64–66). That sharp tone is hard to read. Commentators note two key truths: first, honest faith lives inside the real tension between trusting God's mercy and facing ongoing horror; second, the cry for retribution in 3:64–66 hands all judgment over to God rather than taking private vengeance, voicing the raw anguish of one persecuted without cause (3:52), before the New Testament calls believers to pray for their persecutors through the power of the cross.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, cited Lamentations 3:30 ("Let him offer his cheek to the one who strikes him; let him be filled with reproach") when explaining Jesus' famous teaching on turning the other cheek (Matthew 5:39). **Lamentations Rabbah** (*Midrash Eikhah*), the classic Jewish midrash compiled around the fifth century, pauses in wonder at 3:23 ("They are new every morning; great is Your faithfulness!"). The rabbis explain that each morning when people wake, God renews His work of creation and restores a fresh soul to them after night's sleep; that daily faithfulness serves as the sure pledge that He will likewise raise the dead in the world to come. *(Lamentations Rabbah, on chapter 3 verse 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the rabbinic interpretation of Lamentations 3:23 regarding God renewing creation and restoring souls every morning is well known in Lamentations Rabbah; have not verified exact section number -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, treats 3:21–24 as a model of inner spiritual struggle in which faith refuses to let despair have the last word, deliberately calling God's mercy back to remembrance. Commenting especially on 3:33 ("He does not willingly afflict or grieve the sons of men"), Calvin insists that God is never cruel and takes no pleasure in tormenting anyone; He disciplines reluctantly, like a father or physician compelled to cause pain in order to heal, whereas compassion flows freely from His very nature. On 3:38, he affirms that both calamity and blessing fall under God's providence rather than blind chance, without ever making God the author of sin. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah and the Lamentations, on Lamentations chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's exposition of 3:21–24, 3:33, and divine providence in 3:38 in volume 5 of his Jeremiah and Lamentations commentaries; have not verified exact wording on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, writes on 3:22–23 that believers cannot live forever on yesterday's grace: every sunrise brings fresh mercies tailored for the new day. At 3:27 ("It is good for a man to bear the yoke while he is young"), he notes that learning discipline, humility, and patience early in life is the finest training for all the years ahead, while 3:33 proves that judgment is God's reluctant work whereas mercy is His true delight. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Lamentations chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's familiar observations on 3:22–23, 3:27, and 3:33; have not checked original text verbatim -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the celebrated nineteenth-century London pastor, frequently chose texts from chapter 3—above all 3:24 ("The LORD is my portion") and 3:27 on bearing the yoke in youth—as sermon themes. **Robert Alter**, the American Jewish scholar of biblical literature, regards chapter 3 as the artistic pinnacle of the book, noting how its triple acrostic (sixty-six terse lines, three per letter) slows the tempo and compresses emotion to maximum intensity. He highlights a profound structural choice: after climbing to the luminous peak of hope and wisdom counsel in 3:21–39, the poem deliberately descends back into communal despair and cries from the pit in 3:40–66, refusing to wrap up tragedy with an easy resolution. *(The Hebrew Bible: A Translation with Commentary, 2018, on Lamentations chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Alter's analysis of the triple acrostic and the rising-and-falling emotional arc of Lamentations 3; have not checked the 2018 print edition -->
