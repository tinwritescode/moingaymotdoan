# Lamentations 2 — The Day of the LORD's Anger

## Story

While chapter 1 lingered over Jerusalem's misery and Babylon's cruelty, chapter 2 lifts its gaze straight to the One standing behind it all. The dirge opens with a gasp of astonishment at the fierce wrath of God Himself. Across the first ten verses, the narrator piles up rapid-fire verbs describing God's own terrifying actions, until the Babylonian army fades almost entirely into the background.

In His anger, the Lord has shrouded Daughter Zion in a cloud, hurled down the splendor of Israel from heaven to earth, and failed to remember "His footstool"—the temple housing the ark of the covenant. Without pity He has swallowed up all the dwellings of Jacob, torn down Judah's strongholds, and brought both kingdom and princes down in dishonor. He has cut off every horn of Israel's strength, pulled back His protective right hand before the enemy, and burned in Jacob like a flaming fire consuming all around. Most frightening of all, God "has bent His bow like an enemy," standing "like a foe," devouring palaces, ruining fortresses, and piling up mourning and lamentation in Daughter Judah (2:1–5).

Even the most sacred center of worship is torn down by its own Owner. He has stripped away His tabernacle violently like a temporary booth in a garden, blotted out appointed feasts and Sabbaths in Zion, and spurned both king and priest. He has rejected His altar, abandoned His sanctuary, and handed palace walls over to enemies who roar inside the house of the LORD as though on a festival day. The LORD stretched out a measuring line—normally a builder's tool—to measure out destruction without holding back His hand. The city gates have sunk into the ground, their bars shattered; king and officials wander among the nations where the law is no more, and her prophets find no vision from the LORD. Amid the ruins, Zion's elders sit on the ground in silence, sprinkling dust on their heads and wearing sackcloth, while Jerusalem's young women bow their heads to the earth (2:6–10).

Halfway through the poem, the narrator can no longer keep an observer's distance. He breaks down weeping in the first person: his eyes are spent with tears, his stomach churns, and "my bile is poured out on the ground" at the most unbearable sight of the siege—infants and nursing children fainting in the city streets, gasping to their mothers, "Where is grain and wine?" before breathing their last right in their mothers' arms. Turning to Daughter Jerusalem, the speaker confesses he can find no comparison to comfort her, because "your wound is as deep as the sea; who can heal you?" (2:11–13).

The narrator pinpoints one glaring reason catastrophe struck before the city turned back: her false prophets saw "false and worthless visions" and refused to expose her iniquity so her captivity might have been averted. Now passersby clap their hands, hiss, and shake their heads in mockery, quoting the city's once-famous titles: is this the place they called "the perfection of beauty, the joy of the whole earth"? Enemies open their mouths, hiss, and gnash their teeth, gloating that they have swallowed Jerusalem on the very day they longed for. Yet the narrator insists on a bitter truth: this was no lucky victory for the foe; the LORD has carried out the very warning He decreed long ago (2:14–17).

Knowing it is the LORD's hand, however, does not silence the speaker. On the contrary, he urges Zion's walls and people not to hold back their tears: let tears stream down like a river day and night, rise at the start of every night watch to "pour out your heart like water in the presence of the Lord," and lift up your hands to Him for the lives of the starving children fainting at every street corner (2:18–19).

The final three verses hand the voice back to Jerusalem herself, yielding one of the boldest, rawest prayers in all of Scripture. She does not plead softly; she confronts the LORD directly: "Look, O LORD, and consider! Whom have You ever treated like this?" She holds two unbearable sights right up before God's eyes: mothers so starved they eat their own treasured children, and priests and prophets slain inside the Lord's very sanctuary. Young and old lie dead in the dust of the streets by the sword; God has summoned terrors on every side as though calling a festival assembly, leaving no survivor, so every child she nurtured and raised has been destroyed by the enemy (2:20–22).

## Key Takeaways

**No sacred landmark or ritual can safeguard a people who abandon God.** Judah assumed the Jerusalem temple was untouchable because it was God's "footstool" (2:1). Chapter 2 shatters that illusion: once the people's hearts left God, He Himself tore down His dwelling like a garden hut and handed His altar over to invaders (2:6–7). Genuine relationship with God—not stone walls or religious ceremony—is our real shelter.

**Flattering reassurance is the deadliest kind of lie.** Verse 2:14 condemns the false prophets not for speaking harsh words, but for speaking soothing ones: they refused to confront sin while there was still time to turn back. Any friend or leader who lulls us into comfort while we walk toward a cliff is actually helping destroy us.

**When grief is overwhelming, resist offering cheap platitudes.** Standing before infants dying of hunger in their mothers' arms, the narrator honestly admits, "What can I liken to you, that I may comfort you?" because "your wound is as deep as the sea" (2:13). Some losses are so immense that forced explanations only offend; the truest kindness is to sit down in tears and pour out that grief before God (2:18–19).

**Honest faith dares to question God right through its tears.** The closing prayer hides none of its shock: "Whom have You ever treated like this?" (2:20). Pouring out one's heart like water before the Lord (2:19) means emptying every ounce of agony, outrage, and unanswered bewilderment at His feet instead of walking away in cold bitterness.

## Deeper Meaning

**From "the joy of the whole earth" to scorn from passersby.** The Psalms of Zion once sang that Mount Zion is "the joy of all the earth" (Psalms 48:2) and that "from Zion, perfect in beauty, God shines forth" (Psalms 50:2). In Lamentations 2:15, passersby shake their heads and repeat those very lyrics as cruel mockery. And the image of onlookers shaking their heads, hissing, and opening their mouths in derision (2:15–16) forms a direct bridge from the righteous sufferer's lament (Psalms 22:7) to Jesus mocked upon the cross (Matthew 27:39).

**The temple is never a lucky charm.** Saying God "has not remembered His footstool" (2:1) echoes a familiar Old Testament title for the ark and the temple (1 Chronicles 28:2; Psalms 99:5; 132:7). Before Jerusalem fell, Jeremiah had warned the people against blind trust in the temple, declaring God was ready to treat it just as He treated abandoned Shiloh (Jeremiah 7:12–14). The detail of God stretching out a measuring line for demolition (2:8) also fulfills the judgment spoken in King Manasseh's day: He would stretch over Jerusalem the measuring line of Samaria and wipe the city clean like a dish turned upside down (2 Kings 21:13).

**Exposing the tragedy of prophets who only preached "peace."** Verse 2:14 directly echoes the relentless battle Jeremiah and Ezekiel waged against false prophets who dressed the people's wounds lightly, crying "Peace, peace!" when there was no peace (Jeremiah 6:14; 23:16–17; Ezekiel 13:10). When real disaster hit, empty slogans evaporated, leaving only God's solemn word standing firm (2:17).

**Unthinkable famine and blood spilled in the sanctuary.** The two horrific scenes of 2:20 echo both covenant law and history. Parents eating their own children under siege was the ultimate covenant curse warned of in the Law (Leviticus 26:29; Deuteronomy 28:53). Meanwhile, the cry over priest and prophet murdered inside the sanctuary recalls the stoning of the prophet Zechariah in the temple courtyard under King Joash (2 Chronicles 24:20–21)—a crime Jesus Himself cited centuries later when condemning Jerusalem for shedding the blood of the prophets (Matthew 23:35).

**"Pour out your heart like water" is the classic language of desperate prayer.** The appeal to "pour out your heart like water in the presence of the Lord" (2:19) chimes with the psalmist's call to pour out our hearts before God our refuge (Psalms 62:8). When human strength has nothing left to hold back, prayer ceases to be polished speech and becomes a whole heart spilling out with tears.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did God truly become an "enemy" to His own people?** Many readers shudder at chapter 2's stark language that God "bent His bow like an enemy," became "like a foe," and "destroyed without pity" (2:4–5; 2:17). Notice the crucial little word *"like"*: the poet says God acted *like* an enemy, not that His essential character turned hostile. Even as the temple burned, the author refused to believe Babylon's gods had overpowered the LORD; he insisted everything remained under God's sovereign hand, unfolding the warnings decreed long before when the covenant was broken (2:17). Yet chapter 2 never softens the horror: it holds the full tension between confidence in God's sovereignty and unbearable human pain.

**Starving infants and mothers eating their children are painfully hard passages.** Verses 2:11–12 and 2:20 depict babies dying in their mothers' arms and desperate women consuming their own children during a siege lasting more than a year and a half (2 Kings 25:1–3). Scripture neither sanitizes the brutality of war and famine nor sidesteps the sharpest question about innocent suffering: infants never committed the sins of kings and false prophets, so why do they collapse at street corners? Chapter 2 offers no neat philosophical answer; instead, it holds those very children right up before God's eyes as the most urgent appeal possible to move His compassion (2:19–20).

**The alphabet order shifts slightly at 2:16–17.** Like chapter 1, chapter 2 is a twenty-two-verse Hebrew alphabetic acrostic. Here, however—as well as in chapters 3 and 4—the sixteenth and seventeenth letters swap places: *Pe* (verse 16) comes before *Ayin* (verse 17), reversing the standard sequence used in chapter 1. Modern scholars note that in the sixth century BC the ordering of these two Hebrew letters was not yet completely fixed, while ancient Jewish tradition found deep symbolism in the "mouth" speaking before the "eye."

**What is meant by "wall of Daughter Zion" in 2:18?** Verse 2:18 calls directly on the "wall of Daughter Zion" to let tears flow like a river. Readers sometimes wonder why stone walls are told to weep. This is vivid poetic personification: even the battered walls are summoned to join the citizens in mourning, and many translations understand the phrase as addressing the inhabitants of the walled city as a whole.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the foundational compendium of rabbinic debate and interpretation, immediately notices the swapped order of two Hebrew letters across chapters 2, 3, and 4: *Pe* (meaning "mouth") stands ahead of *Ayin* (meaning "eye"). **Lamentations Rabbah** (*Midrash Eikhah*), the classic Jewish midrash compiled around the fifth century, reads the haunting question in 2:20 ("Should priest and prophet be killed in the sanctuary of the Lord?") and connects it directly to the murder of the prophet Zechariah son of Jehoiada in the temple court (2 Chronicles 24:20–21). **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, lingers carefully on 2:14 to warn against the guilt of unfaithful preachers. When prophets spoke only pleasing words and refused to expose the people's sins, they robbed the nation of its one chance to repent and avert exile. Turning to 2:18–19, Calvin urges sufferers not to bottle up grief within: "pouring out the heart like water" means laying every sorrow openly before God in sincerity, above all pleading earnestly for the helpless and for little children. *(Commentaries on the Book of the Prophet Jeremiah and the Lamentations, on Lamentations chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Calvin's exposition of false prophets in 2:14 and pouring out the heart like water in 2:19 in volume 5 of his Jeremiah and Lamentations commentaries; have not checked exact wording on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, observes from 2:1 and 2:6–7 that no outward religious privilege—not even the temple or the ark as God's "footstool"—can shield a people stubbornly rebelling against Him. Commenting on the night-watch prayer in 2:18–19, he stresses that even under God's heaviest discipline, the door of prayer stays open; crying out for the lives of starving little ones is an appeal that reaches straight to the mercy of the heavenly Father. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Lamentations chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's commentary on the abandoned temple in 2:1–7 and nighttime prayer for children in 2:18–19; have not verified original wording -->

**Robert Alter**, the American Jewish scholar of biblical literature, draws attention to the crushing momentum of destructive verbs attributed directly to God in the opening half of chapter 2 (2:1–8), especially the grim irony of God stretching out a builder's "measuring line" to demolish. In his view, the poem's deepest emotional turning point comes at 2:11–12 when detached third-person reporting shatters before the sight of infants starving to death in their mothers' arms, transforming a dirge over a fallen city into visceral, inconsolable flesh-and-blood agony. *(The Hebrew Bible: A Translation with Commentary, 2018, on Lamentations chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Alter's distinctive literary remarks on the judgment verbs in 2:1–8, the measuring line in 2:8, and the dying children in 2:11–12 in Lamentations 2; have not checked the 2018 print edition -->
