# Esther 10 — The Greatness of Mordecai

## Story

After the sweeping reversal and the pair of letters by Queen Esther and Mordecai establishing Purim in chapter 9, the entire book closes with a compact epilogue barely three verses long. The camera pulls wide once more across the sprawling Persian Empire as every storm in Susa and the provinces settles down: King Xerxes imposed tribute across the mainland and all the distant coastal islands of the sea (10:1). Imperial governance resumed its steady march, stretching from deep inland provinces all the way out to faraway Mediterranean shores.

The narrator pauses neither to list royal military campaigns nor to catalog architectural monuments. Instead, he points readers toward the empire's official archives in the timeless closing formula of ancient historians: every deed of royal power and might, along with the full record of Mordecai's greatness and how the king elevated him, stands written inside the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Media and Persia (10:2).

Immediately the emperor fades into the background, yielding the book's closing spotlight to the quiet hero of the Jews. The reason Mordecai's career earned a permanent place inside imperial chronicles shines forth unmistakably: Mordecai the Jew ranked second only to King Xerxes (10:3). Rising from an obscure exile seated quietly outside the king's gate, he now stood at the highest administrative seat in the empire beneath the crown itself.

Perched at the apex of a pagan superpower, Mordecai neither forgot his roots nor drifted away from his own people. On the contrary, across the vast Jewish communities scattered throughout the provinces, he stood preeminent and warmly esteemed by his fellow Jews (10:3). Court honors never built a wall between him and his kinsmen.

The book closes neither with clashing swords nor with lavish drinking feasts like chapter 1's opening, resting instead on two quiet, steady habits of this Jewish prime minister: he continually sought the good of his people and spoke up for the peace and welfare of all his descendants (10:3). A narrative born inside a drunken emperor's vain spectacle concludes with the calm silhouette of a servant-leader pouring his days into his people's peace.

## Key Takeaways

**True authority is measured by who it blesses, never by how loudly it boasts.** At the opening of Esther, Xerxes spent six full months parading imperial wealth (1:1–4). By chapter 3, Haman occupied this exact grand vizier's chair, obsessing over bowed knees and offering ten thousand talents of silver to purchase a people's slaughter (3:1–2; 3:9). Once Mordecai steps into that very seat, swagger and forced bowing disappear; he invests his authority pursuing the welfare and lasting peace of his people (10:3).

**Rising high in society never requires hiding or disowning who you are.** Back in chapter 2, stepping into the palace, Esther had to conceal her ethnicity and homeland on Mordecai's caution (2:10). By chapter 3, simply being named a Jew nearly brought an empire-wide death sentence (3:5–6). Yet on the final line of the book, Scripture proclaims his identity aloud without flinching: "Mordecai the Jew was second in rank to King Xerxes" (10:3). Diligent public service in a wider society and steady faithfulness to one's people can stand side by side with integrity.

**Durable peace outweighs a single noisy victory.** The book could easily have wrapped up at chapter 9 amid Purim's music and laughter. Appending these three quiet verses signals that real deliverance is never just one dramatic escape. Far more precious are the unglamorous years afterward, when steady leadership works day by day to nurture goodness and safety for generations ahead (10:2–3).

**God's people can thrive steadily even while earthly empires still stand.** Verse 10:1 keeps both feet firmly planted in real life: Xerxes still sits on the throne, still collecting taxes from mainland and distant islands alike (10:1). Rescue in Susa did not magically turn exile into a fairy tale free of taxes or foreign rulers. Even inside an imperfect world under a king who does not know God, His people still experience protection, dignity, and quiet peace.

## Deeper Meaning

**Mordecai joins the great lineage of exiles placed inside pagan courts.** Stepping back across the Old Testament reveals three unmistakable parallel portraits: Joseph serving second to Pharaoh in Egypt (Genesis 41:40–44), Daniel rising to chief administrator inside Babylon and Persia (Daniel 2:48; 6:2–3; 6:28), and now Mordecai serving second to King Xerxes (10:3). Even the opening note about Xerxes levying tribute across the empire (10:1) faintly echoes Joseph structuring Egypt's one-fifth harvest levy for Pharaoh (Genesis 47:24), while God's deeper purpose through both men stayed focused on preserving life for a vast covenant people (Genesis 45:7; 50:20). Whether in Egypt, Babylon, or Susa, God quietly places His servants right where needed to keep His people standing.

**Imperial annals vanish into dust while history inside God's hands endures.** Verse 10:2 adopts the classic closing citation of the Books of Kings pointing readers to royal chronicles (1 Kings 11:41; 14:19; 14:29). Inside Esther itself, the royal chronicle already swung history's hinge during Xerxes' sleepless night, rescuing Mordecai from Haman's pole (2:23; 6:1–2). Now Mordecai's rise enters the official annals of Media and Persia right alongside Xerxes' imperial feats (10:2). Yet history delivers an unforgettable irony: those monumental Persian royal archives have vanished completely off the face of the earth without a scrap remaining—while this slim scroll named after an orphaned Jewish woman is still read across every continent, keeping Mordecai's memory alive today.

**"Seeking the good" and "speaking peace" chime directly with the prophets and Psalms.** The book's closing cadence breathes the best spirit of exilic and post-exilic faith. In Persian-era Nehemiah, rivals bristled bitterly when someone arrived "to seek the welfare of the Israelites" (Nehemiah 2:10). Earlier, as Judeans settled into Babylonian captivity, Jeremiah conveyed God's instruction to "seek the peace and prosperity" of the city where they lived in exile (Jeremiah 29:7), while the Psalms taught pilgrims to pray for peace and seek one another's good for the sake of brothers and friends (Psalms 122:6–9). Mordecai's closing portrait (10:3) proves that God's people never isolate themselves in resentment, becoming channels of peace wherever they dwell.

**A quiet ending on foreign soil reminds us exile is not our final home.** Esther's subdued closing shares a striking kinship with the final lines of 2 Kings, where Babylon's king releases exiled King Jehoiachin from prison, speaks kindly to him, and grants him a lifelong seat at the royal table—yet Jehoiachin still remains in Babylon rather than returning home (2 Kings 25:27–30). Here too in Esther 10, Mordecai rises high and Israel breathes in peace, yet they still live paying tribute under a Persian monarch, miles from Jerusalem and its temple (10:1–3). Genuine and precious as that rescue is, it leaves human hearts aching forward toward the indestructible kingdom God Himself will establish forever (Daniel 2:44).

**From a faithful grand vizier toward an infinitely greater Ruler.** Chapter 10 illustrates Proverbs cleanly: when the righteous thrive, the people rejoice; when the wicked rule, the people groan (Proverbs 29:2). Yet noble as Mordecai was, he stayed one mortal minister serving beneath a moody Persian emperor—his shield could last only a single lifetime. Looking onward into the New Testament, Christian readers spot inside Mordecai—an exalted brother using power not to dominate, but to work for his people's good and speak peace to all his kin—a modest preview of Jesus. He is Isaiah's Prince of Peace, whose righteous reign and peace know no end (Isaiah 9:6–7). Unlike Gentile rulers lording power over subjects, He came to serve and give His life as a ransom (Mark 10:42–45); He Himself *is* our peace (Ephesians 2:14), and He lives forever to intercede for everyone drawing near to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does the book's final chapter open abruptly with King Xerxes raising taxes?** Moving straight from chapter 9's emotional crest into the dry administrative line of 10:1—"King Xerxes imposed tribute throughout the empire, to its distant shores"—can feel startling. Commentators highlight three complementary threads. First, it answers chapters 2 and 3 directly: at Esther's coronation Xerxes granted a provincial tax release (2:18), and Haman later dangled ten thousand talents of silver to purchase genocide (3:9). Now, with Haman dead, the royal coffers are far from empty—perhaps Mordecai's able administration organized steady taxation across mainland and Mediterranean islands right when the historical Xerxes needed revenue after his costly Greek war. Second, framing the empire's sweeping scale magnifies Mordecai's astonishing stature in verses 2–3. Third, the Hebrew word rendered "tribute" (*mas*) frequently denotes harsh forced labor or heavy levies elsewhere in the Old Testament (1 Kings 5:13; 9:15; Judges 1:28)—leading many scholars to sense sober realism tinged with dry irony: Xerxes never turned into a saintly king, leaving Israel all the more thankful for a steadfast advocate like Mordecai standing beside the throne.

**Why does Queen Esther go unmentioned in chapter 10?** After naming the entire book for Esther, leaving her out of its closing three verses makes some readers wonder if she was pushed aside. Reading smoothly across the boundary from late chapter 9 into chapter 10 clears up the symmetry immediately: chapter 9 just culminated in Queen Esther's own supreme authority officially ratifying Purim across all generations (9:29–32). Chapter 10 then supplies a crisp political epilogue resolving the public court rivalry launched back in chapter 3 between Mordecai and Haman for the vizier's office (3:1; 8:2). In ancient Persian protocol, taxes, imperial administration, and state chronicles sat publicly with king and prime minister, while the queen exercised authority within the royal household.

**The nuance behind "held in high esteem by his many fellow Jews" (10:3).** Literally the Hebrew text of 10:3 says Mordecai was pleasing to *the multitude* (or *the majority*) of his brothers. Two classic readings arise. Most modern scholars take the phrase naturally as emphasizing sheer numbers: Mordecai enjoyed deep affection across the vast multitude of Jewish communities spread throughout all 127 provinces. Classic Jewish rabbinic commentary, however, pressed the literal sense of "majority"—*most*, not *every single one*—observing shrewdly that no leader stepping into foreign imperial politics escapes criticism from every faction among his own people. Both insights ring true to human experience: faithful leaders aim never to flatter everyone at any price, earning the trust of the wide community through integrity and steady care.

**Why do Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Bibles continue beyond 10:3?** Opening a Catholic or Orthodox Bible shows Esther continuing right past 10:3 (often numbered starting at chapter 10 verse 4). This closing section is one of six Greek additions preserved in the ancient Septuagint but absent from the original Hebrew text followed by the Berean Standard Bible and Protestant Bibles. That Greek epilogue interprets Mordecai's prophetic dream from the Greek prologue—two dragons, a tiny spring swelling into a river, and shining sunlight—explicitly naming God as Israel's Deliverer, followed by a colophon noting how the Greek translation traveled to Egypt. While the Greek edition spells out God's hand openly to reassure readers, the Hebrew book preserves its poised silence straight to its final syllable at 10:3, inviting readers themselves to trace the unseen Hand steering every turn.

Candidly he noted that the authentic Hebrew book of Esther concludes completely here; everything following—Mordecai's dream interpretation and prayers naming God explicitly—stemmed solely from the popular Greek version of his era, so Jerome gathered those additions separately at the end with clear notes helping readers distinguish original Hebrew Scripture from later expansions. *(Preface to Esther and notes in the Latin Vulgate)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jerome's explicit Vulgate note marking chapter 10 verse 3 as the end of Hebrew Esther and separating the Greek additions is a famous landmark in biblical textual history -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, classic Jewish treasury of oral law and exegesis from the fifth and sixth centuries, zooms in sharply on the Hebrew wording of 10:3 meaning "esteemed by the *majority* of his brothers." Why *most* brothers rather than *all* brothers? The ancient sages explain that once Mordecai assumed high Persian state office and immersed himself in politics, a faction of the Jewish council (*Sanhedrin*) distanced themselves from him, holding Torah study loftier than political statecraft. **Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, draws rich lessons on earthly renown and godly statesmanship from these three brief verses. Reflecting on verses 1 and 2, he remarks that massive royal Persian archives boasting imperial might have vanished without trace, whereas Scripture endures forever because it tells the story of God's people. Turning to verse 3, Henry praises Mordecai as a rare jewel among public officials: promotion never made him forget his kin or line his own pockets, as he poured all his influence into seeking his people's welfare and speaking peace on their behalf. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's twin observations on vanished Persian chronicles versus enduring Scripture and Mordecai's selfless statesmanship in chapter 10, exact wording not verified -->

**Jon D. Levenson**, Jewish scholar and Harvard professor of Old Testament, reads chapter 10 as a refreshingly clear-eyed closing portrait of Jewish life in diaspora. Far from filler, opening with Xerxes taxing mainland and islands deliberately reminds readers that Jews still live under a foreign empire rather than enjoying sovereign independence back in Zion. Set beside Mordecai's prominence and tireless pursuit of welfare and peace in verses 2 and 3, however, the epilogue offers grounded encouragement: even on foreign soil, Jewish communities can dwell in dignity, safety, and flourishing when fidelity joins hands with wisdom. *(Esther: A Commentary, Old Testament Library, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Levenson's realist analysis of diaspora life balancing imperial taxation with Mordecai's advocacy in chapter 10, exact page numbers not verified -->

**Karen H. Jobes**, American Old Testament professor, sets Esther's brief epilogue beside the endings of Joseph, Daniel, and especially the final verses of 2 Kings. Mordecai sitting right beside Persia's king secured genuine, tangible peace for the Jews, yet continuing to pay tribute to Xerxes underscores that every earthly deliverance stays partial—preserving the covenant people across one season without ending exile itself.
