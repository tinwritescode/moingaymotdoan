# Esther 9 — The Day of Reversal and the Feast of Purim

## Story

At last the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, Adar, arrived—the exact date both rival decrees of King Xerxes took force together. It was the day the enemies of the Jews had counted on to crush them, yet the tables flipped completely: the Jews themselves gained the upper hand over those who hated them (9:1). Gathering in their towns across every province of the empire, Jewish communities stood ready to strike back against anyone coming to harm them. Nobody could withstand them, because dread of the Jews had settled over all the peoples (9:2). Even provincial nobles, satraps, governors, and royal officers threw their backing behind the Jews out of deep respect and awe for Mordecai, whose stature in the palace and renown across the provinces grew greater by the day (9:3–4).

In the fierce clashes of that day, the Jews met their attackers with the sword and overcame those who hated them (9:5). Inside the citadel of Susa alone, they struck down five hundred enemies along with all ten sons of Haman—whose names Scripture carefully preserves one by one: Parshandatha, Dalphon, Aspatha, Poratha, Adalia, Aridatha, Parmashta, Arisai, Aridai, and Vaizatha. Yet even though the royal edict explicitly authorized seizing enemy property, the narrator notes one decisive fact: they did not touch a single piece of plunder (9:6–10).

Before the day ended, the tally of the slain inside Susa reached the king (9:11). Xerxes told Queen Esther that if the Jews had struck down five hundred foes plus Haman's ten sons inside the citadel alone, imagine what had happened across the outlying provinces—then asked what further petition or request she wished granted (9:12). Esther replied that if it pleased the king, let the Jews in Susa be permitted one more day tomorrow to act under today's decree, and let Haman's ten sons be hung publicly upon the wooden pole (9:13). Xerxes agreed, issued the order in Susa, and the bodies of Haman's ten sons were displayed aloft (9:14). On the fourteenth of Adar, Susa's Jews assembled once more, struck down three hundred additional foes, and once again laid no hand upon the plunder (9:15).

Out in the provinces beyond the capital, Jewish communities finished defending themselves on the thirteenth, cutting down seventy-five thousand hostile attackers—and for a third emphatic time Scripture records that they never touched the plunder (9:16). On the fourteenth of Adar they rested and turned the day into a joyful banquet (9:17). Because Susa's Jews had fought across both the thirteenth and fourteenth, they rested and feasted on the fifteenth instead (9:18). That single calendar difference birthed a lasting custom: Jews living in rural villages and unwalled towns observe the fourteenth of Adar as a joyful holiday of feasting and exchanging food gifts with one another (9:19).

Mordecai wrote down everything that had happened and dispatched letters to all Jews across every province of King Xerxes, near and far, establishing an annual two-day festival on the fourteenth and fifteenth of Adar (9:20–21). These marked the days when the Jews gained relief from their foes—the month when grief flipped into gladness and mourning turned into a joyful holiday—celebrated with feasting, sending gifts of food to neighbors, and giving generously to the poor (9:22).

Jewish communities gladly embraced the custom they had begun alongside Mordecai's written instructions (9:23). Briefly the author recaps the feast's origin: Haman son of Hammedatha the Agagite had plotted to annihilate the Jews and cast *Pur*—the lot—to pick the day of their ruin; yet when Esther intervened before the king, his wicked plot rebounded straight onto his own head so that he and his sons ended up hung upon the pole (9:24–25). Drawn directly from the word *Pur*, these celebration days received the name *Purim* (9:26). The Jews took it upon themselves, their descendants, and everyone who would join them that every family across every province and city in every generation would faithfully remember and keep these two days without ever letting the memory fade (9:27–28).

Finally, Queen Esther—daughter of Abihail—together with Mordecai the Jew wrote a second letter with full royal authority confirming Purim, sending "words of peace and truth" across all one hundred and twenty-seven provinces (9:29–30). This confirmation sealed the dates of Purim alongside the obligations they had established for themselves and future generations regarding fasting and lamentation (9:31). Esther's decree formally ratified the festival of Purim, and the entire record was inscribed in a book to endure (9:32).

## Key Takeaways

**God's complete reversal transforms a scheduled day of doom into a day of celebration.** The opening verse distills the entire book's pulse into one sharp phrase: "the opposite happened" (9:1). The very date cast by lot to erase the Jewish people became the day they stepped into relief and safety—turning sorrow into laughter and mourning into a feast (9:22). History never belongs to whoever swings the biggest blade, but to the One who can invert any plot overnight.

**Defending innocent life stands worlds apart from greedy looting.** Amid the stark combat of chapter 9, the narrator deliberately repeats one refrain three separate times: "they did not lay a hand on the plunder" (9:10; 9:15; 9:16). Xerxes' edict legally allowed taking spoils (8:12), just as Haman had eyed Jewish property in the initial edict (3:13). Refusing every single coin proved their aim was purely preserving their families' survival, never enriching themselves through bloodshed.

**True joy never turns inward; it opens its hands wide to the poor.** Purim is far more than banquets and cheer. Right inside his founding letter, Mordecai built in two inseparable practices: exchanging food gifts with friends and giving generously to the needy (9:22). A people delivered from looming catastrophe only celebrates worthily when no struggling neighbor is left standing outside the feast.

**Deliverance must be written down and remembered so coming generations never forget.** Human memory fades startlingly fast once danger passes and daily comfort returns. Refusing to let this rescue vanish like yesterday's headline, Mordecai and Esther put it into writing and anchored an annual holiday for all generations (9:20; 9:28; 9:32). Remembering yesterday's mercy fuels courage for tomorrow's storms.

## Deeper Meaning

**King Saul's ancient failure is finally set right.** Repeating three times that the Jews refused every shred of plunder after defeating Haman's forces (9:10; 9:15; 9:16) carries deep resonance across Old Testament history. Centuries earlier, Mordecai's Benjaminite kinsman King Saul (cf. 2:5) was sent by God to fight King Agag of Amalek—ancestor of Haman "the Agagite" (cf. 3:1). Back then Saul failed grievously by sparing Agag and swooping eagerly onto the choicest plunder (1 Samuel 15:8–9; 15:19). Now Benjamin's descendants confront Agag's line one final time, finish the conflict, and refuse to touch a speck of spoil—echoing Abram's own nobility refusing even a thread or sandal strap from conquered enemies (Genesis 14:22–23).

**The lot *Pur* and God's quiet sovereignty.** What rich irony that Judaism's most exuberant holiday takes its name from a foreign word, *Pur*—the divination die Haman rolled to pick their execution day (3:7; 9:24; 9:26). The name itself smiles gently over human pride: schemers trust chance and lucky omens, yet Scripture long affirmed that while people cast lots into the lap, every outcome comes straight from the LORD (Proverbs 16:33). The exact date chosen by Haman's die became the monument to his defeat.

**Purim joins the festival rhythm of Passover and Nehemiah's joyful feast.** The solemn vow to remember and celebrate Purim across every generation without lapse (9:27–28) echoes Moses' charge establishing Passover as an everlasting memorial of Israel's escape from Egypt (Exodus 12:14). Likewise, Purim's double call to feast joyfully and share portions with the needy (9:22) harmonizes directly with Jerusalem's Persian-era renewal in Nehemiah, when crowds were urged to eat rich food, drink sweet wine, and send portions to anyone who had nothing prepared (Nehemiah 8:10). Among God's people, celebration and compassion always walk hand in hand.

**"Sorrow turned into joy" points ahead to the gospel's ultimate reversal.** Describing the day when grief turned into gladness and mourning into celebration (9:22) echoes not only the psalmist praising God for stripping off sackcloth and wrapping His people in joy (Psalms 30:11), but also Jesus' tender promise to His disciples on the eve of the cross: the world would gloat while they wept, yet their sorrow would be turned completely into joy (John 16:20). Every great Old Testament deliverance foreshadows the final reversal of death into resurrection life.

**Even after bitter conflict, the door stays open to outsiders.** In 9:27, binding themselves to keep Purim forever, the Jews explicitly include themselves, their children, and "all who join them." Fresh out of a life-or-death clash with foreign enemies, God's people refused to lock themselves inside suspicious tribalism, leaving seats wide open at the table for outsiders who choose to cast their lot with Israel.

## Notes & Nuances

**Wrestling honestly with 75,000 dead and a second day of fighting in chapter 9.** This is unquestionably the hardest section in the book of Esther—especially when read alongside Jesus' call to love enemies and pray for persecutors (Matthew 5:43–44). Readers naturally pause in sober shock at seventy-five thousand slain across the provinces (9:16), five hundred plus three hundred more in Susa (9:6; 9:15), and Esther requesting an extra day of combat plus displaying Haman's ten dead sons (9:13). Commentators weigh three vital points side by side. First, in raw survival terms, Haman's genocide decree remained legally live on Adar 13; Scripture carefully identifies those killed as "those who sought their harm" (9:2) and "their enemies" (9:16)—hostile anti-Jewish factions who chose to attack despite the political shift. Refusing plunder three times shows disciplined defense rather than chaotic pillaging, while extending Susa's authorization into day fourteen likely neutralized remaining pockets of Haman's partisans right outside the royal palace. Second, textually, the ancient Greek Septuagint records fifteen thousand slain in the provinces rather than seventy-five thousand, and many scholars recognize conventional ancient epic hyperbole underscoring the sweeping scale of survival. Third, many Christian interpreters honestly acknowledge that even within its real defensive setting, chapter 9's stark ferocity reflects the moral horizon of an age before the New Testament's full revelation; Scripture records candidly how God preserved Israel's physical existence without ever setting chapter 9 up as a blueprint for Christian retaliation today.

**Were Haman's ten sons hung alive or after dying in combat?** Skimming 9:13–14 quickly can give the mistaken impression that Esther requested the execution of ten living sons. Reading 9:7–10 carefully clarifies that all ten sons had already died in the fighting on Adar 13. Esther's request at day's end was therefore to display the corpses of those ten slain men publicly on the gallows (9:13–14)—standard ancient Persian deterrence signaling unmistakably that Haman's faction was finished for good.

**Why is Purim kept on Adar 14 in most places and Adar 15 in others?** The reason sits right in verses 9:17–19: provincial and village Jews fought on Adar 13 and rested to celebrate on Adar 14, whereas walled Susa fought across both Adar 13 and 14 and rested on Adar 15. Out of that grew enduring Jewish practice: most communities worldwide observe Purim on Adar 14, while ancient walled cities dating back to Joshua's day (most notably Jerusalem) celebrate *Shushan Purim* on Adar 15.

**Why mention "fasting and lamentation" inside a joyful festival decree?** At the close of 9:31, Esther and Mordecai's letter links Purim's celebration directly to the community's obligations regarding "fasting and lamentation." This reaches back to the urgent three-day fast kept by Esther and Susa's Jews in chapter 4 (4:16). Later Jewish tradition institutionalized Adar 13—the eve of Purim—as the *Fast of Esther*, reminding every generation that deliverance's laughter passes first through valleys of tears and crying out to God.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, famously voiced sharp discomfort with the book of Esther, with chapter 9's fierce violence and nationalistic edge fueling much of his distaste. He went so far as to remark that he was so hostile to the book he wished it did not exist, feeling it leaned too heavily toward pagan ways and fierce Jewish particularity. While later scholarship rightly criticizes both Luther's narrow reading and the painful anti-Jewish polemics of his later years, his candid reaction shows that Christians have long felt the genuine ethical tension inside chapter 9. *(Table Talk; On the Bondage of the Will, 1525)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther's famous critical remarks on Esther in Table Talk and On the Bondage of the Will are widely quoted across Esther scholarship -->

**Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, regards Scripture's threefold assertion that the Jews touched none of the plunder as decisive proof they acted strictly in lawful self-defense against stubborn attackers rather than out of greed. Turning to Purim's institution in the chapter's second half, Henry strongly applauds mandating mutual gifts and charity to the poor: days of thanksgiving among God's people must never sink into selfish indulgence, but awaken warm fellowship and tangible generosity toward the needy. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Matthew Henry's two hallmark themes in chapter 9 regarding untouched plunder proving self-defense and Purim generosity toward the poor, exact wording not verified -->

**Jon D. Levenson**, Jewish-American Old Testament professor, faces the staggering 75,000 casualty count candidly by examining both literary genre and moral boundaries within the text.
