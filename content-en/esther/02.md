# Esther 2 — An Orphan Girl Enters the Royal Palace

## Story

As time passed, King Xerxes' fury gradually cooled. Remembering Vashti, what she had done, and the irrevocable edict he had sealed against her, the monarch grew lonely (2:1). Seeing his gloom, his young royal attendants quickly proposed a remedy: dispatch officers across every province to gather attractive young virgins to the citadel of Susa, house them in the harem under the care of the royal eunuch Hegai for beauty preparations, and crown whichever young woman pleased the king most in Vashti's place. The suggestion delighted Xerxes, and he put it into motion immediately (2:2–4).

Only now does the camera swing toward the story's two central figures. Living in the citadel of Susa was a Jew named Mordecai—son of Jair, son of Shimei, son of Kish, of the tribe of Benjamin—from a family swept up when King Nebuchadnezzar carried King Jeconiah and Jerusalem's exiles off to Babylon. Mordecai was raising his orphaned cousin Hadassah, also called Esther, the daughter of his uncle. Having lost both father and mother, she had been taken in by Mordecai as his own daughter; she was striking in form and loveliness (2:5–7).

Once the imperial decree went out and scores of young women were gathered into Susa under Hegai's oversight, Esther too was taken into the king's palace. Immediately she caught Hegai's eye and won his kindness. Without delay he provided her beauty treatments and daily allowance, assigned seven select palace maids to attend her, and relocated her and her attendants into the finest quarters of the harem. Obeying Mordecai's firm counsel, Esther revealed nothing of her Jewish ethnicity or family heritage. Day after day, Mordecai strolled past the courtyard of the women's quarters to learn how Esther was faring and what lay ahead for her (2:8–11).

Imperial protocol required twelve full months of cosmetic preparation before any young woman's turn came to go before the king—six months with oil of myrrh and six months with perfumes and ointments. On her appointed evening, a woman could take along any gowns or jewelry she wished from the harem. Yet her future hung on a thin thread: entering in the evening, she departed the next morning to a second harem under the eunuch Shaashgaz, keeper of the concubines, never to see the king again unless he took special pleasure in her and summoned her by name. When Esther's turn arrived, she asked for nothing beyond what Hegai recommended—and she won the admiration of everyone who laid eyes on her (2:12–15).

In the tenth month, Tebeth, during the seventh year of Xerxes' reign, Esther was escorted into the royal palace. Xerxes fell deeply for Esther above all the other women; she gained his grace and devotion more than every other virgin. Setting the royal crown personally upon her head, he named her queen in Vashti's stead. Overjoyed, the king threw a lavish celebration for all his officials and attendants called "Esther's banquet," proclaimed a holiday and tax relief across the provinces, and handed out gifts with royal liberality (2:16–18).

When a second gathering of young women took place, Mordecai was now seated at the king's gate—the administrative hub of royal officials. Even wearing the queen's diadem, Esther kept obeying Mordecai just as she had while growing up under his roof, telling no one of her nationality or background (2:19–20).

One day while Mordecai sat at the king's gate, two royal eunuchs guarding the threshold—Bigthan and Teresh—grew furious and hatched a plot to assassinate King Xerxes. Word of the conspiracy reached Mordecai. At once he alerted Queen Esther, who passed the warning to the king explicitly crediting Mordecai. An investigation confirmed the plot, and both conspirators were executed on a gallows. The entire incident was carefully logged in the royal chronicles in the king's presence—and there the episode stopped, with Mordecai receiving no reward at all (2:21–23).

## Key Takeaways

**God positions deliverers well before the storm arrives.** Nobody has heard Haman's name yet, and nobody dreams that Israel stands months from near-extinction. Yet well ahead of the enemy's arrival, an orphaned Jewish woman already wears the queen's crown, and her guardian sits right inside the king's gate (2:17; 2:19). God's preparation always runs ahead of our panic.

**Unmistakable favor follows Esther precisely where she feels most vulnerable.** A parentless young woman inside an alien imperial harem holds zero family clout or political leverage. Yet she wins favor first with Hegai, then with everyone who sees her, and finally with the monarch himself (2:9; 2:15; 2:17). Even without God's name printed on the page, attentive readers recognize His steady fingerprints.

**Humility carries more weight than flashy display.** While other candidates could heap on every jewel and silk robe from the harem, Esther requested nothing beyond the wise advice of Hegai (2:13; 2:15). She prevailed not through beauty alone, but through teachability, poise, and restraint.

**Good deeds forgotten today are never wasted.** Mordecai saves an emperor's life, sees his name jotted into a court ledger, and receives nothing in return (2:22–23). Everyday life often feels the same: quiet faithfulness meets silence. Yet that overlooked line in a chronicle is simply waiting for one sleepless night that will flip an entire empire upside down.

## Deeper Meaning

**Mordecai's family tree quietly unlocks a thousand-year feud.** At first glance, verse 2:5—listing Kish and Shimei of the tribe of Benjamin—reads like routine genealogy. Anyone versed in the Old Testament sits up sharply: Kish was the father of King Saul (1 Samuel 9:1–2), and Shimei belonged to Saul's clan during David's reign (2 Samuel 16:5). Linking him to the 597 BC deportation under Jeconiah also binds him straight to Judah's heartbreak (2 Kings 24:12–16). The narrator deliberately introduces Saul's descendant right here to set up chapter 3's collision with Haman the Agagite—heir to Amalekite King Agag, the very enemy Saul once failed to finish off.

**Echoes of Joseph and Daniel in foreign courts.** Repeating that Esther won favor in the eyes of the harem overseer and the king directly recalls two towering exile predecessors: God granted Joseph favor in the eyes of the prison warden in Egypt (Genesis 39:21), and granted Daniel favor and compassion before the chief eunuch in Babylon (Daniel 1:9). All three were displaced Jews swept involuntarily into pagan empires, then quietly positioned by God at the exact lever needed to save their people.

**A forgotten favor echoing Joseph's prison cell.** Mordecai rescues Xerxes only to see his deed filed away and forgotten (2:23)—mirroring Joseph interpreting the royal cupbearer's dream in prison only to sit forgotten for two full years (Genesis 40:23). Both delays sit inside God's calendar: only on Xerxes' exact sleepless night in chapter 6 does the ledger reopen right to Mordecai's page—the very morning Haman walks into the courtyard aiming to hang him (6:1–3).

**Outward beauty set beside inner courage.** Persia's palace spent an entire year perfuming women because Xerxes prized physical allure above all else (2:12). Scripture reminds us that humans inspect outward appearance while the LORD weighs the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). Across all ten chapters, what makes Esther unforgettable is never the beauty that dazzled an emperor today, but the steel courage that later risks her neck for her nation.

## Notes & Nuances

**The four-year gap between chapters 1 and 2.** Chapter 1 takes place in Xerxes' third year (around 483 BC), whereas Esther becomes queen in the tenth month of his seventh year—late 479 BC (2:16). That four-year silence matches external history uncannily: Xerxes launched his sprawling invasion of Greece, suffered a crushing naval rout at Salamis in 480 BC, and returned defeated to Susa. Very likely it was amid post-war disillusionment that he "remembered Vashti" (2:1) and sought distraction in his palace harem.

**Was Mordecai himself deported from Jerusalem in 597 BC?** Verse 2:6 links back to Jeconiah's exile of 597 BC. Counting down to Xerxes' seventh year (479 BC) spans nearly one hundred and twenty years—far too old for Mordecai to have a young virgin cousin entering the harem. Most interpreters therefore read verse 2:6 as describing his ancestor Kish at the end of verse 2:5: Mordecai's family lineage went into exile during that initial deportation, rather than Mordecai personally being born over a century earlier.

**The meaning behind the names *Mordecai* and *Esther*.** Her Hebrew name *Hadassah* means *myrtle*—an evergreen shrub prized for its fragrance. *Esther* likely stems either from the Persian word for *star* or from the Babylonian goddess *Ishtar*, just as *Mordecai* echoes the Babylonian deity *Marduk*. Carrying such names never implies they worshiped pagan gods; exiled Jews routinely bore everyday local names in imperial society, just as Daniel and his three friends carried Babylonian court names.

**Grappling with Esther entering a pagan harem.** Many readers feel genuine unease here. Mosaic law forbade intermarriage with pagan nations (Deuteronomy 7:3), and spending a trial night with the king before selection (2:14) clashes sharply with biblical marriage ethics. Unlike Daniel refusing royal food (Daniel 1:8), Esther conceals her Jewish faith and complies with harem procedures. Two realities keep perspective balanced: first, women were requisitioned by imperial mandate, leaving Esther virtually no freedom to refuse (2:8); second, Scripture reports history candidly without glorifying Persian harems or instructing believers to copy compromises. God faithfully uses flawed people inside tangled circumstances without turning those circumstances into moral ideals.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, classic Jewish compendium of exegesis, ponders both of Esther's names in 2:7. In Tractate *Megillah*, the sages teach that she was called *Hadassah* (myrtle) because righteous people are likened to fragrant, evergreen myrtles, while *Esther* links to the Hebrew root for *concealing*, since she kept her heritage hidden before the king (2:10).
