# Joshua 10 — The Sun Stands Still Over Gibeon and the Southern Campaign

## Story

Before the shock of Ai's fall even fades, Adoni-zedek, king of Jerusalem, receives even more alarming news: Gibeon has struck a peace treaty and aligned with Israel. Gibeon is no obscure village; Scripture describes it as "a great city, like one of the royal cities," larger even than Ai and famous for its valiant fighters. Losing Gibeon flings open the central highland corridor to Israel. At once Jerusalem's king sends word rallying four neighboring southern Amorite kings—the kings of Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish, and Eglon—to march together and lay siege to Gibeon to punish its defection (10:1–5).

Hemmed in by five armies, the Gibeonites rush messengers down to Joshua's camp at Gilgal begging for rescue: "Do not abandon your servants. Come quickly and save us! Help us, because all the Amorite kings from the hill country have joined forces against us." Joshua never uses their earlier trickery as an excuse to hang back. Gathering his whole army and finest warriors, he marches immediately out of Gilgal. Along the way the LORD reassures him: "Do not be afraid of them, for I have delivered them into your hand. Not one of them shall stand against you." Force-marching uphill through the entire night, Joshua bursts onto the five-king coalition near Gibeon in a stunning surprise attack (10:6–9).

The LORD throws the Amorite coalition into blind panic before Israel. Israel chases them down the ascent of Beth-horon all the way toward Azekah and Makkedah. As the broken ranks race downhill, the LORD hurls massive hailstones down from heaven along the entire escape route—so devastating that more enemy soldiers die from the hailstones than fall to Israelite swords (10:10–11).

On that decisive day, fearing nightfall will let enemy remnants slip back inside fortified walls, Joshua speaks boldly to the LORD in full hearing of all Israel: "O sun, stand still over Gibeon, O moon, over the Valley of Aijalon." And the sun stops in midsky and the moon stands still, delaying its setting for about a full day until the nation finishes routing its foes. Noting that this poetic cry stands recorded in "the Book of Jashar," the narrator pauses in awe: "There has been no day like it before or since, when the LORD listened to the voice of a man, because the LORD fought for Israel." After a brief summary remark noting Israel's return to Gilgal, the narrative zooms back in on the fate of the five kings (10:12–15).

Amid the chaos, the five Amorite kings fled and crawled into a cave at Makkedah to hide. Hearing word of them, Joshua refuses to slow the pursuit: he orders huge boulders rolled across the cave's mouth under guard while the main army keeps chasing the enemy from behind, cutting them off before they reach fortified cities. Only once the mopping-up finishes and the entire army returns safely to camp at Makkedah—where not a single foe dares utter a word against Israel—does Joshua order the cave unsealed and the five kings brought out (10:16–23).

Before all the men of Israel, Joshua summons his field commanders to place their feet upon the necks of the five defeated kings. He strengthens them with God's own words: "Do not be afraid or discouraged; be strong and courageous, for the LORD will do this to all the enemies you fight." Afterward, the five kings are executed and hung on five trees until evening; at sunset Joshua commands their bodies taken down, tossed into the very cave where they hid, and sealed beneath massive stones (10:24–27).

Pressing his momentum, Joshua sweeps cleanly across the south in one continuous campaign. That very day he takes Makkedah, followed swiftly by Libnah, Lachish (crushing a relief army under King Horam of Gezer along the way), Eglon, Hebron, and Debir. Across hill country, Negev, western foothills, and mountain slopes—from Kadesh-barnea to Gaza, from Goshen to Gibeon—the entire southern coalition collapses in one stroke, "because the LORD, the God of Israel, fought for Israel." Mission accomplished, Joshua and all Israel return to base camp at Gilgal (10:28–43).

## Key Takeaways

**Keeping a promise means paying the price to defend those trusting your word.** When five kings besieged Gibeon, Joshua could easily have pointed to their deception and looked away—letting the coalition wipe out an awkward ally for him. Instead, he force-marched all night uphill to rescue them (10:6–9). Real integrity shows itself only when honoring our word demands sweat and risk.

**God can turn a past mistake into an opening for greater victory.** Chapter 9's hasty treaty without prayer was genuinely a blunder. Yet in chapter 10 that very treaty lured the five strongest southern kings out from behind thick stone walls into open battle where they fell in a single stroke (10:4–10). God never endorses carelessness, yet His sovereignty always towers over human missteps.

**Victory belongs to God, never human weaponry.** The story underscores this twice: heavenly hailstones felled more enemy warriors than Israelite swords (10:11), and the whole southern campaign succeeded "because the LORD, the God of Israel, fought for Israel" (10:42). Israel still marched through the night and swung every sword, yet the Sovereign of heaven and earth decided the battle.

**God listens to bold prayers aligned with His purpose.** What leaves the narrator most awestruck on that extraordinary day is not merely the cosmic marvel, but that "the LORD listened to the voice of a man" (10:14). The Creator holding sun and moon in His palm stooped down to heed a servant fighting for His cause.

## Deeper Meaning

**Sun and moon—chief deities of pagan neighbors—bow to the LORD.** Across ancient Canaan and the Near East, the sun (Shamash) and moon (Yarih) were revered as supreme gods ruling human fate. From Genesis 1 onward, Scripture stripped them of divinity, reducing them to two lamps hung in the sky by God (Genesis 1:16). In Joshua 10, God proves it dramatically: hailstones, sun, and moon alike obey their Maker (cf. Job 38:22–23). Centuries later, the prophet Habakkuk still sings of that day in prayer—"Sun and moon stood still in the heavens" (Habakkuk 3:11)—and Isaiah recalls God's mighty rising in the valley of Gibeon (Isaiah 28:21).

**Jerusalem's king Adoni-zedek stands in stark contrast to Melchizedek.** Centuries earlier, when Abraham lived in Canaan, the king of Salem (ancient Jerusalem) was Melchizedek—meaning *King of Righteousness*—priest of God Most High, who brought bread and wine to bless Abraham (Genesis 14:18). Here in Joshua 10, Jerusalem's ruler bears a nearly identical name, Adoni-zedek (*Lord of Righteousness*), yet leads a five-king coalition marching against Abraham's offspring (10:1–5). Noble titles can never substitute for a heart that fears God.

**Feet on enemy necks foreshadow the final triumph of Christ.** Commanders placing feet on defeated kings' necks (10:24) was a familiar ancient victory gesture, yet across Scripture it expands into a vivid emblem of God subduing every rebellious power. In Psalms, the LORD promises the Messiah: "Sit at My right hand until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet" (Psalms 110:1). And in the New Testament, Paul encourages believers with the same triumphant promise: "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Romans 16:20).

**Taking down the five kings before sunset.** Even amid fierce, sweeping victory, Joshua carefully obeys Moses' command never to leave executed bodies hanging overnight lest the land be defiled (10:27; Deuteronomy 21:22–23). Even while executing judgment on hostile kings, God's people stay bound beneath His law—a statute casting its shadow straight forward to Jesus on the cross (Galatians 3:13).

## Notes & Nuances

**How have interpreters understood *the sun standing still* (10:12–14)?** Few biblical miracles have drawn more discussion, especially since modern astronomy confirmed Earth rotates around the sun. Three main interpretive approaches have emerged. First, many believers view this as a literal supernatural miracle extending daylight—whether through God slowing Earth's rotation or miraculously refracting sunlight—while "the sun stood still" simply uses everyday observational speech, just as we still say "sunrise" and "sunset." Second, several Old Testament scholars note that the Hebrew verb rendered *stand still* (*damam*) can also mean *be silent* or *cease shining*; they suggest Joshua prayed for thick storm clouds and hail to block scorching sun over night-weary troops, or for a solar eclipse—though the prose phrase "delayed going down about a full day" in 10:13 keeps many leaning toward an extended daylight miracle. Third, other modern scholars emphasize that 10:12–13 quotes heroic poetry from the Book of Jashar and read it either poetically or in light of ancient Near Eastern celestial omen language asking sun and moon to align favorably for victory. Under every view, the narrator's own punchline sits in 10:14: the LORD intervened unmistakably to fight for Israel.

**Reading *left no survivors* in 10:28–40 with careful attention.** Skimming the chapter's close might leave the impression Joshua annihilated every single Canaanite across the entire south, including Hebron and Debir (10:36–39). Yet just chapters later in Joshua and at the opening of Judges, Canaanites still populate Hebron and Debir in force, requiring Caleb and Othniel to fight and take them (Judges 1:10–13)! Comparing Scripture with Scripture shows that *left no survivors* functions as standard ancient Near Eastern military hyperbole summarizing decisive victory—smashing enemy defenses and field armies—rather than a literal headcount claiming every civilian across the region was wiped out.

**What was *the Book of Jashar* (10:13)?** Not a "lost" book of the Bible, it was an ancient Israelite anthology of heroic historical poems—literally *the Book of the Upright*. Though no longer extant, 2 Samuel also quotes from it David's lament over Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel 1:18).

**Why place feet on the five kings' necks (10:24)?** Shocking to modern sensibilities, stepping onto a defeated monarch's neck was a standard ancient Near Eastern legal-military symbol signifying total subjugation, depicted frequently on Egyptian and Assyrian temple reliefs. Joshua employed that recognized gesture not out of personal cruelty, but specifically to shatter his officers' lingering dread of Canaanite royal power (10:25).

**Why does 10:15 report returning to Gilgal before 10:16 resumes at the Makkedah cave?** Attentive readers notice 10:15 virtually duplicates 10:43. Many scholars explain 10:15 as a summary closing the opening Gibeon battle account—indeed, the ancient Greek Septuagint omits verse 15—before verses 16–42 zoom back in to trace the Makkedah cave and full southern sweep ending in the actual return to Gilgal at 10:43.
