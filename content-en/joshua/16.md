# Joshua 16 — The Allotment of Ephraim

## Story

After Judah receives its vast southern inheritance, the next lot falls to "the descendants of Joseph"—the brother tribes of Manasseh and Ephraim. Rather than separating them into distant regions, the entire house of Joseph first receives one shared southern boundary slicing cleanly across central Canaan from east to west (16:1–4).

That southern border begins at the Jordan opposite Jericho, passes east of Jericho's waters, and climbs through the wilderness into the hill country of Bethel. From Bethel it crosses to Luz, passes through the Archite border at Ataroth, and slopes westward through Japhletite territory down to Lower Beth-horon and Gezer before reaching the Mediterranean Sea (16:1–3).

Across this broad, fertile central heartland, both Josephite tribes—Manasseh and Ephraim—claim their inheritance. Yet when spelling out individual tribal borders, the narrative does not list firstborn Manasseh first; instead, the remainder of chapter 16 focuses exclusively on the younger brother, Ephraim (16:4–5).

Ephraim's eastern and southern borders run from Ataroth-addar up to Upper Beth-horon. To the north, the line skirts north of Michmethath, bends eastward to Taanath-shiloh and Janoah, drops down to Ataroth and Naarah, touches Jericho, and ends at the Jordan. Westward from Tappuah, the border follows the Kanah Ravine straight to the Great Sea (16:5–8).

Notably, the two brother tribes are not walled off like foreign kingdoms. Ephraim also holds designated towns and surrounding villages nestled right inside Manasseh's territory to the north (16:9).

Yet just as chapter 15 ended on a somber chord at Jerusalem, chapter 16 closes with an ominous crack. The Ephraimites "did not drive out the Canaanites living in Gezer." Instead of expelling them, Ephraim left those Canaanites living right in their midst to the day of writing, subjecting them to "forced labor" (16:10).

## Key Takeaways

**The allotment lots fulfill ancient blessings spoken centuries earlier.** Canaan's two mightiest blocks—Judah in the south and Joseph's house in the center—receive strategic heartlands right from the opening lots. Listing younger Ephraim ahead of older Manasseh reminds us how often God's purpose overturns conventional human order (16:4–5).

**Brothers of one household live intertwined and lean on each other.** Sharing one overarching border while nesting Ephraimite towns inside Manasseh's land (16:1–4; 16:9) underscores that Israel's tribes are never rival kingdoms squabbling over turf, but kinsmen sharing one ancestry and one God-given land.

**Faced with a stubborn obstacle, people easily settle for halfway compromise.** Gezer stood at a strategic crossroads guarding the coastal trade route. Dislodging its fortified Canaanite population took hard battle and real sacrifice, so Ephraim chose a seemingly profitable shortcut: keep them alive and put them to work (16:10).

**Compromise for short-term gain opens the gate to long-term ruin.** Imposing forced labor looked like victorious dominance on paper. Across Scripture's larger story, however, this "practical" coexistence became the first slide toward blended worship and outright idolatry.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jacob's crossed hands continue shaping Israel's history.** Dying in Egypt, aged Jacob intentionally crossed his arms to lay his right hand on younger grandson Ephraim rather than firstborn Manasseh, declaring the younger would grow greater than the elder (Genesis 48:17–20). Centuries later, that prophecy shapes the exact sequence of chapters 16 and 17: Ephraim steps forward first, taking the lush central highlands promised in Jacob's and Moses' rich blessings over Joseph (Genesis 49:22–26; Deuteronomy 33:13–17).

**Israel's future national split already shows faintly in the first two lots.** Placing chapters 15 and 16 side by side reveals the two great poles of Israelite power: Judah in the south and Ephraim in the center. Centuries later, after Solomon's death split the kingdom, Judah anchored the Southern Kingdom while Ephraim—with border towns like Bethel and the hill country right out of chapter 16—formed the backbone of the Northern Kingdom, where King Jeroboam even set up a golden calf at Bethel (1 Kings 12:25–29).

**Putting Canaanites to forced labor flatly defied the covenant.** Through Moses, God explicitly forbade Israel to strike treaties or let Canaanite nations linger among them, warning they would become a snare dragging Israel's children into pagan worship (Exodus 23:31–33; Deuteronomy 7:1–4). Hence "forced labor" in 16:10 is no badge of pride; it marks a clear downward slide from 15:63. At Jerusalem Judah simply failed to dislodge the Jebusites; at Gezer Ephraim exploited their disobedience for cheap labor—a failure echoed at the start of Judges (Judges 1:29).

**Gezer's centuries-long holdout.** Back in chapter 10, Joshua crushed the army of Gezer's king Horam when he marched out to relieve Lachish (10:33). Yet defeating a field army did not empty the hilltop fortress. Strongly walled Gezer stayed in Canaanite hands through the entire era of the Judges, Saul, and David. Not until centuries later did Egypt's Pharaoh capture and burn Gezer, presenting it as a wedding dowry to his daughter when she married King Solomon (1 Kings 9:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 16:2 trace the border *from Bethel to Luz* when Genesis says Bethel used to be named Luz?** Genesis recounts Jacob naming the place of his ladder dream *Bethel*, noting the town's former name was *Luz* (Genesis 28:19). Scholars explain naturally that Jacob's outdoor sacred pillar site (Bethel) stood on high ground right adjacent to the ancient walled town of Luz; only gradually did *Bethel* replace the town's older name completely. In Joshua's era the border passed right between those two adjacent landmarks, prompting 16:2 to name both.

**What does subjecting the Canaanites to *forced labor* in 16:10 mean?** The Hebrew term denotes conscripted corvee labor imposed on a conquered people. Modern readers understandably find forced labor troubling alongside the conquest commands. Read fairly within the narrative arc of Joshua and Judges, Scripture never praises or approves this arrangement; rather, 16:10 records a subtle indictment of Ephraim's compromise. Whereas Judah in 15:63 was unable to clear Jerusalem, Ephraim deliberately turned disobediently spared foes into cheap labor despite God's explicit covenant warning.

**Why does chapter 16 lack a long city catalog like Judah's in chapter 15?** While chapter 15 names over a hundred Judean towns, chapter 16 sketches Ephraim's perimeter in ten brief verses. Scholars offer two complementary reasons: ancient administrative archives available to Joshua's author may have preserved detailed district town lists chiefly for Judah (and select tribes), or the author kept Ephraim's outline brisk to flow directly into the dramatic dispute over crowded land involving Joseph's whole house in chapter 17.

**Chapters 16 and 17 form one continuous narrative unit.** Later chapter divisions split Joseph's house down the middle: chapter 16 covers their shared southern boundary and Ephraim, while chapter 17 picks up Manasseh and concludes with both tribes confronting Joshua together. Reading both chapters together restores the full picture.

**Origen**, third-century Christian scholar in Alexandria, draws a searching inward warning from 16:10. When Ephraim left Canaanites living in Gezer under forced labor instead of driving them out, Origen sees believers who refuse to uproot lingering vices and passions, convincing themselves they can keep sin harnessed and under control.
