# Joshua 17 — Manasseh's Allotment and the Complaint of Joseph's House

## Story

Following younger brother Ephraim, the allotment turns to Manasseh, Joseph's firstborn son. The text cleanly distinguishes the tribe's two halves. The first belongs to Machir, Manasseh's eldest son and father of Gilead; renowned as a man of war, his clan already received Gilead and Bashan east of the Jordan. The second half comprises Manasseh's remaining six clans—Abiezer, Helek, Asriel, Shechem, Hepher, and Shemida—who receive their inheritance west of the river (17:1–2).

Midway through naming male clans, the narrative pauses over one remarkable family. Zelophehad, from the clan of Hepher, died leaving no sons—only five daughters: Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah. Stepping boldly before Eleazar the priest, Joshua, and the tribal leaders, the five sisters cite God's own word to Moses: "The LORD commanded Moses to give us an inheritance among our brothers." Joshua obeys God's command immediately and grants them a share alongside their father's brothers. Because all five daughters receive allotments representing their father's line, western Manasseh counts ten allotted portions in all, separate from Gilead and Bashan across the river (17:3–6).

Western Manasseh's border runs from Asher toward Michmethath facing Shechem, dips south around Tappuah to the northern rim of the Kanah Ravine, and meets the Mediterranean Sea. In brief, land south of the Kanah Ravine belongs to Ephraim and land north belongs to Manasseh, bordered on the north by Asher and on the east by Issachar (17:7–10).

Beyond its main territory, Manasseh also receives key fortified towns nestled inside Issachar and Asher along the fertile northern valleys—Beth-shean, Ibleam, Dor, Endor, Taanach, and Megiddo with their surrounding villages. Yet like Judah and Ephraim before them, Manasseh fails to expel those towns' inhabitants, "for the Canaanites were determined to stay in that land." Later, when Israel grows stronger, they impose forced labor on them, yet still fail to drive them out completely (17:11–13).

The chapter closes with a dramatic confrontation. The descendants of Joseph—both Ephraim and Manasseh—march up complaining to Joshua: "Why have you given us only one allotment and one portion for an inheritance, since we are a numerous people whom the LORD has blessed abundantly?" (17:14).

Rather than arguing at length, Joshua turns their very boast back on them: if you truly are so numerous and the hill country of Ephraim feels cramped, then "go up to the forest and clear ground for yourselves there" in the country of the Perizzites and Rephaim (17:15).

Hit right in their weak spot, Joseph's descendants reveal the true motive beneath their grumbling: they dread hard work and fear the enemy. They object that the hills are too narrow, while down in the plains the Canaanites of Beth-shean and the Valley of Jezreel "all have iron chariots"—fearsome battlefield machines (17:16).

Joshua refuses to cave in, yet never belittles their calling. He confirms that Joseph's house truly is a large, mighty people who will not remain boxed into a single cramped share. Still, they must claim that spacious inheritance with sweat and faith: the wooded highlands will become theirs once they clear them, "and you will drive out the Canaanites, even though they have iron chariots and are strong" (17:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**God's word shields those easily pushed to the margins.** In an ancient society where land almost universally passed down male lines, Zelophehad's five daughters stepped up boldly claiming their lawful share based squarely on what the LORD had spoken (17:3–4). Their courage in holding fast to God's word moved the whole leadership to deliver justice for their house.

**A righteous leader shows no favoritism to his own kin.** Joshua himself hailed from the tribe of Ephraim (Numbers 13:8). Joseph's house likely hoped their famous kinsman would cut them a prime extra slice without tough fighting. Yet Joshua upheld impartial fairness before the whole nation, refusing to bend the sacred lot for relatives (17:14–15).

**God's blessing is never an excuse to demand easy comfort, but fuel to tackle hard work.** Joseph's descendants boasted of their huge numbers and divine blessing while asking for pre-cleared land handed on a platter. Joshua flipped their logic instantly: if you truly are vast and blessed, pick up your axes, clear the forests, and confront enemy iron chariots (17:15–18).

**Fear often hides behind loud complaints.** At first Joseph's house complained only of cramped quarters (17:14). Once Joshua pointed toward open forests and broad valleys, the true obstacle slipped out: Canaanite iron chariots (17:16). People routinely blame circumstances or leaders when the real root is fear and shrinking trust in God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Zelophehad's five daughters reveal the deep justice of Mosaic law.** Verses 17:3–6 link directly back to the plains of Moab before crossing the Jordan. There, these five sisters stood before the tent of meeting pleading that their father's name not vanish simply because he had no sons; Moses brought their case before the LORD, who declared their claim completely right and enacted a permanent statute safeguarding daughters' inheritance rights whenever a father died without sons (Numbers 27:1–8; 36:6–9). In chapter 17 that statute leaps off parchment into lived reality—an early beam pointing toward Scripture's full revelation that men and women share equal dignity and inheritance before God (Galatians 3:28).

**The towns Manasseh left behind became long historical wounds.** Key strongholds Manasseh failed to clear in 17:11–12—Beth-shean, Taanach, Megiddo, Dor—commanded the major trade artery cutting through the Valley of Jezreel. Judges opens by repeating this exact failure (Judges 1:27–28). From those very plains, Canaanite oppressors later fielded nine hundred iron chariots crushing Israel for twenty years (Judges 4:3); and centuries later, after King Saul fell on Mount Gilboa, Philistines nailed his body right onto the city wall of Beth-shan—the very Beth-shean Manasseh left standing (1 Samuel 31:8–10).

**God's power towers above iron chariots.** Joseph's house trembling over iron chariots (17:16) showed how quickly memory fades. Before entering Canaan, Mosaic law had instructed Israel clearly: when going to war against enemies with horses and chariots outnumbering you, do not be afraid, because the LORD is with you (Deuteronomy 20:1). Inside this very book, Joshua had already shattered the northern coalition's massive chariot force at the Waters of Merom (11:4–6). Telling Joseph's house they would drive out Canaanites despite iron chariots (17:18) pointed them straight back to the God who fights for His people.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 17:5 count *ten portions* falling to western Manasseh?** Listing western Manasseh's male clans in 17:2 yields six sons: Abiezer, Helek, Asriel, Shechem, Hepher, and Shemida. Hepher's line had no male heirs and passed through his grandson Zelophehad's five daughters. Thus the other five male lines took five shares, while Zelophehad's five daughters took five shares for Hepher's branch—totaling exactly ten allotted portions west of the Jordan.

**Why did Joseph's house claim they got *only one allotment* (17:14) when they held two tribes?** In fact, Joseph's descendants held a huge central expanse—on top of eastern Manasseh's holdings across Gilead and Bashan. However, chapter 16 shows both tribes grouped under one initial central drawing before dividing internally (16:1–4). Moreover, because fertile northern plains still lay under Canaanite iron chariots, they felt practically hemmed into narrow mountain ridges—sparking their grievance that their usable territory looked like *just one lot*.

**What were Canaanite *iron chariots*?** At the dawn of the Iron Age in the ancient Near East, *iron chariots* were not solid-cast iron wagons, which would be impossibly heavy, but swift wooden war chariots reinforced with iron wheel rims, axles, and armor plates for durability. Devastating across flat plains like Jezreel, they turned clumsy and ineffective on steep wooded hillsides—explaining why Israel readily controlled the hill country while struggling in the valleys.

**Subjecting Canaanites to *forced labor* (17:13).** Just like Ephraim in 16:10, once Israel gained strength, they turned Canaanites into forced laborers rather than expelling them as commanded. Readers must always distinguish what Scripture *records* people doing from what God *approves*: across Joshua and Judges, this detail stands not as an achievement, but as halfhearted disobedience that soon bred devastating spiritual compromise.

**Origen**, third-century Christian scholar in Alexandria, reads Joshua's charge to clear the forest (17:15; 17:18) as a vivid metaphor for spiritual growth: God's inheritance seldom arrives pre-cleared like a manicured lawn, often resembling dense forest demanding patient effort to chop away thorns, vices, and inner resistance. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, praises Zelophehad's five daughters warmly for grounding their claim not on selfish presumption, but squarely on God's clear command through Moses. By contrast, Calvin sharply rebukes Joseph's house at chapter's end: boasting of vast numbers yet shrinking from forest clearing and iron chariots while banking on clan kinship with Joshua for special favors. **Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, draws a crisp contrast between the chapter's two scenes: five vulnerable women show fearless faith and solid reasoning in claiming God's exact promise, whereas a huge multitude of men grumble out of laziness and fear of chariots.
