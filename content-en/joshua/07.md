# Joshua 7 — Achan's Sin and Defeat at Ai

## Story

The smoke over Jericho has scarcely settled and the joy of victory has barely cooled when the narrative takes a dark turn. Among the Israelites, someone has violated the strict ban on things devoted to destruction: Achan, from the tribe of Judah, secretly took items set apart exclusively for God. Although the theft took place quietly inside one private tent, the anger of the LORD immediately burns against all Israel (7:1).

Joshua knows nothing of what happened. From Jericho he sends men up to scout the town of Ai near Beth-aven, east of Bethel. The scouts come back brimming with confidence: Ai is small and lightly populated—no need to wear out the entire army; two or three thousand soldiers will easily take it. Taking their advice, Joshua sends roughly three thousand men up to attack. The outcome is stunning: Israelite forces break and flee before the men of Ai, who chase them from the city gate down toward Shebarim and strike down about thirty-six men along the slope. One minor defeat before a tiny town shakes the entire camp: the people's hearts "melted and became like water" (7:2–5).

Hearing of the rout, Joshua tears his clothes, and he and Israel's elders throw dust on their heads, falling facedown before the ark of the LORD from noon until evening. Joshua's prayer pours out raw grief and confusion. He asks God why He ever brought this people across the Jordan only to hand them over to the Amorites to be destroyed, even blurting out a bitter wish: "If only we had been content to stay on the other side of the Jordan!" Yet Joshua's deepest agony is not merely Israel's survival, but God's own honor: once Canaanites hear that Israel turned tail and ran, they will surround and wipe out Israel's name from the earth—"What then will You do for Your own great name?" (7:6–9).

The LORD interrupts him sharply: "Stand up! Why have you fallen facedown?" The fault does not lie in God's faithfulness. Israel has sinned and broken His covenant: someone has stolen devoted things, lied, and hidden them among his own belongings. Because of that, Israel itself has become liable to destruction and cannot stand before its enemies. God states plainly that unless they purge the devoted things from their midst, He will no longer be with them. He directs Joshua to consecrate the people for tomorrow morning, when the whole nation will come forward step by step—tribe, clan, family, then individual—so the LORD Himself can point out the guilty man. Whoever is caught with devoted items will be burned with fire along with all he owns, for he has done an outrageous thing in Israel (7:10–15).

Early the next morning Joshua assembles the entire nation. The narrowing circle tightens in breathless suspense. Tribe by tribe comes forward, and Judah is taken. Clan by clan of Judah approaches, and the Zerahites are taken. Family by family of the Zerahites steps up, and the household of Zimri is taken. Finally, man by man from Zimri's house steps before the LORD, and Achan is singled out (7:16–18).

Standing before the entire nation, Joshua does not erupt in rage. He addresses Achan with solemn, aching tenderness: "My son, give glory to the LORD, the God of Israel, and make a confession to Him. Tell me what you have done; do not hide it from me." Cornered with nowhere left to hide, Achan confesses every downward step: among Jericho's spoils he spotted a beautiful Babylonian robe, two hundred shekels of silver, and a bar of gold weighing fifty shekels—"I coveted them and took them"—and dug a hole beneath the floor inside his tent to bury them, with the silver underneath. Joshua sends runners straight to Achan's tent; they uncover every item exactly as confessed and spread them out before Joshua, all Israel, and the LORD (7:19–23).

Then Joshua and all Israel take Achan along with the silver, the gold bar, the robe, his sons and daughters, his oxen, donkeys, sheep, tent, and everything belonging to him, leading them down to the Valley of Achor. There Joshua pronounces judgment with a grim play on the valley's name: "Why have you brought this trouble upon us? Today the LORD will bring trouble upon you!" All Israel stones Achan, burns them and his possessions with fire, and raises a great heap of stones over him that remains long afterward. Only then does the LORD turn from His fierce anger, and the place bears the name Valley of Achor—meaning *trouble*—ever after (7:24–26).

## Key Takeaways

**One person's hidden sin can wound an entire community.** Achan buried gold and silver under the dirt floor of his private tent imagining no one would ever know (7:21). Yet in the chapter's very first sentence Scripture declares, "Israel was unfaithful" (7:1). Thirty-six innocent soldiers fell outside Ai, and the whole nation lost God's presence. Inside God's community, no sin stays truly *private*.

**Relying on self-confidence and skipping God invites a fall.** Before crossing the Jordan and before marching on Jericho, Joshua waited carefully on clear direction from God. Before tiny Ai, however, not a single line mentions seeking God; they trusted scouts' eyes and simple math (7:2–3). Right after a dazzling triumph is often when hearts grow most careless.

**Prayer can never substitute for obedience.** Joshua lying prostrate in tears before the ark looks intensely pious, yet God tells him to get up at once (7:6–10). Tears and lament cannot fix a crisis while known sin remains unaddressed in the camp.

**Greed follows a timeless path: see, covet, take, and hide.** Achan's confession lays bare the anatomy of temptation (7:21). His eyes lingered on forbidden things, desire took root in his heart, his hand grabbed them, and finally he buried them underground—treasure that never brought a single day of joy, yielding only terror and ruin.

**God's anger relents only when sin is exposed and dealt with completely.** The mound of stones in the Valley of Achor stands as a sobering counterpart to the memorial stones at Gilgal (4:20; 7:26). If Gilgal's stones testified to God's wondrous faithfulness, Achor's stones warned Israel never to trifle with His holiness.

## Deeper Meaning

**From Eden's garden to Achan's tent.** The cadence of Achan's confession—"I saw," "I coveted them," "and took them" (7:21)—echoes almost word for word the very first fall in Eden, when the woman saw the tree was pleasing, desired it, and took its fruit (Genesis 3:6). Centuries later, James summarizes the identical sequence: desire conceives and gives birth to sin, and sin when full-grown brings forth death (James 1:14–15).

**Melting hearts trade places.** In chapters 2 and 5, Canaanites heard of God's power and their hearts melted before Israel (2:11; 5:1). The instant sin cost Israel God's presence, Israel's own hearts "melted and became like water" before one tiny town (7:5). The strength of God's people never comes from headcount or steel, but strictly from whether God walks among them.

**Achan alongside Ananias and Sapphira.** Acts records a startling parallel at the very dawn of the New Testament church (Acts 5:1–11). Just as Achan secretly held back devoted wealth as Israel entered the Promised Land, Ananias and Sapphira secretly pocketed part of proceeds claimed as wholly given to God as the church launched. In both moments, swift, severe judgment warned that God's new community cannot begin on hidden hypocrisy.

**Prayer that puts God's name above all else.** Even amid raw anguish in 7:9, Joshua reaches back to the profound ground his mentor Moses stood on in the wilderness: pleading with God to act for the sake of His own "great name" among the nations (Exodus 32:11–12; Numbers 14:13–16). Mature prayer places God's glory even higher than our own fear.

**The Valley of Achor becomes a door of hope.** The Valley of Achor—marked by stones, smoke, and disgrace—looked permanently sealed under judgment. Yet centuries later, through Hosea, God spoke tender grace to wayward Israel, promising to bring them back and make "the Valley of Achor a door of hope" (Hosea 2:15). Isaiah likewise foresaw a day of restoration when Achor becomes a quiet pasture where herds lie down in peace (Isaiah 65:10). In the gospel, the very place where sin met its heaviest judgment—the cross—opens wide as the doorway of living hope for the world.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why were Achan's sons and daughters brought to the Valley of Achor with him?** This is the hardest, most troubling question in chapter 7, especially given Moses' clear statute forbidding putting children to death for fathers' sins or fathers for children's (Deuteronomy 24:16). Commentators generally discuss three main perspectives. First, many note that digging a pit right in the floor of a shared family tent to bury a fine robe, two hundred silver shekels, and a heavy gold bar (7:21) could scarcely escape the household's notice, making family complicity likely. Second, some scholars observe a shift in Hebrew pronouns in 7:25—first stoning *him* (singular, Achan), then burning *them* and stoning *them* (plural)—suggesting the family may have been brought out solely as solemn witnesses while only Achan, his livestock, tent, and goods were destroyed, though the most natural reading of 7:24–25 implies the family shared his fate. Third, most scholars recognize this as an extraordinary case under the *devoted thing* (*herem*) law: bringing a cursed object into his tent placed Achan and everything under his roof under the same contagion warned of in 6:18 and 7:15. Either way, Scripture never sets this extraordinary event as a precedent for ordinary civil courts.

**The quiet contrast between Rahab and Achan.** Placing chapters 6 and 7 side by side creates a jolting reversal. Rahab—a foreign prostitute from doomed Jericho—believed, saved her whole family, and joined Israel (6:25). Achan—a native Israelite from the royal tribe of Judah—disobeyed, turned himself into a devoted thing, and was cut off from God's people (7:25). Lineage never guarantees belonging to God; faith and obedience decide everything.

**What does *give glory to the LORD* in 7:19 mean?** Telling a doomed thief to "give glory" to God sounds unexpected at first glance. In ancient Hebrew legal usage, it was a solemn oath formula placing the accused under God's direct gaze—essentially meaning, "Speak the plain truth before God, for honest confession honors His justice." The exact formula reappears in the New Testament when Pharisees interrogate the healed blind man (John 9:24).

**How did God *single out* Achan?** Chapter 7 never specifies the mechanism used to narrow down tribe, clan, household, and finally Achan (7:14–18). Most likely Joshua cast sacred lots before the ark—perhaps using the high priest's Urim and Thummim, a standard Old Testament means of seeking God's verdict. Moving slowly stage by stage both proved total fairness before the crowd and offered Achan repeated silent moments to step forward and confess—yet tragically he stayed quiet until exposed.

**How valuable were the Babylonian robe and precious metals?** Shinar is the ancient name for Babylonia, famed for fine textiles and costly royal mantles. Two hundred shekels of silver weighed roughly five pounds (over two kilograms), and fifty shekels of gold over one pound (half a kilogram)—an immense fortune to an ordinary soldier, easily dazzling greedy eyes. Notably, 1 Chronicles spells his name *Achar* to echo *trouble* and the Valley of Achor directly (1 Chronicles 2:7).
