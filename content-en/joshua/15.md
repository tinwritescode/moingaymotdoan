# Joshua 15 — The Allotment of Judah

## Story

Immediately after Caleb receives the promise of Hebron's hill country, the first lot west of the Jordan falls to the tribe of Judah. The text begins by tracing all four borders enclosing a vast southern domain across Canaan. The southern border runs from the southern bay of the Salt Sea, bends past the Ascent of Akrabbim, crosses the Wilderness of Zin south of Kadesh-barnea, and follows the Brook of Egypt out to the sea. The eastern border runs the full length of the Salt Sea up to the mouth of the Jordan (15:1–5).

From the mouth of the Jordan, the northern border winds westward past Beth-hoglah and the Stone of Bohan son of Reuben, climbing from the Valley of Achor up the Ascent of Adummim past springs around Jerusalem. Skirting the southern shoulder of Jerusalem, it runs through the Valley of the Son of Hinnom, over the top of the Valley of Rephaim to the spring of Nephtoah and Kiriath-jearim. From there it descends west through Beth-shemesh and Timnah, curving north of Ekron straight to the sea. Judah's western border is the Great Sea—the Mediterranean (15:5–12).

Right inside this meticulous geography, the narrative pauses to finish Caleb's story from the previous chapter. Joshua grants him Hebron, formerly the city of Arba, father of the Anakim. True to his bold faith, elderly Caleb drives the three giant sons of Anak—Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai—completely out of that hill country (15:13–14).

Not stopping at Hebron, Caleb marches against Debir, formerly named Kiriath-sepher. He issues a challenge stirring the next generation: "I will give my daughter Acsah in marriage to the man who attacks and captures Kiriath-sepher." Othniel son of Kenaz, Caleb's kinsman, steps up, takes the town, and wins Acsah as his wife (15:15–17).

On her wedding journey, Acsah proves every bit as bold and shrewd as her father. Urging her husband to ask her father for a field, she dismounts her donkey right in front of Caleb. When her father asks, "What can I do for you?" she answers straight out: "Give me a blessing. Since you have given me land in the Negev, give me springs of water as well." The arid Negev parches under fierce sun; dry acreage without water yields nothing. Caleb delights to honor his daughter's request and grants her both "the upper and lower springs" (15:18–20).

The longest stretch of the chapter forms a thorough registry of Judah's towns grouped cleanly across four geographical zones from south to north and west to east. First comes the dry southern Negev bordering Edom, counting twenty-nine towns including familiar landmarks like Beersheba, Hormah, and Ziklag (15:21–32). Second come the western foothills and coastal plains, clustered around towns like Adullam, Lachish, Makkedah, Libnah, and Keilah, extending out to the Philistine cities of Ekron, Ashdod, and Gaza (15:33–47).

Third lies Judah's central hill country, home to highland strongholds such as Debir, Hebron, Maon, Carmel, Ziph, and Kiriath-jearim (15:48–60). Fourth come six desert settlements lining the western shore of the Salt Sea, including the oasis of En-gedi (15:61–62). Together, this sweeping ledger displays the breadth and richness of Judah's inheritance.

Yet in the chapter's very last sentence, the celebratory tone drops abruptly with one brief, sobering line: Judah "could not drive out the Jebusites living in Jerusalem." Consequently, the Jebusites stayed living alongside the people of Judah in Jerusalem down to the day the book was written (15:63).

## Key Takeaways

**God's promises are never vague—they land on concrete ground.** Page after page of ridges, valleys, and town names may feel dry to modern readers. Yet to Israelites fresh off forty homeless years in the wilderness, every single name read like a stamped title deed. God does not offer empty slogans; He hands His people real hills, real valleys, and named towns (15:1–12).

**Courageous faith in parents kindles boldness in the rising generation.** Caleb did not merely defeat three giants at Hebron himself; he opened the stage for younger leaders to claim Debir (15:14–17). Living trust in God refuses to end with veteran heroes—it passes fire into younger hands like Othniel and Acsah.

**Ask boldly for what you truly need from a generous Father.** Acsah's cameo takes just two verses, yet shines like a jewel amid dozens of town names (15:18–19). She never hesitated to tell her father that parched soil needs water, and Caleb responded not with one spring, but both upper and lower springs. Asking for what sustains life is never presumptuous before a loving father.

**One piece of unfinished obedience can echo across centuries.** Amid a gleaming inventory of vast territory, verse 15:63 sits like a hairline crack across a grand wall: Judah failed to expel the Jebusites from Jerusalem. Unfinished compromise sitting inside our inheritance looks small at first, yet casts a shadow far into the future.

## Deeper Meaning

**Judah steps visibly into its role as the leading tribe.** It is no accident that Judah draws the very first lot west of the Jordan and receives sixty-three full verses—more space than any other tribe. Centuries earlier, dying Jacob blessed Judah as a lion's cub from whom the royal scepter would never depart (Genesis 49:8–10). Judah's southern frontier also matches almost identically the southern border of the entire Promised Land Moses laid out (Numbers 34:3–5).

**Othniel forms the living bridge into the book of Judges.** Young Othniel, who captures Debir and weds Acsah here (15:17), is no passing extra. His exact exploit opens the next book (Judges 1:10–15), and soon after Joshua dies, when Israel spirals into sin and foreign oppression, the Spirit of the LORD comes upon Othniel to rise as Israel's very first judge and deliverer (Judges 3:9–11).

**Chapter 15's map sets the stage for King David's life.** Scanning Judah's towns unveils sites woven deeply into Israel's royal story: the cave of Adullam where David hid (1 Samuel 22:1), the strongholds of En-gedi beside the Dead Sea where he spared Saul's life (1 Samuel 23:29), and Kiriath-jearim where the ark rested twenty years (1 Samuel 7:1–2). Even the somber closing verse (15:63) waits centuries for David himself to capture the Jebusite stronghold of Zion and make Jerusalem the capital of the kingdom (2 Samuel 5:6–9).

**The Valley of the Son of Hinnom—from border landmark to emblem of judgment.** Verse 15:8 mentions the Valley of the Son of Hinnom running south of Jerusalem as an ordinary geographical marker. Centuries later, wicked Judean kings turned that gorge into a horror site burning children to pagan idols, prompting Jeremiah to foretell its renaming as the Valley of Slaughter (Jeremiah 7:31–32). Its Hebrew name passed into the New Testament as *Gehenna*—Jesus' stark picture of fiery judgment.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why do several town totals differ slightly from counting individual names?** Careful readers tallying the Negev list in 15:21–32 count around thirty-six place names, yet 15:32 summarizes "twenty-nine cities." Similarly, 15:33–36 lists fifteen names yet totals "fourteen cities." This pattern is common in ancient administrative lists: certain paired names denote double names for one site or satellite hamlets grouped under a main town, while some border towns were later reassigned to Simeon (19:2–8) even as an older district total stayed intact.

**Did Jerusalem belong to Judah or Benjamin?** Verse 15:8 places Judah's northern border right along Jerusalem's southern slope, putting most of the city proper inside Benjamin's territory. Thus 15:63 says Judah could not expel the Jebusites, while the next book says Benjamin failed to expel them (Judges 1:21). Both match geography cleanly: Jerusalem straddled the Judah-Benjamin border, leaving both tribes sharing responsibility—and living right beside an unconquered fortress.

**Why does 15:8 call Jerusalem *the Jebusite city* (*Jebus*)?** Centuries before David made Jerusalem his capital, the citadel belonged to the Jebusites, so early boundary texts naturally pair its older name *Jebus* with the familiar *Jerusalem* (15:8; 18:28). That dual phrasing reflects authentic early records preserved from prior to David's capture.

**How were Caleb and Othniel related?** Verse 15:17 calls Othniel "son of Kenaz, Caleb's brother" (cf. Judges 1:13; 3:9). In Hebrew syntax, "Caleb's younger brother" can describe either Kenaz (making Othniel Caleb's nephew) or Othniel himself (making him Caleb's younger half-brother or kinsman). Either way, both belonged to the Kenizzite clan, and Mosaic law did not prohibit marriage between cousins or uncle-niece kin of that degree.

**The timeline clue in *to this day* (15:63).** Recording that Jebusites still lived alongside Judah in Jerusalem at the time of writing—before King David captured the stronghold early in his reign (2 Samuel 5:6–7)—indicates that chapter 15's source material dates very early, prior to David's reign over all Israel.

**Origen**, third-century Christian scholar in Alexandria, pauses over Acsah's brief petition to unfold a deep spiritual picture. The lower springs she requests represent knowledge of earthly life and practical wisdom, while the upper springs signify heavenly wisdom granted by God to everyone seeking Him earnestly. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, urges readers not to breeze impatiently past chapter 15's long roster of borders and towns. The Holy Spirit preserved such exact geography as irrefutable testimony to God's faithfulness: the oath sworn centuries earlier to Abraham materialized down to specific hills and villages. **Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, delights in Acsah's wise request (15:19).
