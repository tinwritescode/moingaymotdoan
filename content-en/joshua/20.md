# Joshua 20 — Six Cities of Refuge

## Story

No sooner does tribal allotment wrap up in chapter 19 than the LORD speaks to Joshua about an urgent institution that cannot wait: setting apart the "cities of refuge" exactly as He commanded earlier through Moses. As Israel settles into everyday life across the land, respect for human life and fair public justice must rest on rock-solid foundations from day one (20:1–2).

The mission of these towns is crystal clear: sheltering anyone who kills another person accidentally and unintentionally from the "avenger of blood"—the victim's nearest kinsman, whom ancient custom charged with demanding life for life on the family's behalf (20:3).

The refuge procedure is crisp and orderly. Someone who accidentally causes a death must run straight to one of these refuge towns, stand at the city gate entrance, and lay out his entire case before the town elders. After hearing him, the elders must welcome him inside the city and provide a place to live among them. Should the avenger of blood arrive hot on his heels, the elders must never surrender the fugitive, "because he killed his neighbor unintentionally, without prior malice" (20:4–5).

Still, a city of refuge never acts as a safe house shielding deliberate criminals from law. The fugitive must remain inside the town until standing fair public trial before the congregation to establish whether the killing was accidental or premeditated. Once verified accidental, he must remain safely inside the city's boundaries "until the death of the high priest who is serving at that time." Only after that high priest dies may the manslayer return completely free to his hometown and family estate (20:6).

Israel promptly selects six towns spaced symmetrically in two north-south columns flanking the Jordan River. West of the Jordan sit three prominent highland cities visible from afar: Kedesh in Galilee in Naphtali's hills to the north, Shechem in Ephraim's hill country in the center, and Kiriath-arba (Hebron) in Judah's hills to the south (20:7). East of the Jordan sit three matching counterparts: Bezer on Reuben's wilderness plateau in the south, Ramoth in Gilead for Gad in the center, and Golan in Bashan for Manasseh in the north (20:8).

This brief chapter concludes with a deeply humane declaration: these six towns are appointed not merely for native citizens, but "for all the Israelites and for the foreigner residing among them." Citizen or resident alien alike, anyone causing an accidental death holds an equal right to flee there—shielded from impulsive blood vengeance until standing fair trial before the community (20:9).

## Key Takeaways

**Every human life is precious to God—both the victim's and the accidental killer's.** Cities of refuge strike a remarkable balance between justice and mercy. An accidental killer cannot shrug off tragedy as nothing: leaving home and farmland to live confined inside a refuge town for years taught all society how sacred every life is. At the same time, God's law stops heated vengeance cold, refusing to spill more innocent blood (20:3–6).

**Justice belongs to fair trial, never private rage.** In ancient societies lacking police departments or professional courts, blood vengeance could easily twist a heartbreaking accident into a multigenerational family blood feud. Cities of refuge created an immediate cooling-off shelter forcing both sides into public community adjudication instead of settling scores with a knife (20:4–6; 20:9).

**God's shelter always stays within reach.** Rather than clustering all refuge towns in one corner, Israel positioned six evenly across north, center, and south on both banks of the Jordan (20:7–8). No matter where an accident struck, a runner on foot could reach open refuge gates within roughly a day's journey.

**God's justice protects vulnerable outsiders equally.** Verse 20:9 explicitly extends asylum to "the foreigner residing among them." In ancient times, an immigrant who accidentally killed a local citizen faced terrifying retaliation with zero clan protection. Before God, justice and mercy recognize neither pedigree nor citizenship papers.

## Deeper Meaning

**Fulfilling a humane cornerstone of Mosaic law.** Cities of refuge were no post-war afterthought; God designed them from the very start. Back at Mount Sinai, God drew a bright line separating premeditated murder from accidental death (Exodus 21:13–14). Later in the wilderness, He spelled out trial procedures and residence rules (Numbers 35:11–12; 35:25–28), even requiring straight, well-kept roads and offering the vivid everyday example of an axe head flying off its handle while chopping wood (Deuteronomy 19:3–5). Moses personally set apart the three eastern cities before dying (Deuteronomy 4:41–43), and now Joshua completes all six as Israel settles in.

**God Himself is humanity's true refuge.** The dramatic image of a terrified runner racing toward wide-open sanctuary gates sank deeply into Israel's prayer language. When the psalmist sings that God is our refuge and strength, an ever-present help in trouble (Psalms 46:1), these very protective cities echo beneath the words.

**Foreshadowing Jesus and the death of our Great High Priest.** The New Testament directly borrows refuge-city imagery, encouraging believers as those who "have fled to take hold of the hope set before us" (Hebrews 6:18). Above all, the striking detail of 20:6—full liberty to go home arrives only upon the serving high priest's death—has long shone as a shadow of Christ's cross. In the New Testament, Jesus stands as our holy High Priest who needed no daily animal sacrifices, offering His own life once for all (Hebrews 7:24–27). His death throws open the gates of freedom, bringing condemned exiles safely home to the Father's house.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did cities of refuge shelter deliberate murderers?** Never. Mosaic law drew an iron line: fleeing inside merely shielded the accused from hot-blooded ambush until fair public investigation. If the congregation established premeditated murder—prior hatred, ambush, or intentional lethal assault—elders had to hand the killer over for execution, even dragging him away from the very horns of God's altar (Exodus 21:14). Refuge cities guarded due process, never impunity.

**Why did an accidental killer have to remain inside until the high priest died?** Scripture does not state the reason explicitly, yet commentators highlight two profound dimensions. Theologically, the high priest stood as Israel's spiritual representative before God; his passing closed an era and carried symbolic atoning weight for bloodshed that had stained the land. Practically, an indefinite stretch away from home both instilled sober caution around human life and gave grieving families time for raw anger to subside. Stepping outside town boundaries early forfeited legal protection (Numbers 35:26–28).

**Why were all six cities of refuge Levitical towns?** Turning to chapter 21 reveals that every single refuge city belonged to the forty-eight towns assigned to Levi. The arrangement was remarkably wise: Levites knew God's law deeply, held no competing tribal farmland biasing them toward local clans, and carried spiritual authority to hold back angry avengers while conducting fair hearings.

**A minor ancient manuscript difference in chapter 20.** The ancient Greek Septuagint transmits a slightly shorter version of chapter 20, omitting the procedural details about testifying at the gate and waiting for the high priest's death (20:4–6). Since those exact statutes already appear fully in Numbers 35 and Deuteronomy 19, the chapter's legal and theological substance remains completely consistent.

**The Mishnah**, foundational Jewish oral law compiled around AD 200, devotes a full chapter to operating the refuge cities. Sages record that roads leading to all six towns had to be maintained smooth and wide, with prominent signposts reading "Refuge! Refuge!" at every crossroads so runners never lost their way.
