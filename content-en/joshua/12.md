# Joshua 12 — Roll Call of the Defeated Kings

## Story

Closing the long years of warfare before turning to allot the land among the twelve tribes, the book of Joshua pauses to draw up a solemn roll call of every defeated king. The ledger begins east of the Jordan River—territory already conquered back in Moses' day. Heading the list stands Sihon, king of the Amorites ruling in Heshbon, whose domain stretched from the Arnon Gorge and half of Gilead up to the Jabbok River bordering Ammon, encompassing the entire eastern Jordan Valley from the Sea of Kinnereth south to the Salt Sea (Dead Sea) beneath Mount Pisgah's slopes (12:1–3).

Second east of the Jordan is Og, king of Bashan—marked out specifically as "one of the last of the Rephaim," the famed ancient race of giants. Ruling from Ashtaroth and Edrei, Og governed from Mount Hermon and Salecah across all Bashan up to the borders of Geshur and Maacah, meeting Sihon's realm in mid-Gilead. Both formidable kingdoms fell to Moses, the servant of the LORD, and the Israelites, and Moses granted their land as an inheritance to Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh (12:4–6).

The ledger then crosses the Jordan westward, where Joshua and Israel fought across long years to win the expanse stretching from Baal-gad in the Valley of Lebanon north to Mount Halak facing Seir in the south. Every landscape of God's gift is captured cleanly—hill country, western foothills, Arabah valley, mountain slopes, wilderness, and Negev—formerly held by six peoples: Hittites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites (12:7–8).

From verse 9 onward, each conquered ruler is named in steady, crisp rhythm like an inscription carved onto a victory monument. Leading off are the two gateway cities of the Promised Land: the king of Jericho and the king of Ai beside Bethel. Next come the southern coalition kings and neighboring towns: Jerusalem, Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish, Eglon, Gezer, Debir, Geder, Hormah, Arad, Libnah, Adullam, Makkedah, and Bethel (12:9–16).

The second half sweeps northward across central ridges into Galilee: Tappuah, Hepher, Aphek, Lasharon, followed by pillars of the northern coalition—Madon, Hazor, Shimron-meron, and Achshaph. Completing the roll are strategic valley and highland towns: Taanach, Megiddo, Kedesh, Jokneam in Carmel, Dor in Naphoth-dor, Goyim in Gilgal, and finally Tirzah. The tally seals cleanly with a crisp total: "thirty-one kings in all" (12:17–24).

## Key Takeaways

**A list that looks dry at first glance is actually a concrete receipt of God's faithfulness.** Modern readers often skim chapters packed with ancient names and towns. Yet for ancient Israel, every name in chapter 12 stood as an undeniable milestone: proud city-states that once barred God's people now filled a ledger of defeated foes. God's promises are never hazy daydreams—they can be counted town by town and border by border.

**Moses' work and Joshua's work form one unbroken story.** Before enumerating Joshua's thirty-one defeated kings west of the river (12:7–24), the chapter deliberately recaps Moses' two eastern victories and the two and a half tribes settled there (12:1–6). Leaders change from generation to generation and a river runs down the middle, yet God's people remain one nation led by one unchanging God.

**Looking back at past mercy builds courage for unfinished work ahead.** Chapter 12 sits right on the hinge dividing the book's two halves: conquest (chapters 1–12) and allotment and settlement (chapters 13–24). In the very first verse of chapter 13, God will remind Joshua that much land still remains to be taken (13:1). Stopping to name everything God has already done anchors confidence that the One who began this good work will carry it through.

**A tiny former slave nation stood upright against thirty-three kingdoms.** Counting both banks of the Jordan brings the total to thirty-three kings—many backed by towering walls, iron chariots, and giant warriors. Reviewing the ledger never served to puff up Israel's pride in battle prowess, but to etch into national memory that victory came strictly from the LORD.

## Deeper Meaning

**Fulfilling the ancient covenant sworn to Abram.** Naming the six peoples in 12:8 alongside Rephaim giant Og in 12:4 echoes directly the covenant God cut with Abram centuries earlier when he stood childless and landless: promising his descendants the land of the Hittites, Perizzites, Rephaim, Amorites, Canaanites, and Jebusites (Genesis 15:18–21). Chapter 12 reads like a stamped historical receipt proving God kept His oath.

**Sihon and Og enter Israel's eternal worship song.** Those two opening eastern victories under Moses (Numbers 21:21–35) never faded from memory. Centuries later, whole congregations still sang their names in joyful liturgy praising the One who struck down mighty kings—Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan—giving their land as Israel's inheritance, "for His loving devotion endures forever" (Psalms 136:17–22). History's victories ripen into songs of praise.

**No *giant* ever outgrows God's reach.** Highlighting Og as "one of the last of the Rephaim" (12:4) connects straight back to chapter 11's clearing of the Anakim (11:21–22). Deuteronomy had noted Og's massive nine-cubit iron bedstead kept on display as a marvel (Deuteronomy 3:11). Setting Og near the head of chapter 12 reminds readers that every terrifying force towering over human eyes shrinks small before the LORD.

**Joshua's victory roll points ahead to Christ's greater triumph.** Joshua recorded thirty-one conquered kings opening an earthly inheritance; the New Testament announces an infinitely greater conquest—won not by shedding enemy blood, but by the cross. Paul declares of Jesus: "And having disarmed the powers and authorities, He made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross" (Colossians 2:15). Every tyrant of sin and death that once ruled humanity stands listed as defeated before the risen Christ.

## Notes & Nuances

**How could one modest strip of land hold thirty-one *kings*?** Imagining *kings* as rulers of sprawling empires makes thirty-one western kings sound implausible. Yet ancient Near Eastern archaeology—especially the fourteenth-century BC Amarna Letters unearthed in Egypt—confirms this exact political landscape: Canaan was not a unified state, but a patchwork of dozens of independent tiny city-states often separated by just a day's walk, each governed by a local ruler styled *king* over his walled town and nearby villages.

**Defeating a king did not automatically mean permanently occupying his city.** Skimming chapter 12 can easily mislead readers here. The list names the kings of Jerusalem (12:10), Gezer (12:12), Taanach, Megiddo, and Dor (12:21–23)—yet subsequent chapters and Judges plainly state Israel failed to expel the residents of Taanach, Megiddo, and Dor (Judges 1:27), while Jerusalem remained unconquered until King David centuries later. There is no contradiction: in chapters 10 and 11, those rulers marched their armies into open field coalitions and fell to Joshua in battle, yet Israel lacked manpower to garrison every town immediately, allowing Canaanite populations to hold or reoccupy the citadels.

**Why does chapter 12 name kings never mentioned in chapters 6–11?** Over a dozen towns listed here—Geder, Hormah, Arad, Adullam, Tappuah, Hepher, Aphek, Megiddo, Tirzah—never appeared in earlier battle accounts. This reminds us that Joshua is not a day-by-day diary of those five to seven years of "long" warfare (11:18), but a curated narrative highlighting pivotal campaigns; chapter 12 fills out a much broader victory record behind those headline battles.

**Clarifying tricky place names: Gilgal, Arad, and Hormah.** Many scholars note that "the king of Goyim in Gilgal" (12:23) does not refer to Israel's Jordan camp at Gilgal, but a northern district—the ancient Greek Septuagint reads *Galilee*, very close in Hebrew spelling to *Gilgal*. Likewise, victories involving southern Hormah and Arad reflect skirmishes that began late in the wilderness journey and continued under Joshua.
