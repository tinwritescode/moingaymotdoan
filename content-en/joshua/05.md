# Joshua 5 — The Sign of the Covenant and the Sword-Bearing Commander

## Story

News that the LORD dried up the Jordan River races across Canaan. Every Amorite king west of the Jordan and every Canaanite king along the coast hears the report and collapses in dread: "their hearts melted and they no longer had the courage to face the Israelites" (5:1).

Facing a paralyzed foe, any ordinary general would launch an immediate assault on Jericho. Yet the LORD's command to Joshua is startling: "Make flint knives and circumcise the Israelites once again." Joshua obeys immediately, circumcising the entire male population at a hill named Gibeath-haaraloth (5:2–3).

Scripture pauses to explain this unexpected move. Every adult male who left Egypt had already been circumcised, yet because of disobedience that entire generation of fighting men died during forty years in the wilderness. Their sons born along the trail had never been circumcised. Now, before inheriting the land flowing with milk and honey, the new generation must bear the covenant sign on their bodies. Once the rite finishes, the entire army stays quietly in camp waiting to heal. The LORD announces to Joshua, "Today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from you"—giving that site its enduring name *Gilgal*, meaning *rolling away* (5:4–9).

Fully restored in covenant standing, Israel solemnly observes the Passover at twilight on the fourteenth day of the first month right on the plains of Jericho. The very next day after Passover, they taste produce of the Promised Land for the first time: unleavened bread and roasted grain. The morning after that, a quiet yet profound transition takes place: the manna stops falling. From that year onward, Israel eats the crops grown right in Canaan (5:10–12).

Soon afterward, Joshua walks alone near Jericho—likely surveying the city's defenses ahead of the coming siege. Looking up, he suddenly sees a man standing opposite him with a drawn sword in his hand. Stepping boldly forward, Joshua challenges him: "Are you for us or for our enemies?" (5:13).

The reply shatters every assumption of Israel's commander: "Neither, but I have now come as Commander of the LORD's army." Without another word, Joshua falls facedown to the ground in worship and asks, "What does my Lord have to say to His servant?" The Commander answers with the very words spoken to Moses at the burning bush: "Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy." Joshua obeys at once (5:14–15).

## Key Takeaways

**Right relationship with God always precedes victory on the battlefield.** Holding complete tactical advantage over terrified foes, God had Israel's entire fighting force undergo circumcision—incapacitating the army for days right outside enemy walls (5:1–8). He wanted them to grasp from day one that entering the Promised Land is never about brute strength, but about living as God's covenant people.

**God rolls away old shame so His people can step into new life.** Through forty wilderness years, Egyptians had reason to mock that God led Israel into the desert only to die, while the young generation grew up missing even the covenant mark. At Gilgal, God "rolled away" past failure and reproach, restoring their standing as free children of promise (5:9).

**God does not stop providing when miracles give way to ordinary harvests.** Manna fell faithfully for forty years, then ceased the very morning after Israel ate Canaan's first grain (5:12). God performs no superfluous miracles once daily crops stand ready at hand. Bread dropping from heaven and wheat sprouting from soil flow from the very same Father's hand.

**God does not take our side—we must align on His side.** Joshua asked the sword-bearing stranger, "Are you for us or for our enemies?" The crisp, majestic reply—"Neither"—instantly resets the true order (5:13–14). God is never a hired ally endorsing our personal agendas. He is Supreme Commander, and humanity's only proper posture is slipping off our sandals, bowing low, and asking, "What does my Lord have to say to His servant?"

## Deeper Meaning

**Circumcision and Passover close the Exodus and open a new era.** Mosaic law barred anyone uncircumcised from eating Passover (Exodus 12:48), and circumcision had stood as the covenant sign since Abraham (Genesis 17:10–14). Carrying out both rites on Canaanite soil links the thread cleanly from Abraham through Moses into the Promised Land. Later, the New Testament reveals a deeper circumcision still: not a knife-mark on flesh, but "the circumcision of Christ," putting off the sinful nature by His Spirit (Colossians 2:11).

**Manna ceases, pointing forward to the Living Bread.** Exodus had foretold that Israel would eat manna for forty years "until they reached the border of Canaan" (Exodus 16:35)—fulfilled right on schedule at Gilgal (5:12). Centuries later, when crowds appealed back to wilderness manna, Jesus revealed that manna was merely a shadow: He Himself is "the bread of life" come down from heaven, so whoever comes to Him will never hunger again (John 6:31–35).

**The holy ground of the burning bush reappears outside Jericho.** The command "Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy" (5:15) echoes almost word for word God's call to Moses at Horeb's burning bush (Exodus 3:5). This unmistakable parallel both confirms Joshua as Moses' true successor and reminds us that what makes any ground holy is never geography—desert wilderness or Canaanite plain—but God's living presence.

**Who is the Commander of the LORD's army?** Several interlocking clues have convinced commentators across centuries that this figure is no ordinary angel. Created angels in Scripture immediately stop humans from bowing in worship (Revelation 19:10). Here, however, the sword-bearing Commander accepts Joshua's worship, declares the ground holy (5:14–15), and in the very next verse of the continuous narrative is named directly as "the LORD" speaking to Joshua (6:2). Many Christian scholars recognize here a visible appearance of God Himself—the pre-incarnate Son of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**The staggering military risk of circumcising the army outside Jericho.** Readers of Genesis remember Shechem's tragedy: when all the town's men were incapacitated on the third day after circumcision, just two of Jacob's sons wiped out the entire city by sword (Genesis 34:24–25). Having Israel's whole fighting force circumcised just a few miles from Jericho's garrison (5:8) left them completely defenseless—safe solely because God had already flooded enemy hearts with terror in 5:1.

**Why use *flint knives* (5:2–3)?** Even though bronze and iron tools existed in Joshua's era, ancient sacred rites commonly preserved traditional stone implements going back to Abraham and Moses—carrying sacred antiquity while keeping blades sharp and rust-free.

**Chapter 5 ends mid-scene because the conversation flows straight into chapter 6.** Verse 5:15 stops right as Joshua removes his sandals to listen, and the Commander's instructions immediately follow in 6:2–5—verse 6:1 being merely a parenthetical note describing Jericho's locked gates. As elsewhere in Scripture, later chapter breaks sliced straight through a single continuous encounter.
