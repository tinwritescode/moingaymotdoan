# Joshua 23 — The Old Commander's Farewell Warning

## Story

Long years of peace have passed since the crisis beside the Jordan in chapter 22. The LORD has granted Israel rest from all their surrounding enemies. Now Joshua is old and advanced in years. Knowing few days remain to him, he summons the pillars of the nation—elders, heads, judges, and officers—to hear his final counsel. He begins simply: "I am old and advanced in years." Rather than recounting his own exploits, he points them to what their own eyes saw: every victory over the Canaanite nations came from God's hand, "for it is the LORD your God who fought for you" (23:1–3).

The aging commander never glosses over unfinished work. He reminds them that he allotted by lot to their tribes the entire expanse from the Jordan to the Great Sea in the west, embracing both conquered regions and territories not yet fully cleared. Yet they have no cause to shrink back: the LORD Himself will keep driving those nations out ahead of them so Israel inherits every square inch just as He promised (23:4–5).

God's share stands rock-solid; now Joshua lays full responsibility on the leaders' shoulders. He passes along the exact command God once spoke to him: "Be very strong, then, to keep and to do all that is written in the Book of the Law of Moses, not turning aside from it to the right or to the left." In everyday life, that means refusing to mingle with the surviving nations around them—never mentioning their gods' names, never swearing by them, never serving or bowing down to them. Instead, they must "hold fast to the LORD your God" just as they have done right up to today (23:6–8).

Joshua strengthens them by pointing back to the true source of Israel's might. The LORD has driven out great and powerful nations so no one could stand before them; indeed, "one of you can put a thousand to flight," solely because the LORD fights for them. Then he distills the secret of national survival into one brief, searching charge: "So be very careful to love the LORD your God" (23:9–11).

Abruptly the old soldier's encouragement turns grave. He maps out the exact slippery slope awaiting them in peacetime: if they turn back, cling to the remnants of neighboring nations, "intermarry with them," and blend into their religious life, then know for certain the LORD will stop driving those peoples out. Instead, those very nations will turn into "a snare and a trap to you, a scourge for your sides and thorns in your eyes," tormenting them until Israel perishes completely from this good land God gave them (23:12–13).

Closing his speech, Joshua faces death with serene dignity: "Now behold, today I am going the way of all the earth." Before closing his eyes, he summons their own consciences to testify to God's faithfulness: of all the good promises the LORD made concerning them, "not one word has failed... all have come to pass for you." Yet right beside that comfort he sets a chilling symmetry: just as every good promise proved utterly true, so every warning of disaster will prove equally true. Should they break covenant to serve and bow before other gods, the LORD's anger will burn hot against them, and they will perish swiftly from the good land He placed in their hands (23:14–16).

## Key Takeaways

**Peacetime often brings deadlier tests than war.** Across his entire farewell, the veteran general breathes not a single worry about enemy swords or iron chariots—he has watched God fight for His people firsthand (23:9–10). His lone agony is that once settled in ease, hearts grow lukewarm, compromise with surrounding idolatry, and slowly lose their soul (23:7; 23:12–13).

**Love for God never stays warm automatically—it demands vigilant care.** Joshua offers no vague sentimentality, warning sharply: "Be very careful to love the LORD your God" (23:11). Love for God resembles a tended garden or hearth fire: left unguarded amid everyday busyness, weeds choke it and embers cool.

**God's people endure on Scripture, not on a single heroic leader.** Knowing death draws near, Joshua appoints no single successor to step into his boots. The sole authority he places into the elders' hands to steer the nation is "the Book of the Law of Moses" (23:6). Even the noblest leaders eventually go the way of all the earth (23:14); God's word alone abides forever.

**God's faithfulness holds equally in both directions: good promises and solemn warnings.** People love remembering that God keeps every gracious promise, yet dislike dwelling on the certainty of His warnings against unfaithfulness. Joshua locks both together in adjacent verses (23:14–15). A God who keeps every syllable of His promises never speaks idle words when warning against sin.

## Deeper Meaning

**A full echo binding the first page of Joshua to its close.** When Joshua succeeded Moses in chapter 1, the LORD gave him his marching orders: "be strong and courageous," carefully obey the law of Moses, "do not turn from it to the right or to the left" (1:7–8). Now silver-haired, Joshua hands that identical torch to the next generation of elders (23:6). Vivid pictures like one chasing a thousand or thorns in their eyes and scourges in their sides likewise trace straight back to Moses' own parting charges (Numbers 33:55; Deuteronomy 32:30).

**A heartbreaking preview fulfilled across centuries of history.** Reading chapter 23 knowing what follows in the Old Testament feels like watching a film fast-forward into the future. Right at the opening of Judges, Israel compromises, lives among Canaanites, intermarries, and worships their gods—turning those nations into "a snare" just as Joshua warned (Judges 2:2–3; 3:5–6). Even wise King Solomon fell into this exact ditch when foreign queens pulled his heart after other gods (1 Kings 11:2–4). Eventually the threat of perishing from the good land (23:15–16; Deuteronomy 28:63–64) unfolded bitterly when Israel was swept off into exile (2 Kings 17:18).

**The parting pattern of faithful shepherds across Scripture.** Joshua's calm description of death as "the way of all the earth" is echoed almost verbatim by dying King David charging his son Solomon (1 Kings 2:2). In the New Testament, Paul bidding farewell to the Ephesian elders reflects the identical shepherd's heart: claiming no personal empire, recalling past grace, warning of fierce wolves threatening the flock after he leaves, and urging vigilant watchfulness (Acts 20:28–31).

**Love for God sits at the core of every command.** Amid solemn warnings and strict prohibitions, verse 23:11 exposes the living heart of biblical faith: true obedience flows from loving the LORD. Over a millennium later, asked to name the greatest commandment in the law, Jesus likewise placed loving God with all heart, soul, and mind dead first (Matthew 22:37).

**Beholding both God's kindness and His severity.** Verses 23:14–15—setting promised blessing side by side with covenant warning—resurface powerfully in Romans, where Paul calls believers to consider "both the kindness and the severity of God" (Romans 11:22). Divine love is never casual or indulgent; God's uncompromising holiness guards His covenant of love from being trampled underfoot.

## Notes & Nuances

**Is forbidding intermarriage with Canaanites (23:12) racial prejudice?** Pulled out of context, this verse can be misread as ethnic bigotry. Read straight alongside 23:7 and 23:12–13—and its source law in Deuteronomy 7:3–4—the concern is purely spiritual worship, never ethnicity or bloodline: marrying active idol worshipers pulls Israel's children away from the LORD. Joshua itself warmly welcomed Canaanite Rahab into Israel when she turned to the LORD, and Ruth the Moabitess later entered King David's royal line. In the New Testament, Paul preserves this same spiritual principle in urging believers not to be unequally yoked with unbelievers (2 Corinthians 6:14).

**How should we read fairly the call to keep driving out remaining nations (23:4–5; 23:13)?** The Canaanite conquest remains among the sharpest tensions in Old Testament reading. Traditional Christian interpreters view it as a singular, historically bounded act of divine judgment on cultures steeped in extreme violence and child sacrifice, never granting license for later religious wars or ethnic violence. Other scholars note conventional ancient military hyperbole while pointing out the bracing impartiality built right into chapter 23: Israel held zero ethnic immunity—if they turned to the exact same idolatry, God would drive Israel itself right off that good land too (23:15–16).

**Why does Joshua close with two back-to-back farewell speeches in chapters 23 and 24?** Readers sometimes wonder if Joshua repeats himself. Actually, the two addresses differ clearly in audience, setting, and aim. Chapter 23 delivers a focused leadership charge to Israel's elders and judges at an unnamed spot; chapter 24 summons the whole nation to Shechem to recite redemption history from Abraham onward and publicly renew the covenant. Many scholars note distinct traditions placed side by side to close the book; either way, they complement each other naturally: chapter 23 presses leaders' vigilance, while chapter 24 calls every citizen to public decision.

**The Hebrew verb *hold fast* reveals an unavoidable choice.** The exact Hebrew verb translated *hold fast* to the LORD in 23:8 appears again in 23:12 for *clinging* to surviving pagan nations—the same verb Genesis uses for husband and wife uniting as one flesh (Genesis 2:24). Human hearts never stay neutral or empty: either we cling tightly to God, or we drift into clinging to whatever pulls us away from Him.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, draws sharp attention to the five-step downward slide listed in 23:7—mingling, naming foreign gods, swearing by them, serving them, and bowing down.
