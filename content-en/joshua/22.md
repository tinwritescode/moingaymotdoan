# Joshua 22 — The Altar Beside the Jordan

## Story

With land allotments completed and Levitical towns assigned in chapter 21, the hour arrives for Joshua to discharge the fighting men of the two and a half eastern tribes—Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh. Summoning them publicly, he commends their loyalty warmly: across long years of campaigning they kept everything Moses commanded and obeyed Joshua in every detail, never abandoning their brothers until the LORD granted "rest" to the entire nation. Blessing them to return home across the Jordan, Joshua leaves them with one overriding parting charge: observe Moses' law diligently—"to love the LORD your God, to walk in all His ways, to keep His commandments, to hold fast to Him, and to serve Him with all your heart and with all your soul" (22:1–6).

Before sending them off, Joshua adds one more generous reminder. The returning warriors carry home immense plunder—"great wealth, very much livestock, silver, gold, bronze, iron, and a great quantity of clothing." He tells them to share that spoil generously with their brothers who stayed back home guarding families and homesteads through the war years. Dismissed with blessing, the two and a half tribes leave Shiloh heading home to Gilead across the Jordan (22:7–9).

Reaching the Jordan district still on Canaan's side, however, the eastern tribes abruptly pause and construct a huge, towering altar right near the river—so massive it stands visibly from far off. News flashes back west immediately. Hearing reports that the eastern tribes erected an unauthorized altar beside the Jordan, western Israel assumes they have turned apostate and raised a rival shrine. Instantly the entire congregation assembles at Shiloh preparing to march to war against their own brothers (22:10–12).

Thankfully, Israel refuses to draw swords purely on rumor. Before attacking, they dispatch a solemn delegation to Gilead to investigate firsthand: Phinehas son of Eleazar the priest, joined by ten tribal chiefs representing every western tribe. Reaching their eastern brothers, the delegation speaks bluntly and sharply: why have you broken faith and turned away from the LORD by building an altar in "rebellion against the LORD"? They invoke two raw national scars: the idolatry at Peor that unleashed a devastating plague whose shadow still lingers, and Achan's sin bringing wrath on the whole camp because "he was not the only one who died for his sin." Yet amid stern rebuke, Phinehas extends remarkable brotherly grace: if your eastern land feels unclean without the tabernacle, come cross over and share our western inheritance—only never raise a rival second altar competing with the LORD's altar (22:13–20).

Faced with blistering accusations, the eastern tribes refuse to flare back in outrage. They open with a solemn double appeal to "The Mighty One, God, the LORD!": if we built this altar in rebellion or to offer unlawful sacrifices, let the LORD Himself call us to account and refuse to save us. Then they open their hearts and share the fear haunting them. The Jordan River divides east from west; they worried that one day western descendants might point across the river and tell eastern children, "You have no share in the LORD," driving their offspring away from revering God (22:21–25).

Out of that anxiety alone they built this structure—never to burn offerings or slay sacrifices. It stands strictly as a replica of the LORD's altar, serving as a visual witness between both banks and across coming generations that eastern Israel retains full worship rights before the tabernacle alongside their brothers. If anyone ever tells their children they have no part in the LORD, their children can point straight at this monument as proof. Far be it from them ever to rebel or raise a competing altar besides the one true altar before God's tabernacle (22:26–29).

Hearing their sincere explanation, Phinehas and the ten chiefs breathe huge relief and are completely satisfied. Phinehas rejoices that today they know the LORD dwells truly among His people, since their eastern brothers committed no treachery and spared Israel from divine wrath. Returning to Shiloh, the delegation reports everything fully. The entire congregation rejoices, praises God, and immediately abandons all talk of war. The eastern tribes name the altar *Ed*—meaning *Witness*—declaring, "It is a witness between us that the LORD is God" (22:30–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Keep your promises faithfully and honor those holding down the home front.** The eastern warriors left wives and children for years to fight alongside western brothers exactly as vowed to Moses (22:2–3). Heading home laden with spoil, Joshua reminds them to share fairly with relatives who guarded farms and families behind the lines (22:8). In God's community, front-line bravery and quiet behind-the-scenes stewardship carry equal honor.

**Zeal for pure faith must verify facts before passing judgment.** Western Israel's alarm had real grounds: fresh memories of Peor and Achan proved how quickly hidden apostasy devastated the entire nation (22:17; 22:20). Their crowning wisdom lay in pausing armed troops at Shiloh to send envoys and talk face to face first (22:12–14). Listening before striking saved the nation from fratricidal civil war.

**A calm, honest answer defuses explosive conflict.** Phinehas's team walked in firing harsh accusations, assuming rebellion before asking a single question. The eastern veterans had ample excuse to bristle—having just bled beside these very brothers for years, only to be accused of treason before unpacking their bags! Yet they refused tit-for-tat fury, calling upon God in humility and speaking openly from the heart (22:22–28). Gentleness turned impending war into shared praise.

**Guard the next generation's faith while we are still alive.** Returning home, the eastern tribes worried less about border defense or farmland than about whether their children might one day be cut off from worshiping the LORD (22:24–25). Passing living faith onward never happens by accident; it takes parents looking ahead and building bridges connecting their children to God's people.

## Deeper Meaning

**A promise arc closes seamlessly across the entire book of Joshua.** Back in chapter 1, stepping into Moses' shoes, Joshua reminded the eastern tribes of their pledge in Moab: cross over armed before your brothers until every tribe enters rest (Numbers 32:20–23; Joshua 1:12–15). Now in chapter 22 that vow reaches completion without a scratch. Sharing spoils with those guarding camp also echoes Moses' precedent after the Midianite battle (Numbers 31:27), while Joshua's heartfelt farewell charge (22:5) distills the very soul of Deuteronomy (Deuteronomy 10:12).

**One single altar preserves one unified people.** Why did an extra altar nearly spark civil war? Because Mosaic law explicitly forbade multiplying local sacrifice sites once settled, mandating all offerings be brought to the single place the LORD chose for His name (Deuteronomy 12:5–6; 12:13–14). One altar bound twelve scattered tribes around one true God; rival regional altars would swiftly fracture unity and lure Israel into pagan polytheism.

**Obeying the law of careful investigation averts civil disaster.** Mosaic law instructed that whenever reports surfaced of an Israelite town turning to idols, the nation must never act on rumor, but "investigate, inquire, and examine thoroughly" first (Deuteronomy 13:12–15). Sending Phinehas's delegation lived out that just, humane statute completely. Sadly, that restraint would not last: in Judges, a later crisis erupted into a catastrophic civil war that nearly wiped out the tribe of Benjamin (Judges 20:1–11).

**A timeless biblical blueprint for peacemaking and honest dialogue.** This tense scene beside the Jordan brings Proverbs vibrantly alive: "He who answers a matter before he hears it—this is folly and shame," and "A gentle answer turns away wrath" (Proverbs 18:13; 15:1). In the New Testament, Jesus sets the same foundation for resolving conflict: when a brother seems to wrong you, go speak directly face to face first (Matthew 18:15). James likewise calls every believer to be "quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger" (James 1:19).

**Willingness to sacrifice personal gain for spiritual unity.** Easily missed amid the drama sits a jewel of generosity in 22:19: suspecting eastern soil felt unclean without the tabernacle, western leaders invited their brothers to cross over and take shares out of western land! They gladly volunteered to shrink their own estates rather than watch brothers drift from God. In the New Testament, Paul calls the church to the same spirit—making every effort "to preserve the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace," since there is one body, one Lord, and one faith (Ephesians 4:3–5).

## Notes & Nuances

**Did the eastern tribes build their altar on the west or east bank of the Jordan?** Verses 22:10–11 leave room for discussion: verse 10 places it in "the region of the Jordan in the land of Canaan," whereas verse 11 places it "on the border of Canaan... on the Israelite side." Many scholars understand the eastern tribes built it on the western side just before crossing back east, anchoring it unmistakably on Canaanite soil as an undeniable link. Others read the Hebrew as referring to the river's eastern lowland directly opposite Canaan. Either bank placed this conspicuous landmark right at the river gateway witnessing to both sides.

**Why did Israel pick Phinehas to lead the delegation?** Phinehas, son of High Priest Eleazar, was no ordinary diplomat. Earlier at Peor, his bold spear stopped blatant Midianite idolatry, halting a plague that claimed twenty-four thousand lives and winning God's covenant of peace (Numbers 25:6–13). When that very champion of holiness invoked Peor's memory (22:17), his words carried immense moral weight. Even more admirable, that same fierce defender of purity was never hotheaded or blind: the moment truth came clear, he rejoiced to declare his brothers innocent and returned to Shiloh as a peacemaker (22:30–32).

**Were the eastern tribes completely blameless in how they acted?** Pure motives aside, their execution clearly lacked foresight. Quietly raising an imposing altar without breathing a word to Joshua, High Priest Eleazar, or their western brothers invited immediate suspicion. Western Israel likewise jumped fast by mobilizing troops before verifying reports. Scripture idealizes neither side: imperfect people stumbled on both banks, yet humble willingness to listen rescued everyone.

**The name *Ed* (*Witness*) in 22:34.** Many ancient Hebrew manuscripts leave the proper name *Ed* unstated in verse 34, retaining simply the explanation, "It is a witness between us that the LORD is God." Several ancient manuscripts and the Syriac version include *Ed*, followed by many modern English translations for clarity. Whether explicitly named or implied, the monument's meaning stands unmistakable: a memorial witness, never an altar for sacrifice.

In his retelling, Joshua and Eleazar stepped in to cool western tempers before brothers shed blood, dispatching Phinehas and the elders to inquire first.
