# Joshua 3 — Crossing the Jordan at Flood Stage

## Story

Rising early in the morning, Joshua and all Israel leave Shittim, reach the edge of the Jordan River, and camp there overnight (3:1). After three days waiting by the water, officers move through the camp with clear instructions: as soon as they see the Levitical priests lifting the ark of the covenant of the LORD, everyone must break camp and follow it. Yet they must keep a distance of about two thousand cubits—nearly half a mile—never crowding close, so everyone behind can clearly see the path ahead, "since you have never traveled this way before" (3:2–4).

On the eve of departure, Joshua issues an order that seems strangely unrelated to crossing a river: "Consecrate yourselves, for tomorrow the LORD will do wonders among you." Then he directs the priests to take up the ark of the covenant and cross ahead of the people, and they obey at once (3:5–6).

At that moment the LORD speaks personally to Joshua: "Today I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel, so that they may know that I am with you just as I was with Moses." God instructs him to command the priests bearing the ark to step right to the edge of the Jordan's water and stand still there (3:7–8).

Joshua gathers all Israel to hear the LORD's words. The miracle about to unfold will serve as living proof that "the living God is among you" and will surely drive out the seven native nations—Canaanites, Hittites, Hivites, Perizzites, Girgashites, Amorites, and Jebusites—before them. The ark of the covenant of "the Lord of all the earth" will go straight into the Jordan ahead of them. Having chosen twelve men, one from each tribe, Joshua announces what will happen: the instant the soles of the priests' feet touch the Jordan's waters, the rushing torrent from upstream will be cut off and stand piled up in a single heap (3:9–13).

It is harvest season, when melting snow from Mount Hermon swells the Jordan overflowing all its banks. Even so, as the people break camp and the priests carrying the ark dip their feet into the river's edge, the raging current abruptly halts. Far upstream, the waters pile high in a heap all the way back at Adam, a town near Zarethan, while the water below drains completely down to the Salt Sea (the Dead Sea), exposing a dry riverbed straight across from Jericho (3:14–16).

The priests carrying the ark stand firm on dry ground right in the middle of the Jordan while the entire nation—men, women, and children—passes calmly across to the far bank without getting their feet wet (3:17).

## Key Takeaways

**Walking an unfamiliar road demands keeping our eyes fixed on God.** The line "since you have never traveled this way before" (3:4) captures a new generation standing on uncharted ground. Keeping respectful distance behind the ark both honored God's holiness and let hundreds of thousands behind see Him leading the way. Facing an unknown future, what matters most is not moving fast, but never running ahead of God.

**Prepare your heart before expecting God's wonders.** Joshua did not tell Israel to sharpen swords or lash rafts together; he said, "Consecrate yourselves" (3:5). God's miracles are never spectacles for idle onlookers, but encounters with a holy God. Setting our lives right to meet Him is always step one.

**Faith often gets its feet wet before the river parts.** At the Red Sea long before, the waters split before Israel stepped in (Exodus 14:21–22). At the flooded Jordan, the current stopped only at the instant the priests' feet stepped into the water's edge (3:13; 3:15). Sometimes God does not pave dry ground ahead of time; He waits for an obedient first step before opening the way.

**God chooses the hardest season to reveal His power.** During dry months, the Jordan has shallow fords easily waded—just as Jericho's guards had searched days earlier (2:7). Yet God brought Israel across at spring harvest flood stage, water spilling over every bank (3:15; 4:19). He did so leaving no room to credit luck or low water, compelling all eyes to confess He is "the Lord of all the earth" (3:11; 3:13).

## Deeper Meaning

**Two parted waters frame a forty-year journey of redemption.** Israel left Egypt walking on dry ground through the Red Sea under Moses (Exodus 14:22; 14:29), and entered the Promised Land walking on dry ground across the Jordan under Joshua (3:17). Psalms sets both wonders side by side in celebration of the Creator's might: "The sea observed and fled; the Jordan turned back" (Psalms 114:3–5). God is mighty not only to pull His people out of bondage, but also to carry them all the way into His promise.

**The ark goes ahead to find rest for God's people.** Throughout wilderness marches, the ark had traveled three days ahead seeking a resting place for Israel (Numbers 10:33). In chapter 3, the ark dominates virtually every scene, from the eastern bank to standing unmoved in mid-channel. The priests holding the ark steady against the blocked floodwaters while the whole nation crosses safely paints a vivid picture of our Mediator standing between us and the rushing flood of judgment to open a path of life.

**From Joshua to Elisha: the river confirms God's chosen successor.** God promised to exalt Joshua in Israel's eyes so they would recognize His presence just as with Moses (3:7). Centuries later, at this exact river, Elijah struck the Jordan with his cloak to divide it, and after Elijah's ascent to heaven, his successor Elisha struck the river with that same cloak and divided it once more (2 Kings 2:8; 2:14). Same river, same testimony: power rests never in human vessels, but in the living God.

**Right at the Jordan, Jesus launches a new exodus.** Centuries later still, at this very river, John the Baptist called Israel to repentance, and Jesus—bearing Joshua's own name—stepped down into the Jordan's waters to be baptized and begin His mission of saving humanity (Matthew 3:13–17). Joshua led Israel across the Jordan into Canaan; Jesus stepped down into that river to open the way for all nations into God's kingdom.

## Notes & Nuances

**What did *consecrate yourselves* (3:5) involve?** To modern ears the phrase can sound abstract. In Old Testament practice—such as Israel preparing to meet God at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19:10–11)—consecration combined outward cleansing (washing bodies and clothes, temporary marital abstinence) with inward devotion, laying aside defilement and turning wholeheartedly toward God.

**Was the stoppage at Adam caused by a natural landslide?** Ancient Adam (widely identified with Tell ed-Damiyeh) sits roughly eighteen miles north of Jericho, where steep clay banks tower over the river. Historical records confirm earthquakes have repeatedly collapsed those clay cliffs, damming the Jordan for hours at a stretch—notably in 1267, 1906, and 1927. Some commentators think God providentially used such a landslide as His instrument, while others view the miracle as wholly supernatural without natural mediation. Either way, the unmistakable miracle lies in split-second timing: the torrent halted at the exact instant the priests' feet touched the water and surged back the moment their feet stepped onto the bank (4:18).

**The seven Canaanite nations in 3:10.** This sevenfold list recurs across the Pentateuch as a comprehensive roster of Canaan's native peoples. The number seven conveys fullness: however numerous and varied Israel's foes, the One leading Israel is no local tribal god, but "the Lord of all the earth" (3:11).

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, third-century Christian scholar in Alexandria, reads the Jordan crossing under Joshua as a rich foreshadowing of New Testament baptism. Distinguishing two water crossings, he treats the Red Sea as leaving Egypt's slavery behind and the Jordan as stepping into the promised inheritance under Joshua—who shares Jesus' name. **John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, highlights two clear divine intentions in this chapter. First, God deliberately timed the crossing during harvest flood season to silence anyone claiming Israel merely got lucky at a low ford.
