# Joshua 2 — Rahab and the Scarlet Cord

## Story

From the camp at Shittim east of the Jordan, Joshua secretly sends two spies across the river to scout out the land of Canaan, especially Jericho. Slipping into the city, the two men lodge for the night at the house of a prostitute named Rahab—a place where travelers routinely came and went without attracting undue notice (2:1).

Still, word reaches the king of Jericho. Someone reports that Israelites slipped in under cover of night to spy out the land. The king immediately dispatches guards to Rahab's door, ordering her to hand over the two strangers who entered her house (2:2–3).

Thinking quickly, Rahab has already guided the two spies up to her flat roof and hidden them beneath stalks of flax drying there. Facing the king's soldiers, she replies calmly: yes, the men did come to her, though she had no idea where they were from; at dusk, just as the city gate was about to shut, they left—if the soldiers chase after them quickly, they are sure to catch them. The king's men believe her, race out toward the fords of the Jordan, and the city gate slams shut behind them (2:4–7).

Once quiet falls, before the spies lie down to sleep, Rahab climbs back up to the roof to speak with them. She opens with a confession that must have stunned both men: she knows for certain that the LORD has given Israel this land, and terror has gripped everyone—"all the inhabitants of the land are melting in fear before you." Everyone in Jericho has heard clearly how the LORD dried up the Red Sea when Israel left Egypt, and how they utterly destroyed the two Amorite kings Sihon and Og east of the Jordan. Hearing those reports, the city's courage melted like water and no spirit remained in anyone, "for the LORD your God is God in heaven above and on the earth below" (2:8–11).

Out of that faith, Rahab asks the two men to swear an oath. She has shown them kindness to save their lives; now she pleads with them to swear by the LORD to show kindness in return to her father's household—sparing her father, mother, brothers, sisters, and all who belong to them when Jericho falls. The spies pledge firmly: "Our lives for your lives!" Provided she keeps their mission secret, they will deal kindly and faithfully with her (2:12–14).

Rahab's house is built right into the city wall. Letting the two men down by a rope through her window outside the wall, she advises them carefully: hide in the hill country for three days until the pursuers return, then head safely back to camp. Before parting, the spies fix a clear rescue signal: when Israel invades, she must tie a scarlet cord right in that window and gather her father, mother, brothers, and whole extended family inside the house. Anyone stepping out into the street will bear responsibility for his own blood; anyone staying inside her house who gets harmed will be on the spies' own heads. Rahab agrees, sends them off, and immediately ties the scarlet cord in the window (2:15–21).

The two spies hide in the hills for three days while Jericho's patrols comb every road without finding a trace and finally turn back. Once the coast is clear, the spies descend, recross the Jordan, and report everything to Joshua. Their verdict is brief and brimming with faith: "Surely the LORD has delivered the entire land into our hands. Indeed, all the people of the land are melting in fear before us" (2:22–24).

## Key Takeaways

**God's grace seeks out the last people anyone expects.** The brightest hero at the opening of Joshua is not an Israelite warrior, but a foreign woman working in a despised trade right inside Jericho (2:1). God does not wait for people to polish up their past before welcoming them; He meets them squarely amid messy lives.

**Hearing the exact same news, some bolt their gates in fear while others turn in trust.** All Jericho heard of the Red Sea and the two Amorite kings (2:10–11). The king and citizens chose to hunker behind thick walls, while Rahab chose to side with the living God. Faith begins the moment we stop resisting God and place our future in His hands.

**Genuine faith acts and risks something real.** Rahab did not merely recite pious words on a rooftop. Hiding enemy scouts under the king's nose was capital treason punishable by death. She staked her own life and her entire family's survival on the promise of a God she had never yet seen (2:4–6; 2:12–13).

**Those who find rescue long to bring their loved ones under the same roof.** Rahab's very first request was neither silver nor her own survival alone, but safety for her parents, siblings, and whole family (2:12–13). Authentic gratitude toward God is never selfish; it aches to gather the people we love into the same place of shelter.

## Deeper Meaning

**From Shittim's old shame emerges unexpected grace.** The spies set out from Shittim—the exact campsite where Israel had recently plunged into immorality and idolatry with Moabite women (Numbers 25:1). This scouting mission also reverses the disaster forty years earlier: back then Moses sent twelve spies and ten came back spreading panic that crippled the nation (Numbers 13:27–33); now Joshua sends just two, and they return with an exuberant report of faith—echoing words spoken by a Canaanite woman (2:24; cf. Exodus 15:14–16).

**Moses' great confession rings out from a Canaanite woman's lips.** When Rahab declares, "the LORD your God is God in heaven above and on the earth below" (2:11), she repeats nearly verbatim the creed Moses had just taught Israel east of the Jordan (Deuteronomy 4:39). Far from regarding the LORD as a tribal desert deity, she recognizes Him as sole Sovereign over heaven and earth.

**The scarlet cord echoes Passover night in Egypt.** Back in Egypt, Israel brushed red lamb's blood across their doorframes and stayed strictly inside during the night of judgment (Exodus 12:13; 12:22–23). In Jericho, a Gentile household hangs a red cord from its window, gathers safely indoors, and survives the city's collapse (2:18–19; 6:22–25). Generations of Christians have seen in both signs a vivid picture of Christ's blood shielding everyone who takes shelter in Him.

**From Jericho's wall into the genealogy of the Messiah.** The New Testament honors Rahab in three remarkable places. Hebrews places her proudly among the great heroes of faith (Hebrews 11:31). James sets her right beside father Abraham as proof of living faith expressed through deeds (James 2:25). Most astonishing of all, Matthew records her name in Jesus' own family tree: she becomes mother of Boaz, great-great-grandmother of King David, and an ancestor of the Messiah (Matthew 1:5). No past is too dark to shut the door of God's grace.

## Notes & Nuances

**How should we evaluate Rahab's lie?** For two thousand years ethicists and commentators have debated Rahab deceiving the king's guards to save two innocent lives (2:4–5), just as the Hebrew midwives deceived Pharaoh to save newborn boys (Exodus 1:17–20). Commentators largely divide into two camps. One holds that lying is always wrong, noting Scripture never commends Rahab's deception—only her faith and welcome of the spies (Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25)—while graciously bearing with the flaws of someone newly emerging from pagan darkness. The other distinguishes harmful falsehood from wartime misdirection protecting innocent life from lethal evil, viewing her act as choosing the higher moral duty when obligations collide. Both perspectives agree on one key point: Scripture never tells this story to excuse everyday dishonesty.

**Was Rahab really a prostitute, or merely an innkeeper?** Several ancient Jewish sources—including Josephus and Rabbi Rashi—glossed the Hebrew word in 2:1 as *food-seller* or *innkeeper*, partly uncomfortable with an ancestor of prophets holding such a reputation. However, most linguists and the New Testament itself (Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25) preserve the literal meaning *prostitute*. Her house may well have functioned as a roadside inn alongside prostitution—and Scripture's refusal to whitewash her background only magnifies the depth of God's grace.

**Why did Joshua send spies if God had already promised the land?** Some wonder whether scouting showed weak faith. Yet biblical faith never contradicts wise preparation. Just as God promised the land while still summoning Israel to march in and claim it, Joshua prudently gathered intelligence—and remarkably, the greatest treasure the spies brought home was not fortification blueprints, but spiritual courage on hearing God had already melted enemy hearts ahead of them (2:24).
