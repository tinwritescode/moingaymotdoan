# Joshua 24 — Renewing the Covenant at Shechem

## Story

Following his private charge to Israel's leaders in chapter 23, Joshua summons every tribe of Israel along with elders, leaders, judges, and officers to Shechem to stand together before God. Here Joshua speaks directly as the LORD's messenger delivering God's own words. God sweeps the whole nation backward in time across the Euphrates River: long ago their ancestor Terah, father of Abraham and Nahor, worshiped other gods. God Himself took the initiative to call Abraham from beyond the river, lead him across Canaan, give him Isaac, multiply his offspring, assign Mount Seir to Esau, and send Jacob and his sons down to Egypt (24:1–4).

God's recitation moves swiftly across the defining chapters of Israel's rescue. He sent Moses and Aaron to strike Egypt with signs and bring their fathers out. When Egyptian chariots and horsemen chased them to the Red Sea, their ancestors cried out; God placed darkness between the two hosts and brought the sea crashing back over their pursuers. After long wilderness years, God brought them into Amorite territory east of the Jordan and delivered those foes into their hand. When Moabite king Balak hired Balaam to curse them, God refused to listen, turning Balaam's mouth to bless them instead and rescuing them from Balak's hand (24:5–10).

Then Israel crossed the Jordan and met Jericho alongside seven Canaanite nations. God declares He handed them all over, sending "the hornet" ahead to drive enemies before them—"not by your sword or bow." He seals His summary of grace with a warm portrait of sheer gift: He gave them a land they did not toil to clear, cities they did not build to live in, and vineyards and olive groves they did not plant whose fruit they now eat (24:11–13).

Out of everything God has done, Joshua issues a resounding summons to the assembly: "fear the LORD and serve Him in sincerity and truth," throwing away the gods their ancestors served beyond the Euphrates and in Egypt. Then rings out the most famous challenge in the book of Joshua: "But if it is unpleasing in your sight to serve the LORD, then choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve, whether the gods your fathers served beyond the Euphrates, or the gods of the Amorites in whose land you are living. As for me and my house, we will serve the LORD!" Immediately the people respond in unison that far be it from them to forsake the LORD for other gods—He rescued them from Egypt's slavery, worked mighty signs, shielded them all along the road, and drove out the nations to give them this land (24:14–18).

Yet instead of applauding their enthusiastic vow, Joshua splashes cold water over the crowd, stopping everyone cold: "You are not able to serve the LORD, for He is a holy God; He is a jealous God; He will not forgive your rebellion or your sins." Should they forsake Him for foreign gods, the very God who blessed them will turn and bring disaster upon them. Unshaken, the people insist a second time: "No! We will serve the LORD!" Calling them as witnesses against themselves, Joshua demands proof right now: "Throw away the foreign gods that are among you," and yield your hearts wholly to God. A third time the people pledge to serve the LORD and obey His voice (24:19–24).

That very day Joshua makes a covenant with the people at Shechem, sets down statutes and ordinances, and writes every word in the Book of the Law of God. Lifting a huge stone, he sets it upright "under the oak that was by the sanctuary of the LORD." That silent stone will stand as a witness against them—as though it had heard every word God spoke—testifying if they ever deal falsely with their God. Ceremony complete, Joshua sends everyone home to their own inheritance (24:25–28).

The book of Joshua closes beside three peaceful graves resting inside the Promised Land. First, Joshua son of Nun—crowned at last with the supreme title "the servant of the LORD"—dies at age one hundred ten and is buried at Timnath-serah in Ephraim's hill country; Israel serves the LORD faithfully throughout Joshua's lifetime and the days of the elders who outlived him and had personally seen God's works. Second, Joseph's bones, carried all the way from Egypt, finally come to rest at Shechem in the exact field Jacob bought from Hamor's sons for one hundred pieces of silver. Third, Eleazar the priest, Aaron's son, dies and is buried at Gibeah of Phinehas in Ephraim's hills (24:29–33).

## Key Takeaways

**God's grace always comes before any human obligation.** Before Joshua speaks a single command in verse 14, God spends twelve straight verses (24:2–13) reciting what He alone has done: I took, I gave, I sent, I delivered, I handed over. Even forefather Abraham sprang from an idol-worshiping family (24:2); Israel held zero merit earning God's choice. Serving God never buys His favor—it answers gratefully to love that already moved first.

**Faith never coasts on inertia—each generation must choose for itself.** Though Israel sat settled securely on promised ground, Joshua refused to let them follow God out of inherited habit. He puts the verdict squarely before them: choose today whom you will serve (24:15). Children of faithful parents must still stand personally before God and make their own choice.

**Following God means total loyalty, never straddling two sides.** Joshua's jolting words in 24:19—that they are unable to serve the LORD—puncture easy crowd enthusiasm. Even as their mouths swore total loyalty, Joshua exposed hidden "foreign gods" still tucked inside their tents (24:23). Nobody can shout loyalty to God while clinging privately to backup idols.

**True leaders set the pace before asking others to follow.** Joshua never polled the crowd to see which way the wind blew. Before a single citizen spoke, the old commander nailed his own banner firmly in place: "As for me and my house, we will serve the LORD!" (24:15). A clear, steadfast life moves hearts far more deeply than lofty rhetoric.

## Deeper Meaning

**Shechem—where the entire patriarchal circle closes completely.** Choosing Shechem for covenant renewal awakens Israel's oldest spiritual memories. Beneath the great tree at Shechem, God first appeared to Abram promising Canaan and receiving his very first altar in the land (Genesis 12:6–7). Generations later, right at Shechem, Jacob paid one hundred silver pieces for his camp field (Genesis 33:18–19) and buried all his household's foreign idols deep beneath that very oak (Genesis 35:2–4). Over a millennium later still, right beside that plot Jacob gave Joseph, Jesus sat by the well teaching the Samaritan woman about true worship in spirit and truth (John 4:5–6).

**Joseph's bones come home—a four-hundred-year promise fulfilled at last.** Genesis ended with an unforgettable open expectation: Joseph dying at age one hundred ten, laid in a coffin in Egypt after making his brothers swear to carry his bones back to Canaan (Genesis 50:24–26). On Exodus night Moses lifted that coffin and carried it through forty wilderness years (Exodus 13:19). In 24:32, those bones finally enter Canaan's soil. Hebrews honors Joseph's dying instructions about his bones as radiant faith conquering death (Hebrews 11:22). Together, the book's three final graves—Joshua, Joseph, and Eleazar—anchor Israel permanently in the Promised Land: God's people are home at last.

**The call to *choose* echoes from Old Testament to New.** Joshua's challenge at Shechem directly continues Moses' plea to choose life over death (Deuteronomy 30:19). Centuries later atop Mount Carmel, Elijah confronts a wavering nation with the same ultimatum: "How long will you waver between two opinions? If the LORD is God, follow Him" (1 Kings 18:21). In the New Testament, Jesus states the unchanging truth cleanly: "No one can serve two masters" (Matthew 6:24).

**Human inability points forward to the New Covenant.** Joshua's stark diagnosis in 24:19—"You are not able to serve the LORD"—shadows every turbulent chapter coming next. Judges opens immediately with heartbreak: once Joshua and his elders die, a new generation arises who "knew neither the LORD nor the work He had done" and plunges straight into unfaithfulness (Judges 2:7–10). Only through Jeremiah does God untie the knot of 24:19 with the New Covenant promise: God Himself will write His law within His people's hearts, giving them a new heart truly able to love and obey Him (Jeremiah 31:31–33). Joshua pressing Israel to think carefully before swearing also echoes Jesus urging would-be disciples to sit down and count the cost first (Luke 14:27–28).

**From *Moses' aide* to *the servant of the LORD*.** At 1:1, Moses bore the noble title "the servant of the LORD," while Joshua was simply "Moses' aide" (1:1). Across an entire life of steadfast faithfulness, claiming his own modest hill town dead last (19:49–50), Joshua earns that highest crown at death in 24:29: "the servant of the LORD." Remarkably too, he lives exactly one hundred ten years—matching Joseph's exact lifespan (Genesis 50:26), the classic ancient Egyptian emblem of a complete, richly blessed life.

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapter 24 mirrors the structure of an ancient Near Eastern suzerain treaty.** Old Testament scholars note that the Shechem renewal follows every classical stage of an ancient Great King–vassal treaty: royal preamble identifying the sovereign (24:2), historical prologue recounting past royal favors (24:2–13), basic stipulation demanding exclusive loyalty (24:14–15), solemn vassal oath and sanctions for treachery (24:16–24), and written deposit with standing witness (24:25–27). Seen in that light, God's address here is no casual reminiscence, but a binding national charter.

**Was *the hornet* in 24:12 literal stinging insects or vivid metaphor?** God sending "the hornet" ahead of Israel also appears in Exodus 23:28 and Deuteronomy 7:20. Some take it literally as swarms of hornets disrupting enemy armies. Most interpreters read it as a striking metaphor for dread and panic God spread among Canaanites breaking their morale—while some historians link the hornet motif to ancient Egypt's royal bee emblem, recalling earlier Egyptian campaigns that weakened Canaanite city-states. Either way, verse 12's core point stays unmistakable: God won the victory, not Israel's swords or bows.

**Why does 24:19 say God *will not forgive your rebellion or your sins*?** Pulled out of context, that line could wrongly imply God never forgives. Read straight through 24:19–20, Joshua is clearly warning against willful, persistent apostasy—turning back to foreign gods while presuming God will shrug it off. Far from denying God's mercy toward repentant sinners, Joshua shatters glib self-confidence imagining anyone can serve God while hiding idols in the back room.

**Who wrote the book's final five verses recording the deaths of Joshua and Eleazar (24:29–33)?** Obviously Joshua did not narrate his own funeral or Israel's faithfulness under elders who outlived him. Just like Deuteronomy's closing account of Moses' death, these verses indicate a later inspired chronicler completed the scroll building upon Joshua's own written records (24:26)—perhaps Phinehas, priests, or subsequent Israelite historians. Notice too how verse 24:13's gift of land not labored for, cities not built, and vineyards not planted echoes Moses' promise verbatim (Deuteronomy 6:10–11).

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer—who finished his Joshua commentary just before dying in 1564—probes deeply into Joshua's startling line in 24:19 ("You are not able to serve the LORD"). Far from driving Israel into despair or telling them to quit, Joshua aimed to crush shallow self-confidence so they grasped no mortal can keep covenant with a holy God without casting out idols and leaning completely on God's grace. **Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, holds up 24:15 as the finest motto for family religion: godly individuals cannot force the whole world's decision, yet they can decide decisively for themselves and those under their roof.
