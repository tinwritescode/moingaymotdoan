# Joshua 4 — Twelve Memorial Stones

## Story

All Israel has reached the western bank safely, yet the Jordan crossing is not quite finished. The LORD directs Joshua to select twelve men, one from each tribe, to walk back into the middle of the riverbed—right where the priests' feet stand firmly planted—lift twelve stones onto their shoulders, and carry them to the spot where Israel will camp that night (4:1–5).

These stones are no idle pile. They will stand as an enduring memorial among God's people: "In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?'" parents will tell them how the Jordan's waters were cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD as it crossed. The twelve men obey, each hoisting a stone representing his tribe to the camp. In addition, Joshua himself sets up a second stack of twelve stones right in the middle of the riverbed where the priests stood (4:6–9).

All this time, the priests bearing the ark stand motionless midstream until every command the LORD gave Joshua is carried out. Meanwhile "the people hurried across"—not in panic, but in eager obedience beneath the towering wall of held-back water upstream. Roughly forty thousand armed warriors from Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh keep their solemn oath, marching in the vanguard before the LORD onto the plains of Jericho. On that day the LORD exalts Joshua in the eyes of all Israel, and they revere him all the days of his life just as they had revered Moses (4:10–14).

At last the LORD tells Joshua to call the priests carrying the ark of the testimony up out of the Jordan. The instant the soles of their feet step onto dry ground on the bank, the Jordan's waters surge back into place and overflow both banks as before (4:15–18).

The date is the tenth day of the first month. Israel pitches camp at Gilgal on Jericho's eastern border. There Joshua erects the twelve stones pulled from the riverbed and instructs Israel to tell future generations: Israel crossed the Jordan on dry ground because the LORD your God dried up the river before you, just as He dried up the Red Sea long ago. The miracle reaches far beyond Israel alone—"so that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the LORD is mighty, and so that you may always fear the LORD your God" (4:19–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Human hearts forget quickly, so we need tangible reminders.** Having just stepped onto hostile soil, the very first task God assigns is neither digging trenches nor building ramparts, but carrying twelve stones to raise a memorial (4:2–7). Faith withers under amnesia; it thrives on visible markers and anniversary memories keeping gratitude warm for what God has done.

**Faith never passes automatically to the next generation—it travels through children's questions.** Twice in this chapter, Scripture paints an ordinary family scene: curious kids pointing at stones beside the road and asking, "What do these stones mean?" (4:6; 4:21). Parents must never brush off children's curiosity, but turn every question into an opportunity to recount God's faithfulness.

**No tribe is left out of God's story.** Twelve stones represent all twelve tribes—including the two and a half tribes who already held eastern land yet still sent forty thousand fighters across beside their brothers (4:5; 4:12–13). A healthy community ensures every member has a name, a stake in shared memory, and a share in common duty.

**God's mighty deeds for His people always aim at a global horizon.** The closing verse throws open the windows to the entire world: the Jordan miracle happened "so that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the LORD is mighty" (4:24). God never chose Israel to hoard grace inside tiny borders, but so that through them every nation might know the true God.

## Deeper Meaning

**The tenth day of the first month—forty years to the day since choosing the Passover lamb.** The date note in 4:19 reads like a simple calendar marker: Israel came up from the Jordan on "the tenth day of the first month." Turn back to Exodus, however, and that is the exact date God commanded every Israelite family to set apart a lamb for the very first Passover in Egypt (Exodus 12:3). Forty years later to the exact day, the arc of redemption comes full circle: from preparing the Passover lamb out of slavery to setting foot on the Promised Land.

**Passing on faith through children's questions.** The question "What do these stones mean?" (4:6; 4:21) directly continues Israel's family storytelling tradition launched on Passover night (Exodus 12:26–27) and reaffirmed by Moses in Deuteronomy (Deuteronomy 6:20–21). Across all eras, Scripture treats the family table and parents telling children God's story as the premier classroom of faith.

**Memorial stones across biblical history.** Generations after Joshua, the prophet Samuel set up a stone named Ebenezer, declaring, "Thus far the LORD has helped us" (1 Samuel 7:12). In the New Testament, preaching right beside the Jordan—perhaps not far from Gilgal's stone cairn—John the Baptist warned proud crowds that "out of these stones God can raise up children for Abraham" (Matthew 3:9). And Jesus Himself gave His church a memorial far more living than stone: broken bread and the cup of wine in the Lord's Supper, saying, "Do this in remembrance of Me" (Luke 22:19).

**A mission to make God's mighty hand known among all nations.** The closing vision of 4:24—that all peoples on earth may know the LORD's mighty hand—is echoed almost verbatim centuries later in King Solomon's temple dedication prayer, asking God to hear foreigners coming from distant lands to pray to His name (1 Kings 8:41–43). God's heart in the Old Testament always beat wide open for all nations.

## Notes & Nuances

**Were there two piles of stones or just one?** In 4:8 and 4:20, twelve men carry twelve stones from mid-river to set up at Gilgal. Yet 4:9 says, "Joshua also set up twelve stones in the middle of the Jordan" where the priests' feet had stood. Many commentators read the text straight: two separate memorials—one visible on shore at Gilgal for all to see, and another right in the riverbed (glimpsed during low-water seasons, or standing as a quiet submerged witness). Other scholars translate 4:9 as referring back to the single stack lifted *from* the middle of the river; still, the most natural reading of the Hebrew points toward two distinct monuments.

**The phrase *to this day* in 4:9.** This comment indicates that Joshua was compiled or edited sometime after the crossing while the memorial stones still stood visibly for contemporary readers to verify. The same phrase recurs repeatedly across the book of Joshua (5:9; 6:25; 7:26).

**Why forty thousand warriors in 4:13?** The census near the end of Numbers counted over one hundred thirty thousand fighting-age men across Reuben, Gad, and all Manasseh (Numbers 26:7; 26:18; 26:34)—far more than forty thousand even counting half of Manasseh. Verse 4:13 makes complete practical sense: the eastern tribes did not strip their homesteads bare, but dispatched a strong vanguard of around forty thousand soldiers while leaving the remainder behind to protect wives, children, herds, and their newly settled borders.

**Why does 4:23 say the Red Sea dried up *before us*?** By now almost everyone old enough to remember crossing the Red Sea forty years earlier had died in the wilderness, apart from Joshua, Caleb, and those who had been young children. Yet Joshua deliberately says *before us* to affirm that God's covenant people form one unbroken stream: what God did for earlier generations remains the living salvation story of today's generation.

**Origen**, third-century Christian scholar in Alexandria, interprets the twelve stones lifted out of the Jordan's depths as a prophetic image of Christ's twelve apostles. Just as those stones were drawn from beneath river waters and raised high as perpetual witnesses for God's people, the twelve apostles rise out of baptism to become living foundation stones testifying to Christ's saving power across generations. *(Homilies on Joshua, Homilies 4 and 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading Joshua 4's twelve stones as symbols of the twelve apostles is famously developed in Origen's fourth and fifth homilies on Joshua -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, explains why God ordered lifeless stones stacked as a monument: human memory is notoriously ungrateful and quick to let God's mercies slip away, so God stoops down to give visible, tangible signs awakening gratitude across generations.
