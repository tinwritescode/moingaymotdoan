# Joshua 13 — Land Yet to Be Taken and the Eastern Inheritance

## Story

The second half of Joshua opens with a gentle yet candid reminder from God. Many years of campaigning have passed, "Joshua was old and advanced in years," and the LORD tells him: "You are old and advanced in years, and very much of the land remains to be possessed." Although major Canaanite alliances have been shattered, sweeping territories still lie outside Israel's hold: in the southwest, the entire coastal plain of the five Philistine lords (Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Gath, and Ekron) alongside the Avvim and Geshurites; in the north, Canaanite, Sidonian, and Gebalite lands stretching across Mount Lebanon from Mount Hermon to Lebo-hamath. Yet God adds a firm promise: "I Myself will drive them out before the Israelites." Joshua's urgent mission now is no longer leading charges in battle, but dividing the entire western land by lot among the nine tribes and the half-tribe of Manasseh (13:1–7).

Before casting lots for the west, the text formally confirms the eastern Transjordan inheritance Moses already granted to two and a half tribes: Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh. Their territory spans the former kingdom of Amorite King Sihon of Heshbon and the entire realm of King Og of Bashan—"the last of the Rephaim." Right inside this triumphant confirmation, however, sounds a quiet warning note: the Israelites failed to drive out the Geshurites and Maacathites, who continued living among them. Alongside that comes the striking exception for Levi: Moses allotted them no territorial land, because the offerings made by fire to the LORD, the God of Israel, are their inheritance (13:8–14).

Next, each eastern tribe's boundaries are recorded with the precision of an official land deed. Furthest south lies Reuben's inheritance, running from Aroer on the rim of the Arnon Gorge across the Medeba plateau through Heshbon, Dibon, Beth-peor, and Mount Pisgah's slopes to Beth-jeshimoth, bounded west by the Jordan. Mid-catalogue, the narrator recalls Moses defeating the Midianite chieftains allied with Sihon and inserts one striking name among those slain by the sword: "Balaam son of Beor, who practiced divination" (13:15–23).

Directly north of Reuben stretches Gad's territory—Jazer, the towns of Gilead, half the Ammonite region up to Aroer facing Rabbah, running from Heshbon through Mahanaim and covering the eastern Jordan Valley (Beth-haram, Beth-nimrah, Succoth, Zaphon) right up to the southern tip of the Sea of Kinnereth (13:24–28).

Farthest north east of the river lies the inheritance of the half-tribe of Manasseh (descendants of Machir), sweeping from Mahanaim across all King Og's former realm of Bashan—including sixty towns of Havvoth-jair, half of Gilead, and the two major centers of Ashtaroth and Edrei. Closing the record of lands Moses distributed on the plains of Moab, the narrator repeats the exception for Levi even more profoundly: Moses gave them no land inheritance, "for the LORD, the God of Israel, is their inheritance, as He promised them" (13:29–33).

## Key Takeaways

**Human leaders grow old, yet God's work never retires with age.** Chapter 13's opening verse never hides the limits of Israel's greatest living hero: "You are old and advanced in years, and very much of the land remains to be possessed" (13:1). No single person finishes everything God assigns across generations. Once battle vigor fades, Joshua steps into a quieter, equally lasting task: setting borders and handing the torch to those coming after him.

**Allotting unconquered land is an act of pure faith.** God commands Joshua to divide Philistine coasts and northern Sidonian valleys by lot while enemy garrisons still occupy them (13:2–7). That is never wishful fantasy; it stands squarely on verse 13:6: "I Myself will drive them out before the Israelites." Faith counts God's spoken promise as solid as title already in hand.

**A tiny crack of compromise can ripple across generations.** Verse 13:13 sounds for the very first time in Joshua a somber refrain: "But the Israelites did not drive out..." Although most of Transjordan lay securely in Israel's grasp, two small pockets—Geshur and Maacah—remained untouched. That refrain *did not drive out* grows louder across upcoming chapters, heralding the dark spiral of Judges.

**The richest inheritance is never acreage—it is God Himself.** Right inside a chapter packed with boundary lines, towns, and villages, the narrator intentionally bookends the text with Levi's unique share (13:14; 13:33). Owning no farmland looks like deprivation at first glance, yet Scripture turns human values upside down: holding the LORD Himself as your inheritance surpasses every estate on earth.

## Deeper Meaning

**The LORD as His people's inheritance stretches from Old Testament to New.** Verses 13:14 and 13:33 reach directly back to God's wilderness promise to Aaron and Levi: "I am your portion and your inheritance among the Israelites" (Numbers 18:20; Deuteronomy 10:8–9). Over time, that truth expanded beyond one priestly tribe into the confession of every faithful heart: King David sang, "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup" (Psalms 16:5–6). In the New Testament, Peter proclaims that through Christ's resurrection every believer is born again into "an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, reserved in heaven" (1 Peter 1:3–4).

**Why does Balaam the diviner pop up abruptly inside Reuben's border list?** Right amid lists of towns, 13:22 pauses to recall Israel slaying "Balaam son of Beor, who practiced divination" (Numbers 31:8). Glance just two verses earlier and there sits Beth-peor (13:20)—the exact site where Balaam engineered the snare using Moabite and Midianite women to drag Israel into immorality and idolatry! Setting Balaam's death right beside Beth-peor whispers a sharp warning: the deadliest threat able to strip Israel of its land was never Sihon's or Og's armies, but seductive entanglement with neighboring idols.

**Unconquered territories become the testing ground for the next generation.** The very roster of remaining peoples in 13:2–6—five Philistine lords, Canaanites, Sidonians, and Lebanon's hills—reappears almost word for word in Judges. There Scripture tells us the LORD left those nations specifically to test the rising generation that had never known Joshua's wars, seeing whether they would obey His commands (Judges 3:1–4).

**Bitter fruit from leaving Geshur untouched.** Verse 13:13 notes quietly that Israel failed to expel the Geshurites. Centuries later, that little surviving kingdom on the northeast border walked straight into David's household: David married Maacah, daughter of the king of Geshur, who bore Absalom (2 Samuel 3:3)—the very son who later murdered his half-brother, fled to Geshur for refuge, and returned to ignite a bloody rebellion that nearly tore Israel apart.

## Notes & Nuances

**How old was Joshua here, and why did so much land remain?** Although 13:1 gives no exact age, comparing Caleb's age of eighty-five at the start of allotment (14:10) and Joshua's death at one hundred ten (24:29), commentators generally place Joshua in his eighties or nineties. The campaigns of chapters 6–11 broke the military spine of Canaanite alliances, yet a newly arrived people simply lacked population numbers to occupy every village immediately from Egypt's border to Mount Lebanon. God therefore shifted strategy: assigning specific regions to each tribe so families could settle, farm, and gradually clear their own allotments.

**Why nine and a half tribes in the west alongside two and a half in the east?** Counting Jacob's twelve sons sometimes confuses new readers wondering where the half-tribes came from. The math stays neat: Levi received no territorial block so they could devote themselves to sanctuary ministry (13:14; 13:33), while Joseph received a double share through his two sons Ephraim and Manasseh—keeping exactly twelve land portions! Manasseh itself split into two halves: the eastern half settled Bashan and Gilead beside Reuben and Gad (Numbers 32:33), while the western half joined the other nine tribes west of the Jordan (13:7–8).

**Two statements about Levi's inheritance complement each other.** Verse 13:14 calls "the offerings made by fire to the LORD" Levi's share, while 13:33 declares directly, "the LORD, the God of Israel, is their inheritance." Far from conflicting, 13:33 names the spiritual reality—God Himself is their treasure—while 13:14 explains the practical daily provision through which God sustained their households from the sanctuary offerings.

**Who are the five Midianite princes named in 13:21?** Verse 13:21 lists Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba—the very same five Midianite kings defeated during Moses' final campaign east of the Jordan (Numbers 31:8). Calling them *princes of Sihon* clarifies that these Midianite chieftains governed as vassal allies under Amorite King Sihon across Moab's borderlands.

No matter how long we have followed Christ or how many victories lie behind us, vast regions inside our hearts still await full surrender to Him.
