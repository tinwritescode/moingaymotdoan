# Joshua 11 — The Northern Campaign and Rest for the Land

## Story

After the southern five-king alliance shatters, word of Israel's advance rockets into northern Canaan. Presiding over the north is Jabin, king of Hazor—the largest fortress in the region, historically head of all those northern kingdoms. At once Jabin sends messengers assembling an unprecedented coalition: the kings of Madon, Shimron, and Achshaph, along with rulers across the northern hill country, the Arabah south of Kinnereth, the western lowlands, the heights of Dor by the sea, and Canaanites, Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Jebusites, and Hivites living below Mount Hermon. Together they field a staggering force: "a huge army, as numerous as the sand on the seashore, along with a great number of horses and chariots." They mass their forces by the Waters of Merom, poised to crush Israel in one decisive blow (11:1–5).

Facing overwhelming troop numbers and cutting-edge chariot warfare, Joshua hears the LORD's calm word: "Do not be afraid of them, for by this time tomorrow I will deliver all of them slain before Israel. You are to hamstring their horses and burn their chariots." Without losing a moment, Joshua and all his fighting men march fast and launch a sudden surprise assault on the northern coalition right beside the Waters of Merom. The LORD delivers the enemy into Israel's hands; Israel chases the routed armies all the way to Great Sidon, Misrephoth-maim, and the Valley of Mizpah to the east, leaving no survivors. Once battle ends, Joshua carries out God's order to the letter: he hamstrings every captured warhorse and burns every enemy chariot in fire (11:6–9).

Turning back, Joshua strikes Hazor directly, puts its king to the sword, destroys everyone inside, and puts the mighty northern capital to the torch. He captures the other northern alliance cities as well, yet Scripture highlights a deliberate restraint: "Israel did not burn any of the cities built on their mounds, except Hazor, which Joshua burned." The people take the goods and livestock as lawful plunder. The author underscores Joshua's meticulous fidelity: of everything the LORD commanded Moses and Moses charged Joshua, "Joshua left nothing undone" (11:10–15).

From here the camera pulls back to summarize the entire conquest stretching south to north—from Mount Halak rising toward Seir in the far south up to Baal-gad in the Valley of Lebanon beneath Mount Hermon in the far north. The narrator inserts a vital realistic note: "Joshua waged war against all these kings for a long time." Across the whole land, apart from the Hivites of Gibeon, not a single city made peace with Israel; every one was taken in battle. Scripture explains the deeper mystery behind their stubbornness: the LORD hardened their hearts to come out and fight Israel so that His ordained judgment commanded through Moses might be fully carried out (11:16–20).

Before concluding the conquest half of the book, the narrator records one specially resonant achievement: Joshua enters the hill country—Hebron, Debir, Anab, and all the hills of Judah and Israel—and cuts off the Anakim, the giant lineage that had terrified Israel's earlier generation forty years prior. No Anakim remain anywhere in Israelite territory, surviving only in three coastal Philistine cities: Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod. Joshua takes the entire land exactly as the LORD promised Moses, ready to divide among the tribes. The closing line brings the first half of Joshua to a peaceful exhale: "Then the land had rest from war" (11:21–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Superior numbers and advanced weaponry cannot withstand God's power.** Jabin's coalition combined every terror of ancient warfare: troops "as numerous as the sand on the seashore" backed by massed chariots and warhorses (11:4). Israel fought strictly on foot. Yet in one sudden strike right on God's timetable, the whole coalition collapsed (11:6–8). Every dread melts before one promise from God: "Do not be afraid."

**God ordered horses hamstrung and chariots burned to keep His people from trusting military hardware.** By earthly military logic, capturing hundreds of prime warhorses and bronze-trimmed chariots was a priceless windfall to build an unbeatable army. God ordered Joshua to destroy them all (11:6; 11:9). He knew how swiftly human hearts shift trust: once boasting powerful chariots, Israel would quickly forget the One who stopped the Jordan and dropped Jericho's walls.

**Obedience is not just one heroic burst, but faithfulness over *a long time*.** Reading chapters 6 through 11 in one sitting can make the conquest feel effortless and overnight. Verse 11:18 brings us back to reality: Joshua fought "for a long time"—campaign after campaign, year after year. What shines brightest in Joshua is not a handful of flashy triumphs, but the quiet epitaph of 11:15: he faithfully carried out every word God gave Moses, leaving nothing undone.

**War is never the goal—peace is the destination.** The trumpet blasts and clashing bronze of Joshua's first half end in one quiet sentence: "Then the land had rest from war" (11:23). Every mile from Egypt through desert trials into Canaan aimed toward one destination: God's people dwelling safely in their inheritance in lasting peace.

## Deeper Meaning

**Burning chariots and refusing trust in earthly force.** The remarkable order of 11:6 ties directly back to Moses' law for Israel's future king: "he must not acquire many horses for himself" (Deuteronomy 17:16). Centuries later, King David forged that principle into timeless worship: "Some trust in chariots and others in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (Psalms 20:7). Across Old and New Testaments alike, God's kingdom never rises by worldly military might.

**From terrified *grasshoppers* to conquering the Anakim.** Verses 11:21–22 heal a national wound open for over forty years. Back at Kadesh-barnea, ten spies shattered Israel's morale because "the descendants of Anak" loomed so huge the spies felt "like grasshoppers" beside them (Numbers 13:28–33)—dooming an entire generation to die in the wilderness. Now Joshua himself—one of only two men back then who trusted God—leads the new generation in clearing the Anakim from the highlands. Faith's tallest giants fall only when we march straight toward them holding God's promise.

**Remnants left in Gath cast the shadow of Goliath.** Verse 11:22 notes an almost throwaway detail: Anakim lingered only in "Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod." Alert Bible readers catch their breath at the name *Gath*: over three centuries later, out of Philistine Gath strode a towering champion named Goliath, defying Israel's whole army until young shepherd David dropped him in the name of the LORD (1 Samuel 17:4). Unfinished corners left behind today sometimes grow into tomorrow's fiercest trials.

**Unburned cities fulfill God's gracious promise.** Sparing the mound-built towns—burning only three symbolic strongholds, Jericho, Ai, and northern capital Hazor (11:13)—literally fulfilled Moses' promise that Israel would inherit "large, flourishing cities you did not build," houses brimming with good things, wells, and vineyards they did not plant (Deuteronomy 6:10–11). The Promised Land stood as a ready gift of grace, not scorched ash.

**Hazor's ghost returns when a later generation forgets God.** Joshua burned Hazor down (11:11), yet the story didn't stop there. In Judges, once Israel again did evil before the LORD, another Canaanite ruler bearing the title Jabin rose right back up at Hazor with nine hundred iron chariots, oppressing Israel for twenty years until God raised Deborah and Barak to deliver them (Judges 4:2–3). Yesterday's victories never replace today's faithfulness.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 11:20 (*it was the LORD's doing to harden their hearts*) raises a deep theological question.** Readers rightly pause: if God hardened their hearts against seeking peace, how could they be held guilty? Commentators consistently read this alongside Pharaoh in Egypt (Exodus 9:12; cf. Romans 9:18). In Exodus, Pharaoh repeatedly hardened his own heart before Scripture speaks of God hardening him; likewise in Joshua, the Canaanite kings willingly forged war alliances against Israel throughout chapters 9, 10, and 11 (9:1–2; 10:3–5; 11:1–5). Most interpreters therefore understand judicial hardening not as forcing innocent people into evil, but as God withdrawing restraining grace once wickedness is ripe and confirmed, handing stubborn rebels over to the very defiance they chose.

**Why hamstring warhorses (11:6; 11:9)?** To modern ears this sounds cruel toward animals. In ancient warfare, severing a warhorse's rear leg tendon did not kill the horse, but permanently ended its usefulness pulling war chariots. For an infantry army barred by Mosaic law from building a cavalry force (Deuteronomy 17:16) and unable to care for thousands of captured warhorses, disabling enemy warhorses and torching chariots was an urgent tactical necessity preventing foes from ever reusing their deadliest weapon.

**How does 11:23 (*Joshua took the entire land... the land had rest*) fit with 13:1 (*very much of the land remains to be possessed*)?** Understanding this unlocks the entire book of Joshua. Verse 11:18 notes the war lasted *a long time*—calculated from Caleb's timeline (14:7–10) at roughly five to seven years. Verse 11:23 summarizes *strategic conquest*: every major central, southern, and northern coalition was shattered and key fortresses broken, leaving no organized army threatening Israel's survival. Localized clearing and full settlement of remaining Canaanite pockets—detailed from chapter 13 onward—was then handed to individual tribes after land allotment.

**What has archaeology uncovered at Hazor?** Ancient Hazor (Tel Hazor, north of the Sea of Galilee) is one of Israel's grandest archaeological sites, covering roughly two hundred acres—many times bigger than typical Canaanite cities, fully matching Scripture's description as "the head of all those kingdoms" (11:10). Extensive excavations led by Yigael Yadin and Amnon Ben-Tor uncovered a thick destruction burn layer wiping out Hazor's royal palace and pagan shrines in the thirteenth century BC, with Canaanite and Egyptian cult statues deliberately smashed or decapitated. This resonates remarkably with Hazor being the sole northern city Joshua burned (11:13), though archaeologists still debate fifteenth- versus thirteenth-century dates and the exact identity of its destroyers.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, faces 11:20 head-on without dodging God's hardening of the Canaanite kings. Carefully holding together human guilt and righteous divine sovereignty, he notes the kings eagerly marched to war out of their own pride and hatred, while God—judging their ripened wickedness—withdrew restraining light and let their chosen blindness carry them to ruin.
