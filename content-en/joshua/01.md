# Joshua 1 — The Successor Steps Across the River

## Story

Moses has died in the land of Moab, and thirty days of mourning for him have just ended (cf. Deuteronomy 34:5–8). Into that immense void, the LORD speaks to Joshua son of Nun—the man who for forty years stood quietly in the background, titled simply "Moses' aide." God voices the stark truth still stunning the camp: "Moses My servant is dead." Yet the journey does not halt. Joshua and all Israel must rise, cross the Jordan River, and enter the land the LORD is giving them (1:1–2).

The LORD spreads His promise wide before the new leader: "I have given you every place where the sole of your foot will tread," exactly as He promised Moses. Their territory will stretch from the wilderness and Lebanon to the great Euphrates River, encompassing all the Hittite country out to the Great Sea in the west. No one will be able to stand against Joshua all the days of his life. God anchors all of this in an unshakable pledge: "As I was with Moses, so I will be with you; I will never leave you nor forsake you" (1:3–5).

Three times the LORD charges Joshua, "Be strong and courageous." First, because he will lead this people to inherit the land God swore to their ancestors (1:6). Second, that courage binds tightly to complete devotion to God's word: he must carefully obey the entire law Moses gave, never turning to the right or to the left, so he may succeed wherever he goes. "This Book of the Law must not depart from your mouth; meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do everything written in it. For then you will prosper and succeed in all you do" (1:7–8). Third, God seals the charge with His own presence: "Be strong and courageous; do not be afraid or discouraged, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go" (1:9).

Joshua swings into action at once. He instructs the people's officers to walk through the camp telling every household to prepare provisions, because within three days they will cross the Jordan to take possession of the land the LORD their God is giving them (1:10–11).

Next, Joshua speaks specifically to the Reubenites, Gadites, and the half-tribe of Manasseh. Earlier, Moses had allotted them territory east of the Jordan where they could settle in rest. Now Joshua reminds them of their solemn pledge: their wives, children, and herds may stay behind in that land, yet all their able fighting men must cross over armed ahead of their brothers, fighting beside them until the LORD grants rest and possession to the other tribes as well. Only then may they return to enjoy their own land east of the river (1:12–15).

The two and a half tribes answer without hesitation. They promise to do everything Joshua commands and go wherever he sends them, obeying him fully just as they obeyed Moses. They pray that the LORD will be with him as He was with Moses, declaring that anyone who rebels against his command deserves death. Finally, the people themselves echo God's exact charge back to their new commander: "Only be strong and courageous!" (1:16–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Human leaders pass away, but God's promises never die with them.** For forty years Israel had grown used to seeing Moses walk ahead on every stage of the road. His death could easily have left the nation adrift and demoralized. The brisk opening line—"Moses My servant is dead"—cuts cleanly through grief to remind them their true anchor was never a great man, but the living God (1:2).

**True courage grows not from natural toughness, but from knowing God is with us.** Four times in one brief chapter Joshua is told to "be strong and courageous" (1:6; 1:7; 1:9; 1:18). Had he felt fearless by nature, God would never have needed to say it so often. Nor does God pep-talk him by saying *you are talented enough*; He gives the sole promise sturdy enough to steady a heart before Canaan's fortified cities: "I will be with you" (1:5; 1:9).

**A leader's real strength lies in living inside God's word.** Facing an unprecedented military campaign, anyone might expect God to brief Joshua on tactics, terrain, or weaponry. Instead, God's deepest charge is to read, meditate on, and obey the Book of the Law (1:7–8). In Scripture, victory or ruin hangs not on human strategy, but on a heart refusing to drift from God's way.

**Our own rest is incomplete while our brothers are still fighting.** The two and a half tribes already held secure pastures east of the Jordan, with families and herds safely settled (1:13–14). They could easily have cited domestic duties to sit out the battles across the river. Yet Israel is one body; those already at rest are called to march on the front lines until every brother shares that same rest (1:14–15).

## Deeper Meaning

**Joshua's name and Jesus' name are one and the same.** Originally named Hoshea, he was renamed *Joshua* by Moses—meaning *the LORD is salvation* (Numbers 13:16). Translated from Hebrew into New Testament Greek, that very name becomes *Jesus*. It is no coincidence that Moses—embodying the law—could bring Israel only to the river's edge, while Joshua led them across into their inheritance. The story sketches the Savior beforehand: the law points the way but cannot save us; Jesus Himself brings God's people into the promise.

**The *rest* in Canaan was only a waypoint pointing to a greater rest.** Twice Joshua speaks of the day the LORD grants the tribes *rest* in the new land (1:13; 1:15). Centuries later, the Letter to the Hebrews looks straight back at Joshua's day and writes plainly: "For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken later about another day. There remains, then, a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:8–9). The land across the Jordan was precious, yet it remained a shadow of eternal rest in Christ.

**Direct echoes of Psalms 1 and the law of the king.** God's charge to Joshua to meditate on the Book of the Law day and night so his path prospers (1:7–8) resonates almost word for word in the opening psalm, where the righteous delight in God's law day and night like a tree planted by flowing streams (Psalms 1:1–3). The warning neither to turn "to the right or to the left" also links directly to Moses' law for Israel's future king, requiring him to copy and read the law throughout his life (Deuteronomy 17:18–20). Under God's reign, no leader stands above the law; every leader stands beneath God's word.

**The promise *never to leave or forsake* runs from the Jordan straight into the New Testament.** First spoken through Moses (Deuteronomy 31:6–8), the pledge is spoken directly to Joshua on the eve of crossing (1:5). In the New Testament, Hebrews hands that exact promise to ordinary Christians worried about money and daily needs, encouraging contentment because God has said, "Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5). And before ascending, Jesus sends His disciples to all nations with the same guarantee: "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (Matthew 28:18–20).

**A lifetime of faithfulness earns one quiet title.** In the book's opening verse, Moses is called "the servant of the LORD," while Joshua is simply "Moses' aide" (1:1). Only after walking faithfully through battles, land allotments, and decades of steady trust until his death at age one hundred ten does Scripture award Joshua that highest title: "the servant of the LORD" (24:29). In God's kingdom, honor is proven at the finish line, not handed out at the starting gate.

## Notes & Nuances

***Meditate* in 1:8 means more than silent mental reflection.** The Hebrew verb rendered *meditate* literally suggests *murmuring* or *speaking softly aloud*, reflecting how ancient readers read scrolls by voicing the words on their lips so their own ears heard them. That connects both halves of 1:8 seamlessly: "must not depart from your mouth" and "meditate on it day and night." God's word is not meant to sit closed on a shelf, but to stay continually on our lips and shape our daily thinking.

***Prosper and succeed* in 1:7–8 is not a guarantee of material wealth.** People often pull 1:8 out of context as a formula for financial riches or effortless ease. Read in context, God is speaking to a leader charged with guiding an entire nation in His mission. The Hebrew terms emphasize *acting wisely* and *fulfilling the path God assigns*, rather than guaranteeing a trouble-free life or overflowing bank accounts.

**The *within three days* timeline (1:11) and the chronology of the opening chapters.** In 1:11 Joshua orders Israel to prepare to cross in three days. Yet in chapter 2 he sends two spies to Jericho who hide in the hills for three days (2:1), and only in chapter 3 do we read, "After three days the officers went through the camp" (3:1–2). Many commentators understand chapter 2 as a flashback describing events that began before the order of 1:10–11, or note that Joshua dispatched the spies secretly before ordering provisions. Hebrew historical narrative regularly arranges episodes thematically rather than ticking off a strict hourly clock.

**The Promised Land is simultaneously a free gift and a courageous step.** On one hand, God declares He has already given the land exactly as sworn to Abraham and Moses (1:2–4; cf. Genesis 15:18; Deuteronomy 11:24). On the other hand, He still says, "every place where the sole of your foot will tread," and Joshua tells the people to cross over "to take possession" (1:3; 1:11). Divine grace never invites passive folded hands; rather, the certainty of God's promise emboldens believers to step forward.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, third-century Christian scholar in Alexandria, opens his homilies on Joshua by pausing immediately at the shared name of Joshua and Jesus. Only after "Moses My servant is dead"—symbolizing the old law completing its preparatory role—does Joshua bring the people across the river into the Promised Land. To Origen, this teaches that the law cannot lead humanity into perfection; Jesus alone, bearing that very name, ushers His people into their true inheritance. *(Homilies on Joshua, Homily 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the typological focus on the shared name Joshua/Jesus and Moses' death is the celebrated opening centerpiece of Origen's first homily on Joshua -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, finished his commentary on Joshua shortly before his death. God's repeated command to "be strong and courageous," he notes, shows how readily fear resurfaces in human hearts—even in a veteran who walked through the wilderness beside Moses. Commenting on 1:7–8, Calvin stresses that even the highest magistrate may never govern by personal whim, but stands wholly under the Book of the Law of God. *(Commentary on the Book of Joshua, on chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin completed his Joshua commentary near his death in 1564, and both insights—addressing recurring fear and binding leaders under Scripture—stand prominently in his notes on chapter 1 -->

**Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, draws deep comfort from the opening line "Moses My servant is dead": though God's most useful servants depart, God Himself lives on and always raises successors to carry forward His work. **Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, emphasizes in recounting this transition that Joshua was no sudden outsider. **Dale Ralph Davis**, American Old Testament scholar, observes incisively how sharply God's counsel in chapter 1 contradicts worldly manuals of success. Standing before Canaan's fortified walls, God hands Joshua not a military manual, but a Book of the Law to read and obey.
