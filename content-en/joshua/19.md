# Joshua 19 — The Remaining Six Tribes and Joshua's Own Inheritance

## Story

Lot-casting at Shiloh moves through the final six tribes. Following Benjamin in chapter 18, the second lot falls to Simeon. Uniquely, Simeon receives no separate outer perimeter of its own, but inherits enclaves nestled squarely inside Judah's sprawling southern territory—seventeen towns including Beersheba, Hormah, Ziklag, and Rimmon. The text states the reason simply: "because Judah's portion was too large for them," Simeon's share is carved straight from Judah's excess (19:1–9).

Lots three through six shift north into lush Galilee. The third lot goes to Zebulun across rolling hills and valleys west of Mount Tabor, counting twelve towns including Gath-hepher and a northern Bethlehem (19:10–16). The fourth lot falls to Issachar, embracing the fertile Valley of Jezreel all the way east to the Jordan, with sixteen towns including Jezreel, Shunem, and land around Mount Tabor (19:17–23).

The fifth lot assigns Asher the rich northwestern coastal strip running from Mount Carmel north alongside the renowned ancient seaports of Sidon and fortified Tyre, totaling twenty-two towns with their surrounding villages (19:24–31). The sixth lot grants Naphtali the highlands of Upper Galilee down along the western shore of the Sea of Kinnereth to the Jordan's headwaters, encompassing nineteen fortified towns including Kinnereth, Hazor, and Kedesh (19:32–39).

The seventh and final tribal lot goes to Dan. Initially Dan receives a western coastal territory squeezed between Judah and Ephraim—towns such as Zorah, Eshtaol, Aijalon, and land facing the port of Joppa. Yet that allotment slips away as Dan fails to hold ground against local resistance. Consequently, the Danites march far north, "went up and fought against Leshem, captured it, and struck it with the sword," settling there and renaming the town Dan after their forefather (19:40–48).

Only after every single tribe has received its territory does the narrative give three quiet closing verses to the aging commander who led Israel through every mile of war. Only now does Israel set aside a personal inheritance for Joshua son of Nun. At the LORD's command, "they gave him the city he requested—Timnath-serah in the hill country of Ephraim. He rebuilt the city and settled in it" (19:49–50).

Chapter 19 closes with a solemn official seal: Eleazar the priest, Joshua, and the tribal heads completed dividing the land by lot before the LORD at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting in Shiloh. "So they finished dividing the land" (19:51).

## Key Takeaways

**True fairness means those with plenty gladly share with those in need.** Once the national survey finished, everyone saw Judah's initial grant exceeded its need. Without bickering or hoarding, part of Judah's excess was peacefully reassigned to Simeon (19:9). Inside God's family, making sure every brother has solid ground outweighs clinging to extra turf.

**Every tribe—large or tiny—holds a secure share in God's promise.** From populous Judah down to small Simeon and Naphtali, nobody is dropped off the map. Every boundary and town logged at Shiloh affirms that God knows and provides for every member of His people by name (19:51).

**A true leader takes his own share dead last.** Across five full chapters, Joshua patiently distributes fertile plains, lush valleys, and bustling towns to every tribe without grabbing prime real estate for himself or his family. Only when nobody remains homeless does he ask for one modest hill town in Ephraim—and rebuilds it with his own hands for his final years (19:49–50).

**Each person still bears responsibility to hold the ground God gives.** Dan's detour in 19:47 sounds a sober note: instead of standing firm in faith to claim their God-allotted western homeland, they walked away to seize a distant northern outpost—launching a troubled chapter of spiritual compromise for Dan later on.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jacob's ancient prophecy over Simeon comes true in an unexpected way.** Centuries before, rebuking Simeon and Levi for their ruthless massacre at Shechem, dying Jacob foretold their descendants would be "scattered in Israel" (Genesis 49:5–7). Sure enough, in Joshua neither tribe receives an unbroken independent territory: Levi scatters through forty-eight towns nationwide (chapter 21), while Simeon inherits scattered towns inside Judah (19:1–9) and gradually blends into Judah's kingdom. Even a father's sober word of discipline folds into God's provident care.

**From Zebulun and Naphtali rises the light of the gospel in Galilee.** Northern regions allotted in chapter 19—especially Zebulun, Issachar, and Naphtali—play luminous roles across the rest of Scripture. From Zebulun's Gath-hepher (19:13) came the prophet Jonah (2 Kings 14:25); in Issachar's Shunem (19:18), a generous woman welcomed Elisha (2 Kings 4:8). Above all, the lands of Zebulun and Naphtali beside the Sea of Galilee (19:35) were later trampled by foreign empires and scorned as dark borderlands, yet Isaiah promised a great light would dawn right there (Isaiah 9:1–2). Seven centuries afterward, Matthew quotes that exact prophecy as Jesus leaves Nazareth to settle in Capernaum—on the border of Zebulun and Naphtali—launching His gospel ministry (Matthew 4:13–16).

**A matching literary frame uniting Caleb and Joshua.** Stepping back across the entire western allotment (chapters 14–19) reveals an exquisite, moving bookend: allotment opens with Caleb's personal grant at Hebron (14:13–14) and closes with Joshua's personal grant at Timnath-serah (19:49–50)—where he will eventually be buried (24:30). The sole two survivors from the Exodus generation, twin friends who stood faithful at Kadesh-barnea, frame the whole inheritance like twin pillars witnessing to God's faithfulness.

**Servant leadership pointing forward to Christ.** Joshua waiting until dead last and accepting an unbuilt mountain town mirrors the upside-down greatness Jesus taught centuries later: whoever wants to be greatest must become servant of all, just as the Son of Man came not to be served, but to serve and give His life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:43–45).

## Notes & Nuances

**Bethlehem in 19:15 is not Jesus' birthplace.** Readers glancing quickly at 19:15 are often surprised to spot *Bethlehem* up north inside Zebulun. Ancient Israel actually had two towns named Bethlehem (*House of Bread*): 19:15 names a small Galilean village roughly six miles from Nazareth, whereas King David and Jesus were born in southern Bethlehem Ephrathah in Judah.

**Why does 19:47 briefly report Dan conquering Leshem, an event occurring after Joshua's death?** Verse 19:47 condenses into one sentence a later migration recounted in full in Judges 18 (where Leshem is called *Laish*). The editor of Joshua included this historical footnote right in the tribal registry so later readers would understand why Dan—allotted western land near Joppa—lived at Israel's extreme northern tip (giving rise to the phrase *from Dan to Beersheba*). Honesty also demands noting what Judges 18 reveals: raiding peaceful, unsuspecting Laish (Judges 18:27–29) was never a God-commended triumph, but went hand in hand with stolen idols and deep moral collapse during the Judges era.

**A few puzzling numbers and place names in ancient lists.** In 19:30, the Hebrew text totals Asher's allotment at *twenty-two cities*, though counting individual listed names varies depending on how compound names group together. In 19:34, Naphtali's far-northern border curiously touches *Judah* at the Jordan to the east—even though Judah lies far south. Scholars suggest this denotes an ancient Jordan crossing or small settlement named Judah, or an early textual variant (the ancient Greek Septuagint omits *Judah* here). Such minutiae confirm the text preserves authentic ancient boundary archives faithfully.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, saves his highest praise in this chapter for Joshua's self-restraint in 19:49–50. In a world where rulers routinely grab the choicest spoils first, Joshua waits until the very end and contents himself with a modest hill town needing rebuilding—a rare jewel of integrity and self-control. **Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, draws two winsome lessons from this chapter. First, Judah gladly parting with surplus land for Simeon (19:9) models Christian generosity: those blessed with abundance never hoard it while neighbors beside them stand short.
