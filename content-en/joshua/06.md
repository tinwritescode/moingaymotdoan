# Joshua 6 — The Walls of Jericho Fall

## Story

Jericho has barred and bolted every gate tightly because of the Israelites; no one goes out, and no one comes in (6:1). Into that tense stillness the LORD speaks to Joshua—continuing seamlessly from the encounter closing chapter 5: "See, I have delivered Jericho into your hand, along with its king and its mighty men of valor." Yet the battle plan God lays out looks nothing like any human military strategy ever conceived (6:2).

For six straight days, Israel's fighting men must march silently around the city once each day. Walking ahead of the ark of the covenant, seven priests carry seven ram's-horn trumpets sounding continuously. On the seventh day, they march around the city seven times in one day. On the seventh lap, when the priests blow one long blast on the horns, all the people must raise a thunderous shout; at that instant Jericho's walls will collapse flat, and every soldier will charge straight ahead to capture the city (6:3–5).

Joshua summons the priests and people and delivers every detail exactly as commanded. The procession forms cleanly: armed vanguard out front, seven trumpet-blowing priests next, then the ark of the covenant of the LORD, followed by the rear guard. Crucially, Joshua enforces complete silence: "Do not shout or let your voice be heard, nor let one word come out of your mouth until the day I tell you to shout." On day one they circle the city once amid blaring horns and return quietly to camp for the night. Rising early the next morning, they march one loop and return again—repeating this for six steady days (6:6–14).

At dawn on the seventh day, the entire host rises early and circles the city seven times. On the seventh circuit, as the trumpets sound, Joshua cries out, "Shout! For the LORD has given you the city!" At the same time, he states the strict war decree governing this first conquered city: the whole city and everything inside it is devoted to the LORD for destruction; only Rahab the prostitute and everyone inside her house are spared because she hid the two messengers. Israel must guard themselves carefully from pocketing a single devoted item, lest they bring doom and disaster upon the whole camp. Silver, gold, and articles of bronze and iron are consecrated exclusively to the LORD's treasury (6:15–19).

The trumpets blast, and the vast crowd roars with a mighty shout. Instantly Jericho's walls collapse flat to the ground. Israelite soldiers charge straight up into the city, capture it, and put every living thing inside to the sword—men and women, young and old, cattle, sheep, and donkeys (6:20–21).

Yet amid the rubble, the sworn oath holds fast. Joshua sends the exact two young men who served as spies straight into Rahab's house to lead her, her father, mother, brothers, entire extended family, and possessions safely out, settling them temporarily outside Israel's camp. Once they are safe, Israel sets the city and everything left in it ablaze, depositing only silver, gold, bronze, and iron into the LORD's treasury. Rahab and her father's household are spared completely, and she goes on to live permanently among the people of Israel (6:22–25).

Finally, Joshua pronounces a solemn oath before the LORD: cursed is anyone who rises up to rebuild this city of Jericho—at the cost of his firstborn he shall lay its foundations, and at the cost of his youngest he shall set up its gates. From that day forward the LORD is with Joshua, and his fame spreads across the whole land (6:26–27).

## Key Takeaways

**God intentionally chooses an *absurd* plan so all glory belongs to Him alone.** Walking silently around towering walls for six days, then blowing ram's horns and shouting on day seven is nobody's battlefield textbook (6:3–5). God picked a method leaving even the finest warrior zero room to boast. When those walls tumbled down, Israelites and Canaanites alike knew beyond doubt: not swords, but the LORD Himself brought Jericho down.

**Silent, patient obedience is harder than charging ahead in our own strength.** Six days of wordless marching under baffled or jeering eyes peering down from Jericho's battlements tested Israel's patience and trust deeply (6:10–14). God rarely acts on day one; often He asks His people simply to keep walking faithful circles until His exact hour arrives.

**Even inside the sternest judgment, God's faithfulness and mercy never fail.** As all Jericho crashed into ruin, the scarlet cord in Rahab's window was not overlooked. The very two men who swore that oath walked straight in to bring her entire clan safely out (6:22–25). God never breaks a promise—not even one given to a foreign outsider carrying a tarnished past.

**The first conquered city belonged wholly to God.** Banning Israel from taking personal plunder at Jericho (6:17–19) proved this march was no greedy raid for enrichment. Jericho stood as Canaan's firstfruits—dedicated completely to the LORD so Israel never forgot who truly owned the land.

## Deeper Meaning

**The walls fell *by faith*.** The Letter to the Hebrews condenses both halves of chapter 6 into two unforgettable back-to-back verses: "By faith the walls of Jericho fell, after the people had marched around them for seven days. By faith the prostitute Rahab, because she welcomed the spies in peace, did not perish with those who were disobedient" (Hebrews 11:30–31). The very faith that toppled fortified stone walls also rescued one Gentile household.

**Seven priests, seven trumpets, and the picture of the final day.** The number seven—Scripture's emblem of divine completeness—saturates chapter 6: seven priests, seven horns, seven days, and seven circuits on the final day. In Revelation, that exact imagery expands to cosmic scale: seven angels hold seven trumpets (Revelation 8:2), and at the seventh blast heaven proclaims that the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of His Christ (Revelation 11:15). Jericho is a miniature preview of the day every fortress defying God on earth collapses.

**Weapons not of the flesh.** Under the New Covenant, God's people no longer fight with swords or topple masonry towns. Paul echoes this fortress-breaking imagery to describe the gospel's mission: "The weapons of our warfare are not the weapons of the world. Instead, they have divine power to demolish strongholds" (2 Corinthians 10:3–4). The thickest walls today stand inside proud human hearts, and God's power alone brings them down.

**Joshua's curse came true five hundred years later.** The solemn oath of 6:26 was no hollow threat. Five centuries afterward, during wicked King Ahab's reign, a man named Hiel of Bethel defied the ban to rebuild Jericho. Scripture notes grimly that he laid its foundations at the cost of his firstborn son Abiram and set up its gates at the cost of his youngest son Segub—just as the LORD had spoken through Joshua (1 Kings 16:34).

## Notes & Nuances

**Putting *men and women, young and old* to the sword in Jericho (6:21) is one of the hardest passages in the Old Testament.** Modern readers—believers included—rightly feel shaken reading of an entire city put to the sword. Rather than dodging or softening the text's starkness, faithful interpretation places three essential points side by side. First, Scripture explicitly frames this as *God's righteous moral judgment*, never ethnic cleansing or land-grabbing imperialism: God waited patiently four hundred years until Canaanite atrocities—including burning children alive to idols and brutal cultic abuses—reached full measure (Genesis 15:16; Leviticus 18:24–28). God also warned Israel bluntly that they received the land not for moral superiority, and if they copied those evils the land would "vomit" them out just the same (Deuteronomy 9:4–5; Leviticus 18:28); sparing Rahab's whole family proves repentance stayed open to Canaanites (6:25). Second, many modern Old Testament scholars note that sweeping formulas like *men and women, young and old* reflect conventional ancient Near Eastern military hyperbole proclaiming decisive victory, and archaeological evidence suggests Jericho functioned primarily as a military fortress rather than a sprawling civilian metropolis. Third, every orthodox Christian tradition insists this was a unique, strictly bounded Old Testament historical mandate, never a template for religious violence—for in the New Testament Christ conquers the world by laying down His own life on a cross and commanding His followers to love their enemies.

**If they marched seven straight days, what about the Sabbath?** Unquestionably one of those seven days fell on the Sabbath. Yet this was a liturgical procession escorting the ark of the covenant by direct divine command—a sacred act of worship rather than everyday labor, just as priests continued serving at the altar every Sabbath.

**What does archaeology say about Jericho's walls?** Ancient Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) is one of the most intensively excavated mounds in the Middle East. Excavations uncovered a mud-brick city wall collapsed outward forming an earthen ramp right up into the city, a deep burn layer, and large storage jars still brimming with charred grain—matching remarkably a harvest-time siege that fell quickly without looting. However, archaeologists sharply debate the date: Kathleen Kenyon dated that destruction layer around 1550 BC (earlier than standard dates for Joshua), whereas scholars such as Bryant Wood reanalyzed the pottery and proposed roughly 1400 BC. Readers should recognize this remains an active scholarly debate.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, third-century Christian scholar in Alexandria, confronts the severity of Jericho's conquest by reading the narrative spiritually: Jericho represents the sinful world order and hostile powers opposing God. Its proud walls collapse not before earthly swords, but before the trumpet blast of apostolic gospel preaching. Rahab's house marked by the scarlet cord stands as an image of the church, the sole refuge sheltering souls from judgment. *(Homilies on Joshua, Homilies 6 and 7)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Origen's sixth and seventh homilies on Joshua are celebrated for reading Jericho's fall through gospel preaching trumpets and Rahab's home as the church -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer, notes God's deliberate irony in the seven-day marching plan: to human eyes—and surely to guards watching from Jericho's ramparts—the spectacle looked laughable, yet God chose it precisely to humble human pride and show His power works wonders through the humblest means. Regarding the total destruction command (6:21), Calvin does not minimize its severity, placing it under the Creator's righteous sovereignty judging ripe wickedness, while noting the strict plunder ban prevented greed from turning judgment into looting. *(Commentary on the Book of Joshua, on chapter 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: noting both the seemingly foolish strategy humbling human pride and the sober justification of judgment restrained by the plunder ban appear prominently in Calvin's commentary on Joshua 6 -->

**Matthew Henry**, English pastor who died in 1714, calls Jericho the *firstfruits* of Canaan consecrated completely to God. Hence no Israelite could touch a coin of silver or a single beast: everything was either burned as a whole offering or placed in God's treasury (6:17–19), ensuring Israel remembered the whole Promised Land came as God's gift rather than self-earned plunder. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Joshua 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: describing Jericho as Canaan's firstfruits dedicated entirely to God is a familiar highlight of Matthew Henry's commentary on chapter 6; exact wording not checked -->
