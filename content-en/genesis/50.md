# Genesis 50 — God Turns Evil into Good

## Story

Joseph throws himself over his father's face, kisses him, and weeps. Then he instructs the physicians in his service to embalm his father according to Egyptian custom. The embalming takes forty days, and the Egyptians mourn for Jacob for seventy days (50:1–3).

Once the mourning period ends, Joseph asks Pharaoh's court to relay a request to the king: before he died, my father made me swear an oath to bury him in the tomb he prepared in the land of Canaan. Please let me go up and bury my father, and then I will return. Pharaoh answers: go up and bury your father, just as he made you swear to do (50:4–6).

It is an immense funeral procession. Traveling up with Joseph are Pharaoh's officials, the elders of his household and of all Egypt, Joseph's own family, his brothers, and his father's household. Only the young children and the flocks and herds stay behind in Goshen. Chariots and horsemen accompany them—a vast company (50:7–9).

Arriving at the threshing floor of Atad, across the Jordan, they hold a loud, solemn lamentation, and Joseph observes seven days of mourning for his father. Watching the scene, the Canaanites remark that this is a great ceremony of mourning by the Egyptians. Because of that, the place is named Abel-mizraim, meaning *the mourning of the Egyptians* (50:10–11).

Jacob's sons carry out every word their father commanded. They bring him into Canaan and bury him in the cave in the field of Machpelah that Abraham had bought. After burying their father, Joseph, his brothers, and everyone who traveled with them return to Egypt (50:12–14).

With their father gone, fear creeps back into Joseph's brothers. They whisper among themselves: what if Joseph still holds a grudge and now pays us back for all the evil we did to him? They send a message to Joseph saying that before he died, Jacob gave instructions to tell Joseph: please forgive the transgression and sin of your brothers, for they did you wrong. Now please forgive the transgression of the servants of the God of your father. When their message reaches him, Joseph weeps (50:15–17).

Then the brothers come in person, throw themselves down before him, and declare, "We are your slaves!" (50:18). The seventeen-year-old boy's dream of the bowing sheaves has come true down to the last detail.

Yet Joseph answers them: "Do not be afraid. Am I in the place of God? As for you, what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good, in order to accomplish a day like this—to preserve the lives of many people. Therefore do not be afraid. I will provide for you and your little ones." He reassures them and speaks kindly to their hearts (50:19–21).

Joseph and his father's household remain in Egypt. He lives to be 110 years old, seeing Ephraim's children to the third generation, and even welcoming the children of Machir, Manasseh's son, onto his knees (50:22–23).

Before he dies, Joseph speaks to his brothers: I am about to die, yet God will surely visit you and bring you up out of this land to the land He swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He makes the sons of Israel swear an oath: when God comes to attend to you, carry my bones up with you from this place. Then Joseph dies at 110 years old. They embalm his body and place him in a coffin in Egypt (50:24–26). Genesis opens with life in a garden and closes with a coffin—yet it is a coffin waiting for the day of departure.

## Key Takeaways

**Guilt lingers far longer than we expect.** Even after seventeen peaceful years under Joseph's care, his brothers still cannot quite believe they are truly forgiven. The moment their father is buried, the terror of payback flares right back up (50:15). Joseph weeps when he hears their message—weeping out of grief that they still doubt his love (50:17).

**No human being has the right to sit in God's judgment seat.** When his brothers fall down offering to be his slaves, Joseph answers with striking humility: "Do not be afraid. Am I in the place of God?" (50:19). Real forgiveness means handing the right of vengeance back to God, the only righteous Judge.

**God turns evil into good.** Verse 50:20 is the theological key to the entire Joseph narrative: "As for you, what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good, in order to accomplish a day like this—to preserve the lives of many people." Joseph does not whitewash his brothers' sin—they truly *intended evil*—yet God's sovereign hand is mightier than every human plot.

**Living a whole lifetime in Egypt, yet dying anchored to Canaan.** Joseph serves eighty years as Egypt's prime minister and enjoys every honor Egypt can offer, yet his dying wish is not a grand pyramid, but that his bones be carried out of Egypt on the day God comes to rescue His people (50:24–25).

## Deeper Meaning

**A blueprint for the cross of Christ.** Verse 50:20 opens up one of the deepest truths in all of Scripture about evil and redemption: human beings can commit genuine evil and bear full responsibility for it, yet God still weaves that very evil into a greater plan of salvation. That truth reaches its summit at the cross of Jesus—wicked hands nailed Him there, yet all of it stood inside God's definite plan to save countless lives (Acts 2:23; Romans 8:28).

**Faith looking four hundred years ahead.** The letter to the Hebrews singles out Joseph's final act for praise: when his end was near, by faith he spoke of Israel's exodus and gave orders concerning his bones (Hebrews 11:22). That unburied coffin resting in Egypt became a silent sermon across generations of slavery, reminding Israel that Egypt was never home—and when the day finally came, Moses carried Joseph's bones out of Egypt (Exodus 13:19) to be laid to rest at Shechem (Joshua 24:32).

**An arc from Eden's garden to a waiting coffin.** Genesis begins with God creating the heavens, the earth, and abundant life (1:1), yet ends with a coffin in a foreign land (50:26). That stark contrast shows the shadow of chapter 3's sin still hanging over humanity, yet the story is far from over—the promise "God will surely visit you" (50:24) swings the door wide open into the book of Exodus.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Jacob really leave the dying message the brothers quote (50:16–17)?** Genesis never records Jacob giving any such command before his death. In their panic, the brothers may have invented a last wish to protect themselves; or Jacob may have spoken privately with them without the text recording it. Either way, Joseph does not cross-examine or expose them—he simply weeps and speaks tenderly to comfort them (50:17; 50:21).

**Why were Jacob and Joseph embalmed according to Egyptian custom?** Embalming here had nothing to do with Egyptian religion or worship of the dead; it was simply the best preservation method available at the time—necessary for carrying Jacob's body all the way back to Canaan (50:2–3) and for keeping Joseph's remains ready until Israel left Egypt (50:26).

**What is the significance of Joseph living to 110 (50:22; 50:26)?** In ancient Egyptian literature, 110 years was celebrated as the ideal human lifespan—the mark of a full, blessed, well-lived life. Scripture records that exact age as a quiet testimony that Joseph's life was complete and honored both before Egypt and before God.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, regarded 50:20 as one of the most important verses in all of Scripture on God's sovereignty. As he explained it, two distinct agents acted in one single event with opposite aims: the brothers acted out of malice and were wholly guilty, while God directed that very deed to preserve many lives and remained wholly righteous and merciful. *(Commentaries on the First Book of Moses Called Genesis, chapter 50)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: distinguishing two opposing intentions within a single event at Genesis 50:20 is one of Calvin's most famous expositions of divine providence -->

**Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, lingered over 50:21: Joseph did not merely spare his brothers, he *reassured* them and *spoke kindly to them*. To Henry, genuine Christian forgiveness is never cold or condescending; it actively calms fear and heals the hearts of those who once did us wrong. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis chapter 50)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: drawing out the lesson of gentle, reassuring forgiveness from 50:21 is highly characteristic of Matthew Henry; original text not cross-checked -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, nineteenth-century English pastor, preached on Joseph's coffin in 50:26. He pictured that unburied coffin standing among the Israelites throughout their years of bondage like a wordless sermon: whenever their spirits sank under Pharaoh's whips, one glance at Joseph's coffin reminded them that God would surely visit them and bring them home to the Promised Land. *(Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: portraying Joseph's coffin as a lasting reminder of faith during Israel's slavery is a familiar Spurgeon sermon theme; unsure of exact title and year -->

**Derek Kidner**, twentieth-century British Old Testament scholar, stressed that Joseph's question—*Am I in the place of God?* (50:19)—is the moral bedrock of forgiveness. Only when a person refuses to set themselves up as supreme judge are they truly free to love and care for those who once harmed them. *(Genesis, Tyndale Old Testament Commentaries)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Kidner's concise insight on 50:19 and the nature of forgiveness in Tyndale Genesis; original text not cross-checked -->
