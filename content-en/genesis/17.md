# Genesis 17 — The Covenant and Circumcision

## Story

Thirteen years had gone by since Ishmael's birth. Abram was now ninety-nine years old. One day, the Lord appeared to him and said, "I am God Almighty. Walk before Me and be blameless." He promised to establish His covenant with Abram and multiply his descendants exceedingly (17:1–2).

Abram fell facedown to the ground. God went on: he would become "the father of many nations." Because of this, he would no longer be called Abram, but Abraham. Nations and kings would come from him. This covenant was not for him alone, but for his descendants through every generation—an "everlasting covenant": God would be their God, and He would give them the whole land of Canaan, where Abraham was currently living as an outsider (17:3–8).

Then God laid out Abraham's part. Every male in his household had to be circumcised—cutting away the flesh of the foreskin. This would be the sign of the covenant, marked directly on the body. Every baby boy was to be circumcised at eight days old—not only biological children, but servants born in the household or bought from foreigners as well. Any uncircumcised male would be cut off from his people for breaking the covenant (17:9–14).

God changed his wife's name too. Sarai would now be called Sarah. God would bless her, and she herself would bear Abraham a son. She would become "the mother of nations," and kings of peoples would descend from her (17:15–16).

Hearing this, Abraham fell facedown once more—and this time he burst out laughing. He thought to himself: "Can a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old?" And Sarah was ninety. Then he offered God a plea straight from the heart: he only asked that Ishmael, the son he already had, might live under God's blessing (17:17–18).

God's reply left no room for doubt. No—Sarah herself would bear a son, and Abraham must name him Isaac, meaning "he laughs." God would establish His covenant with Isaac and his descendants. Still, God heard the father's prayer for Ishmael: Ishmael too would be blessed, multiply greatly, father twelve rulers, and become a great nation. Yet the covenant would run through Isaac, whom Sarah would bear at this same time next year. When He finished speaking, God went up from Abraham (17:19–22).

"On that very day," Abraham did everything God commanded. He circumcised Ishmael, then thirteen years old, every male in his household, and himself at the age of ninety-nine. The entire household was circumcised on that single day (17:23–27).

## Key Takeaways

**God reveals who He is right when human options run out.** Abraham was ninety-nine, and Sarah was past childbearing. At that exact moment, God introduced Himself as "God Almighty." In the previous chapter, the couple had tried to produce an heir through Hagar. Here God makes it clear: His promise does not need human workarounds, because the One who promised has the power to keep it.

**A new name marks a new future.** Abram meant "exalted father." Abraham is explained as "father of many nations." When he received that name, he did not yet have a single child of the promise. Every time someone called him by name, they spoke God's promise aloud before anyone could see it.

**A covenant is a gift that calls for a response.** Most of this chapter consists of what God will do: "I will," "I will," "I will." Yet God also tells Abraham to walk before Him and bear a visible mark on his body. God's grace comes first; a faithful life follows.

**God does not forget the child passed over.** Ishmael was not the child of the covenant, yet God did not cast him aside. He heard a father's prayer and promised to bless Ishmael and make him into a great nation (17:20).

**Prompt obedience.** Abraham had just laughed at how impossible the promise sounded, yet he did not stall. That very same day, he carried out God's command—a painful act for a man nearly a hundred years old.

## Deeper Meaning

**Counted righteous before circumcision.** The apostle Paul paid close attention to the order of events. Abraham was credited as righteous through faith back in chapter 15 (15:6)—more than a decade before he was circumcised here. Circumcision therefore did not make him righteous; it was like a seal stamped on the faith he already had (Romans 4:9–11). Because of that, Abraham is the father of all who believe, whether circumcised or not (Romans 4:11–12).

**Circumcision of the heart.** Even in the Old Testament, the prophets insisted that an outward mark on the body was never enough. Moses declared that God would circumcise the hearts of His people so they would love Him with all their heart (Deuteronomy 30:6). Jeremiah urged the people to circumcise their hearts to the Lord (Jeremiah 4:4). And the New Testament teaches that believers in Christ receive a circumcision not done by human hands (Colossians 2:11).

**Father of many nations.** The promise here reaches far beyond a single nation. Paul quoted this exact verse: "I have made you a father of many nations" (Romans 4:17). Everyone who belongs to Christ, Paul says, is Abraham's offspring (Galatians 3:29). On the closing pages of Scripture stands an uncountable multitude from every nation and tribe before God's throne (Revelation 7:9).

**"I will be their God."** The pledge in 17:7–8 becomes a golden thread running straight through the Bible. Jeremiah repeats it when speaking of a new covenant written on human hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). At the very end of Scripture, it rings out once more when God dwells with humanity in the new heaven and new earth (Revelation 21:3).

**New names.** Abram becomes Abraham; Sarai becomes Sarah. Later, Jacob is renamed Israel (32:28), and Jesus calls Simon Cephas, which means Peter (John 1:42). Whenever God gives someone a new name, He tells them who they are becoming in His hands.

**Laughter turned into a son's name.** Abraham laughed at what seemed impossible. Rather than scolding him, God told him to name his boy Isaac—"he laughs." When Isaac was born, Sarah declared that God had made her laugh (21:6). The laughter of disbelief became the laughter of joy.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Abraham laugh out of doubt or joy?** The chapter does not spell it out. Some readers hear disbelief, especially since he immediately prays for Ishmael—almost as if saying, "Ishmael is enough for me." Others hear stunned joy, pointing to Paul's words that Abraham did not weaken in faith (Romans 4:19–20) and Jesus' words that Abraham rejoiced (John 8:56). Noticeably, when Sarah laughs in the next chapter, God calls her on it (18:12–15), whereas here God does not rebuke Abraham.

**"Blameless" does not mean sinless.** Abraham had just fathered a child through Hagar by his own scheme, and he will soon lie about his wife all over again (20:2). Here "blameless" means wholehearted integrity—walking with God sincerely rather than living with a divided heart.

**Circumcision did not begin with Abraham.** Several ancient peoples practiced circumcision, usually as a rite of passage for young men nearing marriage. What was new here was its meaning: a covenant sign with God performed on an eight-day-old infant, long before the child could accomplish anything.

**Servants bought with money.** The passage mentions servants purchased from foreigners (17:12). Scripture describes an ancient society where slavery existed without endorsing the institution. Even so, one detail stands out: those servants received the exact same covenant sign as biological sons, counting them inside God's household from the very beginning.

**Believers today are not required to be circumcised.** The earliest church gathered specifically to wrestle with this question and decided not to require Gentile believers to be circumcised (Acts 15:1–29). Paul wrote that in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision has any value; all that matters is faith expressed through love (Galatians 5:6).
