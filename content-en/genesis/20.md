# Genesis 20 — Abraham and King Abimelech

## Story

After Sodom's destruction, Abraham moved southward and settled as a temporary resident in Gerar. There he did the exact same thing he had done in Egypt years earlier (12:11–13): he said of Sarah, "She is my sister." Sure enough, Abimelech, king of Gerar, sent for Sarah and had her brought to his palace (20:1–2).

That night, God came to Abimelech in a dream and warned him: you are as good as dead because of the woman you took, for she is a married woman. Abimelech had not gone near her. He pleaded his case before God: Abraham himself told me she was his sister, and she called him her brother; I did this "in the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands." God answered: I know you acted with a clear conscience, and I Myself kept you from sinning against Me. Now give the man's wife back. He is a prophet; he will pray for you and you will live. If you refuse to return her, you and everyone belonging to you will surely die (20:3–7).

First thing in the morning, the king gathered all his servants and told them everything, leaving them terrified. Then he summoned Abraham and confronted him head-on: What have you done to us? How have I wronged you that you nearly dragged me and my entire kingdom into such terrible guilt? What were you thinking when you did this? (20:8–10).

Abraham offered three excuses. First, he assumed "there is no fear of God in this place," so the locals would kill him to take his wife. Second, Sarah really was his sister—his father's daughter, though not his mother's. Third, ever since God sent him wandering from his father's house, he and Sarah had kept a standing agreement: wherever they went, she would tell people he was her brother (20:11–13).

Abimelech returned Sarah along with sheep, cattle, menservants, and maidservants, and invited Abraham to settle wherever he pleased in the land. Turning to Sarah, he told her he was giving her "brother" a thousand pieces of silver—a public vindication, like a covering before everyone's eyes, clearing her honor completely (20:14–16).

Then Abraham prayed, and God healed Abimelech, his wife, and his maidservants so they could bear children again. Earlier, because of Sarah, the Lord had closed every womb in the king's household (20:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**God's people can stumble back into the exact same old rut.** Abraham had told this exact lie in Egypt. Since then, he had received covenant promises, a new name, and an audience with God to plead for Sodom. Yet the old fear still yanked him back into the old habit. Growing in faith does not make our old weaknesses vanish overnight.

**A half-truth is still a lie.** Sarah genuinely was Abraham's half-sister. Still, he used that fact to make everyone assume she was unmarried. A technically accurate statement deployed to mislead someone is still deception.

**Abraham misjudged the outsiders.** He figured nobody in Gerar feared God. Instead, the foreign king spoke honestly with God, dreaded sin, and acted more honorably than Abraham did. People of faith are not automatically more decent than those outside the fold.

**God protects His promise even when its recipient sabotages it.** Sarah was within a year of giving birth to Isaac (18:14), yet Abraham let his wife be carried into another king's palace. God Himself had to step in and stop the king from touching her (20:6). The promise stood firm not because Abraham guarded it well, but because God guarded it.

**God holds people back from sin.** God told Abimelech, "I have kept you from sinning against Me" (20:6). How many wrongs have we avoided not because we were so virtuous, but because God quietly restrained us?

## Deeper Meaning

**"Do no harm to My prophets!"** This chapter uses the word "prophet" for the very first time in Scripture—and applies it to Abraham right in the middle of his blunder (20:7). Centuries later, a psalm looking back on the patriarchs' journeys recalls how God rebuked kings on their behalf: "Do not touch My anointed ones! Do no harm to My prophets!" (Psalms 105:14–15).

**A foreign king echoes Abraham's own question.** Back in chapter 18, Abraham asked whether God would sweep away the righteous with the wicked (18:23). Here, Abimelech asks God almost the identical question: would You destroy an innocent nation? (20:4). This time, the voice appealing to God's justice belongs not to Abraham, but to an outsider.

**Outsiders, too, can fear God.** Stepping into a Roman officer's home, the apostle Peter realized that God shows no favoritism, but welcomes anyone from any nation who fears Him and does what is right (Acts 10:34–35). Abimelech is an early glimpse of that very truth.

**He remains faithful.** The New Testament declares that even if we are faithless, He remains faithful, for He cannot deny Himself (2 Timothy 2:13). Chapter 20 puts that truth on vivid display.

**A father's sin replayed by his son.** Years later, Abraham's son Isaac also moves to Gerar, meets a king named Abimelech, and passes his wife off as his sister (26:6–11). Unchecked habits rarely stop with one person; they travel easily to the next generation.

## Notes & Nuances

**Sarah was nearly ninety years old.** Many readers wonder why a king would summon a woman of that age into his palace. Tellingly, unlike chapter 12, this narrative never mentions her beauty (12:11). Some suggest Abimelech wanted a political marriage alliance with a wealthy chieftain like Abraham rather than being driven by physical attraction; others wonder whether God rejuvenated Sarah as she prepared to bear Isaac. Scripture offers no explanation.

**One story or three?** A patriarch passing his wife off as his sister appears three times in Genesis (12:10–20; 20:1–18; 26:6–11). Many modern scholars view them as three retellings of a single older tradition. Many other readers see three distinct events, arguing that the repetition is the point: human beings keep tripping over the very same stone.

**Marrying a half-sister.** Later, the law of Moses explicitly forbade marrying a half-sister (Leviticus 18:9). In Abraham's era, that law did not yet exist. Genesis describes the customs of the time rather than holding them up as a model.

**Why does this episode sit right before Isaac's birth?** Twice the text makes a point of stressing that Abimelech never touched Sarah (20:4; 20:6). Ancient Jewish tradition saw a clear purpose here: no one could ever whisper that Isaac was really the king of Gerar's son. The child of the promise was unmistakably Abraham's.

**What does "a covering of the eyes" mean?** Verse 20:16 is tricky to translate—literally "a covering of the eyes," often rendered as "vindication." The basic meaning is clear: the thousand pieces of silver stood as public compensation so everyone knew Sarah was innocent and her reputation remained spotless.
