# Genesis 36 — The Descendants of Esau

## Story

The previous chapter closed with Esau and Jacob burying their father side by side. Before moving on with the story of Jacob's household, Scripture pauses for a full chapter to finish telling the story of the older brother. It opens, "This is the account of Esau (that is, Edom)" (36:1). Edom means "red," the nickname Esau had carried ever since he traded his birthright for a bowl of red stew.

Esau married three wives: Adah the Hittite, Oholibamah the Hivite, and Basemath, a daughter of Ishmael. While still living in Canaan, they bore him five sons (36:1–5).

Later, Esau moved his entire household, his livestock, and all his property to a different land, far away from Jacob. The reason was completely ordinary: both brothers had grown so wealthy and their herds so huge that the land could no longer support them both. Esau settled in the hill country of Seir, the red-rock highlands south of the Dead Sea (36:6–8).

Next comes the roster of Esau's descendants in Seir—his sons and his grandsons. One grandson's name stands out: Amalek, born to Eliphaz through his concubine Timna (36:9–14). These grandsons became tribal chiefs, each leading a clan of his own (36:15–19).

Scripture also records the people who lived in Seir before Esau arrived: the Horites, descendants of a man named Seir, along with their chiefs. Tucked inside the list is a charming side note: while pasturing his father's donkeys in the wilderness, Anah discovered hot springs (36:20–30). Timna, Amalek's mother, was the sister of a Horite chief (36:22), and Oholibamah, Esau's wife, was Anah's daughter (36:25). Esau's family and the local inhabitants blended together through marriage.

Then follow eight kings who reigned in Edom "before any king reigned over the Israelites" (36:31). Strangely, not a single king was the son of the king before him; each hailed from a different city. One king, Hadad, defeated Midian in the country of Moab (36:31–39).

The chapter closes with one more list of chiefs, organized by their territories, and a final summary line: "Esau was the father of the Edomites" (36:40–43).

## Key Takeaways

**God keeps His word even to the one not chosen.** While the twins were still in Rebekah's womb, God had said, "Two nations are in your womb" (25:23). This chapter proves that word came true. Esau was not chosen to carry the covenant line, yet he was not cast aside. He had abundant descendants, a homeland, chiefs, and kings.

**Others may rise faster than God's people.** Edom already had kings while Jacob's family did not yet own a real homeland and was about to end up enslaved in Egypt. God's promise to Jacob that "kings shall descend from you" (35:11) would wait centuries to come true. Those chosen by God often have to learn patience.

**The promised land did not have to be won by the sword.** Jacob had lain awake in terror of his brother. In the end, Esau simply packed up and moved away, leaving Canaan to Jacob for a reason as down-to-earth as pastureland. God has ways of keeping His promises that human beings never expect.

**A familiar rhythm in Genesis.** Before tracing the main line in detail, Genesis regularly pauses to wrap up the side branch. Cain's line is recorded before Seth's (4:17–24). Ishmael's line is listed before Isaac's story continues (25:12–18). Now Esau's line is recorded before the story turns to Jacob's sons. The other brother is honored and accounted for, and then the story follows the line of promise.

## Deeper Meaning

**Edom is a brother.** When Israel came out of Egypt, God commanded them not to pick a fight with Edom, because He had given Mount Seir to Esau as his own possession (Deuteronomy 2:5). The Law of Moses went even further: "Do not despise an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). Chapter 36 is the family record behind that command.

**Brothers turned bitter enemies.** Even so, the history between the two nations turned painful. Edom refused to let Israel pass through its territory (Numbers 20:14–21). Centuries later, when Jerusalem fell, Edom sided with Israel's enemies. The entire Book of Obadiah pronounces judgment on Edom for its violence against "your brother Jacob" (Obadiah 1:10; cf. Amos 1:11). The struggle that began inside Rebekah's womb hardened into centuries of national hostility.

**Amalek.** That quiet name buried in the genealogy later became the very first enemy to ambush Israel in the wilderness (Exodus 17:8–16). King Saul lost his throne in part because he spared the Amalekite king (1 Samuel 15:8). An old Jewish legend claims that Timna once wanted to join Abraham's household and was turned away, so she became a concubine in Esau's house instead and gave birth to Amalek. That legend is not in the Bible, yet it shows how deeply ancient readers worried about shutting the door on someone who wanted to draw near to God.

**"Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated."** The prophet Malachi spoke those words after Edom lay in ruins (Malachi 1:2–3), and the apostle Paul later quoted them (Romans 9:13). In ancient idiom, "hate" here means choosing one and not choosing the other. Chapter 36 shows that "not chosen" did not mean abandoned. Esau still received real blessings of his own—land, family, and power—yet those blessings stopped at earthly prosperity. The Letter to the Hebrews holds up Esau as a warning about trading eternal treasure for a single meal (Hebrews 12:16).

**Edomites in the New Testament.** In Greek, Edom came to be called Idumea. According to the Jewish historian Josephus, King Herod the Great—the ruler who ordered the slaughter of the infant boys in Bethlehem—was of Idumean descent. The ancient rivalry between Esau's line and Jacob's line still echoed in the night Jesus was born.

**Eliphaz the Temanite.** Esau's oldest son was Eliphaz, and his grandson was Teman (36:11). In the Book of Job, the first friend who comes to comfort Job is "Eliphaz the Temanite" (Job 2:11). <!-- low: uncertain whether the two figures have any connection beyond the name -->It may be that the Book of Job sets its story in the region of Edom, a land famous for its wise men.<!-- /low --> The ancient Greek translation even added an appendix to the Book of Job identifying Job himself with Jobab, the second king of Edom (36:33).

## Notes & Nuances

**The wives' names do not match earlier chapters.** Earlier in Genesis, Esau's wives are named Judith, Basemath daughter of Elon, and Mahalath daughter of Ishmael (26:34; 28:9). Here they are Adah daughter of Elon, Oholibamah, and Basemath daughter of Ishmael. People in the ancient world often went by more than one name, and Esau may also have had more wives than those mentioned. Others think the two lists came from different written traditions. No single explanation is completely certain.

**Verse 36:31 hints at when it was written.** The phrase "before any king reigned over the Israelites" assumes the writer already knew Israel had kings, while Moses lived centuries before Israel's monarchy. Many readers conclude that this verse—or the whole king list—was added or updated later. Others read it as a forward-looking remark anticipating God's promise that kings would come from Jacob (35:11). The same list reappears almost word for word in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 1:43).

**Hot springs or mules?** The Hebrew word in 36:24 appears only once in the entire Bible, so no one knows its exact meaning. Many modern translations render it "hot springs." Some older translations render it "mules," and one Jewish tradition understood it to mean that Anah was the first person to crossbreed mules.

**Do not skip chapters full of names.** Modern readers are tempted to skim right past genealogies. To ancient readers, however, a genealogy was proof that God's promises spoken over real people came true in flesh-and-blood families with real names and histories.

**Augustine**, a North African bishop who lived more than sixteen hundred years ago, believed Job came from the line of Edom rather than Israel. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, notices that Esau's line had chiefs and kings very early, while Jacob's descendants were still wandering foreigners and soon slaves in Egypt. To him, that is a test of faith: God's chosen people often watch others prosper ahead of them.
