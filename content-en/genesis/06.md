# Genesis 6 — A Corrupt World and the Ark

## Story

Humanity began to multiply across the face of the earth. Then something strange happened: "the sons of God" saw that the daughters of men were beautiful and married whomever they chose (6:1–2). The LORD declared that His Spirit would not contend with humanity forever, because people are mortal; their days would be 120 years (6:3). In those days giants—the Nephilim—walked the earth, mighty men of old, men of renown (6:4). These four verses are brief and enigmatic, and readers have debated them for millennia.

What follows is painfully clear. The LORD looked down upon the earth and saw how widespread human wickedness had become. It was not just their outward actions that had rotted. Every inclination of the thoughts of their hearts was "altogether evil all the time" (6:5). Scripture says the LORD regretted making humanity and was "grieved in His heart" (6:6). He resolved to blot out humanity from the face of the earth, along with animals, crawling creatures, and birds of the air (6:7).

Then, right in the middle of that darkness, comes one short sentence: "Noah, however, found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (6:8). The entire story pivots on that word "however."

Scripture introduces Noah: he was a righteous man, blameless in his generation, who walked with God—just like his great-grandfather Enoch before him. He had three sons: Shem, Ham, and Japheth (6:9–10). Meanwhile, the world had grown corrupt and full of violence. God looked at the earth and saw that all living creatures had corrupted their ways (6:11–12).

God told Noah what He was about to do: because the earth was filled with violence, He would destroy humanity along with the earth. Then He instructed Noah to build an ark out of gopher wood, divide it into rooms, and coat it with pitch inside and out (6:13–14). God gave exact dimensions: 300 cubits long, 50 cubits wide, and 30 cubits high. A cubit is an ancient measure—roughly from elbow to fingertip, about eighteen inches or half a meter—making the ark around 450 feet (nearly 150 meters) long. It was to have three decks, an opening near the top, and a door in its side (6:15–16).

God went on: He would bring floodwaters across the earth, and every creature with the breath of life would perish. "But I will establish My covenant with you" (6:17–18). A covenant is a solemn, binding commitment between two parties, and this is the very first time the word appears in the Bible. Noah was to enter the ark with his wife, his three sons, and their wives. He was to bring a pair—male and female—of every kind of animal, bird, and crawling creature to keep them alive, along with food stored up for both people and animals (6:18–21).

The chapter closes cleanly: "So Noah did everything precisely as God had commanded him" (6:22). He did not argue, and he did not question. He simply built the ark.

## Key Takeaways

**Sin had spread everywhere.** In chapter 4, sin was one man killing his brother. By chapter 6, the entire earth is filled with violence. Sin no longer lives only in what hands do; it has sunk deep into the thoughts of the heart. This is a world ruined down to its roots.

**God grieves over sin.** Scripture does not picture God as a cold, detached judge. He was "grieved in His heart." The coming flood does not spring from a hot-tempered deity, but from a Creator who aches as He watches His creatures destroy themselves.

**Grace comes before righteousness.** Scripture tells us Noah "found favor" before it ever mentions how righteous he was. Noah was no superhero. God showed him mercy first, and Noah's righteous life was his response to that grace.

**One person can live differently from an entire generation.** The whole world had turned corrupt, yet Noah still walked with God. The crowd around us does not get to decide who we become.

**Faith shows itself in obedience.** Noah built a massive ship on dry ground for a flood nobody had ever seen—a project that must have stretched over many years. He trusted God's word, and that trust took shape in wood, pitch, and hammers.

## Deeper Meaning

**Noah built the ark by faith.** The Letter to the Hebrews includes Noah in its gallery of faith: warned about things not yet seen, he built an ark in reverent obedience to save his family (Hebrews 11:7). The apostle Peter even calls him "a preacher of righteousness" (2 Peter 2:5), as though the very act of building the ark preached a sermon to everyone watching.

**The days of Noah.** Jesus said His return will be just like Noah's time. People were eating, drinking, and getting married right up to the day Noah stepped into the ark, completely oblivious until the flood swept them away (Matthew 24:37–39). The most terrifying thing is not always some spectacular crime, but living day to day as though God will never come.

**The ark and baptism.** Peter points out that only eight people in the ark were saved through water, and says that water symbolizes baptism—the rite in which believers enter water to declare they belong to Jesus (1 Peter 3:20–21). The waters brought judgment on the old world while carrying the faithful into a brand-new beginning.

**Baby Moses' basket.** The Hebrew word for Noah's ark appears in only one other place in the entire Bible: the papyrus basket coated with pitch in which Moses' mother placed her infant son and set him afloat on the Nile (Exodus 2:3). Both are pitch-sealed vessels floating on deadly waters, carrying the chosen servant through whom God will rescue His people.

**God is grieved by sin.** This portrait of a grieving God does not fade away in the New Testament. Paul urges believers not to grieve the Holy Spirit of God (Ephesians 4:30). Sin is never just breaking an impersonal rule; it wounds the heart of One who loves us.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who were "the sons of God"?** Three main explanations exist. First, fallen angels who took human women as wives—this is the oldest reading, found in early Jewish writings, and some see echoes of it in Jude and 2 Peter (Jude 1:6; 2 Peter 2:4). Second, godly descendants of Seth who married into Cain's line, causing the two lines to blur and grow corrupt. Third, ancient kings who claimed divine descent and took harems of whichever women they wanted. Jesus said angels in heaven do not marry (Matthew 22:30), a verse cited by people on both sides of the debate. No one can settle it beyond dispute.

**What are the 120 years?** Some take this as a new upper limit on human lifespans—and indeed, human ages gradually decline afterward. Others understand it as a countdown of grace before the flood arrives. Peter writes that God waited patiently while the ark was being built (1 Peter 3:20), which gives that second reading real support.

**What does it mean that God "regretted"?** Elsewhere Scripture states that God is not a man that He should change His mind (1 Samuel 15:29). Here the Bible uses human language to convey God's heart, showing us He is never indifferent to evil. It does not mean God miscalculated and wished He could undo a mistake.

**Was wiping out humanity fair?** This is a hard passage to read. The narrative makes clear that God did not lash out in irritation; the earth had grown so saturated with violence that life itself could barely survive. Nor did He act hastily—He warned beforehand and waited. Even so, the question of the innocent, especially children, is a real and heavy question, and believers should never brush it aside with easy answers.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, wrote at length about "the sons of God." He favored the view that they were descendants of Seth—people who belonged to the city of God, yet allowed physical beauty to draw them into union with those living only for this world. He also saw the ark as a symbol of the church, noting that its proportions matched those of the human body, while the door in its side reminded him of the spear wound in Jesus' side. *(The City of God, Book XV, chapters 23 and 26)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: both points are in Book XV and widely cited—chapter 23 on the sons of God and chapter 26 on the ark -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explained the statement that God "regretted." God does not literally change His mind or feel regret as humans do; rather, Scripture stoops to speak in human terms we can grasp, revealing just how deeply God detests sin. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 6 verse 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: his explanation of God's "repentance" as accommodation is a very frequently cited passage on this verse -->

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, compared the biblical flood to ancient Mesopotamian flood stories. In one of those accounts, the gods send a flood because noisy humans are keeping them awake. In Genesis, the reason is wickedness and violence. That contrast, Sarna argued, shows that the Bible proclaims a righteous God who judges the world on moral grounds rather than out of whim. *(Understanding Genesis, chapter on the flood)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Sarna highlights the moral reason for the flood in contrast to the Atrahasis epic; uncertain of exact chapter placement -->

**John Walton**, an American Old Testament professor, leans toward reading "the sons of God" as ancient kings. He points to King Gilgamesh in the Mesopotamian epic, a ruler who both claimed divine blood and asserted the right to take any young woman he pleased. On this reading, the sin of 6:1–4 is unchecked power abusing the vulnerable. *(Genesis, NIV Application Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Walton favoring the royal-ruler view and citing Gilgamesh; unsure whether he commits to it firmly or simply presents it as most likely -->

**Martin Luther** also understood "the sons of God" to be Seth's descendants—people who once possessed God's word, then abandoned it to chase after the world. The giants, he thought, were arrogant tyrants who oppressed the weak.
