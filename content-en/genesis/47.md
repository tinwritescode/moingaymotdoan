# Genesis 47 — Jacob Settles in Goshen

## Story

Jacob's entire household arrives in Egypt and halts in the land of Goshen. Joseph goes to report their arrival to Pharaoh, then picks five of his brothers to present before the king. Pharaoh asks what their occupation is. They reply just as Joseph coached them: "Your servants are shepherds," just as their fathers were before them. They ask permission to stay for a while, because the famine in Canaan is so severe that there is no pasture left for their flocks (47:1–4).

Pharaoh is more generous than they dared ask. He tells Joseph to settle his father and brothers in the finest part of the land—the region of Goshen. He even adds that if any of the brothers are especially capable, Joseph may put them in charge of the royal livestock (47:5–6).

Next, Joseph brings in his father Jacob. The scene holds a quiet surprise: an aging, needy immigrant from a famine-stricken land stands before the ruler of a rich empire—and it is the old man who blesses the king. Pharaoh asks how many years he has lived. Jacob answers that his "travels" have lasted 130 years, adding that his years have been "few and hard," falling short of the lifespans of his fathers. Before withdrawing, Jacob blesses Pharaoh a second time (47:7–10).

Joseph carries out the king's command. He gives his father and brothers property in the finest part of the country, the district of Rameses, and provides food for the whole household, counting every mouth down to the youngest children (47:11–12).

Outside the sheltered pocket of Goshen, famine continues to crush both Egypt and Canaan. People spend all their money buying grain, and Joseph gathers every coin into Pharaoh's palace. Once the money runs out, the Egyptians crowd around him pleading: why should we die before your eyes just because our funds are gone? Joseph tells them to bring their livestock in trade. All that year, their horses, sheep, cattle, and donkeys are traded away for food (47:13–17).

The following year, the people come back. Nothing is left except their own bodies and their land. They themselves ask Joseph to buy both, making them Pharaoh's slaves, so long as they receive seed to plant, stay alive, and keep the fields from turning barren. So all the farmland of Egypt passes into Pharaoh's hands, except for the land of the priests, who live on regular rations from the crown. Joseph hands out seed and establishes a law: at harvest, one-fifth belongs to Pharaoh, and four-fifths remain for the people's own fields and families. The people answer, "You have saved our lives." That one-fifth tax remains in force long afterward (47:18–26).

Meanwhile, Israel's household thrives in Goshen; they acquire property and "became fruitful and increased greatly in number" (47:27). Jacob lives in Egypt for seventeen more years, reaching the age of 147 (47:28).

Sensing that his death is near, he summons Joseph and asks him to place his hand under his thigh and make a solemn promise: "Do not bury me in Egypt." He wants to be carried back and laid beside his fathers. Joseph replies, "I will do as you have requested." His father insists that he swear an oath; Joseph swears, and Jacob bows in worship at the head of his bed (47:29–31).

## Key Takeaways

**God cares for His people in the middle of famine.** While the whole region starves, Jacob's family has rich pasture and plenty of food, measured out for every single mouth. God promised to go down to Egypt with Jacob (46:3–4), and this chapter shows Him keeping His word.

**The weak blesses the strong.** Jacob has no wealth or gifts to offer Pharaoh—only a blessing. Yet Scripture specifically notes that he blesses the king twice. Even when old and poor, someone who belongs to God carries something kings still need.

**Life is a journey.** Jacob does not simply say, "I have lived 130 years"; he speaks of the years of his "travels"—his pilgrimage. He views his whole life as that of a traveler who has not yet reached home. Nor does he sugarcoat it: his years have been short and hard. Believers are allowed to speak honestly about their pain.

**Concentrated power cuts both ways.** Joseph rescues an entire nation from starvation. Yet by the end of the seven years of famine, all the silver, livestock, farmland, and even the people themselves belong to Pharaoh. The story offers no direct verdict, leaving readers to see the real cost of being saved on those terms.

**Jacob dies with his eyes fixed on the Promised Land.** He enjoys seventeen comfortable years in Egypt, yet refuses to be buried there. God promised Canaan to his descendants (12:7), and Jacob wants even his burial to declare that he still believes that promise.

## Deeper Meaning

**Strangers and exiles on the earth.** Looking back on Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the letter to the Hebrews says they died in faith, acknowledging themselves to be "strangers and exiles on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13). They were longing for a better country, a heavenly one (Hebrews 11:16). Jacob's brief answer to Pharaoh brings that truth to life. Centuries later, King David prays in the same spirit (Psalms 39:12), and the apostle Peter addresses Christians as foreigners and exiles passing through (1 Peter 2:11).

**The promise to bless all nations.** God promised Abraham that every family on earth would be blessed through his offspring (12:3). Here we watch the first fruits of that promise: Abraham's grandson blesses the king of Egypt, while his great-grandson keeps the surrounding world alive through famine (41:57). Hebrews sheds light on this scene with a single line: indisputably, the lesser is blessed by the greater (Hebrews 7:7). In God's eyes, the greater figure in that throne room is the old shepherd.

**Rameses today, Rameses tomorrow.** Joseph settles Jacob's family in "the district of Rameses," the choicest part of the country (47:11). A few hundred years later, that very name returns in a darker key: Jacob's descendants are forced into hard labor building the store city of Rameses for a new Pharaoh (Exodus 1:11). This chapter also watches the Egyptians sell themselves into Pharaoh's service. Any reader familiar with Exodus feels a chill: one day Israel itself will wear the chains of slavery in this very land, just as God foretold to Abraham (15:13).

**Who really owns the land?** In Egypt, every acre eventually ends up belonging to the king. The law God later gives Israel moves in the exact opposite direction: land may never be sold permanently, "because it is Mine," and God's people are only foreigners and residents on His land (Leviticus 25:23). Every fifty years, family land had to return to its original heirs (Leviticus 25:13). That law kept wealth and land from pooling forever in the hands of a powerful few.

**Bread without money.** In Egypt, staying alive costs everything: first silver, then livestock, then farmland, and finally one's own freedom. Centuries later, the prophet Isaiah announces a radically different invitation: let even those with no money come, buy, and eat "without money and without cost" (Isaiah 55:1). Jesus declares Himself the bread of life, promising that whoever comes to Him will never hunger (John 6:35). That bread cannot be purchased with silver or acres; we come empty-handed and receive it as a gift.

**Worshiping at the head of his bed.** Hebrews recalls Jacob's closing moments as an act of faith: he "worshiped as he leaned on the top of his staff" (Hebrews 11:21). A dying old man, living as a guest on foreign soil, still bows his head in worship to the God who promised him a land he never got to possess in his lifetime.

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Joseph do the right thing?** Readers split sharply here. One side defends Joseph: because of him an entire nation survived seven brutal years of famine; the people themselves thank him (47:25); and a one-fifth tax may actually have been lighter than the norm at the time. <!-- low: recall many commentaries noting that ancient tenant-farming rates in Mesopotamia commonly ran from one-third to one-half, though unsure of the exact source -->Many note that in neighboring ancient lands, tenant farmers commonly had to hand over a third to half of their harvest.<!-- /low --> Another side feels uneasy: Joseph uses the famine to funnel all wealth and land into Pharaoh's hands, turning a free population into royal serfs. Scripture records the facts without praising or condemning them. Reading the whole narrative, we can both appreciate Joseph's life-saving leadership and feel the sobering weight of how absolute power grows.

**"Servants" or "slaves" here is not the same as chattel slavery.** The Egyptians remain on their own farms, keep working the soil, and keep four-fifths of every harvest for their households. Their status looks closer to royal tenant farmers than to captives bought and sold in chains. Even so, they lost ownership of their land and their independence, and "slaves" or "servants" is the very word they themselves chose.

**One hundred and thirty years described as "few."** Jacob is measuring himself against his grandfather Abraham, who lived 175 years, and his father Isaac, who lived 180 (25:7; 35:28). Yet it is the word "hard" that carries the greatest weight. His life was marked by flight, trickery given and received, the death of his beloved wife, and twenty years believing his favorite son was dead. He refuses to put on a polished face for Pharaoh just to sound blessed.

**Who says, "Swear to me"?** In the original Hebrew of 47:31, the text simply says "he said," without naming the speaker—a detail that has occasionally caused confusion in older translations. From the flow of the scene, the speaker is unmistakably Jacob (as the Berean Standard Bible makes clear), since it is the dying father who binds his son with an oath. Placing a hand under the thigh to swear an oath was an ancient custom; Abraham asked his servant to do the very same thing (24:2).

**Head of his bed or top of his staff?** Genesis says Jacob bowed "at the head of his bed," whereas Hebrews says he worshiped leaning "on the top of his staff." Ancient Hebrew was written using only consonants, without vowels. The Hebrew words for "bed" and "staff" share the exact same three consonants and differ only when pronounced aloud. The ancient Greek translation read the consonants as "staff," and the author of Hebrews quoted that version. Either way, both paint the same moving picture: a frail old man mustering his strength to worship God.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, paused over the word "travels" (or pilgrimage) that Jacob used before Pharaoh. To Calvin, Jacob regarded his entire earthly life as that of a passing traveler, which is how every believer ought to view life in this world. **Leon Kass**, American Jewish scholar, viewed Joseph's economic policy with deep skepticism. In Kass's reading, Joseph has become thoroughly Egyptian—a brilliant statesman serving Pharaoh's absolute rule. By reducing the Egyptian populace to royal servitude, Joseph unwittingly laid the groundwork for the system that would later enslave Jacob's own descendants. *(The Beginning of Wisdom: Reading Genesis, 2003)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Kass portrays Joseph as assimilated into Egypt and critiques his land policy; unsure how explicitly he links it to Israel's later slavery -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament professor, read chapter 47 as a case study in an economy driven by fear of scarcity. Haunted by a dream of famine, Pharaoh hoards everything in royal storehouses. Whoever controls the food soon controls the people, and the farmers step by step surrender their money, livestock, land, and freedom.
