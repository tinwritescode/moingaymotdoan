# Genesis 25 — Abraham Dies, Jacob and Esau Are Born

## Story

After Sarah died, Abraham took another wife named Keturah. She bore him six sons, including Midian, the ancestor of the Midianites. Abraham left everything he owned to Isaac. To his other sons he gave gifts, and while he was still alive he sent them eastward, away from Isaac (25:1–6).

Abraham lived to be a hundred and seventy-five years old. He died at a ripe old age, "old and contented," and was gathered to his people. Both of his sons, Isaac and Ishmael, came together to bury their father in the cave of Machpelah beside Sarah. Afterward God blessed Isaac, who lived near Beer-lahai-roi (25:7–11).

Next comes the line of Ishmael, the son Abraham had by Hagar the Egyptian. Ishmael had twelve sons, each a ruler over his own tribe, just as God had promised. He lived a hundred and thirty-seven years and died. His descendants settled across the desert from Havilah to Shur, near the border of Egypt (25:12–18).

Then the story returns to Isaac. He married Rebekah when he was forty. Just like her mother-in-law before her, Rebekah was unable to have children. Isaac prayed to the LORD for his wife, and the LORD answered him. She conceived twins, yet the two children jostled so violently inside her womb that she went to ask the LORD why this was happening (25:19–22).

The LORD answered: "Two nations are in your womb." Two peoples would come from her, one stronger than the other, "and the older will serve the younger" (25:23). When the time came, the first boy came out red and covered in hair like a fur coat, so they named him Esau. His brother came out right behind him grasping Esau's heel, so he was named Jacob, meaning "he grasps the heel." Isaac was sixty years old when the twins were born (25:24–26).

The two brothers grew up completely different. Esau became a skillful hunter, a man of the open field. Jacob was a quiet man who preferred staying home among the tents. Their parents took sides too: Isaac loved Esau because he had a taste for wild game, while Rebekah loved Jacob (25:27–28).

One day Jacob was cooking stew when Esau came in from the field exhausted and starving. Esau said, "Let me eat some of that red stew, for I am famished." That is why he was also called Edom, meaning "red" (25:29–30). Jacob did not hand it over right away. He named his price: first sell me your birthright. Esau answered, I am about to die—what good is a birthright to me? Jacob made him swear an oath to seal the deal. Esau swore, ate some bread and lentil stew, then got up and walked away. Scripture sums it up in one sharp line: "Thus Esau despised his birthright" (25:31–34).

## Key Takeaways

**A life closed in peace.** God had promised Abraham he would go to his fathers in peace and be buried "at a ripe old age" (15:15). That promise came true. He had not yet seen descendants numerous as the stars, nor did he own the whole land, yet he died deeply content (25:8).

**God keeps His promises even outside the main line.** Ishmael was not the child of the covenant promise, yet God still kept every word He had spoken about him: twelve tribal rulers (17:20; 25:16). God chooses one line for His great purpose, yet He never forgets the rest.

**Children are a gift from God, not something taken for granted.** Isaac waited twenty years to have children (25:20; 25:26). Just like his parents before him, he had to pray. The line of promise does not come through human strength, but through prayer answered by God.

**God chooses against human expectations.** By custom, the firstborn received the greater share. Yet before the twins were even born, God declared that "the older will serve the younger" (25:23). From Cain and Abel, to Ishmael and Isaac, to Esau and Jacob, God repeatedly chooses the younger one.

**Trading what lasts for what satisfies right now.** Esau traded a lifelong inheritance for a single bowl of stew. His hunger was real, yet nobody dies from waiting for one more meal. Esau's failure was not being hungry; it was treating something priceless as cheap in a moment of craving.

## Deeper Meaning

**Chosen before doing anything at all.** Paul uses this story to show that God's choice does not rest on human performance. Before the twins were born or "had done anything good or bad," God had already declared that the older would serve the younger (Romans 9:11–12). Jacob was not chosen because he was better; the coming chapters will reveal plenty of his flaws. He was chosen by grace.

**"Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated."** Paul quotes the prophet Malachi (Romans 9:13; Malachi 1:2–3). In Malachi, that saying refers to two nations—Israel and Edom—not merely two individuals. In Hebrew idiom, "hated" here means "not chosen," much like when Jesus says His followers must "hate" their own parents—meaning love Him above everything else (Luke 14:26).

**Do not be like Esau.** The letter to the Hebrews warns readers to make sure no one is "godless like Esau, who for a single meal sold his birthright" (Hebrews 12:16). It goes on to remind us that later, when Esau wept and wanted the blessing back, he could not get it (Hebrews 12:17). Some impulsive choices cannot be undone.

**The heel-grasper.** The prophet Hosea recalls Jacob grasping his brother's heel in the womb (Hosea 12:3). That name followed Jacob through half his life: he was a striver, always angling to get ahead. Only when he wrestled with God would he finally receive a new name (32:28).

**Two brothers, two nations.** God's words to Rebekah about "two nations" played out across centuries of history. Edom, Esau's descendants, and Israel, Jacob's descendants, were neighbors yet frequent enemies. King David later subdued Edom (2 Samuel 8:14), and the entire book of Obadiah is a prophecy against Edom.

## Notes & Nuances

**Was Keturah a wife or a concubine?** Verse 25:1 calls her a wife, while Chronicles refers to her as Abraham's "concubine" (1 Chronicles 1:32), and verse 25:6 mentions "the sons of his concubines." It is also unclear whether he married her after Sarah's death or earlier. Biblical storytelling often groups events by theme rather than strict calendar order.

**Jacob is not the hero here.** Some assume that because God chose Jacob, everything Jacob did must have been right. Look closer, though, and Jacob is taking advantage of an exhausted, hungry brother to grab what he wants. Scripture tells the story without praising him. The closing line blames Esau, yet that hardly makes Jacob innocent.

**What does "quiet" mean?** Verse 25:27 calls Jacob a "quiet man" (some translations say "mild" or "peaceful"). The Hebrew word here can mean settled, even-tempered, and content to stay at home, in contrast to Esau who belonged out in the open country. It should not be read as saying Jacob was morally better than his brother.

**Parents playing favorites.** "Isaac loved Esau… but Rebekah loved Jacob" (25:28). That single brief sentence plants the seed for the entire tragedy of chapter 27. Scripture adds no commentary, letting the fallout speak for itself.

**What was the birthright?** The oldest son normally received a double share of the inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:17) and became head of the family after his father died. In Abraham's family, that birthright was also tied to God's covenant promise. Treating it lightly meant treating God's promise lightly too.
