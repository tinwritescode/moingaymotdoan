# Genesis 18 — Three Visitors and a Plea for Sodom

## Story

In the blazing heat of midday, Abraham was sitting at the entrance of his tent under the Oaks of Mamre. Looking up, he saw three men standing nearby. At once he ran to meet them, bowed low to the ground, and urged them to stop. He offered water to wash their feet, shade beneath the tree, and "a bit of bread" to refresh them before they went on. The three travelers agreed (18:1–5).

"A bit of bread" turned into a full-blown banquet. Abraham hurried into the tent and told Sarah to bake bread from three seahs of fine flour; then he ran out to the herd, picked a tender, choice calf, and handed it to a servant to prepare. He set curds, milk, and the roasted calf before his guests, and stood beside them under the tree while they ate (18:6–8). Reading the scene as a whole, we learn that one of those three visitors was the Lord Himself (18:1; 18:22).

The visitors asked, "Where is your wife Sarah?" Then one of them declared that he would return this time next year, and Sarah would have a son. Sarah was listening behind the tent entrance. She was old, long past the age of bearing children. She laughed quietly to herself: worn out as I am, and with my husband so old, could I really know that joy now? (18:9–12).

The Lord asked Abraham why Sarah had laughed. Then He asked a question that rings across the whole Bible: "Is anything too difficult for the LORD?" He repeated the promise: at the appointed time next year, Sarah would have a son. Frightened, Sarah denied it: "I did not laugh." The Lord answered simply, "No, but you did laugh" (18:13–15).

The three men rose to leave and looked out toward Sodom, while Abraham walked alongside to see them off. The Lord wondered aloud whether He should hide from Abraham what He was about to do. After all, He had chosen Abraham to teach his children and household to keep the Lord's way by doing "what is right and just." So God told him: the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah has grown overwhelming, and their sin is grievous; He was going down to see for Himself (18:16–21).

Two of the men walked on toward Sodom, while Abraham stayed standing before the Lord. He stepped closer and asked: will You really sweep away the righteous along with the wicked? What if there are fifty righteous people in the city? Then he ventured a stunningly bold question: "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?" God answered: if I find fifty righteous people in Sodom, I will spare the entire city for their sake (18:22–26).

Abraham knew he was "but dust and ashes," yet he dared to press on. What about forty-five? Forty? Thirty? Twenty? Each time he pleaded for the Lord not to grow angry, and each time God said yes. At last he asked: what if only ten are found there? The Lord promised that even for ten He would not destroy the city. Then the Lord departed, and Abraham returned home (18:27–33).

## Key Takeaways

**Hospitality opens the door for God to step in.** Abraham had no idea who his guests were. He simply spotted three travelers walking through the midday heat and ran out to welcome them. He spoke modestly—"a bit of bread"—and gave lavishly. Right inside that ordinary meal, God arrived and spoke His promise.

**Nothing is too hard for God.** Sarah laughed because having a baby at her age defied every law of nature. God did not lecture her; He asked one piercing question. That question sets every human impossibility directly in front of an almighty God.

**God notices even a silent laugh.** Sarah laughed silently behind a tent flap, yet God heard it. Out of fear, she denied it. God did not punish her, nor did He let the denial slide. He brought her face to face with the truth so she could move on to trust.

**God shares His heart with His friend.** God let Abraham in on what was coming to Sodom because Abraham had been chosen to train his descendants in justice. To teach justice well, he needed to see firsthand how God practices justice.

**Intercession means standing between God and others.** Abraham was not praying for himself. He was pleading for a corrupt city where his nephew Lot lived. He prayed with striking boldness wrapped in deep humility, and God listened patiently to the very last word.

## Deeper Meaning

**Welcoming angels without knowing it.** The letter to the Hebrews urges believers never to neglect hospitality, because through it some people have entertained angels without knowing it (Hebrews 13:2). Readers have long recognized Abraham under the oaks of Mamre—and Lot in the very next chapter (19:1–3)—in those words.

**A question echoed in Nazareth.** "Is anything too difficult for the LORD?" Nearly two thousand years later, an angel announced to a young virgin that she would bear a son. The angel assured her that nothing is impossible with God—for "no word from God will ever fail" (Luke 1:37). Sarah bore a child in old age; Mary bore a child as a virgin. Both times, God stepped straight past human impossibility to carry His promise forward.

**Friend of God.** God refused to keep His plans hidden from Abraham. Later, Scripture calls him "a friend of God" (James 2:23; cf. Isaiah 41:8). Jesus spoke the very same language to His disciples: I have called you friends, because everything I learned from My Father I have made known to you (John 15:15).

**Standing in the gap.** Abraham is the first person in Scripture to intercede at length for other people. Centuries later, Moses stood before God pleading for Israel, and God relented (Exodus 32:11–14). The New Testament says Jesus lives forever to intercede for everyone who draws near to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25).

**From ten people down to one.** Abraham stopped at ten. Centuries later, through the prophet Jeremiah, God told people to search the streets of Jerusalem for even one righteous person, and He would forgive the whole city (Jeremiah 5:1). The New Testament points to the one truly righteous Man whose obedience makes many righteous (Romans 5:19).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who were the three visitors?** The chapter opens with "the LORD appeared to Abraham" (18:1), yet Abraham sees three men. The next chapter calls the two who reach Sodom "the two angels" (19:1). The most common view is that Abraham hosted the Lord accompanied by two angels. Many early Christians saw this as the Son of God appearing before His birth in Bethlehem, while others saw an image of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—immortalized in Andrei Rublev's famous Russian icon. Scripture leaves the mystery open, so we should avoid speaking more dogmatically than the text does.

**God "going down to see" does not mean He was uninformed.** Scripture often speaks of God in human terms, just as it pictured Him coming down to inspect the tower of Babel (11:5). The point is that God never judges rashly or on rumor; like a careful judge, He examines the facts before passing sentence.

**Abraham is not haggling with a stingy God.** The back-and-forth sounds like bargaining at a market stall, yet God never pushes back once. Every time Abraham lowers the number, God agrees immediately. Abraham is the one who stops. The scene reveals God's mercy, not a strategy for wearing Him down.

**Why did Abraham stop at ten?** Scripture does not say. Some think ten represented the minimum number needed to form a community. Others suggest Abraham figured Lot's household plus a few acquaintances would add up to ten. The next chapter shows the outcome: there were not even ten, yet God still remembered Abraham and pulled Lot out of the catastrophe (19:29).

**Sarah is not written off as unbelieving.** She laughed and she denied it, yet Hebrews still places Sarah in its roll call of faith, because in the end she trusted that the One who promised was faithful (Hebrews 11:11).

While open to seeing an intimation of the Trinity here, Augustine cautioned against using this passage to prove that only the Son appeared. **Robert Alter**, the American Jewish scholar, caught a subtle shift in wording. Sarah laughed and said to herself that she was worn out and her husband was old (18:12). Yet when God repeated her words to Abraham, He quoted only "when I am old," quietly dropping her jab at her husband's age (18:13).
