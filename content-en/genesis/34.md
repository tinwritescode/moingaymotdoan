# Genesis 34 — Dinah and the Vengeance at Shechem

## Story

Jacob pitched his camp near the city of Shechem. One day, Dinah, his daughter by Leah, went out to visit the young women of the region. Shechem, the son of Hamor and prince of that area, saw her. He seized her, slept with her by force, and humiliated her (34:1–2).

Afterward, Shechem's heart was drawn to Dinah. He loved her, spoke tenderly to her, and urged his father Hamor to get her as his wife (34:3–4). Scripture tells of the crime first and the affection second; nothing in that order erases what he did.

Word reached Jacob that his daughter had been defiled. Because his sons were out in the field tending the livestock, he kept silent and waited for them to return (34:5). Meanwhile, Hamor came to talk with Jacob about arranging a marriage.

When Jacob's sons came in from the field and heard what had happened, they were filled with grief and fury. Shechem had committed an outrage in Israel—"a thing that should not be done" (34:6–7).

Hamor spoke smoothly. My son longs for your daughter, he said; please give her to him in marriage. Let our two peoples intermarry—give us your daughters and take ours—and settle freely in our land, trade here, and acquire property. Shechem chimed in: ask whatever dowry or gift you want, and I will pay it, just give me the girl as my wife (34:8–12). Neither father nor son uttered a single word of apology. And throughout the entire negotiation, Dinah was still inside Shechem's house.

Jacob's sons answered with a trap. We cannot give our sister to an uncircumcised man, they said; that would be a disgrace to us. Only if every male in your city is circumcised like us will we intermarry with you and become one people. Otherwise, we will take our sister and leave (34:13–17).

Hamor and Shechem agreed at once. Father and son went to the city gate to persuade their townspeople: these men are peaceful, let them live among us. Their only condition is circumcision. Then they quietly added the real bait: won't all their livestock and possessions eventually become ours? Every male in the city listened and agreed to be circumcised (34:18–24).

On the third day, while the men of the city were still in severe pain, Simeon and Levi, two of Dinah's full brothers, took their swords and entered the unsuspecting city. They slaughtered every male, killed Hamor and Shechem, and brought Dinah out of Shechem's house (34:25–26).

The rest of Jacob's sons came in behind them and looted the town. They took all the sheep, cattle, donkeys, and every valuable thing in the city and the fields, and carried off all the women and children (34:27–29). One man had committed a crime, and an entire town paid the price.

Jacob rebuked Simeon and Levi: you have brought trouble on me and made me hated by the people of this land. Our numbers are small; if they band together and attack, my entire household will be wiped out. His two sons answered with a single question—and the chapter ends right there, with no reply: "Should he have treated our sister like a prostitute?" (34:30–31).

## Key Takeaways

**A wedding cannot erase a crime.** Shechem violated Dinah first and asked to marry her afterward, without a single word of remorse. Father and son tried to smooth things over with bride-price and business deals while Dinah remained stuck in Shechem's house throughout the talks (34:2–4; 34:11–12; 34:26).

**A father's silence makes everything worse.** Jacob hears that his daughter has been harmed yet says nothing, and even when Hamor arrives he leaves his sons to handle the confrontation (34:5–7; 34:13). Once disaster erupts, his first concern is his own reputation and safety rather than Dinah's suffering (34:30).

**Turning a sacred sign into a trap is a deep offense.** Circumcision was the sign of the covenant God made with Abraham (17:10–11). Jacob's sons twisted the mark of belonging to God into a ploy to leave their enemies defenseless and slaughter them (34:13–15; 34:25).

**Righteous anger can twist into an even greater evil.** Simeon and Levi had every reason to burn with anger for their sister, yet they massacred an entire city of men, and their brothers followed behind to plunder and take women and children captive (34:25–29). Unchecked vengeance only spills more blood.

**God's name is never spoken once in the entire chapter.** This is one of the rare episodes in Genesis where God is completely absent from both the words and the choices of Jacob's family. No one asks for His guidance, and everyone acts purely out of fear or rage.

## Deeper Meaning

**Jacob's deathbed verdict on Simeon and Levi.** Years later, on his deathbed, Jacob has not forgotten what happened at Shechem. He curses the fierce anger of those two sons and declares they will be scattered throughout Israel (49:5–7). Indeed, the tribe of Simeon eventually faded into Judah's territory, and the tribe of Levi received no land of its own—yet God's grace turned Levi's scattering into the high calling of serving as priests distributed among all the tribes (Numbers 18:20; Joshua 19:1).

**The danger of losing covenant identity.** Hamor's invitation—to intermarry and become a single people (34:16)—actually threatened to swallow up the distinct identity of Abraham's line. The Law of Moses later forbade Israel from intermarrying or making covenants with the Canaanite nations for that exact reason, lest they be drawn away after foreign gods (Deuteronomy 7:3–4).

**Jesus breaks the cycle of hatred in this very region.** Shechem stood right in the heart of what would become Samaria. Centuries later, at Jacob's well just outside Shechem, Jesus sat down with a Samaritan woman and brought living water to an entire town (John 4:5–6; 4:39–42). The place once soaked in deceit and bloodshed became the setting where the gospel crossed ethnic lines.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does the story blame Dinah for going out?** Some older readers suggested Dinah was at fault for going out to visit the local women (34:1). Yet the biblical text breathes not a single word of criticism against Dinah; all the guilt rests squarely on Shechem for seizing and violating her (34:2; 34:7).

**Why does the chapter end abruptly with Simeon and Levi's question?** Scripture records no answer from Jacob (34:31). That open ending leaves a real moral tension hanging in the air: the brothers' massacre was brutal and wrong, yet their outrage at their father's passivity cannot be brushed aside lightly either.

**How could two men kill an entire city?** A Canaanite city in the era of the patriarchs was usually small—more like a fortified village with a few dozen to a few hundred men. On the third day after circumcision, swelling and pain are at their worst, allowing two armed men striking by surprise to overwhelm men who had no strength to fight back (34:25).

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor and commentator of the early eighteenth century, treats the episode as a warning about unrestrained anger. **Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish biblical scholar, notes that Dinah never speaks a word in the entire story and is only brought out of Shechem's house after the slaughter (34:26). **Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, highlights the complete absence of God's name in chapter 34.
