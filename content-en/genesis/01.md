# Genesis 1 — God Creates the Heavens and the Earth

## Story

In the beginning, when nothing existed yet, God created the heavens and the earth (1:1).

At first, however, the earth had no real shape. The Bible describes it as "formless and void"—without form, and with nothing living upon it. Darkness lay over everything, and there was only deep water. The Spirit of God was hovering over the surface of the waters, like a mother bird spreading her wings over her nest (1:2; cf. Deuteronomy 32:11–12).

Then God said, "Let there be light," and there was light (1:3). He needed no raw materials and no tools. He simply spoke, and what He spoke came to be.

In this way, across six days, God arranged the world. During the first three days, He built the "homes." On day one, He separated light from darkness. On day two, He separated the sky from the waters. On day three, He gathered the waters into seas so that dry land could appear, and then called forth plants and trees (1:3–13).

During the next three days, He placed the "inhabitants" into each of those homes. On day four, He set the sun, moon, and stars in the sky. On day five, He filled the waters with fish and the sky with birds. On day six, He brought forth animals to move across the land (1:14–25).

At the close of the sixth day, God did something altogether different. He said, "Let Us make man in Our image, after Our likeness" (1:26). He created them male and female, blessed them, and placed the whole earth in their care (1:27–28). He gave plants and fruit as food for human beings and for every living creature (1:29–30).

When everything was finished, God looked over all that He had made, and saw that it was "very good" (1:31).

## Key Takeaways

**The world has a Creator.** The heavens and the earth did not simply happen on their own. Behind all things stands Someone who wanted them to exist and called them into being by His word.

**God turns chaos into order.** At first the earth was "formless," and God gave it form. It was "void," and God filled it. Wherever God goes to work, confusion becomes order, and empty places fill with life.

**The physical world is good.** Seven times in this chapter, Scripture says God saw that what He had made was good. Our bodies, nature, food, and drink are not evil things to be rejected. They are gifts God crafted, and He delights in them.

**Human beings are made like God.** Plants, fish, birds, and animals were all made by God, yet only human beings are made "in His own image." That holds true for men and women alike, for rich and poor alike. No one enters the world worth less than anyone else.

**Humanity is given a calling.** God tells human beings to be fruitful, fill the earth, and care for its creatures (1:28). The earth does not belong to us. We are only stewards, tending the land on behalf of its true Owner, who is God.

## Deeper Meaning

**"In the beginning" and Jesus.** The Gospel of John in the New Testament opens with the exact same words as Genesis: "In the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1). In Genesis 1, God creates everything by speaking His word. John reveals that this Word is a Person—Jesus Himself—and that through Him all things were made (John 1:3). Set the two books side by side, and we see that the One who later came as a human being is the very One who made the world.

**"Let Us make."** There is only one God, so why does He say "Us"? From early times, many readers have seen here a glimpse of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Within a single passage we meet God, His spoken word, and His Spirit hovering over the waters. Scripture does not spell out the whole truth here, but it already leaves a quiet trace.

**The sun is just a lamp.** In those days, the nations surrounding Israel worshiped the sun and moon as gods. Genesis 1 does not even bother to name them. It simply calls them the greater light and the lesser light (1:16), like two lamps God hung up to light the day and the night. Ancient listeners would have caught the point immediately: the things everyone else worships are merely objects God made.

**The world was born from a word of peace.** Babylonian mythology also told a story of how the world came to be, but it was drenched in blood. One god killed a goddess, sliced her body apart to form sky and earth, and molded human beings to serve as slaves waiting on the gods. Genesis tells a completely different story. The world comes into being through a peaceful spoken word, and human beings are made to be blessed.

**Every person is an image of God.** In the ancient world, a king would set up statues of himself across the cities he ruled, so anyone passing by would know who owned the land. Genesis 1 says every human being is a living "statue" of God on earth. That is why harming another person is an offense against God Himself (9:6). The New Testament calls Jesus the image of the invisible God (Colossians 1:15). Looking at Him, we see what human beings were always meant to be.

**Light came before the sun.** Light appears on day one, while the sun is not made until day four. Some people treat this as an absurdity. Yet Scripture may be making a deeper point: the true source of light is not the sun, but God. On the final page of the Bible, the new city needs no sun at all, because the Lord God gives it light (Revelation 22:5). The apostle Paul writes along the same lines: the God who commanded light to shine out of darkness has also shone into human hearts (2 Corinthians 4:6). What God once did for the world, He can still do for a darkened soul.

**Six days of work, one day of rest.** This rhythm later became the commandment to keep the Sabbath (Exodus 20:11). Human beings are not machines. We were designed to work and then rest, just as God Himself did.

## Notes & Nuances

**Don't force this chapter to be a science textbook.** People constantly ask, "Is a day here twenty-four hours or millions of years?" Believers in Scripture answer that question in different ways. Some take them as ordinary days, some as long ages, and others see the six days as a literary framework that makes the story memorable. But this chapter was not written to answer "how long" or "by what process." It answers bigger questions: Who made the world? Why? Who are we? On those questions, all who trust Scripture stand together.

**"Us" can be understood in more than one way.** Beyond seeing the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit here, some take "Us" as the royal plural used by kings, while others think God is addressing the angels gathered around Him. A single verse is not enough to insist on one answer too dogmatically.

**"Rule" does not mean doing whatever we please.** Verse 1:28 tells humanity to rule over every living creature. Yet the very next chapter clarifies what that looks like: God places the man in the garden to "cultivate and keep it" (2:15). A faithful steward nurtures what is entrusted to him; he does not strip it bare.

**Day two has no "it was good."** On every other day God declares His work good, except on the second day. The most common explanation is that the work of separating the waters was still unfinished and only reached completion on day three. Accordingly, day three receives "it was good" twice (1:10; 1:12).

**The story does not end at the close of chapter 1.** The seventh day, when God rested, sits at the start of chapter 2 (2:1–3). Chapter divisions were added much later, around the thirteenth century, and often cut awkwardly across the flow of a passage. It is best to read straight through to 2:3.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa who lived more than 1,600 years ago, ran into a question skeptics loved to ask: "What was God doing before He made heaven and earth?" Someone joked that God was preparing hell for people who pry into such things. Augustine refused to settle for a joke. He argued that the question was flawed from the start, because time itself is something God created. God made the world *with* time, not *at some point* within time. Augustine also warned Christians not to talk recklessly about the natural world when they do not understand it, lest outsiders hear their nonsense and dismiss Scripture along with it. *(Confessions, Book XI; De Genesi ad litteram, I.19)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Confessions XI.12–13 and De Genesi ad litteram I.19 are very widely quoted -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, noticed that Scripture calls the moon one of the "two great lights," even though astronomers of his day already knew Saturn was far larger than the moon. He explained that Moses wrote for ordinary people, describing things as they appear to the naked eye rather than writing a treatise for astronomers. Calvin also pictured the world as a magnificent theater where God displays His glory for everyone to behold. *(Commentary on Genesis, 1554)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the Saturn remark is likely in his commentary on 1:16; the theater image is definitely Calvin's, though unclear whether from the commentary or the Institutes -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, observed that the opening sentence of the Bible speaks with greater certainty about the origin of the universe than all the books of the philosophers combined. And if God made us, he added, then we belong to Him. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: main idea on 1:1 remembered, exact phrasing uncertain -->

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, a German pastor executed by the Nazis in 1945, pointed out that no human being can stand "before the beginning" to watch it happen. We only know about the beginning because God tells us. In his view, being created "in the image" of God means being created to live in freedom for others—something seen most clearly when man and woman stand side by side. *(Creation and Fall, 1933)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: both ideas—about the beginning and freedom to live for others—are central to the book -->

**Tim Keller**, a pastor in New York, wrote that before the world existed, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit already loved one another and delighted in one another. God did not create the world out of loneliness or lack, but out of a desire to share that joy. He also viewed Genesis 1 as written in elevated, almost poetic prose, while still affirming Adam and Eve as real historical people. *(The Reason for God, chapter 14; BioLogos paper "Creation, Evolution, and Christian Laypeople")* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Trinity point is in chapter 14; view on Genesis 1 and a historical Adam is in the BioLogos paper -->

**John Walton**, an Old Testament professor, reads Genesis 1 as the inauguration of a cosmic temple. Across the first six days, God arranges everything so the world can function. On the seventh day God "rests," meaning He takes up residence and begins to rule, like a king taking His seat upon the throne. *(The Lost World of Genesis One, 2009)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: this is the central thesis of the entire book -->
