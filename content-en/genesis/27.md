# Genesis 27 — The Stolen Blessing

## Story

Isaac had grown old, and his eyes were so weak he could no longer see. He called his older son Esau and said: I am old and do not know when I will die. Take your bow and quiver, go out and hunt some wild game, and prepare the tasty dish I love so I can eat and bless you before I die (27:1–4).

Rebekah overheard. As soon as Esau left for the field, she called Jacob and laid out a scheme. Go fetch two choice young goats, and I will cook the dish your father loves; take it in for him to eat so he will bless you instead (27:5–10). Jacob did not worry about right or wrong—only about getting caught. My brother is hairy, and I am smooth-skinned. If Father touches me, I will bring a curse on myself instead of a blessing. She replied, "Your curse be on me, my son" (27:11–13).

Jacob obeyed. Rebekah cooked the meal, dressed Jacob in Esau's finest clothes, and wrapped goatskins around his hands and smooth neck so he would feel hairy like his brother (27:14–17). Jacob carried the food in. His father asked, which one are you? He answered, "I am Esau, your firstborn." Isaac wondered how he had found game so quickly. Jacob even dared to bring God's name into his lie: because the LORD your God brought it right to me (27:18–20).

Isaac was still suspicious. He told his son to come close so he could touch him, then muttered, "The voice is the voice of Jacob, but the hands are the hands of Esau." He asked one more time if he was really Esau, and Jacob lied again (27:21–24). Isaac ate, drank wine, and asked his son to come close and kiss him. Catching the scent of Esau's clothes, he spoke the blessing: may God give you the dew of heaven and the richness of the earth, abundant grain and new wine; may peoples serve you and your brothers bow down to you; may those who curse you be cursed and those who bless you be blessed (27:25–29).

Jacob had barely stepped outside when Esau walked in from the hunt. He too cooked a tasty meal and brought it in for his father's blessing. Isaac began to tremble violently and cried out: who was it, then, who brought me game? I already ate it and blessed him—"and indeed, he will be blessed!" (27:30–33). Esau let out a loud, bitter cry, begging his father to bless him too. He said his brother was rightly named "heel-grasper": first he took my birthright, and now he has taken my blessing (27:34–36).

Isaac answered: I have made him your master—what is left for me to give you? Esau asked: do you have only one blessing, Father? Then he broke down and wept aloud (27:37–38). Finally Isaac spoke over Esau: your dwelling will be away from the richness of the earth and the dew of heaven. You will live by the sword and serve your brother. Yet the day will come when you rebel and tear his yoke off your neck (27:39–40).

From then on Esau hated Jacob. He told himself: the days of mourning for my father are near; then I will kill my brother. Someone passed those words on to Rebekah. She called Jacob and urged him to flee to her brother Laban in Haran, and stay there "for a while" until his brother's fury cooled. Why, she asked, should I lose both of you in one day (27:41–45)? Then she went to Isaac and complained: I am sick of living because of these Hittite women; if Jacob marries one of them too, what good is my life (27:46)? She never breathed a word about Esau plotting murder.

## Key Takeaways

**Nobody in this house has clean hands.** Isaac tries to hand the blessing to Esau behind closed doors, even though God had said the older would serve the younger. Rebekah schemes to deceive her own husband. Jacob lies to his father three times, once dragging God's name into it. Esau despised his birthright earlier, yet still wants the blessing tied to it. Four people, and every single one is at fault.

**A good goal never justifies a crooked method.** Rebekah likely remembered the word God spoke while she was pregnant (25:23). Yet instead of trusting God to keep His promise, she took matters into her own hands through trickery. God's promises never need human lies to come true.

**A divided family tears itself apart.** Each parent favored a different child (25:28), and now each acts behind the other's back. The bill comes due in full: a brother plotting murder, a mother sending her favorite son into exile, and a household shattered.

**God still carries out His purpose.** Even when people deceive one another, God's plan marches on. Isaac realizes it in that trembling moment when he says, "and indeed, he will be blessed!" (27:33). God does not excuse deception, yet He does not let deception derail His promise either.

**The ache of the one left out.** Esau's cry is one of the most heartbreaking sounds in all of Scripture (27:34; 27:38). The Bible does not brush his pain aside. Esau had his faults, yet he was genuinely wronged too.

## Deeper Meaning

**You reap what you sow.** Jacob fooled his father with goatskins and his brother's clothes. Years later, Uncle Laban will trick him on his wedding night (29:25). Later still, Jacob's own sons will deceive him with goat's blood and Joseph's robe (37:31–33). As Paul writes, "Whatever a man sows, he will reap in return" (Galatians 6:7). And Rebekah, who promised Jacob would only be gone "for a while," probably never saw her beloved son again: Scripture never records a reunion.

**Blessing by faith.** The letter to the Hebrews says, "By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning the future" (Hebrews 11:20). Though deceived, once Isaac saw the truth, he did not try to take the blessing back. He accepted that God's will had stood, and right there is where his faith shines through.

**The blessing joins the ancient promise.** The words "May those who curse you be cursed, and those who bless you be blessed" (27:29) repeat what God had promised Abraham (12:3). Jacob's blessing is not merely about wealth; it carries God's covenant promise onward through this line.

**Edom breaks the yoke.** Isaac's word over Esau came true in history. Edom served Israel for a time under King David, yet during the reign of King Joram they revolted, crowned their own king, and broke free (2 Kings 8:20–22).

**Human schemes, God's purpose.** Proverbs says, "Many plans are in a man's heart, but the purpose of the LORD will prevail" (Proverbs 19:21). Chapter 27 is a vivid case study: everyone in the tent has a scheme of their own, yet in the end only God's purpose stands.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why didn't Isaac just take the blessing back?** Modern readers wonder why a blessing obtained by fraud couldn't simply be canceled. In the ancient world, a father's deathbed blessing carried real binding force, much like a legal will. Deeper still, Isaac recognized God's hand in what had happened. He "began to tremble violently" (27:33)—the Hebrew describes a deep, uncontrollable shaking—because he realized he had been trying to work against God's will.

**Scripture records without endorsing.** Jacob is the ancestor of Israel, yet the Bible lays bare his deception without sugarcoating a thing. The chapters ahead show the steep price he pays: twenty years away from home, cheated again and again. Never read this story as permission to lie for a good cause.

**"Away from" or "of" the earth's richness?** The Hebrew phrasing in Isaac's word to Esau can be read two ways: your home will be "away from" rich land, or it will share "of" rich land. The Berean Standard Bible chooses "away from" (27:39). The rest of the word—living by the sword and serving his brother—fits that reading better, and Edom itself was rugged, arid hill country southeast of the Dead Sea.

**Rebekah holds back half the truth.** When asking her husband to send Jacob away, she brings up only the question of marriage, never mentioning Esau's plan to kill him (27:46). What she says is not false, yet it is not the whole story. Even when trying to protect her son, she still leans on half-truths.

He argues that Jacob's words were not a lie, but a "mystery": the younger brother wearing the older brother's clothes to receive a blessing serves as a symbol of those who receive God's blessing by being clothed in another.
