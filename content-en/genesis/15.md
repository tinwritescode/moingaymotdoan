# Genesis 15 — The Stars and the Fire in the Night

## Story

After the battle, God came to Abram in a vision—something shown to him like a dream while awake. He said, "Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your very great reward" (15:1).

Abram did not hide his ache. He answered: what good can anything You give me do when I remain childless? The heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus, a servant born in my household (15:2–3).

God replied: that man will not be your heir; a son from your own body will inherit from you. Then He brought Abram outside beneath the night sky and told him: look up at the heavens and count the stars, if you can. Your offspring will be just like that (15:4–5).

Then comes one brief sentence the entire New Testament will echo: "Abram believed the LORD, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (15:6). Abram held no proof in his hands—nothing but God's word and a sky scattered with stars.

God reminded him: I am the Lord who brought you out of Ur to give you this land. Abram asked: how can I know for certain that I will possess it? God instructed him to bring a heifer, a goat, and a ram, each three years old, along with a turtledove and a young pigeon. Abram cut the animals in half and laid the pieces facing each other, leaving the birds undivided. When birds of prey swooped down on the carcasses, he drove them off (15:7–11).

As the sun went down, Abram fell into a deep sleep, and a thick, dreadful darkness closed over him. God spoke to him about the years ahead: his descendants would live as foreigners in a land not their own, enslaved and mistreated for four hundred years. Then God would judge that nation, and Abram's descendants would come out with great wealth. Abram himself, however, would go to his grave in peace at a ripe old age (15:12–15).

God explained the long delay: "for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete." Only in the fourth generation would Abram's descendants return to this land (15:16).

Once full darkness fell, a smoking firepot and a flaming torch passed right between the divided animal pieces. On that day, the Lord cut a covenant with Abram, promising his descendants the entire stretch of territory from the river of Egypt to the great River Euphrates—the land of ten peoples living there (15:17–21).

## Key Takeaways

**Faith is trusting God's word when you see nothing yet.** Abram was old, and his wife was barren. He had nothing to lean on except a promise. He believed, and God counted that trust as righteousness. He was counted righteous not because of good deeds, but because he believed.

**Believers are allowed to ask hard questions.** Abram voiced his sorrow and questioned God twice. God did not scold him. He answered the first question with the stars, and the second with a solemn covenant. Honest doubt can lead into deeper faith.

**God binds Himself to His own promise.** In this covenant ceremony, only God's fire passed between the animal halves. Abram was fast asleep. God took the entire weight of keeping the covenant onto Himself.

**God's promise is sure, even when it is unhurried.** God did not promise instant results. Abram's descendants would endure four hundred years of hardship. God always keeps His word, yet He keeps it on His own timetable.

**God deals fairly even with outsiders.** God held off driving out the Amorites because their sin "is not yet complete." He waited and gave them time. God's judgment is never hasty or unjust.

## Deeper Meaning

**Paul's cornerstone verse.** The apostle Paul built his whole case for salvation on 15:6: "Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Romans 4:3). Paul stressed that Abraham was counted righteous long before he was circumcised and long before the law was given. Therefore, everyone who believes is a true child of Abraham (Galatians 3:6–7).

**Real faith shows up in how we live.** James quotes the very same verse, adding that Abraham's faith became unmistakable through his obedience, and he was called "a friend of God" (James 2:23). Paul speaks of the root of righteousness; James speaks of its fruit.

**Walking between the divided animals.** The prophet Jeremiah sheds light on this ritual: people ratified a covenant by cutting a calf in two and walking between its pieces (Jeremiah 34:18). Whoever walked that path was declaring, in effect: if I break this promise, let me be torn apart like this animal. In chapter 15, God Himself walks through.

**Hoping against hope.** Paul described Abraham's trust under that starry sky: "Against all hope, Abraham in hope believed" (Romans 4:18). The letter to the Hebrews adds that God backed His promise with an oath so its heirs could see beyond doubt that His purpose never changes (Hebrews 6:17).

**From prophecy to exodus.** God's word about four hundred years of slavery and leaving with great wealth came true in the book of Exodus (Exodus 12:40). The smoking firepot and blazing torch in the night also foreshadow the pillars of cloud and fire that would guide Israel through the wilderness.

## Notes & Nuances

**What does "credited to him as righteousness" mean?** The word "credited" belongs to the world of bookkeeping: God enters Abram's trust onto the ledger as righteousness. It does not mean Abram was flawless—in the very next chapter he stumbles badly. It means God accepts him as standing right in relationship with Him, on the basis of faith.

**Four hundred years or four hundred and thirty?** Here God speaks of four hundred years; Exodus states that Israel stayed in Egypt four hundred and thirty years. Four hundred is likely a round number. As for "the fourth generation," a "generation" may mean a full long lifespan of roughly a century rather than thirty or forty years. Scholars calculate these timelines in several different ways.

**The land promise and the Amorites.** The promise of Canaan eventually led to the Israelite conquest, one of the hardest parts of Scripture to read. Verse 15:16 indicates that, from the Bible's perspective, it was a long-delayed judgment rather than an unprovoked land grab. Even so, the ethical questions around it continue to be deeply debated.

**The land promise today.** Some readers appeal to the borders "from the river of Egypt to the great River Euphrates" in discussions of modern Middle Eastern politics. Christians hold widely different views on how this promise applies after the coming of Jesus. Taken on its own terms, the passage records God's promise to Abram.

## What Commentators Say

**Martin Luther**, the 16th-century German Reformer, regarded 15:6 as one of the most important verses in the entire Old Testament. In his view, Scripture teaches here with unmistakable clarity that people are counted righteous by faith alone, apart from works—because Abram did nothing at all except believe the promise. *(Lectures on Genesis, Genesis 15)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Luther devotes extensive treatment to 15:6 as a foundational text for justification by faith in Lectures on Genesis -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, pointed out that Abram had already trusted God long before 15:6—all the way back when he left Ur. Moses records this line here not to suggest Abram was just starting to believe, Calvin argued, but to show that throughout his entire life Abram stood righteous in the exact same way: by trusting God's word. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 15, verse 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin stresses that Abram believed earlier and that 15:6 characterizes his whole life, in his commentary on 15:6 -->

**Gerhard von Rad**, the German Old Testament scholar, wrote a celebrated essay on 15:6. He observed that the verb "credited" (or "reckoned") originally belonged to priestly language declaring whether a sacrifice was accepted. Here God accepts not an offering on an altar, but Abram's trust—as though faith itself were the gift that pleased Him. *(essay "Faith Reckoned as Righteousness," and Genesis, Old Testament Library)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: von Rad's essay "Faith Reckoned as Righteousness" on the cultic background of "reckoned" is well known -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, illuminated the ritual of halving animals through ancient Near Eastern treaty customs, where the party taking the oath walked between the pieces as a self-curse against breaking faith.
