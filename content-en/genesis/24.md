# Genesis 24 — A Wife for Isaac

## Story

Abraham was now old, and the LORD had blessed him in every way. One task still weighed on him: finding a wife for Isaac. He called his oldest and most trusted servant, the manager of his entire estate, and had him put his hand under his thigh and swear before the LORD. He was not to choose a wife for Isaac from the Canaanite women, but to go back to Abraham's homeland and relatives to find a bride for him (24:1–4).

The servant asked: what if the woman refuses to come back with me—should I take Isaac back there? Abraham answered firmly: "Make sure that you do not take my son back there." God had sworn to give this land to his descendants, and God Himself would send His angel ahead. If the woman refused, the servant would be free from the oath, yet under no circumstances was Isaac to be taken back (24:5–9).

The servant took ten camels loaded with costly gifts and traveled to Nahor's town in Mesopotamia. Toward evening, he had the camels kneel beside the well outside the town, right at the hour when women came out to draw water. He prayed to the God of his master. He asked for a sign: let the girl who, when he asks for a drink, replies, "Drink, and I will water your camels as well," be the one God had chosen for Isaac (24:10–14).

Before he had even finished praying, Rebekah came out with a water jar on her shoulder. She was the granddaughter of Nahor, Abraham's brother—very beautiful and unmarried. The servant ran to ask for a drink. She quickly lowered her jar for him, then offered on her own to draw water for his camels "until they have had enough to drink." Ten camels fresh off a long journey drink an enormous amount of water, yet she kept running back and forth between the well and the trough. The servant watched in silence, waiting to see whether the LORD had made his journey succeed (24:15–21).

Once the camels finished drinking, he gave her a gold ring and two gold bracelets, and asked whose daughter she was. Hearing that she was the daughter of Bethuel, he bowed down and worshiped the LORD, who "has not withheld His kindness and faithfulness" from his master and had guided him straight to his master's relatives (24:22–27). Rebekah ran home to tell the news. As soon as her brother Laban saw the gold ring and bracelets on his sister's wrists, he hurried out to invite the guest in, providing straw and feed for the camels and water to wash their feet (24:28–32).

A meal was set before him, yet the servant refused to eat until he had stated his errand. He told the whole story from the start: how God had blessed his master, the oath he had sworn, the prayer he had prayed by the well, and how everything had unfolded word for word. Then he asked for a straight answer so he would know what to do next (24:33–49). Laban and Bethuel replied that this clearly came from the LORD—they had nothing to add; he could take Rebekah and go. The servant bowed to the ground before the LORD once more, then brought out gifts for the bride and her family (24:50–53).

The next morning, he asked to leave at once. Her mother and brother wanted her to stay about ten days. They called Rebekah in to ask her. Her answer was short and clear: "I will go." Her family blessed her, praying she would become the mother of thousands upon thousands and that her descendants would possess the gates of their enemies. Then she mounted the camels with her nurse and servant girls and followed the servant (24:54–61).

Meanwhile, Isaac was living in the Negev, having just come back from Beer-lahai-roi. In the evening, he went out into the field to meditate, looked up, and saw camels approaching. Rebekah saw him too, got down from her camel, and asked who the man was. Learning he was her husband-to-be, she took her veil and covered herself. The servant told Isaac everything that had happened. Isaac brought Rebekah into the tent of his mother Sarah, married her, and loved her. For the first time since losing his mother, he was comforted (24:62–67).

## Key Takeaways

**God leads through ordinary moments.** Across this entire long chapter, no angel appears, no dream comes, and God speaks not a single audible word. There is only a well, a girl drawing water, and a quiet prayer. Yet the servant knows for certain that the LORD led him (24:27). Most of a believer's life looks just like this: God guiding through very everyday things.

**A short prayer, yet deeply genuine.** The servant prays before acting, gives thanks the moment he sees God's answer, and worships again when the matter is settled (24:12; 24:26; 24:52). He does not pray for himself, but for his master. He is a faithful servant with no thought of personal gain.

**The sign he asked for tested character.** He did not ask for a dramatic miracle like fire from heaven. He asked to meet a young woman who would volunteer for exhausting extra work on behalf of a stranger. What he was looking for was generosity and grit—the very qualities that make a good partner in life.

**Rebekah steps out just like Abraham.** Asked a single question, she answers, "I will go" (24:58). Like Abraham years earlier, she leaves her homeland, her relatives, and her father's house for a country she has never seen and a husband she has never met.

**Love grows after commitment.** The chapter closes with three things in order: Isaac marries Rebekah, then loves her, then finds comfort (24:67). Here, love is not merely a feeling required before marriage; it is something that grows and deepens inside marriage.

## Deeper Meaning

**Answered before the prayer is finished.** "Before the servant had finished praying," Rebekah walked out (24:15; 24:45). Later God speaks through the prophet Isaiah: "Even before they call, I will answer, and while they are still speaking, I will hear" (Isaiah 65:24). The scene beside the well is a living picture of that promise.

**No turning back to the old homeland.** Twice Abraham insists that Isaac must never be taken back there (24:6; 24:8). The letter to the Hebrews says that if the patriarchs had kept longing for the country they left, "they would have had opportunity to return," yet they chose a better country instead (Hebrews 11:15–16). The line of promise must remain in the land of promise.

**Meetings beside a well.** Several marriages in the Bible begin at a well. Jacob meets Rachel at a well (29:9–11). Moses meets the daughters of the priest of Midian at a well and marries one of them (Exodus 2:15–21). Many readers of John's Gospel hear an echo of these scenes when Jesus sits beside a well and asks a Samaritan woman for a drink (John 4:6–7).

**Kindness and faithfulness.** The servant praises the LORD because He "has not withheld His kindness and faithfulness" (24:27). That very pair of words is how God later introduces Himself to Moses: "abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6). The apostle John echoes the same pairing when describing Jesus as "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).

**A blessing that echoes the promise.** Rebekah's family prays that her offspring will "possess the gates of their enemies" (24:60). They had no idea that back on Mount Moriah, God had just promised Abraham almost the exact same thing: his descendants would "possess the gates of their enemies" (22:17). The blessing of outsiders unknowingly mirrors the promise of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Place your hand under my thigh" was an ancient oath.** It was a solemn way of swearing in that era. Later, Jacob likewise tells Joseph to put his hand under his thigh and swear not to bury him in Egypt (47:29). Many think the gesture was tied to offspring and lineage, since the oath here concerned the future of the family line. Scripture itself gives no explanation.

**Should we ask for signs the way the servant did?** Some readers take this story as a pattern for "asking for signs" in every decision, much like Gideon later testing God with a fleece (Judges 6:36–40). Yet Scripture merely records what he did; it does not command everyone to copy it. Nor was his prayer a game of chance: he asked to see real character, and he still checked her family background before giving thanks.

**Marrying within the extended family.** Rebekah was the granddaughter of Nahor, Abraham's brother, making her and Isaac close relatives. In that culture, marrying within the clan was normal. Abraham's concern was not bloodline, but faith: he did not want his son marrying into local peoples who worshiped other gods. The Law of Moses later forbade intermarriage with those nations for the very same reason (Deuteronomy 7:3–4).

**Did the bride have a voice?** The marriage itself was arranged between the two families, following the custom of the time. Yet when it came to leaving immediately or staying a few more days, the family called Rebekah herself and asked what she wanted (24:57–58). Her wishes were respected, and she made her own decision.

**Keep an eye on Laban.** Scripture notes that Laban rushed out to greet the visitor the moment he saw the gold ring and bracelets on his sister's wrists (24:30). That small detail hints at the kind of man he is: in later chapters, Laban will prove to be a shrewd and calculating operator in his dealings with Jacob.

**Origen**, a third-century biblical interpreter in Alexandria, reads this story allegorically. Rebekah came to the well every day to draw water, which is why she met Abraham's servant. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, examines whether the servant was right to ask for a sign. He believes the servant was not acting rashly, because God was guiding him in a special way for this task. **Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, praises the servant as a model of faithful service: careful when taking on an assignment, quick to pray before acting, and putting his master's business ahead of his own dinner. He also comments on Isaac going out to the field at evening, viewing it as a quiet hour set aside for meditation and prayer. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis 24)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Henry praises the servant's faithfulness and prayerfulness and comments on Isaac's meditation; unsure of his exact wording -->

**Gerhard von Rad**, a twentieth-century German Old Testament scholar, remarks that God never appears directly in this chapter. He works behind the scenes, through events that look like mere coincidence, where only eyes of faith recognize His hand. He sees this as a fresh style of storytelling compared with earlier chapters where God speaks and appears openly. *(Genesis, Old Testament Library)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall von Rad highlighting God's hidden providence in chapter 24; unsure of his exact wording -->

**Robert Alter**, an American Jewish scholar, identifies the scene by the well as a recurring "type-scene" used by Hebrew storytellers: a traveler arrives in a foreign land, meets a young woman at a well, water is drawn, she runs home to tell the news, and a meal and betrothal follow. What stands out here is that the groom is absent, while the bride supplies all the energy: she runs, she draws water, she decides for herself. Alter argues that from her very first entrance, Rebekah is portrayed as the more decisive partner compared with Isaac. *(The Art of Biblical Narrative, Chapter 3)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the betrothal type-scene at a well and chapter 24's shift of initiative onto Rebekah are Alter's famous thesis in the chapter on type-scenes -->
