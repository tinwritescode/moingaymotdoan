# Genesis 35 — Returning to Bethel

## Story

After the massacre at Shechem, Jacob lived in dread. The surrounding peoples could unite and strike back at any moment (34:30). Right into that fear, God spoke. He told Jacob to go up to Bethel—the place where He had appeared to him years earlier when he was fleeing from Esau—and build an altar there (35:1).

Jacob knew he could not bring his household to meet God the way they were. He told his family and everyone with him, "Get rid of the foreign gods that are among you," purify yourselves, and change your clothes. He reminded them that God had answered him in his day of distress and stayed with him everywhere he went (35:2–3). Everyone handed over their foreign idols along with their earrings, and Jacob buried them beneath the oak near Shechem (35:4).

Then they set out. God caused a terror to fall over the surrounding cities, so no one dared pursue them (35:5). Arriving at Bethel, Jacob built an altar and named the place El-bethel, meaning "God of Bethel." There too, Deborah, the old nurse who had cared for Rebekah since her youth, died. She was buried beneath an oak tree, which was named Allon-bachuth, meaning "oak of weeping" (35:6–8).

At Bethel, God appeared to Jacob once more. He reaffirmed Jacob's new name: no longer Jacob, but Israel. He declared, "I am God Almighty." He told him to be fruitful and multiply, promised that nations and kings would descend from him, and confirmed that the land promised to Abraham and Isaac now belonged to him and his descendants (35:9–12). After God went up from him, Jacob set up a stone pillar on the spot, poured a drink offering and oil over it, and named the place Bethel, just as he had done more than twenty years before (35:13–15).

From Bethel, the family traveled south. While they were still some distance from Ephrath, Rachel went into labor. The birth was agonizing. The midwife tried to reassure her: "Do not be afraid, for you are having another son" (35:16–17). Yet Rachel did not survive. With her dying breath she named the boy Ben-oni, meaning "son of my sorrow." Jacob renamed him Benjamin, "son of the right hand" (35:18). Rachel was buried beside the road to Ephrath, that is, Bethlehem, and Jacob set up a pillar over her grave (35:19–20).

Moving on again, Israel pitched his tent beyond the Tower of Eder. There a shameful thing took place: Reuben, his firstborn son, slept with Bilhah, his father's concubine. Scripture records it in one stark line: "and Israel heard about it" (35:21–22). No rebuke, no punishment. Only on his deathbed would Jacob finally bring it up (49:4).

Immediately after comes the list of Jacob's twelve sons, grouped by their four mothers: six by Leah, two by Rachel, two by Bilhah, and two by Zilpah (35:22–26).

At last Jacob reached Hebron and saw his father Isaac again. Isaac lived to be one hundred and eighty years old, then died, old and "full of years." Esau and Jacob, the two brothers who had once been bitter enemies, stood side by side to bury their father (35:27–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Returning to Bethel means keeping an old promise.** More than twenty years earlier, a runaway Jacob had vowed at Bethel that if God kept him safe and brought him home in peace, that place would be God's house (28:20–22). God kept His part. Yet once back in Canaan, Jacob lingered at Shechem, and tragedy struck there. Now God calls him back to where it all began.

**Meeting God requires cleaning house.** For years, idols had been sitting inside Jacob's household. This time he stops looking the other way. He tells everyone to hand over the idols, wash themselves, and change their clothes. Coming to God does not mean adding one more deity to the shelf; it means clearing the shelf completely.

**God renews His promise to an undeserving man.** Jacob had cheated his brother, deceived his father, and just stood by while his sons committed a massacre. Even so, God calls him by his new name and repeats the promise of descendants and land. The promise holds firm not because Jacob is worthy, but because God is faithful.

**Blessing and sorrow walk the same road.** Inside a single chapter, Jacob receives God's blessing—and also loses his mother's beloved nurse, loses the wife he loved most, suffers betrayal from his oldest son, and buries his father. Being chosen by God does not spare a life from grief.

**Twelve sons at last.** With Benjamin's birth, Jacob's household reaches twelve sons. From these twelve men will come the twelve tribes of Israel. The promise of "a nation" is finally taking visible shape.

## Deeper Meaning

**Putting away foreign gods at Shechem.** Centuries later, in the very same town of Shechem, Joshua gathers all Israel and speaks nearly identical words: "get rid of the foreign gods among you" (Joshua 24:23). He too sets up a stone beneath an oak tree there (Joshua 24:26). What Jacob does here for his family becomes what the entire nation must do every time it wants a fresh start with God.

**The blessing of Adam, Abraham, and Jacob.** The command "Be fruitful and multiply" echoes God's very first blessing over humanity (1:28). The promise that "kings shall descend from you" repeats what God told Abraham (17:6). From Jacob's line will rise King David, and from David's line will come Jesus.

**Rachel weeping for her children.** Rachel's tomb stood near Bethlehem, and Israel remembered her as a mother of the nation. When the people were carried off into exile, the prophet Jeremiah pictured Rachel weeping for her children and refusing to be comforted (Jeremiah 31:15). Matthew echoes those same words when King Herod slaughters the baby boys of Bethlehem (Matthew 2:18). The mother who died giving birth on the road to Bethlehem became an emblem of every grieving mother.

**Bethlehem.** This roadside grave marks the very first time Bethlehem is named in the Bible. Later, the prophet Micah foretells that out of small Bethlehem Ephrathah will come a ruler for Israel (Micah 5:2). The very place where Rachel died giving birth would one day be the birthplace of Jesus.

**Son of sorrow, son of the right hand.** <!-- low: this is a typological reading used by some preachers, uncertain who first proposed it -->Some Christian readers see in Benjamin's two names a foreshadowing of Jesus. He was "a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief" (Isaiah 53:3), who was then exalted to sit at the right hand of God (Hebrews 1:3).<!-- /low --> This chapter itself does not make that claim. Still, it shows us something moving: Jacob refuses to let grief name his son's future.

**Reuben forfeits his birthright.** In the ancient Near East, taking a father's concubine was a way of claiming the father's authority and place. Centuries later, Absalom did the exact same thing when rebelling against King David (2 Samuel 16:21–22). Reuben thought he was seizing power, yet he lost his birthright instead (49:3–4). Chronicles later states plainly that because Reuben defiled his father's bed, the birthright passed to the sons of Joseph (1 Chronicles 5:1).

**Two brothers burying their father together.** Years earlier, Isaac and Ishmael, half-brothers divided by family strife, had stood together to bury Abraham (25:9). Now Esau and Jacob stand side by side at their father's grave. A rivalry that began in the womb closes in peace, even though the two brothers continue along separate paths.

## Notes & Nuances

**Where did the idols come from?** The chapter does not spell it out. We know Rachel had stolen her father's household idols (31:19). There may also have been idols taken in the looting of Shechem (34:29), or brought along by servants in Jacob's household. As for the earrings buried with them, they were likely worn as protective amulets tied to pagan worship.

**Why are Israel and Bethel named twice?** Jacob had already received the name Israel at the Jabbok (32:28) and had already named Bethel when he fled home (28:19). Here both events are told again. Some scholars think Genesis weaves together more than one literary source. Others read this as a public confirmation: the earlier moments happened when Jacob was alone in the dark, while this scene takes place before his whole household after he has finally come home.

**What exactly did Reuben do?** Scripture reports it in a single spare sentence without commentary. Most readers recognize it as both a sexual sin and a bid for leadership inside the family. Some ancient Jewish teachers tried to soften the act, suggesting Reuben merely moved his father's bed to defend his mother Leah's honor. That idea does not appear in the text, and Jacob's dying words (49:4) make clear the offense was far more serious. Jacob's silence is also striking—this is not the first time he stays silent when his sons commit a grave wrong (34:5).

**Benjamin was not born in Paddan-aram.** Verse 35:26 closes the list of sons by saying, "These are the sons of Jacob, who were born to him in Paddan-aram," even though Benjamin was just born near Bethlehem. That kind of sweeping summary is common in ancient writing: it speaks generally for the group as a whole without stopping to note the one exception.

**Isaac's death is placed out of strict chronological order.** If you add up the ages given in Genesis, Isaac actually lived about twelve years longer, past the time Joseph was sold into Egypt (25:26; 37:2; 47:9). The storyteller wraps up Isaac's life story here cleanly before turning the page to the story of Joseph.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, marvels that Jacob allowed idols to remain inside his home for so long. In Calvin's view, Jacob had been negligent, and only when God summoned him did he finally exercise his authority as head of the household to purge them. He likewise believes the earrings were buried because they were tied to superstition. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 35 verses 2 and 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin faulting Jacob for tolerating idols and viewing the earrings as superstitious objects; exact phrasing uncertain -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, calls Jacob's action in 35:2 a "reformation of his family." From it he draws a lesson: heads of households must use their influence to keep their homes faithful in worship, and anyone who wants to go up to Bethel to meet God must first put away strange gods. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis 35)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain of Henry's point about reforming the household before going to Bethel; exact wording unverified -->

**Gerhard von Rad**, a German Old Testament scholar, drawing on a study by Albrecht Alt, suggests that burying foreign gods at Shechem before going up to Bethel reflects an actual ancient Israelite pilgrimage rite: worshipers renounced foreign deities at Shechem and then processed up to Bethel. He reads this passage alongside Joshua 24. *(Genesis, Old Testament Library)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall von Rad citing Alt's Shechem-to-Bethel pilgrimage thesis on 35:1–7; uncertain how strongly he endorses it -->

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, points out that the Hebrew root in Ben-oni can carry two opposite senses: "sorrow" or "strength." Rachel surely meant her sorrow. Jacob changed the name to Benjamin, a name of good fortune and favor, so the child would not have to carry a lifelong reminder of his mother's death. *(Genesis, JPS Torah Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Sarna discussing the double sense of "oni" and the shift to a favorable name; exact details uncertain -->
