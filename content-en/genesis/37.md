# Genesis 37 — The Robe of Many Colors and the Dreams

## Story

Jacob settled in the land of Canaan, where his father had lived. From here on, the story centers on his son Joseph. At seventeen years old, Joseph was tending the flocks alongside his brothers, the sons of his father's two maidservants, and he used to bring his father bad reports about them (37:1–2).

Jacob loved Joseph more than all his other sons, because Joseph was the son of his old age. He made him a robe of many colors. Seeing that their father loved Joseph more than any of them, his brothers hated him and could not speak a single kind word to him (37:3–4).

Then Joseph had a dream and told it to his brothers. He dreamed they were out in the field binding sheaves of grain; his sheaf suddenly stood upright while his brothers' sheaves gathered around and bowed down to his. Do you really think you're going to reign over us? his brothers snapped. They hated him all the more (37:5–8).

Soon he had a second dream: the sun, the moon, and eleven stars were bowing down to him. This time he told both his father and his brothers. Jacob scolded him: are your mother, your brothers, and I actually going to come and bow down to the ground before you? His brothers burned with jealousy, yet his father kept the matter in mind (37:9–11).

One day, the brothers took the flocks far away to pasture near Shechem. Jacob sent Joseph to check on his brothers and the sheep and bring word back. Joseph set out from Hebron. When he wandered lost in the fields, a stranger pointed him the right way: your brothers moved on to Dothan. Joseph kept walking and found them there (37:12–17).

His brothers spotted him in the distance. Before he even reached them, they plotted to kill him: "Here comes that dreamer!" Let's kill him, dump him in one of the pits, and say a wild animal devoured him; then we'll see what becomes of his dreams (37:18–20).

Reuben, the oldest, heard this and stepped in to save him: don't take his life, don't shed his blood—just throw him into this pit out in the wilderness. He secretly planned to come back later and bring the boy safely home to his father (37:21–22). As soon as Joseph walked up, his brothers stripped off his robe of many colors and threw him down into an empty pit with no water in it (37:23–24).

Then they sat down to eat. Looking up, they saw a caravan of traders heading down to Egypt, their camels loaded with spices, balm, and myrrh. Judah spoke up: what do we gain by killing our brother and hiding his blood? Let's sell him to the Ishmaelites instead and not lay a hand on him—after all, he is our brother, our own flesh. The others agreed. They hauled Joseph up out of the pit and sold him for twenty shekels of silver (37:25–28).

Reuben had been away when it happened. Returning to the pit and finding Joseph gone, he tore his clothes, ran back to his brothers, and cried out: the boy is gone—what am I going to do now? (37:29–30).

The brothers slaughtered a young goat, dipped the robe of many colors in its blood, and sent it back to their father with a message: we found this—examine it to see if it is your son's robe or not. Jacob knew it at once: it is my son's robe; a fierce beast has torn him to pieces (37:31–33). The father who had once deceived his own father with his brother's clothes and goat meat was now deceived by his own sons with a robe and goat's blood.

Jacob tore his clothes, wrapped sackcloth around his waist, and mourned his son for many days. His sons and daughters tried to comfort him, yet he refused to be comforted. I will go down to Sheol mourning for my son, he said. Meanwhile, down in Egypt, Joseph was sold to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh and captain of the guard (37:34–36).

## Key Takeaways

**Favoritism sows division inside a family.** Jacob repeated the exact mistake of his own parents, when Isaac favored Esau and Rebekah favored Jacob (25:28). That robe of many colors was not just a handsome gift; it was a public banner declaring that Joseph stood above all his brothers (37:3–4).

**Unchecked envy grows into murder.** The brothers started by being unable to speak kindly to Joseph (37:4), and before long they were plotting his murder the instant they saw him on the horizon (37:18–20). Only a few short steps separate bitter jealousy from sitting down calmly to eat lunch beside the pit where your brother is trapped (37:24–25).

**Deception circles back on the deceiver.** Years before, Jacob had used his brother's clothes and goat meat to fool his blind father Isaac (27:15–16). Now his own sons soak Joseph's robe in goat's blood to fool him into thinking a wild animal tore his son apart (37:31–33).

**Human schemes cannot kill a dream from God.** The brothers sold Joseph off to see what would become of his dreams (37:20). Yet that very caravan carrying him down to Egypt was the first step toward making every one of those dreams come true.

## Deeper Meaning

**An early shadow of the Savior rejected by His own.** Joseph is the beloved son of his father, envied by his own brothers, stripped of his robe, sold for silver to foreigners, and counted as dead (37:23–28). Later in the New Testament, Jesus too is rejected by His own people and betrayed for thirty pieces of silver (Matthew 26:15), yet through that very betrayal salvation opens up for many (Acts 7:9–10).

**The road that leads Israel into Egypt begins here.** Earlier, God had told Abraham that his descendants would live as foreigners and slaves in another land for four hundred years (15:13). Chapter 37 shows that prophecy beginning to unfold—not through a grand migration, but through a heartbreaking family betrayal.

**Human sin still stands under God's sovereign hand.** Although God's name never appears once in chapter 37, His hand is quietly weaving every thread—even the nameless stranger who happens to point Joseph toward Dothan (37:15–17) and the spice caravan passing by at just the right hour (37:25). Years later Joseph will sum it all up: you intended to harm me, but God intended it for good (50:20; Romans 8:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was Joseph being arrogant when he shared his dreams?** Scripture never spells out the seventeen-year-old's motives. Joseph may well have been naive and tactless, tattling on his brothers and blurting out dreams of ruling over them (37:2; 37:5–9). Yet however immature he might have been, nothing could ever excuse his brothers selling him into slavery.

**Was Joseph's robe multicolored or long-sleeved?** The original Hebrew phrase in 37:3 is quite rare, appearing elsewhere only in 2 Samuel 13:18 to describe the garments of royal princesses. Traditional translations render it *a robe of many colors*, while many modern translations render it *a long-sleeved* or *ornate robe*—the garment of nobility, worn by someone excused from heavy fieldwork. Either way, the robe announced that Joseph enjoyed his father's special favor.

**Did Ishmaelites or Midianites buy Joseph?** Within the same passage, the traders are called Ishmaelites in some verses (37:25; 37:27) and Midianites in others (37:28; 37:36). In the ancient world those two names were sometimes used interchangeably for desert trading tribes (Judges 8:24), or Midianite merchants may have been traveling together in a wider Ishmaelite caravan.

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observes that Jacob is partly to blame for showing such blatant favoritism and fanning the flames of jealousy at home. **Matthew Henry**, an English pastor of the early eighteenth century, pauses over the chilling detail that the brothers sat down to eat right after dropping Joseph into the pit (37:25). **Robert Alter**, an American scholar of biblical literature, highlights the story's subtle artistry of repetition.
