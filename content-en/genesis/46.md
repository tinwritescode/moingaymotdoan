# Genesis 46 — Jacob's Whole Household Goes Down to Egypt

## Story

Israel sets out with everything he owns. Reaching Beersheba, where his father Isaac once lived, he offers sacrifices to the God of his father Isaac (46:1). This is the southern edge of the promised land; stepping past it means leaving the country God swore to give them.

That night, God calls to him in a vision: "Jacob, Jacob!" He answers, "Here I am." God tells him: I am God, the God of your father. Do not be afraid to go down to Egypt, for there I will make you into a great nation. I Myself will go down with you to Egypt, and I will surely bring you back again. And Joseph's own hands will close your eyes when you die (46:2–4).

Jacob leaves Beersheba. His sons place their father, along with their wives and little children, in the wagons Pharaoh sent to carry him. Taking all the livestock and possessions they acquired in Canaan, the whole clan—sons and daughters, grandsons and granddaughters—travels down to Egypt together (46:5–7).

Scripture records every name in the group, arranged by the four mothers. The descendants of Leah—including Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, and Jacob's daughter Dinah—number thirty-three. The descendants of Zilpah, Leah's maidservant, number sixteen (46:8–18).

The descendants of Rachel include Joseph with his two sons born in Egypt, Manasseh and Ephraim, along with Benjamin and his ten sons—fourteen in all. The descendants of Bilhah, Rachel's maidservant, number seven (46:19–25).

Not counting the sons' wives, the direct descendants of Jacob who journey down to Egypt come to sixty-six people. Adding Joseph's two sons born there, the entire household of Jacob entering Egypt numbers seventy (46:26–27). From those seventy lives, a whole nation will be born.

Jacob sends Judah ahead to Joseph to get directions into Goshen. Joseph readies his chariot and drives up to Goshen to meet his father. The moment they meet, Joseph throws his arms around his father's neck and weeps for a long time. Israel tells him: now I can die in peace, since I have seen your face and know you are still alive (46:28–30).

Joseph briefs his brothers and his father's household: I will go up and inform Pharaoh that my family has arrived, that they are shepherds who have brought their flocks and herds. When Pharaoh asks your occupation, tell him that from your youth until now, both you and your fathers have raised livestock. That way you will be allowed to settle in Goshen, because all shepherds are detestable to the Egyptians (46:31–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Stepping into unfamiliar territory with God's assurance.** Leaving Canaan meant leaving the land of promise—the very place where God once told Isaac not to go down to Egypt during a famine (26:2). Jacob pauses at Beersheba to offer sacrifices and seek God, and God meets him with reassurance: do not be afraid, because I Myself will go down with you and bring you back (46:1–4).

**God turns a place of exile into the nursery of a nation.** God's promise to Jacob is startling: right inside foreign Egypt, He will grow this family into a great nation (46:3). God is never fenced in by the borders of Canaan.

**Every name on the list is remembered by God.** The seventy people moving down to Egypt are carefully recorded by name (46:8–27). What looks at first glance like a dry family roster is concrete proof that God is keeping His promise to multiply Abraham's offspring, starting with one small extended family inside a mighty empire.

**Wisdom that protects the identity of God's people.** Joseph instructs his brothers to tell Pharaoh plainly that they are shepherds—a livelihood Egyptians despised (46:31–34). Because of that, the family is settled apart in fertile Goshen: close enough to Joseph to be cared for, yet separate enough not to dissolve into Egyptian religion and culture.

## Deeper Meaning

**Fulfilling the promise to Abraham and opening the curtain on Exodus.** The seventy people going down to Egypt in chapter 46 are the exact same seventy who open the book of Exodus (Exodus 1:5). Four centuries later, a vast nation will walk out of Egypt from those seventy beginnings, fulfilling what God foretold to Abraham (15:13–14) and what He promised Jacob here (46:4).

**The Savior's own journey into Egypt and back.** In the New Testament, the child Jesus must likewise flee down into Egypt and later be called back to the land of Israel, fulfilling the prophetic word that God called His Son out of Egypt (Matthew 2:13–15; Hosea 11:1). Israel's passage through Egypt becomes a pattern for the story of the Savior Himself.

**In the world, yet not absorbed by the world.** Settling in Goshen lets Jacob's family live peacefully inside Egypt while keeping their faith and distinct identity intact. That picture echoes Jesus' prayer for His disciples: He does not ask the Father to take them out of the world, but to protect them from the evil one (John 17:15).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Genesis 46:27 say seventy people, while Acts 7:14 says seventy-five?** The Hebrew text of Genesis counts sixty-six people traveling with Jacob, then adds Jacob himself, Joseph, and Joseph's two sons to reach seventy (46:26–27). In Acts 7:14, Stephen follows the ancient Greek translation of Genesis, which also counts five grandsons and great-grandsons of Joseph through Manasseh and Ephraim (Numbers 26:28–37), bringing the total to seventy-five. Both are simply different ways of tallying the same family, not a contradiction.

**How was the promise *I will surely bring you back* (46:4) fulfilled?** First, it was fulfilled literally for Jacob's body when Joseph carried his father back to be buried in the cave of Machpelah in Canaan (50:12–13). More broadly, it was fulfilled when God brought the entire nation of Israel—carrying Joseph's bones with them—out of Egypt and home to the Promised Land.

**Why are ten sons of Benjamin listed (46:21) when he has just been called *the boy*?** In Hebrew idiom, *the boy* is an affectionate way an aging father and much older brothers refer to the youngest son in the family; by this point Benjamin is actually in his twenties or thirties. In addition, the list in chapter 46 may include grandchildren born shortly after the household settled in Egypt in order to record all the founding clans.

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, explained why Jacob paused at Beersheba: eager as he was to see Joseph again, he would not presume to leave the promised land without God's explicit permission. **Matthew Henry**, early eighteenth-century English pastor, warmly praised Joseph's wisdom in coaching his brothers to identify themselves as shepherds (46:31–34). Rather than feeling embarrassed by his family's humble trade or trying to push them into court life, Joseph used Egyptian prejudice to keep his relatives safely apart in Goshen, shielded from temptation and assimilation. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis chapter 46)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the lesson about Joseph keeping his family separate in Goshen to avoid court assimilation is a familiar theme in Matthew Henry; original text not cross-checked -->

**Nahum Sarna**, American Jewish biblical scholar, pointed out the symbolic resonance of the number seventy in 46:27.
