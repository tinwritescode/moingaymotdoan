# Genesis 14 — Abram Rescues Lot and Meets Melchizedek

## Story

In the east, four kings forged an alliance headed by Chedorlaomer, king of Elam. Five kings of the Jordan plain—among them the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah—had paid tribute to Chedorlaomer for twelve years. In the thirteenth year, they rebelled (14:1–4).

The next year, the four eastern kings marched west. They carved a wide campaign across several peoples, sweeping from east of the Jordan all the way south before turning back toward the plain. The five local kings drew up their battle lines in the Valley of Siddim—"four kings against five" (14:5–9).

The five kings suffered a crushing defeat. The valley was dotted with tar pits; as the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, men tumbled into the pits while the survivors escaped to the hills. The victors stripped Sodom of its goods and food supplies, and they also carried off Abram's nephew Lot—who was now living in Sodom—along with everything he owned (14:10–12). Lot had chosen the neighborhood of Sodom for its fertile ground; now he was swept up in the city's fate.

An escapee brought word to Abram, who was living near the Oaks of Mamre. Hearing that his relative had been taken captive, Abram rallied 318 trained men born in his own household and gave chase all the way north to Dan (14:13–14).

Under cover of night, Abram split his forces, launched a surprise attack, and drove the invaders north of Damascus. He recovered all the plunder and brought back Lot, his belongings, the women, and the rest of the captives (14:15–16).

On his return, two kings came out to meet him. The king of Sodom met him in the King's Valley. Then a mysterious figure stepped onto the stage: Melchizedek, king of Salem, who was also a priest of "God Most High." He brought out bread and wine and blessed Abram in the name of the Creator of heaven and earth. Abram gave him a tenth of all the plunder (14:17–20).

The king of Sodom, meanwhile, proposed a deal: "Give me the people, but take the goods for yourself." Abram turned him down flat. He had raised his hand in an oath to the Lord that he would not accept from the king of Sodom "even a thread, or a strap of a sandal," lest the king ever boast that he had made Abram rich. Abram took nothing beyond what his men had already eaten, while letting his local allies claim their fair share (14:21–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Faith does not mean standing on the sidelines.** Lot had grabbed the better land and walked away. Yet when disaster struck him, Abram did not shrug and say he had it coming. He risked his life to bring his nephew home. Trusting God never excuses us from stepping in when people we love are in danger.

**Two kings, two offers.** Melchizedek came with bread, wine, and a blessing in God's name. The king of Sodom came with a transaction. Abram welcomed the first and refused the second. Some gifts, even when offered free, are best left untouched.

**Victory belongs to God.** Melchizedek stated it plainly: God Most High had delivered Abram's enemies into his hand. Abram answered with a tenth of the spoils, acknowledging that the victory was not his own doing.

**Guarding God's reputation.** Abram refused to let anyone credit the king of Sodom for his wealth. God had promised to bless him, and Abram wanted everyone to see that what he had came from God—not from a corrupt city.

## Deeper Meaning

**A priest "forever."** Melchizedek steps into the story and vanishes just as suddenly, with no genealogy and no hint of where he came from. Psalm 110 speaks of a coming king: "You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" (Psalms 110:4). The letter to the Hebrews identifies that king as Jesus.

**King of righteousness, king of peace.** Hebrews translates Melchizedek's name as "king of righteousness" and Salem as "peace." Because Scripture records neither his birth nor his death, the author writes that, "like the Son of God," he remains a priest for all time (Hebrews 7:1–3).

**Someone greater than Abraham.** Hebrews presses the point further: Abraham, the great ancestor of God's people, paid a tenth to Melchizedek, and "the lesser is blessed by the greater" (Hebrews 7:4–7). That means Melchizedek's priesthood—and Christ's—stands higher than the Levitical priesthood descended from Abraham.

**Bread and wine.** Many readers have seen in Melchizedek's bread and wine a foreshadowing of the Lord's Supper, when Jesus took bread and a cup and gave them to His disciples (Matthew 26:26–27). Scripture itself does not draw that link explicitly; it is a connection treasured by generations of Christians.

**The promise already at work.** God had promised Abram: I will bless those who bless you (12:3). Melchizedek is the first outsider to pronounce a blessing over Abram—and he himself shares in that blessing as they meet.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who was Melchizedek?** Scripture does not tell us. Ancient Jewish tradition sometimes identified him with Shem, the son of Noah. Some Christians have thought he was Jesus Himself appearing before His birth in Bethlehem. Most interpreters take him to be a historical king of Salem, an outsider beyond Abram's family who nonetheless worshiped the true God. When Hebrews calls him "without father or mother," it is pointing to the way Genesis presents him—with no genealogy—rather than claiming he literally had no parents.

**Where was Salem?** Psalm 76 uses the name Salem for Jerusalem, and most readers understand Salem here to be Jerusalem as well. If so, the very city where Melchizedek ruled as king and priest would centuries later become the city of David and of the temple.

**The kings and nations in this chapter.** Archaeologists have not found definite outside evidence for the four eastern kings. For a time, scholars linked Amraphel to the famous Babylonian king Hammurabi, though few hold that view today. Genesis tells the story as a real military campaign grounded in concrete geography, and many of its place names can still be identified.

**Abram as a warrior.** This is the only time Abram takes up arms. He fights to rescue captives, not to seize territory or enrich himself. The chapter does not glorify war, yet it recognizes that there are moments when using strength to defend the vulnerable is the right thing to do.

**The tithe.** This is the Bible's first mention of giving a tenth, long before the law of Moses. Some appeal to this passage to teach that Christians today are still bound to tithe; others view it as a one-time voluntary gift rather than a binding rule. Believers hold differing views on this point.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, wrote that when Melchizedek blessed Abraham, the sacrifice Christians now offer around the world appeared for the very first time. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, rejected the reading that Melchizedek offered bread and wine as a sacrifice to God. Instead, Calvin argued, the bread and wine were hospitality to refresh Abram and his exhausted men after battle—a king's generous welcome to travelers. What mattered most, Calvin stressed, was Melchizedek's blessing and priestly office, not the meal itself. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 14, verse 18)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin rejects reading the bread and wine as a sacrifice, explaining them as refreshment for the soldiers, in his commentary on 14:18 -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, commended Abram for turning down the king of Sodom's riches.
