# Genesis 4 — Cain and Abel

## Story

Outside the garden, Adam and Eve began having children. Their firstborn they named Cain. Holding him, Eve exclaimed, "With the help of the LORD I have brought forth a man." Later she gave birth to his brother Abel. As they grew up, Cain worked the soil while Abel kept sheep (4:1–2).

In time, both brothers brought offerings to the LORD. Cain brought some of the fruit of the soil. Abel brought the best portions of the firstborn of his flock. The LORD looked with favor on Abel and his offering, but had no regard for Cain and his offering. Cain grew furious, and his face fell (4:3–5).

God did not abandon Cain. He asked, "Why are you angry?" Then He warned him: if you do what is right, will you not be accepted? But if not, "sin is crouching at your door"; it desires you, yet you must master it (4:6–7). Sin is pictured like a wild predator lurking outside the doorway, waiting for the person inside to step out.

Cain did not listen. He invited his brother out into the field, and there attacked and killed him (4:8). The very first child born on earth became the first murderer, and the first human being to die was his own younger brother.

God came asking again, just as He had once asked Cain's father: "Where is your brother Abel?" Cain shot back insolently, "I do not know! Am I my brother's keeper?" Yet God already knew. He said, "The voice of your brother's blood cries out to Me from the ground" (4:9–10). Because the ground had opened its mouth to drink Abel's blood, it would no longer yield its crops to Cain. From then on, he would be a restless wanderer across the earth (4:11–12).

Cain cried out, "My punishment is greater than I can bear." He feared that anyone who found him would kill him. Astonishingly, God still showed mercy to this murderer. He placed a mark on Cain so no one would kill him, declaring that anyone who took his life would suffer vengeance sevenfold. Cain left the presence of the LORD and settled in the land of Nod, east of Eden (4:13–16).

Cain's descendants pioneered the early arts of civilization. Cain built a city. Six generations down came Lamech, the first man to take two wives. Lamech's sons became the fathers of nomadic livestock herders, musicians who played harp and flute, and metalworkers forging bronze and iron (4:17–22). Yet Lamech himself composed a boastful song bragging about killing a man: if Cain is avenged sevenfold, he sang, then Lamech will be avenged "seventy-sevenfold" (4:23–24). Human skill kept advancing while the human heart grew fiercer.

At the chapter's end, the camera turns back to Adam and Eve. They had another son and named him Seth, to take the place of Abel. Seth had a son named Enosh. And the chapter closes with a quiet shaft of light: "At that time men began to call upon the name of the LORD" (4:25–26).

## Key Takeaways

**Sin spreads fast.** In chapter 3, sin was eating a forbidden piece of fruit. Just one generation later, it has become murder. By Lamech's day, murder is something to brag about in song. Sin never stays politely in one place; it grows from the family table out into all of society.

**God looks at the heart of the giver.** Scripture says the LORD "looked with favor on Abel and his offering"—He looked at the person first, and then at the gift. What God wants is never merely the offering, but the heart behind it.

**God warns us before sin takes over.** Before Cain ever lifted a hand, God came to talk with him. Anger is not yet murder. Between feeling furious and striking a blow lies a stretch of ground where we can still turn back—and God meets us right there.

**We are responsible for one another.** Cain's sharp retort—"Am I my brother's keeper?"—is a question people still ask today. The whole story answers yes. Each of us bears responsibility for the neighbor beside us.

**Even the guilty still encounter mercy.** God punished Cain, yet refused to abandon him to mob vengeance. The mark placed on Cain was a sign of protection, not a badge of disgrace.

## Deeper Meaning

**Abel offered by faith.** The Letter to the Hebrews explains why Abel's offering was accepted: he offered it "by faith," and though he is dead, by faith he "still speaks" (Hebrews 11:4). First John adds that Cain murdered his brother because Cain's own deeds were evil while his brother's were righteous (1 John 3:12).

**Abel's blood and Jesus' blood.** Abel's blood cried out from the ground demanding justice. The Letter to the Hebrews points to another blood that "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Hebrews 12:24). Abel's blood cries for vengeance; the blood of Jesus poured out on the cross cries for forgiveness.

**Seventy-seven times.** Lamech boasted of vengeance seventy-sevenfold. Centuries later, Peter asked Jesus how many times he had to forgive a brother who sinned against him—up to seven times? Jesus replied: not seven times, but "seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:21–22). Jesus turned Lamech's song upside down: where there was once limitless revenge, there is now limitless forgiveness.

**The first righteous martyr.** Jesus named Abel as the very first in a long line of righteous people put to death (Matthew 23:35). The story of the innocent suffering at the hands of the wicked begins right here and reaches its climax at the cross.

**The line of Seth.** Luke's genealogy of Jesus traces His lineage backward through Seth all the way to Adam (Luke 3:38). Cain's family line stops after Lamech. The promise in 3:15 of someone who would crush the serpent will travel down through the line of Seth.

## Notes & Nuances

**Where did Cain's wife come from?** This is one of the most common questions people ask. Chapter 5 tells us Adam had many other sons and daughters (5:4). The usual explanation is that Cain married a sister or a niece. In that very earliest generation, laws against marrying close relatives did not yet exist; those came much later under the Law of Moses. Since Scripture does not spell it out, there is no need to be overly dogmatic.

**Why did God reject Cain's offering?** The passage never states the reason directly. Some think it was because Cain brought produce while Abel brought a blood sacrifice—yet later, the Law of Moses happily accepted grain offerings (Leviticus 2:1). A stronger clue lies in the wording: Abel brought the firstborn and the choicest portions, whereas Cain merely brought "some of the fruit of the soil." Above all, as Hebrews tells us, Abel offered in faith.

**The mark of Cain has nothing to do with skin color.** For centuries, some people falsely taught that Cain's mark was dark skin, using it to justify racism and slavery. That idea has zero basis in Scripture. The Bible never describes what the mark looked like; it only tells us its purpose was to protect Cain.

**Reporting is not endorsing.** Scripture records Lamech marrying two wives and singing a song of vengeance without applauding him. From here on, Genesis recounts several stories of polygamy, and nearly every one breeds jealousy and heartbreak.

**Who was Cain afraid would kill him?** Cain feared other people, even though the story has only named his parents so far. Most likely Adam and Eve already had many children and grandchildren by then, as chapter 5 indicates. The narrative selectively follows the people who matter to the main storyline rather than listing everyone alive.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, saw Cain and Abel as the starting point of two "cities" running all through history. Cain built an earthly city; Abel lived like a pilgrim and built no city, because his true home was with God. Augustine also compared Cain to Romulus, the legendary founder of Rome who likewise killed his own brother. *(The City of God, Book XV, chapters 1 and 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the two cities beginning with Cain and Abel and the comparison to Romulus and Remus in Book XV are widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, pointed out that Scripture says God looked with favor on "Abel" first and then on "his offering." In Calvin's view, God was pleased with Abel himself because of his faith, and for that reason accepted his gift. He lied by claiming he did not know, and then snapped back at God as though God had no business asking. **Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, highlighted the word "crouching" in verse 4:7. It resembles an ancient Babylonian term for a demon that lurked beside the threshold of a house. Scripture borrows that familiar image and reshapes its meaning: what crouches outside your door is not a demon, but your own sin—and you have the power to master it. *(Genesis, JPS Torah Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: remembers the comparison with the Akkadian rabisu; unsure whether Sarna makes it in this volume or in Understanding Genesis -->
