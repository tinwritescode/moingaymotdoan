# Genesis 16 — Hagar and the God Who Sees

## Story

Ten years had passed since Abram arrived in the land of Canaan, and his wife Sarai still had no children. She owned an Egyptian maidservant named Hagar. Sarai told her husband: the Lord has kept me from bearing children, so sleep with my servant; perhaps I can build a family through her. Abram went along with his wife's plan (16:1–3).

Hagar conceived. Once she knew she was pregnant, she began looking down on her mistress. Sarai rounded on Abram: "May the wrong done to me be upon you!" Abram washed his hands of the mess: your servant is in your hands; do whatever you want with her. Sarai treated Hagar so harshly that Hagar ran away (16:4–6).

The pregnant woman fled into the desert along the road leading back toward her homeland of Egypt. Beside a spring of water, the angel of the Lord found her. Calling her by name, the angel asked, "Hagar, servant of Sarai, where have you come from, and where are you going?" She could answer only half the question: I am running away from my mistress Sarai (16:7–8).

The angel told her to return and submit to her mistress. Yet along with that command came a promise: her offspring would be multiplied beyond counting. She would give birth to a son and name him Ishmael—meaning "God hears"—"for the LORD has heard your cry of affliction" (16:9–11).

The angel also foretold what the boy would become: he would be a wild donkey of a man—free and untamed—his hand against everyone and everyone's hand against him, living in tension beside all his brothers (16:12).

Hagar gave God a name of her own. She called Him "the God who sees me," saying: right here I have seen the One who sees me. From then on, that well was called Beer-lahai-roi—the well of the Living One who sees me (16:13–14).

Hagar went back and gave birth to a son. Abram named the boy Ishmael, just as the angel had said. Abram was eighty-six years old at the time (16:15–16).

## Key Takeaways

**Growing impatient with God's promise.** God had promised Abram a child, though He had not yet spelled out how. Unable to wait any longer, Sarai and Abram took matters into their own hands using a custom of their day. Their shortcut looked completely acceptable by cultural standards, yet it brought heartache to all three of them.

**No one walks away blameless.** Sarai hatched the scheme and then blamed her husband. Abram went along with it and then shrugged off responsibility. Hagar gained leverage and looked down on her mistress. Scripture refuses to airbrush anyone into a hero here.

**God sees the person everyone else overlooks.** Hagar sits at the very bottom of this household: a woman, a slave, a foreigner, with no rights of her own. Yet she is the very first person in the Bible visited by the angel of the Lord, and the only person in all of Scripture who gives God a name.

**God calls her by name.** The angel does not address her simply as "servant," but says, "Hagar." To her owners, she was a tool for producing an heir. To God, she is a person with a name and a future.

**God hears the sound of suffering.** The boy's name, Ishmael, became a lifelong reminder: God has heard. Even when people cannot put their grief into formal prayers, God still hears their pain.

## Deeper Meaning

**The God who sees and hears.** What Hagar experienced beside a desert spring would later be echoed for an entire nation. At the burning bush, God told Moses: I have surely seen the affliction of My people, I have heard their cry, and I know their suffering (Exodus 3:7). Ironically, this time it was the Hebrews who were slaves in Egypt.

**God looks upon the lowly.** Centuries later, Mary, the mother of Jesus, sang of a God who "has looked with favor on the humble state of His servant" (Luke 1:48). From Hagar to Mary, the God of Scripture repeatedly turns His gaze toward the people nobody else notices.

**Hagar in Paul's letter to the Galatians.** Paul uses this story as an illustration: Hagar and her son represent a life relying on human effort and the law, while Sarah and the child of promise represent a life built on God's grace (Galatians 4:22–24). Believers, like Isaac, are children of the promise (Galatians 4:28).

**God hears a second time.** Years later, Hagar and Ishmael find themselves out in the desert once more, and once again "God heard the voice of the boy" (21:17). Ishmael's name comes true a second time.

**Tears remembered.** A psalmist prays: put my tears into Your bottle; are they not recorded in Your book? (Psalms 56:8). Hagar's story is proof of that prayer: her tears were never forgotten.

## Notes & Nuances

**Ancient household customs.** A barren wife giving her maidservant to her husband to bear an heir was a recognized practice in ancient Mesopotamian law, with the child legally counted as the mistress's own. Scripture records the custom without endorsing it as God's will, and the fallout speaks for itself.

**Why did the angel tell Hagar to go back?** This is a hard moment for modern readers, because going back meant returning to the woman who mistreated her. One explanation is that a lone pregnant woman had little chance of surviving in the desert, and the child needed to be born within Abram's household to receive his standing. The passage never justifies Sarai's abuse, and it should never be used to tell people suffering abuse that they must simply endure it.

**Who is "the angel of the LORD"?** At first an angel speaks, yet moments later Hagar calls the One who spoke to her the Lord Himself, and the angel makes promises only God can fulfill. Many early Christian interpreters understood this as God appearing directly—some suggesting the Son of God before His incarnation—while others understand the angel as a heavenly messenger speaking in God's name and authority.

**"A wild donkey of a man."** That phrase sounds like an insult to modern ears, yet in nomadic desert culture the wild donkey was admired as free, hardy, and impossible to tame. It foretells a fiercely independent desert life—marked by conflict, yes, yet answering to no master.

**Ishmael and the Arab peoples.** From ancient times, many—including Islamic tradition—have regarded Ishmael as an ancestor of many Arab peoples. Verse 16:12 should never be read as a curse over any modern ethnic group. God explicitly promised to bless Ishmael and his descendants.

## What Commentators Say

**Phyllis Trible**, the American Old Testament scholar, included Hagar's story among Scripture's "texts of terror"—narratives recounting the suffering of women. She underscored how Hagar is used, mistreated, and eventually cast out, yet stands alone in the Bible as the one person who dares to name God.
