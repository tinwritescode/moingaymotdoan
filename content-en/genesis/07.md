# Genesis 7 — The Flood

## Story

The ark stood finished. The LORD said to Noah, "Go into the ark, you and all your family," because He had found Noah righteous in this generation. This time God added further instructions: bring seven pairs of every clean animal—the kinds later acceptable for sacrifice—along with one pair of every unclean animal, and seven pairs of every kind of bird, to keep their kinds alive across the earth. In seven days, God would send rain for forty days and forty nights. Once again, Noah did everything the LORD commanded him (7:1–5).

Noah was 600 years old. He entered the ark with his wife, his sons, and his sons' wives. Animals, birds, and crawling creatures came to him in pairs and boarded the ark, just as God had said. Seven days passed, and the floodwaters arrived (7:6–10).

Scripture records the exact date like a ship's log: the six hundredth year of Noah's life, the second month, the seventeenth day. On that day, "all the fountains of the great deep burst forth, and the floodgates of the heavens were opened." Water erupted from beneath the earth and poured down from the sky. Rain hammered down for forty days and forty nights (7:11–12).

The narrator pauses to replay the boarding scene. Noah, his wife, his three sons Shem, Ham, and Japheth, and their three wives—eight people in all. Every creature with the breath of life filed in by pairs. Then comes one brief sentence readers never forget: "Then the LORD shut him in" (7:13–16). Noah did not pull the door shut. God closed it Himself.

The waters rose and lifted the ark clear off the earth. Higher and higher the waters surged, and the ark floated on the surface. Eventually water covered every high mountain under heaven, rising fifteen cubits—about twenty-two feet, or seven meters—above the tallest peaks (7:17–20).

Every land-dwelling creature perished: birds, livestock, wild beasts, swarming creatures, and all humanity. Scripture repeats the loss like a tolling bell: everything on dry land with the breath of life in its nostrils died. Only Noah and those with him inside the ark survived (7:21–23).

The chapter ends in stillness: the waters prevailed over the earth for 150 days (7:24). For nearly five months, there was only water, sky, and one lone ark drifting across the vastness.

## Key Takeaways

**God means what He says.** In chapter 6, God issued a warning. In chapter 7, every word unfolds exactly as spoken, right down to the count of days. God's warnings are never empty threats.

**Obedience happens one step at a time.** Twice Scripture notes that Noah did all that God commanded him (6:22; 7:5). He did not know how high the waters would climb or how long they would last. He only knew the next step in front of him, and he took it.

**God closes the door.** That shut door kept Noah safe inside while signaling that the time of waiting had ended for everyone outside. Rescue and judgment arrived together, from the very same hand.

**No one can save themselves.** The renowned mighty men of chapter 6 are nowhere to be seen. Even the highest mountaintops could shelter no one. Only those inside the ark God provided remained alive.

## Deeper Meaning

**Creation undone.** Back in chapter 1, God formed the world by separating the waters—waters above from waters below, with dry land emerging in between (1:6–9). In the flood, "the fountains of the great deep" and "the floodgates of the heavens" burst open, and those two bodies of water rush back together (7:11). The earth slides back into being "formless and void" (1:2). The flood is not merely a freak rainstorm; it is God unmaking what He had made so He can begin anew.

**The door shuts.** Jesus told a parable of ten bridesmaids. Five were ready and entered the wedding banquet, "and the door was shut," leaving the five who arrived late locked outside (Matthew 25:10). He also said that He Himself is the gate, and anyone who enters through Him will be saved (John 10:9). There is a window of time to step inside, and that window does not stay open forever.

**Eight people through water.** Peter notes the number of people saved through water—only eight (1 Peter 3:20). Second Peter warns that scoffers will laugh and claim everything goes on as it always has, forgetting that the ancient world once perished by water and that the present world also awaits a day of judgment (2 Peter 3:3–7).

**The righteous kept through trial.** Second Peter also reminds us that the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trials, just as He preserved Noah (2 Peter 2:5; 2:9). One psalm declares that the LORD sits enthroned over the flood, reigning as King forever (Psalms 29:10). No matter how high the floodwaters climb, they remain beneath His feet.

## Notes & Nuances

**One pair or seven pairs?** In chapter 6, God tells Noah to bring one pair of every kind (6:19). In chapter 7, clean animals are increased to seven pairs (7:2). The straightforward explanation is that chapter 6 gives the general instruction while chapter 7 supplies the specifics: Noah needed extra clean animals because he would offer some of them as sacrifices after the flood (8:20). Some scholars suggest two older accounts were woven together. The Hebrew phrasing here can also be read as "seven animals" rather than "seven pairs."

**A global flood or a regional flood?** Scripture says water covered "all the high mountains under all the heavens" (7:19). Some understand this to mean the entire planet. Others read "the earth" as the whole known world of the ancient Near East—the vast Mesopotamian basin—described from the vantage point of someone inside the ark. Faithful believers hold both views. Where they agree is what matters most: God truly judged humanity, and only Noah's family was saved.

**Many cultures tell flood stories.** Ancient Mesopotamians told of a man warned by a god to build a boat, load his family and animals aboard, and later release birds to search for dry ground. Many details echo Genesis. Readers who trust Scripture generally see these as shared memories of a real catastrophe, with Genesis alone preserving its true meaning: one righteous God judging evil and showing mercy to those who trust Him.

**How many animals fit inside the ark?** People have run the math over and over. Some point out that a biblical "kind" may be broader than a modern biological species, meaning far fewer animals were needed than we might assume. Scripture does not try to answer engineering questions like these. It tells the story to show us who God is, not to calculate cargo capacity.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, warned against turning the flood into an either-or choice. Some wanted only a symbolic lesson; others wanted only bare history. In Augustine's view, it was both a real historical event and a living portrait of the church, where people find shelter from judgment. He even tackled practical objections about how predators lived alongside prey and how the ark could hold them all. *(The City of God, Book XV, chapter 27)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Book XV chapter 27 rejects both purely historical and purely allegorical extremes and discusses the ark's capacity; widely cited -->

**Gordon Wenham**, a British Old Testament scholar, demonstrated that the flood account is built like two mirror halves. In the first half, the waters rise in stages: seven days, forty days, 150 days. In the second half, the waters recede through those same numbers in reverse order. Sitting right at the exact center is the sentence, "God remembered Noah" (8:1). To Wenham, this symmetry proves the story is a unified whole whose turning point is God's faithfulness in remembering. *(Genesis 1–15, Word Biblical Commentary)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the symmetrical chiastic structure of the flood narrative is Wenham's famous thesis, appearing in this commentary and his 1978 article -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observed that the distinction between clean and unclean animals was already known in Noah's time, long before the Law of Moses. To Calvin, this showed that early believers offered sacrifices according to God's own instruction rather than inventing rituals on their own. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 7 verse 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalls Calvin noting the clean/unclean distinction predates the Law of Moses here; unsure of the exact reason he gives -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, lingered over the moment God shut the ark's door. God shut it both to keep Noah safe within and to shut out those who remained outside. The door of mercy had stood open a long time, Henry noted, yet there comes a moment when it closes. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis 7)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Henry discusses both sides of God shutting the door; exact wording uncertain -->
