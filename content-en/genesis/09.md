# Genesis 9 — The Rainbow and the Covenant with All Creation

## Story

Stepping off the ark, Noah and his sons heard the original blessing spoken all over again: "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth." Yet the world had changed. From now on, animals would dread human beings, and God permitted humanity to eat meat, just as He had earlier given them green plants (9:1–3).

God set two clear boundaries. First, they must not eat meat with its lifeblood still in it, because blood represents life. Second, whoever sheds human blood must answer with their own blood, "for in His own image God has made mankind." Then He repeated the blessing: be fruitful and spread across the earth (9:4–7).

Next God established a covenant—a solemn, binding promise—with Noah, with his descendants, and with every living creature that had stepped off the ark. Never again, He swore, would floodwaters cut off all life or destroy the earth (9:8–11).

As the sign of that pledge, God placed His rainbow in the clouds. Strikingly, the rainbow is not primarily a reminder for people, but for God Himself. Whenever clouds gather and the rainbow appears, He will "remember the everlasting covenant" He made with every living creature on earth (9:12–17).

Then the focus turns to Noah's own family. His three sons were Shem, Ham, and Japheth, and from these three the whole earth was eventually populated. Scripture slips in one extra detail: Ham was the father of Canaan (9:18–19).

Noah farmed the soil and planted a vineyard. One day he drank too much of its wine, got drunk, and lay uncovered inside his tent. Ham saw his father's nakedness and went outside to tell his two brothers. Shem and Japheth laid a garment across their shoulders, walked backward into the tent with their faces turned away, and covered their father (9:20–23).

When Noah sobered up and learned what had happened, he pronounced a curse on Canaan, Ham's son: he would become a servant to his brothers. Then he blessed Shem, praising the LORD as the God of Shem, and prayed that God would expand Japheth's territory and let him dwell in the tents of Shem (9:24–27).

Noah lived 350 years after the flood and died at the age of 950 (9:28–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Human life is sacred.** The reason God forbids murder is not merely social order, but that every human being bears God's image. To kill a person is to strike at the Creator whose likeness is stamped upon them.

**All life belongs to God.** People may eat meat, yet they must not consume blood. Every meal becomes a quiet reminder that life—even an animal's life—belongs to God, not to us to treat carelessly.

**God binds Himself by His own word.** In this covenant, God places no conditions on Noah. God alone makes the promise, and He extends it to every living creature, not just humans. The rainbow is the pledge He sets up to hold Himself to His word.

**Even the best people stumble.** Noah was a righteous man who obeyed God through years of building the ark. Yet immediately afterward, Scripture shows him drunk in his tent. The Bible never airbrushes the flaws of its heroes.

**Covering another person's shame.** Ham spotted his father's disgrace and went out to broadcast it. Shem and Japheth quietly walked backward and covered him without looking. How we respond to someone else's weakness exposes what lives in our own hearts.

## Deeper Meaning

**A rainbow around God's throne.** The prophet Ezekiel saw the brilliance of God's glory shining like "a rainbow in a cloud on a rainy day" (Ezekiel 1:28). The apostle John also saw a rainbow encircling God's throne in heaven (Revelation 4:3). The emblem of mercy after judgment rests right beside the throne of the Ruler of all things.

**Noah's promise guarantees later promises.** Through the prophet Isaiah, God reassured His people: just as I swore that the waters of Noah would never again cover the earth, so I swear I will not be angry with you again (Isaiah 54:9). Because God kept His first promise, we can trust Him to keep the next.

**The life is in the blood.** The Law of Moses later explained that the life of a creature is in its blood, and God gave blood on the altar to make atonement for sin (Leviticus 17:11). That thread leads straight to the cross, where Jesus poured out His blood to give us life. Even the early church still instructed Gentile believers to abstain from blood (Acts 15:20).

**God's image in every neighbor.** The apostle James draws directly on 9:6 when talking about how we speak: with the same tongue we praise our Lord and then curse human beings, "who have been made in God's likeness" (James 3:9). Not only murder, but even words that demean another person insult the image of God.

**The blessing of Shem.** Noah's blessing on Shem opens the trail to chapter 11, where Shem's family line leads directly to Abraham (11:10–26). Through that lineage, God will bless every nation on earth—including the descendants of Japheth.

## Notes & Nuances

**The "curse of Ham" has been tragically misused.** For centuries, some people claimed Black people were cursed descendants of Ham in order to justify enslaving Africans. That reading is false from top to bottom. The curse was never spoken over Ham, but over Canaan, Ham's son. The passage never mentions skin color at all. Canaan was the ancestor of the Canaanite nations whose land Israel would later enter.

**What exactly did Ham do?** The text only says Ham "saw" his father's nakedness and went out to tell his brothers. Some suspect a worse offense veiled behind delicate wording. Yet the simplest reading makes full sense on its own: Ham shamed his father by broadcasting his vulnerability instead of covering it—a grave act of disrespect in the ancient world.

**Why did the curse fall on Canaan rather than Ham?** Scripture does not explain. Some think Noah spoke prophetically about the future character of Canaan's descendants rather than punishing an individual child. Commentators continue to debate this point.

**Verse 9:6 and capital punishment.** Some read this verse as a permanent command requiring governments to execute murderers. Others take it as a vivid statement of how grave murder is rather than a binding criminal code. Believers today still disagree over capital punishment, and both sides appeal to this verse.

**Reporting is not endorsing.** Genesis records Noah's drunkenness without editorial commentary. Yet the fallout—a fractured family and a curse echoing across generations—speaks loudly enough about where drunkenness leads.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, read Noah's drunkenness as a foreshadowing of Jesus. Wine from Noah's vineyard pointed to Christ's suffering; Noah's nakedness pointed to Christ's exposed body on the cross. Ham's mockery pictured those who scorn Christ's suffering, while Shem and Japheth's reverent covering pictured those who believe. *(The City of God, Book XVI, chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: his typological reading of Noah's drunkenness in City of God XVI.2 is frequently cited -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, observed that because human beings carry God's image, no one can injure a fellow human without wounding God Himself. Even though sin has marred God's image in us, Calvin wrote, enough of it still endures for God to stand guard over every human life. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 9 verse 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the points that harming a human wounds God and that God's image remains though marred are in his commentary on 9:6 -->

**Gerhard von Rad**, a German Old Testament scholar, pointed out that the Hebrew word for rainbow is the very same word for a warrior's hunting or battle bow. After the flood, God hangs His war bow up in the clouds: the battle of wrath is over. *(Genesis, Old Testament Library)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the image of the rainbow as a warrior's bow hung up in the clouds is commonly linked with von Rad; many other scholars note it too, exact wording uncertain -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, remarked that Noah stood firm amid an entire wicked generation, yet fell when alone in his own tent. Even good people, Henry warned, must watch themselves most carefully in quiet, comfortable seasons. He also sharply faulted Ham: a son who exposes and laughs at his father's stumble reveals a corrupted heart. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: certain Henry draws lessons about good people falling and about Ham's sin; unsure of his exact wording regarding Noah standing firm amidst a wicked generation -->

**Leon Kass**, an American Jewish scholar, read Ham's act as an assault on paternal authority. By looking and then gossiping about it, Ham was not merely being rude—he was pulling his father down to his own level.
