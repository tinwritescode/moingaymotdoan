# Genesis 10 — The Table of Nations

## Story

After the flood, Noah's three sons—Shem, Ham, and Japheth—had children of their own. This chapter does not tell a plot-driven story; instead it lays out a catalog of who descended from whom and where each family settled. Readers have long called it the "Table of Nations," the Bible's very first map of the world (10:1).

First come the descendants of Japheth. They are the peoples stretching far to the north and west, many of them "maritime peoples" living along coastlines and islands. Scripture says they spread into their territories "according to their languages, by clans within their nations" (10:2–5).

Next come the descendants of Ham: Cush, Mizraim (that is, Egypt), Put, and Canaan (10:6–7). Suddenly, right in the middle of a dry roster, one vivid figure steps forward: Nimrod, "who began to be a mighty one on the earth," a hunter so legendary his name became a proverb (10:8–9).

Nimrod built his kingdom in the land of Shinar, beginning with Babylon (Babel) and several neighboring cities. From there he pushed north into Assyria, building Nineveh and the great cities around it (10:10–12). Babylon and Nineveh—those two names would one day belong to the two towering empires that weighed heavily on Israel.

Mizraim fathered several peoples, including the group from whom the Philistines came (10:13–14). Canaan fathered Sidon, the Hittites, and a string of peoples readers of the Bible will encounter again and again: Jebusites, Amorites, and Hivites. Scripture even traces the borders of their territory, running from Sidon down to Gaza and across toward Sodom and Gomorrah (10:15–20).

Last come the descendants of Shem, introduced as the forefather of "all the sons of Eber." From Shem came Arphaxad, then Shelah, then Eber. Eber had two sons. One was named Peleg—meaning "division"—"because in his days the earth was divided." His brother was Joktan, father of thirteen clans in the east (10:21–31).

The chapter closes with a neat summary: these are the clans of Noah's three sons, and from them the nations spread across the earth after the flood (10:32).

## Key Takeaways

**Every nation belongs to one family.** Everyone on earth appears on this single family tree. No people dropped from another planet; no nation is a separate species. Long before any distinction between Jew and Gentile, Scripture declares that all of us are kin.

**Diversity is part of God's blessing.** God had told Noah's family to fill the earth. Here that blessing comes to life: peoples spread out, each with its own land, language, clan, and culture. The chapter describes this calmly, as something good.

**Power can bend in the wrong direction.** Nimrod is the first person called a "mighty one," and he turns his strength toward building kingdoms and cities. Scripture passes no open verdict on him here, yet Babel—the city he founded—will be the very site of the Tower of Babel in the next chapter.

**Even the chosen line is just one branch.** Shem received Noah's blessing, yet in this table Shem's family is not placed on a separate pedestal. God is about to call one man from this line, Abraham—not to ignore everyone else, but to bring blessing to all the nations listed right here.

## Deeper Meaning

**All nations from one man.** Preaching in Athens, Paul voiced the exact truth this chapter reveals: "From one man He made every nation of men," determining where they should live across the whole earth (Acts 17:26). On that basis, he urged people everywhere to seek God.

**From the Table of Nations to the promise for the nations.** Immediately after this list and the Tower of Babel, God calls Abraham and promises that "all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (12:3). Those "families" are the very nations cataloged right here.

**The Day of Pentecost.** When the Holy Spirit was poured out, devout Jews "from every nation under heaven" had gathered in Jerusalem, and everyone heard the disciples speaking in their own native language (Acts 2:5–11). The list of lands in Acts reads like a brand-new Table of Nations—only this time gathered together rather than scattered.

**The crowd before God's throne.** The Bible's final book reveals how the story ends: a multitude too large to count, "from every nation and tribe and people and tongue," standing together before God's throne (Revelation 7:9). Those four terms echo the very fourfold refrain of Genesis 10: lands, languages, clans, and nations.

**The genealogy of Jesus.** Luke traces Jesus' ancestry back through Arphaxad, Shem, and Noah all the way to Adam (Luke 3:36–38). Luke's point is clear: Jesus is not merely a descendant of Abraham; He belongs to the entire human race.

## Notes & Nuances

**This is not a modern family tree.** Many names in this list are names of peoples or regions rather than individual people. "Mizraim" is the Hebrew name for Egypt; "Canaan" refers to both an ancestor and a land; and several names carry the Hebrew plural ending *-im* (rendered as *-ites* in English, such as the Ludites) to denote a people group. "Fathered" here can mean "gave rise to," not strictly biological parentage.

**How are the nations grouped?** The peoples are organized not only by bloodline, but also by geography and historical ties. That explains some surprising placements: Canaan, for example, sits under Ham even though Canaanite languages were closely related to Hebrew. Ancient writers grouped nations through political and geographical relationships, not solely by language families as modern linguists do.

**Story order versus time order.** Chapter 10 already mentions nations having their own "languages," even though chapter 11 tells the story of the Tower of Babel where human speech was first confused. The two chapters are not arranged in strict chronological sequence. Chapter 10 gives the wide-angle panorama; chapter 11 rewinds to explain *why* humanity scattered.

**"The earth was divided" in Peleg's days.** Scripture does not specify what this division was. Many take it as the scattering of peoples after the Tower of Babel. <!-- low: uncertain whether any notable scholars hold this or only popular theories -->Some read this as the continents drifting apart, though that view has no support in the context.<!-- /low -->

**The number seventy.** Count up the nations listed in this table, and you arrive at roughly seventy. Ancient Jewish tradition often spoke of the "seventy nations" of the world. Exact counts vary slightly depending on how one tallies the names, so seventy is best understood as a symbolic number representing fullness and completeness.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, read Nimrod through the ancient Greek Bible, which called him a giant hunter "against the Lord." To Augustine, Nimrod embodied human pride rising up in defiance of God, and he was the one who built Babel—the beginning of the earthly city set in opposition to the city of God. *(The City of God, Book XVI, chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine reads "before the LORD" as "against the LORD" following the Greek text and links Nimrod with Babel in City of God XVI.4 -->

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, noticed something remarkable: Israel is nowhere to be found in the Table of Nations. Scripture sketches the entire world first, before ever telling the story of the chosen people. In Sarna's view, this table looks down on no nation; it declares that the Bible sees all peoples as one shared humanity created by God. *(Understanding Genesis)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Israel's absence from the table and its universal scope are familiar points of Sarna's; unsure whether in Understanding Genesis or his JPS Genesis commentary -->

**Gerhard von Rad**, a German Old Testament scholar, argued that placing the Table of Nations immediately before Abraham's call was deliberate.
