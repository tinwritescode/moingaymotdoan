# Genesis 23 — Sarah's Burial Place

## Story

After the events on Mount Moriah, Sarah died. She lived to be a hundred and twenty-seven years old. She passed away in Hebron, in the land of Canaan—the very land God had promised to her descendants. Abraham came to mourn over his wife and weep for her (23:1–2).

When he rose from weeping, he went to speak with the Hittites, the local people living at Hebron. He stated his situation plainly: "I am a foreigner and an outsider among you." After decades in this land, he did not own even a patch of ground to bury his wife. He asked them to let him buy a burial site (23:3–4).

The Hittites answered with warm courtesy. They called him "God's chosen one among us" and told him to choose the finest of their tombs; no one would refuse him (23:5–6). It sounded generous, yet look closely: they offered to lend him a grave, not to sell him land.

Abraham bowed low to them, then stated clearly what he wanted. He asked them to speak on his behalf to Ephron son of Zohar, so he could buy the cave of Machpelah at the end of Ephron's field. He wanted to pay "full price," so the site would truly belong to him (23:7–9).

Ephron happened to be sitting right there among the crowd at the city gate. He spoke up loudly before everyone, offering to give Abraham not just the cave, but the entire field as well (23:10–11). Abraham bowed again and still insisted on paying (23:12–13). So Ephron replied as if it were a trifle: the land is worth four hundred shekels of silver, "but what is that between you and me?" (23:14–15). Behind that polite modesty sat a steep asking price.

Abraham did not haggle. Right there in front of the Hittites, he weighed out the full four hundred shekels of silver, according to the standard used by merchants (23:16). So the field, the cave, and even all the trees within the field's borders became Abraham's property. Everyone gathered at the city gate served as witnesses (23:17–18).

Then Abraham buried Sarah in that cave near Mamre, that is, Hebron. The chapter closes like a legal deed: the field and its cave were officially transferred by the Hittites to Abraham as a burial site (23:19–20).

## Key Takeaways

**People of faith still weep.** Abraham trusted God enough to lay his own son on an altar. Yet when his wife died, he mourned and wept for her (23:2). Faith does not require anyone to harden their heart before death. Grieving the people you love is not a lack of faith.

**God promised a whole country, yet he owns only a grave.** God had promised Abraham the entire land of Canaan. Still, when his wife died, he remained "a foreigner and an outsider," forced to pay for a place to bury her. The promise had not yet arrived, yet he did not let go of the promise.

**A small plot of land as a pledge of a huge promise.** Abraham did not carry his wife back to his old homeland in Mesopotamia. He buried her right inside the land God had promised. Buying that piece of ground was a way of declaring: I believe this country will one day belong to my children.

**Keep your dealings clean and aboveboard.** Abraham refused to take the land as a free gift. He paid the full price, in public, at the place where legal business was done. He did not want anyone claiming later that the plot was merely a favor that could be taken back. Living among outsiders, he kept his integrity spotless.

**Courtesy worth imitating.** Both sides showed deep respect. Abraham bowed twice before the local people (23:7; 23:12), and they called him a man chosen by God. Believers living among people who do not share their faith can still earn respect through the way they carry themselves.

## Deeper Meaning

**Strangers on the earth.** The letter to the Hebrews says Abraham lived in the promised land "as a stranger in a foreign country," and that all the patriarchs died in faith without receiving what was promised, yet they "acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth" (Hebrews 11:9; 11:13). Abraham's words to the Hittites are a concrete example. That same letter says they were longing for a better homeland—a heavenly one (Hebrews 11:16).

**Not even a foot of ground.** Retelling Abraham's story, Stephen notes that God gave him no inheritance in the land, "not even a foot of ground," only a promise (Acts 7:5). The tomb at Machpelah was the only soil Abraham ever owned. Everything else he held by faith.

**A family burial ground.** The cave of Machpelah later became the resting place of Abraham, Isaac, Rebekah, Leah, and Jacob (25:9; 49:29–32). Jacob died in Egypt yet still charged his sons to carry his body back here (50:13). That grave became an anchor tying the whole family to the promised land, even when they lived far away.

**Buying land in dark times.** Centuries later, with the Babylonian army besieging Jerusalem, God told the prophet Jeremiah to buy a field and weigh out the silver properly (Jeremiah 32:9). That purchase was a sign that "Houses, fields, and vineyards will again be bought in this land" (Jeremiah 32:15). Just like Abraham, buying land was an act of trust in the future God had promised.

**Weeping with hope.** Jesus Himself wept at the tomb of His friend (John 11:35). Paul does not tell believers not to grieve when loved ones die; he tells them not to grieve "like the rest, who are without hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). Abraham wept, then stood up to secure a resting place in the land of promise. That is grief anchored in hope.

## Notes & Nuances

**A whole chapter about a land deal.** Some readers find this chapter dry: God speaks no words, no miracle happens, and all we get is bargaining and weighing silver. Yet Scripture tells it in such detail on purpose. Every detail—paying the full price, public witnesses, even the trees within the boundaries—proves the plot was bought legally and beyond dispute.

**Was four hundred shekels a lot or a little?** The Bible does not say outright, yet comparisons show it was steep: the prophet Jeremiah bought a field for just seventeen shekels (Jeremiah 32:9). Ephron's "what is that between you and me?" is classic ancient Near Eastern polite bargaining: his mouth offers a gift while his hand names a price. Abraham took the hit without arguing, perhaps out of grief, and perhaps because he wanted the ownership settled once and for all.

**Who were the "Hittites" (sons of Heth)?** They were descendants of Heth, in the line of Canaan (10:15). <!-- low: unsure which specific scholars say this, just a common view -->Many scholars think they were not necessarily the Hittites of the great empire in Asia Minor, but a smaller group living in Canaan who shared the same name.<!-- /low --> The question is still debated.

**A tomb in Hebron or in Shechem?** In Stephen's sermon, he mentions a tomb that Abraham bought from the sons of Hamor in Shechem (Acts 7:16). Yet this chapter says Abraham bought from Ephron in Hebron, while Jacob was the one who bought land in Shechem (33:19). Some suggest Stephen compressed two purchases into one, the way people condense history in a speech. Others see a slip in the retelling. It remains a difficult point that commentators still discuss.

**Jewish tradition on Sarah's death.** Because Sarah's death is told right after Abraham offers Isaac, ancient Jewish teachers suggested she died of shock upon hearing what her husband had done with her son. The Bible says nothing of the kind. The two events may have been separated by many years: when Isaac was bound, he was old enough to carry firewood up a mountain, whereas when his mother died he was thirty-seven years old (17:17; 22:6).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, points to Abraham weeping over his wife to show that God does not forbid believers from grieving when they lose someone dear. He pushes back against the Stoic philosophers, who thought the wise person should stay unmoved as stone. Still, he adds that grief should have its measure, because believers hold onto hope. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 23 verse 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin defending moderated grief and critiquing Stoic apathy here; unsure of his exact wording -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, notes that the very first piece of ground Abraham owned in the promised land was a grave. He also praises Abraham for turning down the free offer: he wanted to pay fairly so he owed no one favors, and so his descendants could hold the land without anyone ever reclaiming it. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Henry discusses buying the tomb as a sign of faith in the promise and refusing the free gift; unsure of his exact wording -->

**Gerhard von Rad**, a twentieth-century German Old Testament scholar, argues that this chapter matters precisely because of its careful legal detail. That purchased plot is the first installment—tiny as it is—of the promise of the land. As he puts it, in life the patriarchs were outsiders, yet in death they lay in soil of their own. *(Genesis, Old Testament Library)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall von Rad treating the burial plot as a real down payment on the land promise; unsure of his exact wording -->

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, explains the negotiation through ancient Near Eastern sales customs: offering land for free was polite etiquette, not a literal gift. He views four hundred shekels as a very high price, which Abraham accepted because he needed secure, indisputable ownership. *(Genesis, JPS Torah Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Sarna discussing polite bargaining customs and the high price; unsure which other sales he compares it to -->
