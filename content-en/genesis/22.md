# Genesis 22 — Abraham Offers Isaac

## Story

Some time later, God tested Abraham. He called his name, and Abraham answered, "Here I am." Then God spoke an unthinkable command: "Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love," go to the land of Moriah, and offer him as a burnt offering on one of the mountains God would show him (22:1–2). The son he had waited twenty-five years for, the son on whom every promise rested, God was now asking back.

Abraham did not say a word. Early the next morning, he saddled his donkey, split the wood, took Isaac and two servants, and set out. On the third day, he looked up and saw the place in the distance. He told the two servants to stay with the donkey: "The boy and I will go over there to worship, and then we will return to you" (22:3–5).

Abraham placed the wood on Isaac's back, while he himself carried the fire and the knife. The two walked on together. Along the way, Isaac called out, "My father!" Abraham answered, "Here I am, my son." Isaac asked, "The fire and the wood are here, but where is the lamb for the burnt offering?" Abraham replied, "God Himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering, my son." And the two walked on together (22:6–8).

When they reached the place, Abraham built an altar, arranged the wood, bound Isaac, and laid him on top of the wood. He reached out his hand and took the knife to slaughter his son. At that very moment, the angel of the LORD called to him from heaven: "Abraham, Abraham!" Again he answered, "Here I am." The angel told him not to lay a hand on the boy or do anything to him, for now God knew that Abraham truly feared Him, since he had not withheld his son, his only son (22:9–12).

Abraham looked up and saw behind him a ram caught by its horns in a thicket. He took the ram and offered it as a burnt offering in place of his son. He named that place Jehovah-jireh, meaning "The LORD Will Provide." From then on people had a saying: "On the mountain of the LORD it will be provided" (22:13–14).

The angel called to Abraham a second time. God swore by Himself: because Abraham had not withheld his only son, God would surely bless him, multiply his descendants like the stars in the sky and the sand on the seashore, and bless all the nations of the earth through his offspring, because he had obeyed God's voice. Then Abraham went back down to his servants, and together they returned to Beersheba (22:15–19).

The chapter ends with family news. Word reached Abraham that his brother Nahor back in the old homeland had eight sons by his wife Milcah and four more by a concubine. Among Nahor's grandchildren was a girl named Rebekah (22:20–24). Read on to chapter 24, and you will learn who she is.

## Key Takeaways

**God tests us right where we love most.** God did not ask for Abraham's flocks or silver and gold. He said, "your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love"—word by word, pressing right on the tenderest spot. The question behind the test is simple: does Abraham love the gift more, or the Giver?

**The test is agonizing because it seems to contradict the promise itself.** God had promised that Abraham's descendants would come through Isaac (21:12). Now God tells him to offer Isaac up. Abraham cannot see how those two words fit together. All he can do is trust that God knows how to keep His own promise.

**Obedience that never loses hope.** He tells his servants "we will return," and tells his son "God Himself will provide." Those are not comforting lies. They are the words of a man who does not know what God will do, yet is sure God will do what is right.

**God provides.** The key line of the entire chapter is "The LORD Will Provide." Abraham speaks it before he sees anything, and then names the mountain after it once he sees. The ram was already there, just waiting for him to look up.

**God does not want human sacrifice.** The story ends with God stopping Abraham's hand and supplying an animal in the boy's place. The surrounding nations practiced child sacrifice to their gods. Abraham's God asks for the heart, not a child's blood.

## Deeper Meaning

**Abraham believed God could raise the dead.** The letter to the Hebrews explains why Abraham was willing to go: he reasoned that God had the power to raise the dead, and in a figurative sense, he did receive his son back from death (Hebrews 11:17–19). His words to the servants—"we will return"—probably carried that very hope.

**God did not spare His own Son.** The angel praises Abraham because he "did not withhold" his only son (22:12; 22:16). Paul uses nearly the same words to speak of God Himself: He "did not spare His own Son, but gave Him up for us all" (Romans 8:32). What God stopped Abraham from carrying through to the end, God Himself carried through to the end. The voice from heaven at Jesus' baptism calls Him "My beloved Son" (Matthew 3:17), echoing "your only son Isaac, whom you love."

**Where is the lamb?** Isaac's question hangs in the air across centuries. On Mount Moriah, God provided a ram. Yet the full answer arrives when John the Baptist points to Jesus and says, "Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29).

**Wood on his back, a cross on His shoulders.** Isaac carried the wood up the mountain for the offering (22:6). Jesus carried His own cross outside the city to Golgotha (John 19:17). From very early on, Christians heard those two scenes answering each other.

**Mount Moriah.** Centuries later, King Solomon built the LORD's temple in Jerusalem "on Mount Moriah" (2 Chronicles 3:1). The very place where Abraham offered his son and God provided a substitute became the place where Israel offered sacrifices for generations.

**Faith made visible through action.** James points to this exact story to show that real faith must show up in deeds. Abraham had believed God long before (15:6), yet on Mount Moriah that faith was brought out into the open and completed (James 2:21–23).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why would God ever demand something like this?** This is the hardest part of the chapter, and it should not be glossed over. Scripture makes clear from the very first verse that this was a test—the reader knows up front what Abraham did not. Later on, God fiercely condemns child sacrifice, calling it "something I never commanded, nor did it even enter My mind" (Jeremiah 7:31). Many read chapter 22 as a decisive lesson for God's people: this God is not like the pagan deities who demand the blood of children. Even so, the story leaves a lingering ache, and interpreters in every age have wrestled with it.

**"Testing" is not "tempting."** The letter of James states that God tempts no one (James 1:13). Temptation tries to pull someone into evil. Testing gives faith a chance to be proven and strengthened, like gold refined in fire.

**"Now I know" does not mean God did not know before.** The Bible often speaks of God in human terms. The meaning here is that what was already in Abraham's heart has now been demonstrated in action, out in the open for all to see.

**Isaac was not a small child.** Isaac was strong enough to carry a full load of firewood up a mountain, so he must have been a teenager or young man. A father past a hundred years old could hardly have bound him against his will. Ancient Jewish tradition therefore held that Isaac willingly let his father bind him. The Bible itself stays silent about what Isaac felt.

**The genealogy at the end is not a detour.** A list of Nahor's children looks dry right after such a tense drama. Yet tucked inside it is one name: Rebekah. Isaac has just been spared from death, and the story is already preparing his bride. The promise of descendants is moving forward.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, writes that Abraham never doubted God's promise, because he trusted God could raise his son from the dead after the sacrifice. He sees Isaac carrying the wood as a picture of Jesus carrying the cross, and the ram caught by its horns in the thicket as a picture of Jesus crowned with thorns, dying in our place. *(The City of God, Book XVI, Chapter 32)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Book XVI chapter 32 explicitly presents all three points, widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, argues that the hardest part of the test was not a father's grief. The hardest part was that God's command seemed to collide head-on with God's own promise. Abraham had to trust that God does not contradict Himself, even when he could not see how God would untie the knot. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 22 verses 1 and 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the apparent contradiction between command and promise is the centerpiece of Calvin's exposition of early chapter 22 -->

**Søren Kierkegaard**, the nineteenth-century Danish philosopher, devoted an entire book to this chapter. He says people retell Abraham's story far too casually, forgetting the terror of that three-day walk. To him, Abraham is the "knight of faith": he gives Isaac up completely while still believing he will receive Isaac back in this very life. He also asks whether God's call can ever stand above ordinary moral duty—a question he leaves open rather than answering glibly. *(Fear and Trembling, 1843)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the knight of faith and the teleological suspension of the ethical are the central arguments of the entire book -->

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, points out that ancient Israelites heard this story in a world where child sacrifice to gods was nothing unusual. What stood out to them was not that God asked for the child, but that God stopped the father's hand. In his reading, the story teaches that God asks for total devotion, never human life. *(Understanding Genesis; Genesis, JPS Torah Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Sarna reads chapter 22 against the backdrop of ancient child sacrifice; unsure of his exact wording and which book -->

**Tim Keller**, a New York pastor who died in 2023, opens his book on modern idols with this very story. In his view, Isaac was in danger of becoming the thing Abraham leaned on more than God Himself. God tested Abraham to free him from that idol, not to rob him of Isaac. Keller adds that Abraham was spared from sacrificing his son because one day God Himself would sacrifice His own Son. *(Counterfeit Gods, Chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: chapter 1 of Counterfeit Gods focuses on Abraham and Isaac, centering on Isaac as a potential idol and God giving His own Son -->
