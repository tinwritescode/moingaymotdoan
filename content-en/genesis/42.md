# Genesis 42 — The Brothers Come for Grain

## Story

The famine reaches the land of Canaan. Hearing that there is grain in Egypt, Jacob rebukes his sons: "Why are you staring at one another?" He tells them to go down and buy grain so the household will live and not die. Ten of Joseph's brothers set out. Only Benjamin, Joseph's full brother, stays behind, because Jacob fears harm might come to him (42:1–5).

By this time Joseph is governor of the land and personally oversees the sale of grain. His brothers arrive and bow before him with their faces to the ground. Joseph knows them at once, yet acts like a stranger and speaks harshly to them. As Scripture puts it, "Although Joseph recognized his brothers, they did not recognize him." Seeing them bow down, he remembers his old dreams and accuses them of being spies come to see where Egypt is vulnerable (42:6–9).

Panic-stricken, the brothers protest their innocence: they are honest men, twelve brothers from one father; the youngest is at home with their father, and "one is no more." Joseph refuses to yield. Swearing by the life of Pharaoh, he declares they will not leave this place unless their youngest brother comes down. One of them must go fetch him while the rest stay locked up. Then he puts all ten in prison for three days (42:10–17).

On the third day, Joseph changes his terms. He tells them, "I fear God." Only one brother will remain in custody; the rest may carry grain home to their hungry families, provided they bring their youngest brother back to prove they are telling the truth (42:18–20).

Standing right in front of Joseph, the brothers turn to one another and speak in their native tongue: "Surely we are being punished because of our brother." They remember his anguish when he pleaded with them and they refused to listen; that is why this distress has overtaken them. Reuben scolds them: did I not tell you not to sin against the boy, and you would not listen? Now we have to answer for his blood! They have no idea Joseph understands every word, since he has been using an interpreter. Joseph turns away from them and weeps. Then he turns back, singles out Simeon, and has him bound before their eyes (42:21–24).

Joseph orders his servants to fill their sacks with grain, slip each man's silver back into his sack, and give them provisions for the road. At their lodging place that night, one brother opens his sack to feed his donkey and spots his silver right in the mouth of the sack. Trembling, they look at one another and ask, "What is this that God has done to us?" (42:25–28).

Back home, they tell their father everything that happened. As they empty their sacks, every single man finds his pouch of silver inside, and both they and their father are gripped with fear (42:29–35).

Jacob laments: Joseph is gone, Simeon is gone, and now they want to take Benjamin too; everything is working against him. Reuben offers to take charge of Benjamin, telling his father he may kill Reuben's own two sons if he fails to bring him back. Jacob will not hear of it. His youngest son will not go, for his brother is dead and "he alone is left." If harm should come to him, they will bring this gray-haired old man down to Sheol in sorrow (42:36–38).

## Key Takeaways

**The dream comes true, and almost no one notices.** More than twenty years earlier, Joseph dreamed that his brothers' sheaves bowed down to his sheaf (37:7). Now those same brothers bow facedown before him—begging for grain. The ones bowing have no idea they are fulfilling the dream; only Joseph knows.

**A sleeping conscience eventually wakes up.** For more than twenty years the brothers lived with what they had done. Yet three days in prison and one false accusation are enough to bring their younger brother rushing straight back into their minds. No one mentions him, yet they confess it on their own. No matter how deeply a sin is buried, trouble has a way of bringing it to the surface.

**Hard on the outside, tender within.** Joseph speaks harshly, throws his brothers in prison, and binds Simeon in front of them. Yet the moment he hears them own their guilt, he has to turn away to weep. The narrator shows us both sides at once so we do not rush to judge him.

**Jacob still plays favorites.** He clings to Benjamin just as he once kept Joseph close at home. Saying "he alone is left" (42:38) right in front of his ten other sons makes them sound as though they barely count. Meanwhile Simeon sits bound in an Egyptian prison, and Jacob is in no hurry to rescue him. The old family wound is still raw.

**For the first time, the brothers speak of God.** Through all of chapter 37—when they hated Joseph and sold him—not once did they mention God. Here, staring at the silver in their sacks, they ask what God is doing to them. They are finally beginning to see that what happens to them has to do with God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Recognizing and failing to recognize.** The verb "recognize" runs like a thread through the whole story. The brothers handed their father a bloodstained robe and asked him to examine and recognize whether it was his son's (37:32–33). In chapter 38, Tamar handed Judah his seal and staff and told him to recognize whose they were; he recognized them and had to admit his guilt (38:25–26). Now Joseph recognizes his brothers, while they look right at him and cannot recognize him (42:8). The men who once tricked others by asking them to look closely now fail to recognize the very man standing before them.

**Blood calling for a reckoning.** Reuben says they must now account for their brother's blood. His words echo God's words to Cain, the first brother-killer: your brother's blood cries out to me from the ground (4:10). Later, the law of Moses gives the same warning: be sure your sin will find you out (Numbers 32:23). The brothers never physically killed Joseph, yet deep down they know they are just as guilty.

**From the pit to the prison.** Years earlier, the brothers threw Joseph into an empty cistern and ignored his cries for mercy (37:24; 42:21). Now they spend three days locked in prison, uncertain of their fate. No one preaches a sermon to them, yet they taste a small measure of what their brother endured—and that taste opens their eyes.

**Standing right before them, unrecognized.** Again and again, Scripture tells of people meeting the one who saves them and failing to recognize him. John's Gospel says of Jesus that He came to His own, and His own did not receive Him (John 1:11). The two disciples on the road to Emmaus walked beside the risen Jesus for miles while their eyes were kept from recognizing Him (Luke 24:16). Just like Joseph's brothers, they recognized him only when he chose to reveal himself.

**Two kinds of sorrow over sin.** Paul distinguishes godly sorrow, which leads to repentance, from worldly sorrow, which brings death (2 Corinthians 7:10). In this chapter, the brothers feel fear and remorse. The story still has to test whether that sorrow actually changes their hearts. The answer arrives in chapter 44.

## Notes & Nuances

**Scripture never states why Joseph treats his brothers so harshly.** Readers across the centuries have understood his motives in different ways. Some believe he is testing whether his brothers have truly changed. Others think he still carries anger and is paying them back a little. Others think he wants the whole family—Benjamin and Jacob included—to come bow before him as his dreams foretold. It is also possible Joseph himself is torn, unsure what to do with the men who sold him. The narrator shows us his actions and his tears, and leaves us to weigh the reasons.

**Joseph lies and swears by the life of Pharaoh.** He knows full well his brothers are not spies, yet he accuses them anyway. He also swears, "As surely as Pharaoh lives" (42:15). Scripture records these actions without praising them. Readers should not assume everything Joseph does is meant to be imitated.

**Why Simeon?** The text never says. Some notice that Reuben, the oldest, had once tried to save Joseph (37:21–22)—a fact Joseph has just overheard with his own ears—so the second-born, Simeon, is taken instead. Others recall Simeon's violent streak when he and Levi slaughtered the men of Shechem (34:25). Both remain educated guesses.

**Reuben's offer sounds noble, yet it is reckless.** Reuben tells Jacob to kill his own two sons if he fails to bring Benjamin back (42:37). How would killing two grandsons console a grandfather mourning his son? Jacob rejects the offer. In the next chapter, Judah will make a very different pledge: he puts himself on the line (43:9).
