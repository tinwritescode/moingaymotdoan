# Genesis 11 — The Tower of Babel

## Story

After the flood, all humanity still spoke a single language. As people migrated eastward, they came across a wide plain in the land of Shinar—what is today southern Iraq—and settled there (11:1–2).

There was no stone on that plain. So they said to one another, "Come, let us make bricks and bake them thoroughly." They used brick instead of stone, and tar—a dark, asphalt-like pitch—instead of mortar. Then they went on: let us build a city for ourselves and a tower "that reaches to the heavens." They stated their reason plainly: to make a name for themselves, so they would not be scattered across the face of the earth (11:3–4).

The tower looked enormous in human eyes. Yet the story tells us God had to "come down" just to see the city and the tower the sons of men were building (11:5). He looked and said: they are one people speaking one language, and this is only the beginning of what they will do; from now on, nothing they devise will be beyond them (11:6).

They had said, "Come, let us make bricks," and "Come, let us build." Now God echoes them: "Come, let Us go down and confuse their language, so that they will not understand one another's speech" (11:7). At once, the project ground to a halt. God scattered humanity across the whole earth—the very thing they had tried to prevent. The city was named Babel, which sounds like the Hebrew word for "confuse" (11:8–9).

Then the pace shifts back to a genealogy. Nine generations stretch from Shem, the son of Noah, to Terah. Each follows the same steady pattern: how old the man was when he fathered his son, how many years he lived afterward, and that he had other sons and daughters. Lifespans shrink with every generation: Shem lived another five hundred years after his son was born, while by Nahor's time it was only a little over a hundred (11:10–26).

The family tree stops at Terah's household. Terah had three sons: Abram, Nahor, and Haran. Haran fathered Lot, then died young in his homeland, Ur of the Chaldeans, while his father was still alive. Abram married Sarai, and Nahor married Milcah. Then the story drops in one brief, heavy line: "But Sarai was barren; she had no children" (11:27–30).

Terah led Abram, Sarai, and his grandson Lot out of Ur, heading for the land of Canaan. Yet when they reached Haran, a city to the north, the whole family stopped and settled there. Terah died in Haran (11:31–32).

## Key Takeaways

**Humanity wants to make a name for itself.** The problem at Babel was not bricks or towers. Making bricks and building cities are not wrong in themselves. The problem lay in why they were building: to make a name for themselves, and to avoid spreading across the earth as God had intended (9:1). It was humanity trying to stand secure on its own without God.

**Even the tallest tower is tiny before God.** They set out to build a tower reaching the heavens. Yet God had to "come down" to see it. The story tells this with quiet irony: humanity's grandest monument, viewed from where God sits, is still minuscule.

**Judgment can also be a form of protection.** God did not smash the tower or kill anyone. He simply made it impossible for them to understand one another. How much evil could a united humanity accomplish when set against God? Dividing them curbed the reach of sin.

**The grand story zooms in on a single family.** From chapter 1 until now, Scripture has told the story of the entire human race. At the end of chapter 11, the camera narrows: one clan, then one household, then one childless couple. God is about to begin again—not with an empire, but with a single man.

## Deeper Meaning

**Babel and Pentecost.** At Babel, God confused human speech and scattered the peoples. Centuries later, at Pentecost in Jerusalem, the Holy Spirit came down on the disciples. People from every nation were gathered there, and each heard the disciples speaking in their own native language (Acts 2:5–11). The languages were not erased; instead, right within those different tongues, people understood one another again.

**A name made by human hands and a name given by God.** The builders of Babel said, let us make a name for ourselves. In the very next chapter, God tells Abram, "I will make your name great" (12:2). The people at Babel tried to construct their own fame and failed. Abram built nothing at all—he simply obeyed and set out—and God made his name great.

**Babel becomes Babylon.** The name Babel is also the name of Babylon. Throughout the Bible, Babylon stands as the symbol of arrogant human power rising up against God. In the very last book, Babylon appears as a great city crashing down in ruin (Revelation 18:2). A city built by people for their own glory never stands in the end.

**A city with foundations.** The people of Babel built a city so they would never have to wander. Abram did the exact opposite: he left the city behind and lived in tents, because he was looking forward to "the city with foundations, whose architect and builder is God" (Hebrews 11:10). Two ways of living are placed side by side at the close of chapter 11 and the opening of chapter 12.

**Every nation, every language.** On the final pages of Scripture, the apostle John sees a crowd too vast to count, from every nation, tribe, people, and language, standing together before God's throne (Revelation 7:9). The division that began at Babel is not the last word. The unity God brings does not wipe out our languages; it gathers them all home.

## Notes & Nuances

**Chapters 10 and 11 are not in strict chronological order.** Chapter 10 already describes the nations spreading out, each with its own language (10:5; 10:31). Chapter 11 then circles back to explain how that happened. This is a familiar storytelling pattern in Genesis: summarize the outcome first, then step back to tell the story in detail.

**The tower was likely a temple tower.** In ancient Mesopotamia, people commonly built stepped brick temple towers—ziggurats—with a shrine at the top. Many scholars believe the writer had that kind of structure in mind. Whether it refers to one particular ruin archaeologists have uncovered, no one can say for sure.

**The genealogy may not list every generation.** The genealogy in Luke includes a man named Cainan between Arphaxad and Shelah, who is not listed in chapter 11 (Luke 3:36). Ancient genealogies often skipped generations. For that reason, adding up the ages here to calculate exact historical dates calls for caution.

**Terah worshiped other gods.** Chapter 11 says nothing about Terah's faith. Yet the book of Joshua reveals that Israel's ancestors, Terah among them, "worshiped other gods" (Joshua 24:2). God did not call Abram because his family was more devout than everyone else.

**Who led whom out of Ur?** Chapter 11 reads as though Terah decided on his own to leave. In the New Testament, however, Stephen says God appeared to Abram while he was still in Mesopotamia, before he ever settled in Haran (Acts 7:2–4). The call may have come to Abram in Ur, and the whole family set out together. Stephen also says Abram left Haran after his father died, whereas the numbers here seem to suggest Terah was still living. The common explanation is that Abram was not the eldest son; his name simply appears first because he is the central figure in the story.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa over 1,600 years ago, argued that the ringleader behind the tower was Nimrod, introduced in the previous chapter as a mighty hunter "before" the Lord—which Augustine read as "against" the Lord. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, saw the sin of Babel as pride and deliberate defiance of God's will. God wanted humanity to spread across the earth, while they built a city to keep themselves clustered in one place. **Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, read the story as a sharp satire aimed at Babylon. Babylonians boasted that their city's name meant "gate of the god," yet Scripture turns it into a pun meaning "confusion." **Robert Alter**, the American Jewish scholar, pointed to the soundscape of the passage. In Hebrew, the words for "make bricks," "brick," "tar," "mortar," and "confuse" all chime against one another. Humanity's "Come, let us" is answered by God's own "Come, let Us." **Leon Kass**, the American Jewish scholar, read Babel as the story of a city striving for total self-sufficiency. Human-made bricks replace God-made stone, paired with one shared tongue and one shared name—humanity's dream of mastering its own destiny through technology and uniformity.
