# Genesis 40 — Two Dreams in Prison

## Story

Some time later, two officials of the king of Egypt offended their master: the chief cupbearer, who served the king's wine, and the chief baker, who oversaw the king's bread and pastries. Pharaoh grew angry and threw both of them into prison—the very prison where Joseph was being held. The captain of the guard assigned Joseph to attend to them, and they remained in custody for some time (40:1–4).

One night, both men dreamed, and each dream carried a meaning of its own. When Joseph came in the next morning, he saw that their faces were downcast and asked what was wrong. We both had dreams, they answered, and there is no one here to interpret them. Joseph replied, "Don't interpretations belong to God?" Tell me what you dreamed (40:5–8).

The chief cupbearer went first. He saw a vine with three branches. As soon as it budded, blossoms opened and clusters ripened into grapes. Holding Pharaoh's cup in his hand, he took the grapes, squeezed them into the cup, and placed the cup into Pharaoh's hand (40:9–11).

Joseph gave the interpretation: the three branches stand for three days. Within three days, Pharaoh will release you and restore you to your post, and you will place the cup in his hand just as before (40:12–13).

Then Joseph made one quiet request. When things go well for you, please remember me and mention me to Pharaoh so I can get out of this prison. I was kidnapped from the land of the Hebrews, and even here I have done nothing to deserve being thrown into this dungeon (40:14–15).

Hearing how favorable that interpretation was, the chief baker spoke up with his own dream. He was carrying three baskets of white bread on his head. The top basket held all kinds of baked goods for Pharaoh, yet birds kept swooping down and eating out of the basket (40:16–17).

This time, the interpretation was grim. The three baskets are three days. Within three days, Pharaoh will bring you out as well—only to take off your head and hang your body on a tree, where the birds will eat your flesh (40:18–19).

The third day was Pharaoh's birthday. The king threw a banquet for all his officials and had the two prisoners brought out. The chief cupbearer was restored to his post and once again placed the cup in Pharaoh's hand. The chief baker was hanged, just as Joseph had said (40:20–22).

Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph. He forgot all about him (40:23).

## Key Takeaways

**Suffering injustice does not have to harden your heart toward others.** Joseph was locked away for a crime he never committed, yet the moment he walked in that morning he noticed the troubled look on two other prisoners' faces and asked how they were doing (40:6–7). Pain easily turns people inward, yet Joseph kept his eyes open to the sorrow around him.

**All true wisdom belongs to God.** When the two officials sighed that no one could interpret their dreams, Joseph claimed no credit for himself and pointed straight upward: "Don't interpretations belong to God?" (40:8). Inside a country famous for its magicians and dream manuals, he placed God above them all.

**Tell the whole truth, even when the news is hard.** Joseph gladly delivered good news to the cupbearer, yet he did not dodge or sugarcoat the death sentence waiting for the baker (40:12–13; 40:18–19). Being faithful to God's word takes the courage to speak unwelcome truth.

**People quickly forget kindness, yet God never forgets.** Joseph asked the cupbearer for one small favor—just to remember him once freed—and the man forgot him completely (40:14; 40:23). The human door Joseph hoped for slammed shut, so that at God's appointed hour Pharaoh himself would send for him (41:14).

## Deeper Meaning

**Waiting in the dark is never wasted.** From the outside, two more years in prison (41:1) looked like wasted youth caused by an ungrateful man. Yet right inside the captain of the guard's prison, Joseph got to know officials who stood right beside Pharaoh and sharpened his gift for leadership, preparing for the day he would stand before Egypt's throne.

**A plea to be remembered between two fellow prisoners.** Joseph standing between two of the king's prisoners—one spared to live, the other lifted up to die—and asking to be remembered (40:14) has reminded many Christian readers of Golgotha, where Jesus hung between two criminals and promised Paradise to the one who asked Jesus to remember him (Luke 23:39–43).

**God does not forget those whom people leave behind.** Joseph's disappointment in 40:23 echoes a promise woven all through Scripture: even when human beings forget favors and walk away, God remembers the labor and faithfulness of those who belong to Him (Isaiah 49:15; Hebrews 6:10).

## Notes & Nuances

**Did Joseph lack faith by asking the cupbearer for help?** Some older commentators faulted Joseph for putting his trust in a human official rather than God, suggesting that was why he had to stay in prison two more years. Yet the text never criticizes him. An innocent prisoner using an honest, lawful avenue to clear his name is completely reasonable (Acts 25:11).

**Why does the text use the same expression for two opposite fates?** In the original Hebrew, the phrase Joseph speaks to both officials literally means *Pharaoh will lift up your head* (40:13; 40:19)—for the first man, raising him back to honor, and for the second, lifting his head right off his shoulders. That chilling wordplay underscores both Pharaoh's absolute power over life and death and the uncanny precision of Joseph's interpretation.

**What offices did these two men hold?** The chief cupbearer and chief baker were not ordinary kitchen servants, but high-ranking court officials trusted to guard the king's drink and food against poisoning. That is why the slightest suspicion inside the palace sent both of them straight to prison (40:1–3).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, reads the cupbearer's ingratitude (40:23) as a blunt portrait of human nature: people make eager promises while in trouble, then quickly forget those who helped them once comfort returns.
