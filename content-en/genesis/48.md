# Genesis 48 — Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh

## Story

Some time later, word reaches Joseph that his father is ill. Joseph sets out to visit him, bringing his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim. Told that Joseph has arrived, Israel rallies his strength and sits up on the bed (48:1–2).

Jacob recalls for Joseph what happened long ago at Luz, which is Bethel. God Almighty appeared to him there, blessed him, and promised: I will make you fruitful and multiply you into a multitude of peoples, and I will give this land to your descendants after you as an everlasting possession (48:3–4).

Then he makes a solemn declaration: your two sons born to you in Egypt, Ephraim and Manasseh, will now be counted as mine, on equal footing with Reuben and Simeon. Any children born to you after them will belong to you. Suddenly an old grief surfaces in his memory: as I was returning from Paddan, Rachel died along the way near Ephrath, which is Bethlehem, and I buried her there (48:5–7). Rachel lived long enough to bear only two sons; by adopting these two grandsons, Jacob is in a sense giving her two more.

Israel's aging eyes have grown dim. He asks, "Who are these?" Joseph replies, "They are the sons God has given me in this place." Jacob asks him to bring them close, then embraces and kisses them. He tells Joseph: I never expected to see your face again, and now God has let me see your children as well (48:8–11).

Joseph gently lifts the boys from his father's knees and bows facedown to the ground. Then he positions them carefully: Manasseh, the firstborn, toward Jacob's right hand; Ephraim, the younger, toward Jacob's left. Yet Israel deliberately crosses his arms. He lays his right hand on the head of Ephraim, the younger son, and his left hand on Manasseh (48:12–14).

Then he speaks his blessing: may the God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has been my shepherd all my life to this day, the angel who has redeemed me from all harm—may He bless these boys. May they carry my name and the names of my fathers Abraham and Isaac, and grow into a great multitude on the earth (48:15–16).

Joseph is displeased when he sees his father's right hand resting on Ephraim's head. He takes hold of his father's hand to move it onto Manasseh: "Not so, my father! This one is the firstborn; put your right hand on his head" (48:17–18).

Jacob refuses: "I know, my son, I know!" He too will become a people, and he too will be great. Yet his younger brother will be greater still, and his descendants will become a multitude of nations. That day he blesses them, declaring that Israel will pronounce this blessing in their name: "May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh." In so doing, he places Ephraim ahead of Manasseh (48:19–20). The man who once schemed for his older brother's birthright now knows firsthand how often God chooses the younger.

Finally, Israel tells Joseph: I am about to die, yet God will be with you and bring you back to the land of your fathers. And to you I give one ridge of land above your brothers, which I took from the Amorites with my sword and bow (48:21–22).

## Key Takeaways

**Adopting two foreign-born grandsons into the birthright.** Jacob adopts Manasseh and Ephraim—boys born in Egypt to the daughter of an Egyptian priest—as his own full sons, standing alongside Reuben and Simeon (48:5). Through this adoption, Joseph receives the double portion of the inheritance, the birthright Reuben had forfeited (1 Chronicles 5:1–2).

**God is not bound by human birth order.** Joseph carefully lines Manasseh up with his father's right hand and Ephraim with his left, yet Jacob deliberately crosses his hands to place his right hand on the younger boy (48:13–14; 48:19). God's choice rests on grace, not on who happened to be born first.

**God as the Shepherd of a whole lifetime.** Looking back over 147 turbulent years, Jacob does not boast of his own cleverness or achievements. He calls God the One who has shepherded him all his life to this very day, and the angel who has redeemed him from all harm (48:15–16).

**Faith facing the Promised Land from a deathbed in exile.** Even as he lies dying in wealthy Egypt, Jacob's heart stays anchored in Canaan: he rehearses God's promise at Luz and declares with certainty that God will bring his family back to the land of their fathers (48:3–4; 48:21).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jacob's defining act of faith in the New Testament.** When the letter to the Hebrews surveys Jacob's eventful life, the single moment it singles out to commend his faith is right here in chapter 48: when he was dying, he blessed each of Joseph's sons and worshiped leaning on his staff (Hebrews 11:21). The man who once used deceit to steal a blessing from a blind father (27:18–29) is now nearly blind himself, yet spiritually clear-sighted as he passes on God's blessing in faith.

**The younger placed before the older across Genesis.** Jacob's crossed hands continue a recurring pattern throughout the entire book: Abel over Cain, Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, and now Ephraim over Manasseh (48:19–20). Centuries later, Paul appeals to this very principle to speak of God's free choice by grace (Romans 9:10–13; 1 Corinthians 1:27–28).

**The first time God is called a Shepherd.** Jacob's blessing in 48:15 introduces the image of God as the shepherd who tends and feeds His people. That intimate image blossoms in Psalm 23, runs through Ezekiel's prophecy (Ezekiel 34:11–16), and reaches its fullness when Jesus calls Himself the good shepherd (John 10:11).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why do verses 48:15–16 pair *the angel* alongside God?** In his blessing, Jacob invokes the God of his fathers, the God who has shepherded him, and *the angel who has redeemed me from all harm*—and in the Hebrew, the verb "may He bless" is singular. That shows *the angel* here is no ordinary created messenger, but God Himself appearing to rescue Jacob (cf. 31:11–13; 32:24–30).

**Where is the *ridge of land* Jacob says he took from the Amorites with *sword and bow* (48:22)?** The Hebrew word translated *ridge of land* (literally "shoulder") is the exact same word as the city name *Shechem*. Genesis never recounts a battle fought personally by Jacob against the Amorites; earlier he purchased a plot near Shechem (33:19), and his sons later raided the city in chapter 34. Whether Jacob refers to an unrecorded conflict or speaks prophetically of territory Joseph's descendants would claim, Joseph's bones were indeed eventually buried right at Shechem (Joshua 24:32).

**How did Ephraim truly become greater than Manasseh?** In Israel's later history, Ephraim grew into the dominant tribe of the northern kingdom—so prominent that prophets regularly used the name *Ephraim* as shorthand for all ten northern tribes together (Isaiah 7:2; Hosea 5:3).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, saw Jacob's adoption of Ephraim and Manasseh as a profound lesson for Joseph: Jacob was lifting the two boys out of Egyptian court privilege and grafting them into the humble family that carried God's promise.
