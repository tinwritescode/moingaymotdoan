# Genesis 26 — Isaac and the Wells

## Story

Famine struck the land again, just as it had in Abraham's lifetime. Isaac moved to Gerar, where Abimelech, king of the Philistines, ruled. Perhaps he meant to keep going down to Egypt like his father before him. Yet the LORD appeared and told him not to go down to Egypt, but to stay in this land. God repeated to Isaac the promises He had made to Abraham: I will be with you, I will give all these lands to your offspring, I will make your descendants numerous as the stars in the sky, and through your offspring all nations of the earth will be blessed—all because Abraham obeyed God's voice (26:1–6).

In Gerar, Isaac repeated his father's exact mistake. When the local men asked about Rebekah, he said she was his sister, afraid they would kill him to take his beautiful wife (26:7). Some time later, King Abimelech looked down from a window, saw Isaac caressing Rebekah, and realized at once they were husband and wife. The king summoned Isaac and rebuked him: someone among his people could easily have slept with her, and Isaac would have brought guilt on the whole nation. Then the king issued a warning: anyone who harmed Isaac or his wife would be put to death (26:8–11).

That year Isaac sowed crops and reaped a hundredfold harvest, because the LORD blessed him. He grew wealthier and wealthier, with so many flocks, herds, and servants that the Philistines envied him. They filled in all the wells Abraham's servants had dug years before. Finally King Abimelech told Isaac, "Depart from us," because he had grown far too powerful for them (26:12–16).

Isaac moved into the Valley of Gerar. He reopened the wells his father had dug and gave them back the very names his father had given them. His servants dug in the valley and struck fresh spring water, yet the herdsmen of Gerar came and quarreled over it, claiming the water was theirs. Isaac named the well Esek, meaning "dispute." They dug another well and quarreled over that one too, so he named it Sitnah, meaning "opposition." He moved on again and dug a third well, and this time nobody fought over it. He named it Rehoboth, meaning "room," saying that at last the LORD had made room for them (26:17–22).

From there Isaac went up to Beersheba. That very night the LORD appeared to him: "Do not be afraid, for I am with you." God promised to bless him and multiply his descendants for Abraham's sake. Isaac built an altar, called on the name of the LORD, and pitched his tent there, while his servants started digging a well (26:23–25).

Unexpectedly, King Abimelech came to visit, bringing his adviser and his army commander. Isaac asked him straight out: you hated me and sent me away, so why come to me now? They answered that they could plainly see the LORD was with him, so they wanted to make a treaty promising he would not harm them. Isaac prepared a feast for them. Early the next morning they swore an oath to one another, and Isaac sent them off in peace (26:26–31). That very day, his servants came with news that they had struck water. Isaac named the well Shibah, and that is how the town came to be called Beersheba (26:32–33).

The chapter closes on a sad note. At forty years old, Esau married two Hittite women. Those two daughters-in-law brought bitter grief to Isaac and Rebekah (26:34–35).

## Key Takeaways

**The promise passes to the next generation.** God is not only Abraham's God. He appears to Isaac and speaks the ancient promises again, this time directly to him (26:3–4). A parent's faith does not automatically become a child's faith, yet the God of the parents wants to be the God of the children too.

**Children easily repeat their parents' mistakes.** Isaac may not even have known how his father lied twice about Sarah, yet out of fear he does the exact same thing. Family faults tend to roll downhill to the next generation unless someone stops them.

**Yielding without losing.** Driven off two wells in a row, Isaac neither fights back nor demands his rights. He simply moves on and digs again. In the end, he gets wide-open space, and the very ruler who sent him away comes knocking to make peace.

**When God is with someone, outsiders notice.** Abimelech did not worship Isaac's God, yet he says, "We can plainly see that the LORD has been with you" (26:28). God's blessing on a person cannot stay hidden, and it can win respect even from those outside the faith.

**Keeping a father's legacy alive.** Isaac reopens his father's wells and calls them by the names his father gave them (26:18). Not everyone is called to blaze a brand-new trail. Some are called to guard and clear out what the generation before them built.

## Deeper Meaning

**Blessed are the meek.** Isaac gives up his rights and still ends up with plenty of room. Jesus says, "Blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth" (Matthew 5:5). Proverbs likewise says that when a person's ways please the LORD, He makes even their enemies live at peace with them (Proverbs 16:7). Abimelech coming to Isaac is a textbook example.

**A wide-open place.** The name Rehoboth means "room" or "open space." The Psalms pick up the same Hebrew image: in distress, God relieves His servant by making room for him (Psalms 4:1), and He "brought me out into the open" (Psalms 18:19). After cramped places of strife and rivalry, God opens up breathing room.

**Faith and obedience.** God says He blesses Isaac because Abraham obeyed His voice (26:5). Yet the New Testament emphasizes that Abraham was counted righteous through faith (Romans 4:3). Those two truths do not clash. James explains that Abraham's "faith was working with his actions," and his faith was perfected through what he did (James 2:21–22). Obedience is the fruit that faith grows.

**Living water.** This whole chapter revolves around wells, because in a dry land, water means life. Later, God confronts His people for abandoning Him, "the fountain of living water," to dig cracked cisterns of their own (Jeremiah 2:13). And Isaiah promises a day when God's people will joyfully "draw water from the springs of salvation" (Isaiah 12:3).

**Living at peace with everyone.** Isaac had every right to be angry, and he said so honestly to Abimelech (26:27). Yet he still threw a feast, swore peace, and sent his visitors home safely. Paul teaches: "If it is possible on your part, live at peace with everyone" (Romans 12:18).

## Notes & Nuances

**Three times calling a wife a sister.** This is the third time Genesis tells of a patriarch passing off his wife as his sister (12:13; 20:2; 26:7). Some scholars view these as three retellings of a single older story. Others read them as three separate events showing a recurring weakness running through the family. Either way, the narrator invites us to compare them: this time no one is taken into the royal palace, and once again it is the foreign king who upholds moral decency.

**What does "caressing" mean?** The word translated "caressing" in verse 26:8 is a Hebrew verb that sounds like Isaac's name, whose root meaning is "to laugh" or "to play." Here it describes intimate affection between husband and wife—enough for the king to tell at once they were not brother and sister.

**Philistines in Abraham's era?** According to historical records outside the Bible, the Philistines rose to power along the Canaanite coast around the twelfth century BC, long after the patriarchs. Some suggest the writer used the familiar name of his own day for the people living in that area. Others think an earlier group bearing that name had already settled there. The question remains debated.

**Did Abraham keep the law before there was a law?** Verse 26:5 says Abraham kept "My charge, My commandments, My statutes, and My laws"—terms later used for the Law of Moses. Ancient Jewish teachers took this to mean Abraham kept the whole Torah before it was even given. A simpler reading is that he faithfully obeyed everything God asked of him based on what God had revealed.

**Two Hittite daughters-in-law.** Esau married local women—the very thing Abraham had gone to great lengths to prevent for Isaac (24:3). That brief closing note sets up the next chapter: Rebekah will use this very heartache as her reason to send Jacob far away (27:46).

## What Commentators Say

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, makes no excuse for Isaac lying about his wife. In Calvin's view, Isaac let fear drown out the promise God had just spoken to him, putting his wife in danger to save his own skin. **Gerhard von Rad**, a twentieth-century German Old Testament scholar, points out that this is the only chapter in Genesis devoted solely to Isaac. Everywhere else, Isaac stands in the middle between his father's story and his sons' story. To von Rad, Isaac's figure is much quieter and less vivid than Abraham or Jacob, and many of his episodes mirror his father's life. *(Genesis, Old Testament Library)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall von Rad noting that Isaac is a subdued figure whose stories parallel Abraham's; unsure of his exact wording -->

**Gordon Wenham**, a British Old Testament scholar, argues that the narrator deliberately shapes Isaac's story to mirror Abraham's: famine, calling a wife a sister, King Abimelech, disputes over wells, and a treaty at Beersheba. In his reading, this design shows that Isaac is Abraham's true heir—both in the promise he carries and in the way God deals with him. *(Genesis 16–50, Word Biblical Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Wenham tracing the parallels between chapter 26 and Abraham's life; unsure of his exact wording and specific conclusion -->
