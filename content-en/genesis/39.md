# Genesis 39 — Joseph in Potiphar's House

## Story

Joseph was brought down to Egypt, where Potiphar, an Egyptian officer of Pharaoh and captain of the guard, bought him from the Ishmaelites (39:1). Overnight, a pampered favorite son had become a slave in a foreign land.

Yet Scripture tells us right away: the LORD was with Joseph, and everything he did prospered. His master saw it clearly. Potiphar grew to trust him, made him his personal attendant, and soon put him in charge of his entire household and everything he owned (39:2–4).

From the day Joseph took charge, the LORD blessed Potiphar's household because of Joseph, both indoors and out in the fields. Potiphar handed everything over to him and did not worry about a single thing except the food he ate. Then Scripture slips in one telling detail: Joseph was well-built and handsome (39:5–6).

That detail soon brought trouble. His master's wife set her eyes on Joseph and spoke bluntly: sleep with me (39:7).

Joseph refused. My master has entrusted everything he owns to me, he told her; no one in this house is greater than I am, and he has held nothing back except you, because you are his wife. "So how could I do such a great evil and sin against God?" (39:8–9).

She did not give up. Day after day she pressured him, yet Joseph refused to listen—and made sure not even to be near her (39:10). Then one day Joseph came into the house to do his work when not a single servant was inside. She grabbed his cloak and demanded: sleep with me! Joseph left his cloak right in her hand and ran outside (39:11–12).

Clutching the cloak, she flipped the story into an accusation. She called the household servants in: look at this—he brought a Hebrew in here to insult us! He came in to sleep with me, I screamed at the top of my lungs, and he left his cloak and ran out. She kept the cloak beside her until her husband came home (39:13–16).

When Potiphar arrived, she repeated the tale, subtly pinning part of the blame on her husband: that Hebrew slave *you* brought into our house. Potiphar burned with anger and had Joseph thrown into prison, the place where the king's prisoners were kept (39:17–20). For the second time in his young life, a piece of Joseph's clothing was used to tell a lie about him.

Yet even inside the prison, the LORD stayed with Joseph. He showed him steadfast kindness and won him the trust of the prison warden. The warden placed all the prisoners under Joseph's care and never had to check up on a thing he did, because the LORD was with Joseph and gave him success in whatever he touched (39:21–23).

## Key Takeaways

**God's presence does not guarantee an easy life.** The refrain running through the entire chapter is that the LORD was with Joseph (39:2; 39:21; 39:23). Yet Joseph is still sold as a slave, falsely accused, and thrown into prison. God being with us does not mean injustice will never hit us; it means He will never abandon His people in the middle of it.

**Faithfulness starts in ordinary tasks.** Whether serving as a slave in an official's home or locked away as a prisoner, Joseph never gave up or did sloppy work. He carried out his duties so faithfully that both his master and his jailer felt completely safe putting everything in his hands (39:4–6; 39:22–23).

**At bottom, every sin is a sin against God.** When turning down Potiphar's wife, Joseph mentions his master's trust first, yet his deepest reason is that he cannot sin against God (39:9). Knowing he lived every moment before God's eyes kept him standing when nobody else was in the room.

**Some temptations are not meant to be debated—they are meant to be fled.** Potiphar's wife pursued him day after day, so Joseph stayed out of her way; and when she seized his cloak in an empty house, he left the cloak behind and bolted out the door (39:10–12). Keeping a clean heart is worth far more than keeping a coat or saving face.

**Doing the right thing can cost you in the short run.** Joseph was not rewarded on the spot for his integrity; he was slandered and locked in a dungeon (39:19–20). This chapter warns us never to measure faithfulness by immediate results.

## Deeper Meaning

**Blessing for the nations comes alive inside a pagan home.** God had promised Abraham that every nation on earth would be blessed through his offspring (12:3). Now, for Joseph's sake, God pours blessing over Potiphar's entire estate—the home of an Egyptian outsider (39:5).

**Fleeing temptation with your feet.** Joseph leaving his cloak and running out the door (39:12) gives vivid flesh to the New Testament's advice: flee from sexual immorality (1 Corinthians 6:18) and flee youthful passions (2 Timothy 2:22).

**Suffering for doing good in the light of Christ.** Joseph is falsely accused by lying testimony and locked up with criminals despite being completely innocent (39:17–20). That picture of a righteous man suffering unjustly without bitter complaint points forward to Jesus standing before an unjust court, and to Peter's encouragement for believers who suffer for doing what is right (1 Peter 2:19–23).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why didn't Potiphar execute Joseph?** A foreign slave accused of assaulting the wife of Pharaoh's captain of the guard would normally have been put to death on the spot. That Potiphar merely placed Joseph in prison—specifically the royal prison under his own supervision—has led many readers to suspect he knew both his wife's character and Joseph's integrity well, yet still felt forced to punish Joseph to save face (39:19–20).

**What does the phrase *except the food he ate* in 39:6 mean?** There are two common explanations. Taken literally, Egyptians kept strict religious taboos that prevented them from letting Hebrews touch their food (43:32). Taken figuratively, Potiphar turned over so much responsibility that he had nothing left to think about except sitting down for dinner—and some readers also hear a subtle euphemism hinting at his wife.

**Clothing as a recurring prop in Joseph's life.** In chapter 37, his robe of many colors was used to convince Jacob that Joseph was dead (37:31–33). In chapter 39, his cloak is used to convince Potiphar that Joseph was guilty (39:16–18). Twice losing his garment drags him down to the bottom, before he is finally dressed in Pharaoh's fine linen robes in chapter 41 (41:42).

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, the fourth-century church father of Constantinople, hails Joseph's victory over Potiphar's wife as greater than any soldier's triumph on the battlefield. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, focuses closely on Joseph's words in 39:9. The fear of God, he notes, is the only wall strong enough to hold when temptation strikes in secret: if Joseph had only feared being caught by his master, he could easily have fallen in an empty house. *(Commentaries on the First Book of Moses called Genesis, chapter 39)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the argument that fearing God protects Joseph in secret is very characteristic of Calvin on chapter 39; exact wording unverified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor of the early eighteenth century, sums up the scene in a crisp line: better to lose a good coat than a good conscience. He adds that human anger could throw Joseph into a dungeon, yet it could never lock out God's favor toward him. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Genesis 39)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the epigram about preferring to lose one's coat rather than a clear conscience is a well-known line of Matthew Henry's; exact wording unverified -->

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish biblical scholar, compares this chapter with a famous ancient Egyptian story called *The Tale of Two Brothers*, which shares a strikingly similar plot: an older brother's wife tries to seduce the faithful younger brother, is rejected, and falsely accuses him. Set beside that ancient tale, Genesis 39 stands apart because Joseph treats moral purity not merely as social decency, but as loyalty to the one true God. *(Genesis, JPS Torah Commentary)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: comparing Genesis 39 with the ancient Egyptian Tale of Two Brothers is a widely known analysis included in Sarna's JPS Genesis commentary -->
