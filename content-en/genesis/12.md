# Genesis 12 — The Call of Abram

## Story

At the close of the previous chapter, Terah's family had settled in Haran, and Sarai had no children. Now the story narrows to a single man. God speaks to Abram: "Leave your country, your kindred, and your father's household, and go to the land I will show you" (12:1). He does not tell Abram where the land is. He simply says: go, and I will show you.

With the call comes a cascade of promises. God will make Abram into a great nation, bless him, make his name great, and make him a blessing. Whoever blesses him will be blessed, and whoever curses him will be cursed. And then comes the largest promise of all: "all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (12:2–3). A childless old man is promised a great nation—and blessing for the entire world through him.

Abram went, "as the LORD had directed him." He was seventy-five years old. Taking Sarai, his nephew Lot, all their possessions, and the people of their household, he set out for the land of Canaan (12:4–5).

Reaching Shechem in the heart of the land, he stopped by the Oak of Moreh. Scripture slips in a telling detail: at that time, the Canaanites were in the land. The promised land was not empty. God appeared to him and said, "I will give this land to your offspring." Abram built an altar there (12:6–7). Moving on toward Bethel, he pitched his tent, built another altar, and called on the name of the Lord. From there he kept traveling by stages, gradually working his way south (12:8–9).

Then a severe famine struck the land. Abram left the promised land and went down to Egypt to survive (12:10). As they neared the border, fear crept in. Sarai was a beautiful woman, and once the Egyptians saw her, they would kill her husband to take her. So he asked his wife to say she was his sister, so he would be treated well for her sake and his life would be spared (12:11–13).

Everything unfolded just as he feared—only worse. Pharaoh's officials saw Sarai, praised her beauty to the king, and she was taken into the royal palace. On her account, Pharaoh lavished gifts on Abram: sheep, cattle, donkeys, camels, menservants, and maidservants (12:14–16). The man God had promised to bless was now growing rich by handing over his own wife.

Then God stepped in. He struck Pharaoh and his household with severe plagues because of Sarai. Pharaoh summoned Abram and confronted him: "What have you done to me? Why didn't you tell me she was your wife?" The king gave Sarai back and ordered his men to escort Abram, his wife, and all their possessions out of the country (12:17–20). A foreign king ends up speaking the upright words, while God's chosen man has nothing left to say.

## Key Takeaways

**Faith begins by stepping out.** God did not hand Abram a map. He gave him only a promise. Abram had to leave behind everything familiar: his country, his extended family, his father's house. Faith here is not understanding everything before you move; it is trusting the One who calls you, and going.

**Blessed to be a blessing.** God did not merely say, "I will bless you," but also, "you will be a blessing." The blessing given to Abram was never meant to stop with him. It was meant to flow through him to the entire world.

**Worshiping in a land that is not yet yours.** Everywhere Abram stopped, he built an altar. He did not own a single square foot of ground, and the Canaanites still lived there. Each altar stood like a small flag planted in the soil, declaring that this land belonged to God's promise.

**People of faith still stumble into fear.** Not long after obeying God and leaving everything behind, Abram lied to save his skin. Scripture does not cover it up. The people God calls are not flawless; they are people God refuses to abandon.

**God guards His promise even when people jeopardize it.** Abram put Sarai—the future mother of the promised line—in grave danger. God Himself had to step in and rescue her. The promise held firm not because Abram managed it well, but because God is faithful.

## Deeper Meaning

**God's answer to Babel.** At Babel, humanity said, let us "make a name for ourselves," and ended up scattered (11:4). Here God says, "I will make your name great," and through one man, all those scattered families of the earth will be blessed (12:2–3). From chapter 3 through chapter 9, the word "curse" echoed again and again (3:14; 4:11; 9:25). Now the word "blessing" begins to take its place.

**The gospel announced in advance to Abraham.** The apostle Paul read 12:3 and wrote that Scripture preached the gospel in advance to Abraham: "All nations will be blessed through you" (Galatians 3:8). According to Paul, that blessing reaches the nations through Abraham's offspring, who is Christ (Galatians 3:16). Matthew's Gospel opens by introducing Jesus as the son of Abraham (Matthew 1:1).

**Going without knowing where.** The letter to the Hebrews recalls this very moment: by faith Abraham obeyed and went, "without knowing where he was going" (Hebrews 11:8). He stands as a model of faith because he anchored his future in God's word rather than in what his eyes could see.

**A miniature exodus.** Compare Abram's detour with Israel's story centuries later. Famine drives them down to Egypt. Pharaoh keeps them there. God strikes Pharaoh with plagues. Then they leave Egypt carrying great wealth (Exodus 11:1; 12:35–36). The ancestor's journey foreshadows the journey of his descendants.

**History repeating itself.** Abraham will pull this exact same deception again with the king of Gerar (20:2), and later his son Isaac will also pass his wife off as his sister (26:7). Genesis shows that parents pass down to their children not only their faith, but their fears and bad habits too.

## Notes & Nuances

**Abram's half-truth.** In chapter 20, Abraham explains that Sarai really was his half-sister, the daughter of his father though not his mother (20:12). Some argue he did not technically lie. Yet telling half the truth to make someone believe a falsehood is still deception. Pharaoh rebuked him for it, and Scripture offers no defense for Abram.

**Sarai is the victim here.** The narrative never lets us hear a single word from Sarai. Her husband uses her as a shield, and she is swept into a foreign king's palace. Reading this episode, we should not simply admire Abram's shrewdness or blame Pharaoh's appetite. Sarai paid the highest price, and God took her side.

**Why was Pharaoh punished?** Pharaoh had no idea Sarai was married, so many readers find it unfair that plagues fell on his house. There are two main explanations. One is that the plagues served as a warning to expose the truth and prompt Sarai's release, not as an act of destruction. The other is that a ruler casually taking attractive women into his palace was already committing a wrong, whether he knew she was married or not. Scripture does not explain further.

**Was Abram wrong to go down to Egypt?** The text never says God told him to go, nor that God forbade it. Some commentators treat leaving the promised land as the first misstep. Others see looking for food during a famine as plain common sense, locating the sin strictly in the lie. The narrative leaves the question open.

**The promise of land does not ignore its current residents.** The remark that "the Canaanites were in the land" shows the writer knew well that the land was already inhabited (12:6). Later, God will tell Abram that his descendants must wait generations before receiving it, because the iniquity of the Amorites "is not yet complete" (15:16).

## What Commentators Say

**Gerhard von Rad**, the German Old Testament scholar, viewed 12:1–3 as the hinge of the entire book of Genesis. Across chapters 1 to 11, sin spreads and judgment falls, leaving an unanswered question: what will God ultimately do with the nations? The call of Abram is the answer. God chooses one man, with the goal of blessing every family on earth. *(Genesis, Old Testament Library, chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading 12:1–3 as God's answer to the primeval history is von Rad's famous thesis, widely cited -->

**Gordon Wenham**, the British Old Testament scholar, counted the word "bless" or "blessing" appearing five times in 12:1–3. He argued that this forms an intentional counterweight to the repeated curses running through the earlier chapters. *(Genesis 1–15, Word Biblical Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall the observation of five blessings balancing five curses; uncertain whether Wenham originated it or echoed another scholar -->

**Augustine**, bishop in North Africa in the 4th and 5th centuries, defended Abram. In his view, Abram did not lie, since Sarai truly was his sister; he merely concealed part of the truth. He trusted God to protect his wife while doing what he could on his own part so as not to put God to the test. *(The City of God, Book XVI, chapter 19)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the argument that Abram concealed truth rather than lied appears in Book XVI, chapter 19, and is widely cited -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, refused to excuse Abram that way. He held that Abram sinned by trading away his wife's honor to save his own skin. As Calvin saw it, God allowed even a man of deep faith like Abram to stumble so we might see that the saints' faith can falter, and that they are kept safe only by God's grace. *(Commentary on Genesis, chapter 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin does not fully excuse Abram here; uncertain of his exact wording and severity of criticism -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, the American Old Testament scholar, focused on 11:30 standing right before the call: Sarai was barren. To him, barrenness embodies a human race with no future left. God's call to Abram speaks straight into that hopelessness and creates a future out of nothing. *(Genesis, Interpretation)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Brueggemann treats 11:30's barrenness as the crucial backdrop to the call; uncertain of his exact phrasing -->

**Nahum Sarna**, the American Jewish scholar, examined the wording of 12:3. The phrase "will be blessed through you" can also be rendered "will bless themselves by you"—meaning people will wish one another, "May you be as blessed as Abram." Read that way, Abram becomes the living standard of what blessing looks like. *(Genesis, JPS Torah Commentary)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Sarna and the JPS commentary discuss both translations of 12:3; uncertain which rendering he favors -->
