# Genesis 31 — Jacob Flees from Laban

## Story

Laban's sons began to mutter that Jacob had taken everything that belonged to their father and built his wealth at their father's expense. Jacob looked at his uncle's face and saw that Laban's attitude toward him had changed. Just then, the LORD spoke to him: go back to the land of your fathers, and I will be with you (31:1–3).

Jacob sent word for Rachel and Leah to come out to the field, where no one could overhear, and told his two wives everything. Your father has changed my wages ten times, he said, yet God has not allowed him to harm me. He told them of a dream in which an angel showed him the spotted flocks and said, "I have seen all that Laban has done to you." It was the God of Bethel Himself who was telling him to return home (31:4–13).

For the first time, the two sisters spoke with one voice. What share or inheritance is left for us in our father's house? they asked. He sold us and used up everything paid for us. The wealth God has taken from our father belongs to us and to our children. Do whatever God has told you (31:14–16).

Jacob put his wives and children on camels, drove all his livestock ahead of him, and slipped away while Laban was out shearing his sheep. He did not say a word to his uncle. Meanwhile, without anyone knowing, Rachel stole her father's household idols (31:17–21).

It was three days before Laban heard the news. He gathered his relatives and pursued Jacob for seven days, overtaking him in the hill country of Gilead. Yet the night before, God came to Laban in a dream and warned him: be careful not to say anything to Jacob, either good or bad (31:22–25).

Laban confronted his nephew: why did you sneak off as though carrying my daughters away like captives of war? Why didn't you let me kiss my daughters and grandchildren goodbye and send you off with joyful singing and music? I have the power to harm you, he said, but the God of your father warned me last night. Then he asked: why did you steal my gods? (31:26–30).

Jacob knew nothing of what Rachel had done. He swore that whoever was found with the idols would not live. Laban searched tent after tent. Rachel had hidden the idols in her camel's saddlebag and sat on top of them. She asked her father not to be angry that she could not stand up, explaining that she was having her period. Laban searched everywhere and found nothing (31:31–35).

Now it was Jacob's turn to erupt in anger. Twenty years of pent-up bitterness poured out. What crime have I committed that you hunt me down like this? For twenty years I tended your flocks; whenever wild beasts tore an animal apart, I bore the loss myself. Heat consumed me by day and frost by night, and sleep fled from my eyes. Fourteen years I worked for your two daughters, six years for your flocks, and you changed my wages ten times. If the God of Abraham, "the Fear of Isaac," had not been with me, you surely would have sent me away empty-handed (31:36–42).

Laban refused to admit fault, still insisting that the daughters were his, the children were his, and the flocks were his. Even so, he proposed a covenant. They piled up a mound of stones as a witness and called it Galeed, and also Mizpah, meaning "watchtower." Laban declared: may the LORD keep watch between you and me when we are absent from each other. Neither of us may cross past this mound to harm the other (31:43–52).

Both men swore an oath before God, offered a sacrifice, and shared a meal together. Early the next morning, Laban kissed his grandchildren and daughters, blessed them, and went back home (31:53–55).

## Key Takeaways

**God watches over those He has promised to keep.** At Bethel, God promised to stay with Jacob and bring him back. Through twenty years of exploitation, God was still watching. He saw how Laban treated Jacob, and He stepped in to warn Laban before Laban could strike.

**Injustice will eventually come into the open.** For twenty years Jacob labored hard while being cheated. His outburst is one of the fiercest cries against unfair treatment in all of Genesis. God does not turn a blind eye to workers who are exploited.

**Faith can still be tangled with old habits.** Jacob still leans on craftiness, slipping away in secret. Rachel steals the household idols, perhaps because she still clings to them. This family is on its way back to God, but it has not left all its old ways behind.

**Peace does not require friendship.** Laban and Jacob never truly reconcile as warm relatives. They simply agree not to harm one another and call on God to stand as witness. With some relationships, that boundary is as far as things should go—and it is still far better than war.

## Deeper Meaning

**The promise at Bethel is kept.** God had promised Jacob: I am with you, I will watch over you, "and I will bring you back to this land" (28:15). Chapter 31 is the moment that promise begins to come true. God calls Himself "the God of Bethel" so that Jacob will remember.

**The gods of Laban's house.** Later, Joshua reminds Israel that Abraham's ancestors beyond the Euphrates "worshiped other gods" (Joshua 24:2). Laban's household still held on to those idols. Before long, Jacob will command his entire household to get rid of the foreign gods among them (35:2).

**The Keeper who never sleeps.** Laban names the heap of stones Mizpah, "watchtower," and asks the LORD to keep watch. The psalmist describes God as the Protector of Israel who "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalms 121:4). For twenty years Jacob could barely sleep in peace, yet God stayed awake watching over him.

**Leaving judgment to God.** Jacob does not try to take revenge on Laban. He lays out the wrong done to him and leaves God as witness. The New Testament teaches the same posture: do not avenge yourselves, but leave room for God's judgment (Romans 12:19).

## Notes & Nuances

**What were the "household idols"?** The Hebrew word is *teraphim*, small figurines kept in a family home. Centuries later, even Michal, the wife of David, had one in her house (1 Samuel 19:13). Some scholars once argued that possessing a family's household idols carried legal rights to an inheritance, though many historians doubt that theory today. Scripture never explains why Rachel took them.

**The "Mizpah" saying is not a blessing of affection.** The line "May the LORD keep watch between you and me" is sometimes engraved on jewelry today as a tender wish for loved ones far apart. In context, however, it is spoken by two men who do not trust each other: let God police the border between us, because we cannot keep an eye on each other.

**Rachel's deception.** Rachel fools her father by claiming she is having her period. Scripture tells the scene with a touch of irony: Laban's "gods" end up sat upon by a woman. Still, the narrative never praises theft or lying. And Jacob's rash vow that the thief "shall not live" hangs uneasily over the reader when Rachel dies in childbirth in chapter 35.

**Are there two versions of how Jacob got the spotted flocks?** In chapter 30, Jacob uses peeled branches at the watering troughs. In chapter 31, he says God showed him the outcome in a dream. The two accounts do not have to contradict each other: Jacob tried his own scheme, yet in hindsight he came to recognize that God was the One behind the result.

## What Commentators Say

**Nahum Sarna**, an American Jewish scholar, discusses the household idols. He mentions ancient texts from the city of Nuzi that were once used to argue that whoever held the family gods held the right of inheritance, yet he notes that this explanation is uncertain. **John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, does not defend Rachel. In his view, stealing the idols proves she was still caught in superstition and had not broken free from her father's religion. He uses the episode to show that even inside the family chosen by God, traces of idolatry cling stubbornly. *(Commentary on Genesis, on chapter 31 verse 19)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin criticizing Rachel as superstitious for stealing the idols is a familiar reading; exact wording unverified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, reads Jacob's summary of his twenty years of labor as a portrait of a conscientious worker. **Robert Alter**, an American Jewish literary scholar, draws attention to the unusual title "the Fear of Isaac," which carries the stark sense of "the Dread of Isaac."
