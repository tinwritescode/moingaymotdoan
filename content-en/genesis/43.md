# Genesis 43 — Benjamin Goes Down to Egypt

## Story

The famine remains severe across the land. Once the family finishes the grain they brought back from Egypt, Jacob tells his sons to go down and buy a little more food (43:1–2).

Judah reminds his father of the hard fact everyone knows and no one wants to say aloud: the governor of the land solemnly warned them they would not see his face again unless their youngest brother was with them. If Jacob sends Benjamin, they will go; if not, going is pointless (43:3–5).

Jacob complains: why did they bring this misery on him by telling the man they had another brother? They answer that the man questioned them closely about their family—whether their father was still alive, whether they had another brother. How could they possibly have guessed he would order them to bring him down? (43:6–7).

Then Judah steps forward. Send the boy with me, he urges, so that all of us—you, we, and our children—may live and not die. I will guarantee his safety myself; you may hold me personally responsible. If I do not bring him back and set him before you, I will bear the guilt before you all my life. If we had not lingered so long, we could have gone and returned twice by now (43:8–10). The very brother who once proposed selling Joseph now pledges himself to protect Benjamin.

Jacob gives in. He tells them to pack a gift from the finest products of the land—balm, honey, spices, myrrh, pistachios, and almonds—and to carry double the silver so they can return what was placed back in their sacks; perhaps it was a mistake. Then he lets go with a quiet prayer: may God Almighty grant you mercy before the man, so that he releases Simeon and Benjamin. As for me, if I am bereaved, I am bereaved (43:11–14).

The brothers hurry down to Egypt and stand before Joseph. The moment Joseph sees Benjamin, he instructs his house steward to bring the men to his home, slaughter an animal, and prepare a meal, because they will dine with him at noon (43:15–17).

Once again, fear seizes the brothers. They assume they have been summoned to the governor's house because of the silver returned in their sacks last time—a pretext to overpower them, enslave them, and seize their donkeys. Standing at the doorway, they rush to explain everything to the steward: they have no idea who put the silver in their sacks, and they have brought every piece of it back (43:18–22).

The steward answers calmly: do not be afraid. Your God, the God of your father, has placed treasure in your sacks; I received your silver. He brings Simeon out to them, leads them into the house, gives them water to wash their feet, and feeds their donkeys. The brothers arrange their gift and wait for Joseph's arrival at noon (43:23–25).

Joseph comes home. The brothers present their gift and bow to the ground before him. He asks how they are, and then asks about the elderly father they had mentioned: is he well? Is he still alive? They answer that their father is well and still alive, and bow low once more (43:26–28). The old dream of the bowing sheaves is unfolding right before their eyes, though they have no clue.

Joseph looks up and sees Benjamin, his own mother's son. Is this your youngest brother, the one you told me about? Then he says, "May God be gracious to you, my son." Overwhelmed with longing for his brother, Joseph hurries out to find a place to weep. He goes into a private room and cries, then washes his face, comes back out, regains his composure, and orders the meal to be served (43:29–31).

They serve Joseph by himself, the brothers by themselves, and the Egyptians by themselves, because Egyptians refuse to eat with Hebrews. The brothers are seated in exact order from the firstborn down to the youngest, and they stare at one another in amazement. Portions are sent to them from Joseph's own table, and Benjamin's serving is five times larger than anyone else's. They feast and drink freely with him (43:32–34).

## Key Takeaways

**Clinging to the past out of fear can choke the present.** Jacob grips Benjamin so tightly out of fear of losing another son that he stalls even while the whole family faces starvation and Simeon sits in an Egyptian prison (43:6; 43:10). Only when the grain runs completely out does he finally open his hand and entrust the outcome to God Almighty (43:14).

**True maturity shows itself in taking responsibility.** Where Reuben offered an empty vow backed by his own sons' lives (42:37), Judah steps forward and offers himself as personal surety for Benjamin (43:8–9). The man who once suggested selling his brother is now ready to stand in the line of fire to protect his youngest brother and save his family.

**A guilty conscience sees a threat around every corner.** Invited to dine at the governor's house, the brothers feel no relief—only terror that they are about to be trapped and enslaved (43:18). They blurt out their defense to the steward at the door because the shadow of their old sin still haunts them.

**The deepest love often hides behind a stern exterior.** Joseph keeps up a strict front to test his brothers' hearts, yet the very sight of Benjamin moves him so deeply he has to slip into a private room to weep (43:30). Behind every test he sets is not a desire for revenge, but a deep ache for home and reconciliation.

**The test touches the exact old wound of jealousy.** Joseph seats his brothers in precise birth order—leaving them stunned—and then gives Benjamin five times as much food as anyone else (43:33–34). Quietly, he is watching to see whether they still resent a favored brother the way they once resented him.

## Deeper Meaning

**A brother who stands as guarantee.** Judah's pledge—I will guarantee his safety; if I do not bring him back, I will bear the guilt forever (43:9)—marks a turning point for the tribe that will one day produce King David and the Savior. The picture of a brother pledging his own life for another points forward to Jesus, who became the guarantee of a better covenant (Hebrews 7:22).

**A foreign steward speaking of Israel's God.** When the brothers tremble over the silver in their sacks, it is the steward in Joseph's Egyptian household who reassures them that the God of their father put treasure in their bags (43:23). Living in the heart of Egypt, Joseph's faith has quietly shaped the very people who serve under him.

**An old dream fulfilled in reconciliation.** The brothers bow to the ground before Joseph, fulfilling the dream of the sheaves (37:7; 43:26; 43:28). Yet unlike everything they once feared, Joseph does not use his authority to crush them; he spreads a feast to keep them alive—a faint mirror of Christ, who reigns to serve and redeem (Luke 22:27).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why would Egyptians not eat with Hebrews?** Verse 43:32 tells us that eating with Hebrews was detestable to the Egyptians. Ancient historians also note that Egyptians observed strict ritual purity rules and looked down on nomadic shepherds (46:34). That is why Joseph—serving in his official capacity as an Egyptian nobleman—sits at one table, his brothers at another, and the Egyptian guests at a third.

**Why is Benjamin's portion five times larger?** At ancient Near Eastern banquets, serving an oversized portion was a recognized way for a host to show special honor to a guest (1 Samuel 1:5). The number five also recurs throughout Egyptian passages in Genesis (41:34; 45:22). Joseph is both expressing genuine affection for his full brother and testing whether blatant favoritism will spark the old envy among the older brothers (43:34).

**What does "regaining his composure" in 43:31 reveal?** Before walking back out to order the meal, Joseph washes his face and forces his emotions under control so he does not reveal himself too soon. That restraint shows he is not rushing into an emotional reunion before knowing for certain that his brothers' hearts have truly changed.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, drew attention to Jacob's brief but weighty prayer in 43:14. After days of lament and fear, Jacob finally commits his sons to the mercy of God Almighty and yields to whatever God wills.
