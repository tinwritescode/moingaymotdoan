# Genesis 21 — Isaac Is Born, Hagar and Ishmael Depart

## Story

At last, what God had promised came true. Scripture says the LORD attended to Sarah "as He had said," and did for her "what He had promised." Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age, "at the very time God had promised." Abraham named the boy Isaac, just as God had instructed, and circumcised him when he was eight days old. That year Abraham turned a hundred (21:1–5).

Sarah, who had once laughed silently behind the tent entrance, now laughed out loud. She said, "God has made me laugh, and everyone who hears of this will laugh with me." She added, who would have ever told Abraham that Sarah would nurse children? Yet she had borne him a son in his old age (21:6–7).

Isaac grew and was weaned. On that day, Abraham held a great feast. During the feast, Sarah saw Ishmael, Hagar's son, "mocking" him. She told her husband: expel the slave woman and her son, for the slave woman's son will never share in the inheritance with my son Isaac. Abraham was deeply distressed, because Ishmael was his son too. But God told him not to grieve and to listen to Sarah, for his offspring would be reckoned through Isaac. God promised that He would also make a nation of the slave woman's son, because he too was Abraham's offspring (21:8–13).

Early the next morning, Abraham took bread and a skin of water, placed them on Hagar's shoulders, handed over the boy, and sent her away. Hagar wandered in the Wilderness of Beersheba. When the water in the skin ran out, she laid the boy under a small bush, then walked off and sat down about a bowshot away. She said, "I cannot bear to watch the boy die!" Then she lifted up her voice and wept (21:14–16).

"God heard the voice of the boy." The angel of God called to Hagar from heaven, telling her not to be afraid and to lift the boy up, for God would make him into a great nation. Then God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water. She filled the skin and gave the boy a drink. God was with the boy. He grew up in the Wilderness of Paran, became a skilled archer, and his mother got him a wife from Egypt (21:17–21).

Around that same time, King Abimelech and Phicol, the commander of his army, came to see Abraham. The king said, "God is with you in all that you do." He asked Abraham to swear that he would not deal falsely with him or his descendants, but would show the same kindness the king had shown him. Abraham swore. Yet he also brought up a well he had dug that the king's servants had seized. Abimelech said he knew nothing about it. Abraham brought sheep and cattle as a gift to the king, and the two made a covenant. Abraham set apart seven ewe lambs and asked the king to accept them as a witness that he himself had dug that well. So the place was named Beersheba, because the two of them swore an oath there (21:22–32).

Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba, and there he called on the name of the LORD, "the Eternal God." He lived for a long time in the land of the Philistines (21:33–34).

## Key Takeaways

**God keeps His word down to the letter.** In just the first two verses, Scripture repeats three times that everything happened just as God said: as He had said, what He had promised, at the very time He had promised. Twenty-five years of waiting—ever since Abram left Haran at seventy-five (12:4)—have finally found their answer.

**Laughter made new.** Abraham had laughed (17:17), and Sarah had laughed (18:12), both because the whole thing seemed impossible. Now God turns that disbelief into joyful laughter, and the little boy named "he laughs" is the living proof.

**Blessing can still bring heartache.** The happiest day in the family also becomes the start of a painful parting. The rushed choices of chapter 16, when Sarai gave Hagar to her husband, now leave everyone wounded: Sarah is jealous, Abraham is heartbroken, and Hagar and her son are sent away.

**God hears the voice of the one cast out.** Ishmael is not the child of promise, yet God still hears him crying. Ishmael's name means "God hears" (16:11). Here God lives up to the very name He gave the child.

**Living uprightly among outsiders.** A foreign king recognizes that God is with Abraham. Abraham keeps the peace with the king, yet he does not stay silent when his well is taken. He settles the dispute with plain speaking, generous gifts, and a public oath.

## Deeper Meaning

**Two mothers, two covenants.** Paul uses this story as an illustration. Hagar and Sarah stand for two covenants: one bearing children into slavery, the other bearing children who are free. Ishmael was born in the ordinary human way; Isaac was born through God's promise. Paul quotes Sarah's words: "Expel the slave woman and her son" (Galatians 4:30). His point is that believers live by promise and grace, not by trying to earn their place through keeping the law (Galatians 4:22–31).

**Children of the promise.** Paul also quotes verse 21:12 to show that not everyone physically descended from Abraham is truly his child. Only the children of the promise are counted as his offspring (Romans 9:7–8). Belonging to God does not come from bloodline, but from promise and faith.

**At the very time God appointed.** Isaac was born "at the very time God had promised" (21:2). Paul uses a similar phrase for the birth of Jesus: when the time had fully come, God sent His Son, born of a woman (Galatians 4:4). Both times, people waited a very long time, and God arrived neither too early nor too late.

**Mouths filled with laughter.** A later psalm describes the joy of God's people returning from exile: "our mouths were filled with laughter" (Psalms 126:2). Sarah's joy is the joy of someone watching God do what she never dared to dream.

**The Eternal God.** At the end of the chapter, Abraham calls God by a new name, "the Eternal God" (21:33). He had just waited twenty-five years, and he still had to live as a foreigner in someone else's land. He worshiped a God whom time cannot wear down. Later the prophet Isaiah likewise declares that God "will not grow tired or weary" (Isaiah 40:28).

## Notes & Nuances

**What was Ishmael doing when he was "mocking"?** The Hebrew word shares the same root as Isaac's name ("laughter"). Some read it as mocking Isaac, and Paul says the son born by the flesh "persecuted" the son born by the Spirit (Galatians 4:29). Others take it simply as playing, and it was the sight of him playing as an equal alongside Isaac that made Sarah worry about her son's inheritance. The text does not say explicitly.

**How old was Ishmael?** When Isaac was born, Ishmael was already fourteen (16:16; 21:5). Children back then were usually weaned around two or three years old, so by the time he was sent away, Ishmael was a teenager. Yet the story pictures him like a small child laid under a bush. Some point out that the Hebrew word for "boy" can refer to a young man, and even a teenager dying of thirst in the desert could collapse in exhaustion. Others think two older strands of the story were woven together here.

**Was sending Hagar and Ishmael away cruel?** The story does not hide the pain. Abraham is distressed, Hagar weeps, and the boy nearly dies of thirst. God tells Abraham to listen to Sarah, yet that does not mean God applauds the way Sarah treated them. God steps in personally to care for the very ones who were driven out. He does not abandon anyone in this story.

**"The land of the Philistines" in Abraham's time?** According to history, the Philistines settled along the coast of Canaan around 1200 BC, long after Abraham's era. Some explain that the writer used a place-name familiar to later readers, just as we might speak of "Britain in the days of Julius Caesar." Others suggest an earlier seafaring group of the same origin had already settled in the region.

**Ishmael in other traditions.** Arab peoples have long looked to Ishmael as their ancestor, and Islamic tradition connects Hagar and Ishmael with a well at Mecca. The Bible simply says Ishmael lived in the Wilderness of Paran and became the father of twelve tribes (25:12–16). When reading, it helps to distinguish what Scripture itself says from what later traditions added.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, a bishop in North Africa more than 1,600 years ago, follows Paul in reading the two mothers as two cities. Hagar and her son represent the earthly city, born according to nature. Sarah and Isaac represent the city of God, born by grace when human strength has run dry.
