# 2 Kings 5 — General Naaman, the Jordan River, and Gehazi's Greed

## Story

Naaman was the illustrious commander of the army of Aram—a mighty warrior held in highest esteem by his king because through him the LORD Himself had granted victory to Aram. Yet that glittering introduction ends with one painful crack: "but he had leprosy" (5:1). Serving Naaman's wife was a young Israelite girl captured during an Aramean raid. One day she spoke up simply to her mistress: if only my master would visit the prophet in Samaria, he would surely cure him of his leprosy (5:2–3).

A child's innocent remark sparked sudden hope. Naaman reported it to the king of Aram, who promptly sent a diplomatic letter to the king of Israel. Naaman set out with a lavish entourage carrying ten talents of silver, six thousand shekels of gold, and ten sets of fine clothing to pay for his cure (5:4–5). Yet when Israel's king read the letter asking him to heal Naaman's leprosy, he tore his robes in panic: "Am I God, able to kill and make alive?" He convinced himself Aram was merely manufacturing a pretext for war. Hearing the uproar, Elisha sent word telling the king to send Naaman straight to him so the foreign general might discover "that there is a prophet in Israel" (5:6–8).

Naaman rolled up in grand style with horses and chariots right to Elisha's doorstep. Yet the prophet did not even bother stepping outside; he simply sent a messenger out with brief instructions: "Go, wash yourself seven times in the Jordan, and your flesh will be restored and you will be clean" (5:9–10). Naaman exploded in rage and wheeled away. He had fully expected the prophet to come out personally, stand solemnly calling on his God's name, and wave his hand dramatically over the spot! And as for bathing in muddy rivers—weren't the Abana and Pharpar of Damascus far cleaner than all the waters of Israel combined? Mercifully, his attendants stepped up with quiet wisdom: if the prophet had asked you to do something heroic and hard, wouldn't you have done it? How much more when he merely says, "Wash and be clean!" (5:11–13).

Swallowing his pride, Naaman went down and dipped himself in the Jordan seven times just as told. Instantly his skin was completely healed, smooth and healthy "like that of a young boy" (5:14). Returning with his entire retinue to stand before Elisha, he confessed warmly: "Now I know that there is no God in all the earth except in Israel!" He urged Elisha to accept a rich gift, yet the prophet swore flatly he would take nothing at all (5:15–16). Accepting that refusal, Naaman asked instead for two mule-loads of Israelite soil to haul home so he could build an altar and offer sacrifices from now on solely to the LORD. He also voiced one honest scruple, asking God's pardon: by official duty, whenever the king of Aram entered the temple of Rimmon to bow, he leaned on Naaman's arm, forcing Naaman to bow beside him. Refusing to argue, Elisha simply sent him off warmly: "Go in peace" (5:17–19).

Naaman had traveled only a short distance when Gehazi, Elisha's trusted servant, felt greed flaring inside him. Grumbling that his master had let this wealthy Aramean walk away without paying a cent, he sprinted after the chariots. Spotting Gehazi running up, Naaman promptly leaped down from his chariot to greet him courteously. Gehazi spun a smooth lie about two needy prophetic students just arriving from the hill country of Ephraim, claiming his master wanted one talent of silver and two sets of clothes for them. Delighted to help, Naaman pressed double on him—two talents of silver—and even sent two servants to carry the load back near the hill where Elisha lived. Gehazi stashed the loot safely inside the house and stepped back in before his master as if nothing had happened (5:20–24).

Elisha asked straight out where he had been. "Your servant did not go anywhere," Gehazi lied. Heartsick, the prophet stripped every disguise away: did my spirit not go with you when the man turned back from his chariot to meet you? At a moment when Israel needed to witness God's free grace, was this a time to grab silver, fine robes, olive groves, vineyards, and servants? Then came the stark verdict: Naaman's leprosy would cling to Gehazi and his descendants forever. Stepping out from Elisha's presence, Gehazi walked away "leprous—as white as snow" (5:25–27).

## Key Takeaways

**God's grace is a free gift no amount of wealth or status can buy.** Naaman hauled an immense fortune in gold, silver, and garments, expecting to purchase healing like a royal trade deal. Elisha deliberately stayed indoors and declined every coin (5:10; 5:16) so the pagan general would grasp one bedrock truth: God's mercy is never for sale; it is received solely through humble obedience.

**Pride is the thickest wall blocking human beings from healing.** Naaman nearly rode home to Damascus still covered in leprosy solely because God's cure looked too plain and bruised a great commander's ego (5:11–12). Human nature prefers grand heroics that showcase our worth over one simple act requiring us to lay pride down.

**Small, humble voices open the door for God's power.** Inside a story packed with chariots and diplomatic letters between kings, the one person who truly knew God's power was an unnamed captive servant girl (5:2–3), while the ones who rescued Naaman from his blind temper were ordinary servants walking beside him (5:13). Meanwhile, Israel's own king tore his robes in panic, forgetting he even had a prophet of God in the land (5:7).

**Living close to God's work never automatically purifies the heart.** The reversal between Naaman and Gehazi sends a chill down the spine. Naaman, an enemy outsider, entered the story proud and leprous yet rode home clean and worshiping God. Gehazi lived daily beside Elisha, witnessed countless miracles, yet let greed turn God's free grace into a cash scheme—walking away wearing the outsider's leprosy on his own skin (5:26–27).

## Deeper Meaning

**Jesus cites Naaman right inside His opening sermon at Nazareth.** Rejected by His own hometown, Jesus pointed straight back to this story: there were many lepers in Israel during Elisha's lifetime, yet not one of them was cleansed—only Naaman the Aramean (Luke 4:27). Jesus held Naaman up to declare that God's compassion has never been fenced inside one nationality, flowing freely even to enemies whenever someone humbles themselves in faith.

**Dipping seven times and biblical cleansing.** Under the Law of Moses, people with severe skin disease were pronounced unclean and lived outside the community (Leviticus 13:45–46), while the ritual declaring them cleansed included sevenfold sprinkling (Leviticus 14:7). In the New Testament, Jesus likewise told a man born blind to go wash in the Pool of Siloam to receive sight (John 9:6–7)—a humble step of obedience opening the door to miracle.

**Refusing payment to keep God's name pure.** Elisha's firm refusal to touch a single coin (5:16) walks in the footsteps of Abram declining plunder from the king of Sodom so nobody could claim mortals made him rich (Genesis 14:22–23), and points ahead to Jesus's charge to His disciples: "Freely you have received; freely give" (Matthew 10:8). Conversely, Gehazi's deceit and greed foreshadow Ananias and Sapphira lying to the Holy Spirit (Acts 5:1–4) and Paul's warning that the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil (1 Timothy 6:9–10).

## Notes & Nuances

**"Leprosy" in Scripture covers a wider range of conditions than modern Hansen's disease.** The Hebrew term *tsara'at* encompassed various chronic, disfiguring, scaly skin conditions—not exclusively modern leprosy. That explains why Naaman in Aram could still command troops and stand close beside his king, unlike the strict quarantine rules required under Moses's law inside Israel (Leviticus 13:45–46).

**Why did Naaman ask to haul two mule-loads of Israelite dirt back to Aram (5:17)?** Ancient Near Eastern people commonly associated deities with their native soil. Though Naaman had just confessed "there is no God in all the earth except in Israel" (5:15), his thinking still carried older habits: he wanted to erect an altar of genuine Israelite soil back home in Aram so he could worship the LORD faithfully. Admirably, Elisha did not rush to lecture or mock his young, growing faith.

**What does "Go in peace" mean regarding the temple of Rimmon (5:18–19)?** Naaman felt genuinely troubled that his official duty required supporting his aging king inside Rimmon's shrine, proactively asking God's pardon. Elisha neither waved it off as fine nor demanded his immediate resignation, answering simply, "Go in peace." Commentators debate this richly: some see wise pastoral patience giving a brand-new believer living in pagan lands room to let conscience mature under God's guidance, while others stress that this unique dilemma should never be twisted into permission for idolatrous compromise.

**Gehazi's stark punishment.** Hearing leprosy decreed upon him "and your descendants forever" (5:27) strikes many readers as harsh. Yet Gehazi's sin went far deeper than petty theft: he swore falsely in the LORD's name (5:20), sabotaged the priceless lesson of free grace Naaman had just discovered, and lied shamelessly to the prophet's face (5:25). Greed led him to trade away integrity and inherit the exact disease God had just lifted off a Gentile stranger.

## What Commentators Say

**Ambrose**, the fourth-century bishop of Milan, sees Naaman washing seven times in the Jordan as a luminous Old Testament picture of Christian baptism. Measured purely as natural water, Damascus's rivers were every bit as clear as the Jordan, Ambrose notes; what cleansed Naaman's flesh was never river water itself, but God's grace operating through obedient faith. *(On the Sacraments, Books 1 and 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Ambrose famously uses Naaman's washing in the Jordan as a primary type of baptism in On the Sacraments and On the Mysteries, very widely cited -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the ancient rabbinic commentary, reflects on both contrasting figures in this chapter. In tractates *Gittin* and *Sanhedrin*, the sages rank Naaman as a God-fearing Gentile resident who renounced idols even without full conversion to Israel; meanwhile in *Sotah* and *Sanhedrin*, they list Gehazi among four commoners forfeiting their share in the world to come through greed and deceit, yet remarkably also fault Elisha for pushing his disciple away with both hands instead of letting "the left hand push away while the right hand draws near" to leave room for repentance. *(Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 57b; Sotah 47a; Sanhedrin 107b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: classifying Naaman as a ger toshav and the famous rabbinic critique of Elisha pushing Gehazi away rather than pushing with the left hand and drawing near with the right are celebrated Talmudic passages -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Genevan Reformer, repeatedly addressed Naaman's petition in 5:18 when debating believers of his own day who tried to conceal their faith by attending ceremonies their consciences condemned. Calvin opposed using Naaman as an excuse for double-minded living: Naaman openly professed sole devotion to the LORD before everyone and worried strictly over physically assisting an elderly king, while Elisha's "Go in peace" was a courteous farewell rather than a license for idolatry. *(Anti-Nicodemite treatises — exact treatise and location to be verified)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Calvin argued this exact interpretation of 2 Kings 5:18 in his anti-Nicodemite writings, though exact treatise title and location were not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, warmly praises the captive Israelite girl in 5:2–3: stripped of her freedom and forced to wait on her people's enemies, she harbored no bitterness, longing instead for her master to be healed and come to know Israel's God.
