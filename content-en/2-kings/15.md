# 2 Kings 15 — The Shadow of Assyria and Relentless Coups in the North

## Story

Picking up from the close of chapter 14, in the twenty-seventh year of northern King Jeroboam II, Azariah—whom later verses inside this very chapter also call Uzziah—took the throne in Jerusalem. Just sixteen years old at accession, he reigned fifty-two years, one of the longest reigns in Judah's history. Scripture commends him for doing what was right in the sight of the LORD just as his father Amaziah had, yet notes the familiar flaw: the local high places remained untouched, with people still sacrificing and burning incense there. Eventually the LORD afflicted the king with leprosy until the day he died. Forced to live in a separate house, Azariah handed palace administration and governance of the people over to his son Jotham. When Azariah died, he was buried with his ancestors in the City of David, and Jotham officially succeeded him (15:1–7).

While southern Judah enjoyed over half a century of relative stability under Azariah and Jotham, northern Israel plunged into a dizzying spiral of bloodshed. In Azariah's thirty-eighth year, Zechariah son of Jeroboam II ascended Samaria's throne. Ruling barely six months while walking in the sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat, he was struck down in public view by a conspirator named Shallum son of Jabesh, who seized the crown. His assassination fulfilled word for word the LORD's ancient promise to Jehu: "Your descendants will sit on the throne of Israel to the fourth generation." Zechariah stood as that fourth generation, bringing Jehu's dynasty to its end (15:8–12).

A throne stolen in blood did not last long. In Uzziah's thirty-ninth year, Shallum had sat in Samaria barely one full month when Menahem son of Gadi marched up from Tirzah, assassinated him, and claimed the throne (15:13–15). To terrorize holdouts who refused to recognize his coup, Menahem marched from Tirzah to sack the town of Tiphsah and its surrounding territory simply because it would not open its gates—ruthlessly "ripping open all its pregnant women" (15:16).

Menahem reigned ten years in Samaria, clinging stubbornly all his life to Jeroboam's golden-calf sins. Right inside his reign, a terrifying new shadow from afar fell across Israel: Pul, king of Assyria, invaded the land. Instead of seeking God or rallying his people, Menahem scrambled to pay the Assyrian emperor a thousand talents of silver to buy backing that would "strengthen his own hold on the kingdom." To raise that staggering sum, Menahem levied a flat tax of fifty shekels of silver on every wealthy landowner across Israel. Pocketing the tribute, Assyria's king withdrew home. Menahem died quietly in bed—the lone northern monarch in this chapter not murdered—leaving the throne to his son Pekahiah (15:17–22).

Even that fragile lull shattered almost immediately. In Azariah's fiftieth year, Pekahiah took the crown and lasted just two years while repeating his predecessors' sins. His own military officer, Pekah son of Remaliah, conspired with fifty men from Gilead, stormed right into "the citadel of the royal palace" in Samaria, murdered Pekahiah alongside Argob and Arieh, and seized the throne (15:23–26).

In Azariah's fifty-second year, Pekah began a reign recorded as twenty years, continuing to do evil in the LORD's eyes. During Pekah's reign, Assyria came roaring back with a far heavier fist: King Tiglath-pileser swept through and captured major cities and territories across northern Israel and Transjordan—Ijon, Abel-beth-maacah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, all Gilead, Galilee, and the entire tribal land of Naphtali—deporting their populations into exile in Assyria. The northern kingdom was now carved down to a fraction of its former size. Amid national ruin, Hoshea son of Elah conspired against Pekah, assassinated him, and took the crown in the twentieth year of Jotham (15:27–31).

At the chapter's close, the camera returns south. In Pekah's second year, Jotham son of Uzziah took Judah's throne at twenty-five and ruled sixteen years in Jerusalem. Walking uprightly before the LORD like his father, he rebuilt the Upper Gate of the temple—yet once again left the rural high places intact for sacrifice. Even under a largely faithful king, war clouds began rolling south: the LORD Himself began sending King Rezin of Aram and King Pekah of Israel together against Judah. Jotham died, was buried in the City of David, and left both crown and approaching storm to his son Ahaz (15:32–38).

## Key Takeaways

**Abandoning God flings the door wide open to chaos.** Inside one brief chapter, northern Israel churns through five kings—four of them assassinated right inside palace corridors (15:10; 15:14; 15:25; 15:30). While Judah preserves David's unbroken line across more than sixty years under two monarchs, Israel rots apart from within before Assyria ever arrives. Once a nation cuts loose from God's righteousness, power turns into raw prey for whoever strikes fastest and hardest.

**Buying security through tribute and compromise never preserves real freedom.** Menahem squeezed Israel's landowners dry to buy Assyrian backing for his shaky chair (15:19–20). On the surface, Assyria left and Menahem kept the crown a few years longer. Yet that payoff clamped the vassal collar straight around Israel's neck, paving the road for Tiglath-pileser's return years later to chop up the country and haul citizens away (15:29). Leaning on worldly powers instead of turning back to God always charges far steeper interest than anyone expects.

**God's spoken word never drops unfulfilled.** For nearly a century, Jehu's house held Samaria's throne securely, even hitting booming heights under Jeroboam II. Yet at the exact fourth generation—Zechariah—just as the LORD had pledged to Jehu, the line halted on cue (15:12). Long decades may lull human memories to sleep, yet God never forgets a syllable He has spoken.

**Even a largely faithful life requires daily vigilance right to the finish line.** Both Azariah and Jotham receive commendation for upright rule and steady stability in Judah (15:3; 15:34). Yet both tolerated local high places, and Azariah spent his closing years isolated behind leprous walls under divine judgment (15:4–5; 15:35). A bright start never guarantees a clean finish; faithfulness must be guarded every single day.

## Deeper Meaning

**Uzziah's leprosy connects Chronicles's explanation straight to Isaiah's throne vision.** Second Kings states crisply that the LORD struck Azariah with leprosy (15:5), leaving readers pausing to ask why. Second Chronicles pulls back the veil: grown proud after decades of success, Uzziah barged into the temple to burn incense himself—a sacred duty reserved exclusively for Aaron's priestly line—and raged when priests blocked him, causing leprosy to erupt instantly across his forehead (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). Under Mosaic law, lepers had to dwell quarantined apart from the community (Leviticus 13:46), cutting the mighty king off from the temple for the rest of his life. In the very year that impressive yet flawed monarch died, Isaiah stepped into the temple and beheld the true King who never grows proud, sick, or mortal: "In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord seated on a throne, high and exalted" (Isaiah 6:1).

**Samaria's bloody carousel sets the live backdrop of Hosea.** Reading four back-to-back assassinations in chapter 15 (15:10–30) brings to life the brokenhearted cries of Hosea, who prophesied right inside that storm. Earlier God announced through Hosea He would punish Jehu's house for Jezreel's blood and end Israel's kingdom (Hosea 1:4)—fulfilled the instant Zechariah fell (15:10–12). Watching kings crown and butcher one another, Hosea voiced God's lament: "They set up kings, but not by Me," and "All their kings have fallen, and none of them calls upon Me" (Hosea 8:4; 7:7). When Menahem sent silver begging Assyria for help (15:19), Hosea exposed the folly: Ephraim saw its sickness and ran to Assyria, yet Assyria cannot heal your wound (Hosea 5:13).

**The atrocity at Tiphsah reveals Israel morphing into its cruelest enemies.** Earlier, gazing at Aram's Hazael, Elisha broke down weeping because Hazael would burn towns, slay youths, and rip open Israelite pregnant women (8:12). Amos likewise thundered divine wrath against Ammon for ripping open pregnant women in Gilead to enlarge their border (Amos 1:13). Chillingly, by chapter 15, the perpetrator of that exact monstrosity is no longer Aramean or Ammonite invaders, but Menahem—an Israelite king savaging his own countrymen (15:16). When God's people cast God aside, they sink straight down to the very barbarity that once horrified them.

**The very first region plunged into exilic darkness becomes the first to see the light of Jesus.** Verse 15:29 records Israel's inaugural Assyrian deportation, hitting Galilee and Naphtali's northern borderlands first. Right inside the gloom of that invasion, Isaiah gazed far down the centuries with an astonishing pledge: the humiliated lands of Zebulun, Naphtali, and Galilee of the nations will be honored, for "the people walking in darkness have seen a great light" (Isaiah 9:1–2). Over seven hundred years later, Matthew's Gospel quotes that exact prophecy as Jesus moves from Nazareth to Capernaum on the border of Zebulun and Naphtali to launch His gospel ministry (Matthew 4:13–16). The exact territory where exile struck first in 2 Kings 15 becomes the birthplace where salvation's dawn breaks in the New Testament.

**The Aramean-Israelite alliance signals a looming crisis for David's house.** Verse 15:37 closes Jotham's reign with a brief, ominous note: the LORD began sending King Rezin of Aram and King Pekah of Israel against Judah. That joint threat explodes directly against Jerusalem under Ahaz in chapter 16 and provides the exact historical stage for Isaiah's famous Immanuel sign (Isaiah 7:1). History never drifts aimlessly; even hostile coalitions form under the sovereign hand of God.

## Notes & Nuances

**Azariah and Uzziah are two names for the same Judean king.** Readers easily wonder why chapter 15 calls Judah's king Azariah in some verses (15:1; 15:6–8; 15:17; 15:23; 15:27) and Uzziah in others (15:13; 15:30; 15:32; 15:34). In Hebrew, both names sound closely alike and celebrate God: Azariah means "the LORD has helped," while Uzziah means "the LORD is my strength." Scholars generally regard one as his birth name and the other as his throne name—or parallel variants for the same monarch, with 2 Chronicles and Isaiah consistently favoring Uzziah.

**Facing Menahem's atrocity at Tiphsah honestly within the text.** Verse 15:16 reports Menahem ripping open pregnant women at Tiphsah with zero editorial commentary—one of the most jarring lines in Kings. Remember that Scripture reporting a crime never means God approves it. Two verses later, the author delivers an unambiguous indictment: Menahem "did evil in the sight of the LORD" (15:18). The deadpan brevity exposes the moral rot of a dying state where usurpers wield sheer terror to crush resistance among their own people.

**Pul and Tiglath-pileser were the very same Assyrian emperor.** Seeing "Pul king of Assyria" in 15:19 and "Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria" in 15:29—and even both listed beside each other in 1 Chronicles 5:26—easily suggests two different emperors. Ancient Babylonian and Assyrian inscriptions unearthed by archaeologists solved the puzzle cleanly: Tiglath-pileser III (reigned c. 745–727 BC) bore the personal or Babylonian throne name Pulu (Pul). That single conqueror thrust Assyria westward, first extracting silver tribute from Menahem and years later annexing northern Israel outright under Pekah.

**Why regnal numbers in chapter 15 tangle due to overlapping coregencies.** Adding up raw reign lengths across chapter 15 yields apparent snags: 15:33 assigns Jotham sixteen years, yet 15:30 cites "the twentieth year of Jotham"; likewise Pekah is credited with twenty years (15:27) inside a far narrower calendar window between Menahem's tribute and Pekah's murder. Biblical chronologists explain this convincingly through overlapping coregencies and rival claims. Down south, Jotham governed actively as regent for years during Uzziah's leprous quarantine (15:5), and his twentieth year counts on past Ahaz's coregent accession. Up north, many scholars deduce Pekah set up a rival power base in Transjordanian Gilead well before assassinating Pekahiah, counting all twenty years from that starting point. Meanwhile, 2 Chronicles 27 adds impressive military and building successes under Jotham (2 Chronicles 27:3–6) that 2 Kings trims away to keep eyes glued on the approaching Assyrian storm.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, weaves together details across Scripture in narrating Uzziah and the northern coups. Recounting Uzziah's leprosy, Josephus explains his unlawful burning of temple incense and ties the moment dramatically to the great earthquake of Uzziah's reign recalled by the prophets. **Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, pauses over the reigns of Uzziah and Jotham when setting earthly empires beside God's redemptive history. Right at this historical pivot—as distant Assyria rose and Israel hurtled toward ruin—God raised up the great writing prophets like Hosea, Amos, and Isaiah both to pronounce judgment and to sow messianic promises reaching out to all nations. *(The City of God, Book 18)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Augustine locating the rise of the writing prophets during Uzziah and Jotham in Book 18 of The City of God, though exact chapter numbers and phrasing were not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, holds chapter 15's two kingdoms side by side as a living lesson: in Judah, David's throne passes calmly from father to son under covenant care; in Israel, kings slash their way to the chair only to hold it for months or a few turbulent years. On Menahem taxing Israel's wealthy to buy Assyrian protection, Henry observes that leaders refusing to lean on God pay a steep ransom for counterfeit human safety—only to be swallowed by that very ally anyway. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 2 Kings 15)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Matthew Henry's contrast between Judah and Israel and his remarks on Menahem's tribute in 2 Kings 15, though not verified verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Gerhard von Rad**, the twentieth-century German Old Testament scholar, highlights the closing note on Zechariah's death in 15:12 as a key structural pillar showing how Kings reads history. Rather than viewing events as random political drift, the writer frames history as an unbroken chain of prophetic word and fulfillment: God's promise to Jehu concludes on the dot at generation four, demonstrating that God's spoken word—not palace muscle—steers dynastic destiny. *(Old Testament Theology, Volume 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: von Rad's celebrated analysis of the prophecy-and-fulfillment structure in Kings citing 10:30 and 15:12 in Old Testament Theology Vol. 1; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 15 as a rapid-fire indictment of northern political self-destruction. Flashing past five kings in twenty-four verses—coup stacked on bloody coup—creates the breathless sensation of a regime devouring itself alive. Crucially, Brueggemann observes, Assyria does not topple a healthy kingdom; imperial darkness simply floods into the moral and political vacuum carved out by Israel's own unfaithfulness and internal bloodshed. *(1 & 2 Kings, 2000)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Brueggemann's reading of the rapid coup cadence and imperial expansion filling Israel's inner vacuum in chapter 15 of 1 & 2 Kings; exact page numbers not verified -->
