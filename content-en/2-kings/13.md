# 2 Kings 13 — Arrows of Victory and the Bones of Elisha

## Story

At the end of chapter 12, King Hazael of Aram had marched far south, forcing Judah's King Joash to empty temple and palace treasuries to buy peace (12:17–18). Chapter 13 now turns back north to Israel, where the storm named Hazael struck even harder. In the twenty-third year of Judah's King Joash, Jehoahaz son of Jehu took Samaria's throne and ruled seventeen years. Like his father, he did evil in the sight of the LORD and refused to abandon the two golden calves Jeroboam son of Nebat had made at the start (13:1–2).

The LORD's anger burned against Israel, and throughout those years He handed them over repeatedly to King Hazael of Aram and his son Ben-hadad. Crushed to the breaking point, Jehoahaz sought the LORD's favor. Astonishingly—even though the king had not yet torn down his idols—the LORD heard him because He saw how bitterly Aram was oppressing Israel. God gave Israel "a deliverer" so they escaped Aram's grip and returned to living safely in their homes as before (13:3–5).

Yet human hearts stayed stubborn. Freshly rescued, Israel still clung to the sins of Jeroboam's house—even leaving the Asherah pole standing openly right inside Samaria. So devastating had Aram's attrition been that Jehoahaz's entire army had shrunk to just fifty horsemen, ten chariots, and ten thousand foot soldiers, because Aram's king had destroyed them and "made them like the dust at threshing." Jehoahaz died, was buried in Samaria, and left the crown to his son Jehoash (13:6–9).

In the thirty-seventh year of Judah's King Joash, Jehoash son of Jehoahaz became king over Israel and reigned sixteen years. Following his father, he too did evil in the sight of the LORD and kept walking in Jeroboam's ways. Right here the narrator inserts Jehoash's standard closing summary—mentioning his war against Judah's King Amaziah, his death, his burial in Samaria, and his son Jeroboam succeeding him (13:10–13).

Immediately after that summary, the narrative pauses to record the final farewell of the aging prophet Elisha. Absent from the text for decades after anointing Jehu, Elisha now lay fatally ill. Israel's King Jehoash came down to his bedside, wept openly over him, and cried out: "My father, my father! The chariots and horsemen of Israel!"—repeating the exact heartbroken cry Elisha himself had shouted when his master Elijah was taken up to heaven (13:14).

Even from his deathbed, Elisha carried the full weight of God's word. Telling the king to fetch a bow and arrows and draw the bow, the dying prophet laid his frail hands directly over the king's hands. Ordering the east window thrown open—facing the Aramean-occupied territories—Elisha commanded, "Shoot!" As the arrow flew out, Elisha declared: "The LORD's arrow of victory! The arrow of victory over Aram!"—foretelling that the king would defeat Aram at Aphek until he completely destroyed them (13:15–17).

Next, Elisha told the king to take the remaining arrows and strike the ground. Jehoash struck three times and stopped. Instantly the man of God flared in anger: you should have struck five or six times to crush Aram completely; because you quit too soon, you will defeat Aram only three times (13:18–19).

Not long afterward, Elisha died and was buried. The next spring, when raiding bands normally roamed the land, Moabite raiders swept into Israel. While a funeral party was burying a man, they spotted a raiding band approaching; panicking, they tossed the corpse straight into Elisha's tomb and fled. The instant the dead man's body touched Elisha's bones, he sprang back to life and stood up on his feet! (13:20–21).

Chapter 13 closes with a sweeping window onto God's stubborn mercy. Throughout Jehoahaz's entire reign, Hazael had ground Israel underfoot. Yet the LORD remained gracious, felt deep compassion, and turned toward them—not because they deserved it, but because of His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; He was unwilling to destroy them or banish them from His presence yet. Just as Elisha had spoken from his deathbed, once Hazael died and his son Ben-hadad took Damascus's throne, Israel's King Jehoash defeated Ben-hadad exactly three times and recovered the towns his father had lost to Aram (13:22–25).

## Key Takeaways

**God's mercy runs wider than human faithfulness.** King Jehoahaz was an idolatrous ruler, yet when the nation groaned under crushing oppression and he cried out, the LORD still listened and rescued them (13:4–5). God intervened not because Jehoahaz earned it, but because He saw His people's misery and kept faith with His ancient oath to their ancestors (13:23). Grace invariably arrives before humans ever manage to fix themselves.

**A nation's true strength never lives in chariot counts.** Israel's military had been pounded down to a pathetic ten chariots and fifty horsemen (13:7). Yet leaning over the sickbed of an unarmed dying old man, the king wept calling him "the chariots and horsemen of Israel!" (13:14). God's word and His presence with a faithful servant form the truest defense any nation ever possesses.

**Half-heartedness shrinks the blessing God wants to pour out.** God opened the door for Jehoash to crush Aram decisively. Yet instructed to strike the ground with arrows, the king tapped perfunctorily three times and quit (13:18–19). He showed genuine outward respect for the prophet, yet lacked the eager faith and grit needed to seize everything God was placing in his hands.

**God's servants pass away, yet God's power never dies.** Elisha received no fiery chariot ride into heaven like his teacher Elijah; he aged, fell ill, and lay down in a tomb like every mortal (13:14; 13:20). Yet that astonishing graveside miracle—a corpse springing upright at brushing his bones—declares that life's power never sat inside Elisha's flesh, but in the living God whose work keeps surging forward long after His servant rests (13:21).

## Deeper Meaning

**The Judges cycle replays inside Israel's monarchy.** The chapter's opening rhythm—sin, God's anger, foreign oppression, desperate cry, and God raising up "a deliverer" (13:2–5)—echoes beat for beat the recurring spiral of the book of Judges (Judges 3:9). Centuries of kingship had never healed the human heart: the moment relief arrived, people slid right back to idols. History underscores that no mortal monarch can cure a rebellious heart.

**A bedside cry bridges two generations of prophets.** King Jehoash's sob—"My father, my father! The chariots and horsemen of Israel!" (13:14)—repeats almost word for word Elisha's own cry watching Elijah carried skyward (2:12). Both moments confront survivors with the exact same question: once the voice of God departs, what will the nation lean on? Sadly, Elisha once picked up Elijah's mantle and pressed forward in faith, whereas Jehoash wept sincerely yet still left Jeroboam's golden calves untouched (13:11).

**Faithfulness to the ancestral covenant is the red thread binding the Old Testament.** Verse 13:23 explains why God refused to erase Israel using the exact wording of the Exodus deliverance: He remembered His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and looked upon them with compassion (Exodus 2:24–25). Alongside that ancient oath, God's four-generation pledge to Jehu (10:30) also continues holding firm through Jehoahaz and Jehoash. Salvation history never hangs on fragile human loyalty, but on God's unshakable oath.

**Hazael's nightmare and the field of Aphek fulfill earlier prophecies.** Aram grinding Israel's army "like the dust at threshing" (13:7) painfully fulfills the tears Elisha wept years earlier when foreseeing Hazael's cruelty (8:12). Meanwhile, Elisha naming Aphek as the coming victory site (13:17) revives memory of Israel's earlier miracle crushing Aram right on Aphek's plain to prove the LORD is God of hills and valleys alike (1 Kings 20:26–30).

**Life erupting from a grave reverses the law of ritual defilement.** Under Mosaic law, touching any corpse, human bone, or grave made a person unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:11; 19:16). Death spread uncleanness—that was the old world's fixed rule. Inside Elisha's tomb, that current snaps into reverse: instead of a corpse defiling the prophet's bones, God's life-giving power surges into the dead man and lifts him back onto his feet (13:21). Many readers glimpse here a distant silhouette of the Messiah: Jesus declares Himself the resurrection and the life (John 11:25), and at His death and resurrection tombs split open and many sleeping saints rose to life (Matthew 27:52–53).

## Notes & Nuances

**Keep the two contemporary Kings Joash distinct.** Across chapters 12 and 13, two rulers sharing the same name (*Joash* / *Jehoash*) reign simultaneously: Joash son of Ahaziah rules southern Judah (12:1; 13:1), while Jehoash (also spelled Joash) son of Jehoahaz rules northern Israel (13:9–10). Paying close attention to the tags "king of Judah" and "king of Israel" prevents tangling the two timelines.

**Who was the unnamed "deliverer" in 13:5?** Scripture deliberately leaves the deliverer anonymous, prompting three main proposals. First, Israel's own Kings Jehoash and Jeroboam II, who soon beat Aram back and restored Israel's frontiers (13:25; 14:25–27). Second, Assyria's King Adad-nirari III, who hammered Damascus in the late ninth century BC, unwittingly relieving Aram's stranglehold on Israel. Third, Elisha himself. Whichever human instrument God employed—an Israelite king or even a foreign emperor—the narrator's point stays crystal clear: the LORD Himself delivered them.

**Why does Jehoash's obituary appear before his visit to Elisha's deathbed?** Verses 13:12–13 already record Jehoash's death and burial in Samaria, yet verse 13:14 immediately shows him alive visiting dying Elisha, and verse 13:25 reports his three victories over Ben-hadad. This reflects standard topical structuring in Kings: the editor completes the standard regnal frame for the king first, then appends the Elisha farewell narrative and theological commentary on Aram's defeat. Similarly, Jehoash's clash with Amaziah is previewed in 13:12 before receiving full narration in chapter 14 (14:8–14).

**Why did Elisha get angry when Jehoash stopped at three arrow strikes?** Modern readers often sympathize with Jehoash—after all, Elisha never told him how many times to strike! (13:18–19). Yet seconds earlier, Elisha had plainly declared the bow-and-arrow sign to be "the LORD's arrow of victory over Aram" (13:17). Inside that prophetic enactment, striking the ground symbolized striking the enemy down. Giving three halfhearted taps and stopping—neither exhausting his quiver nor waiting for Elisha to call halt—betrayed passive formality instead of wholehearted hunger for complete victory from God.

**The miracle inside Elisha's tomb is not an endorsement of relic veneration.** The flash miracle of a dead man reviving on contact with Elisha's bones (13:21) passes in a single stark verse, with zero hint of pilgrims flocking to worship at his grave afterward. Its true role sits right inside chapter 13's plot: one verse earlier, Elisha died before his promise of three victories over Aram had come to pass (13:19–20). That graveside shockwave stamped God's seal of guarantee onto the prophet's final words—proving God's promise remained vibrantly alive even after His prophet lay in the dust.
