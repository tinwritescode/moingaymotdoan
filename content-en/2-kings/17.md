# 2 Kings 17 — The Fall of Samaria and the Exile of the Northern Kingdom

## Story

While King Ahaz stumbled down south in the previous chapter, the northern kingdom of Israel entered its final years. In the twelfth year of Ahaz, Hoshea son of Elah took Samaria's throne and reigned nine years. Though he still did evil in the sight of the LORD, Scripture inserts a striking nuance: he was "not like the kings of Israel who preceded him." Soon into his reign, Assyrian Emperor Shalmaneser marched against him, forcing Hoshea into vassalage and annual tribute. Before long, however, Hoshea secretly sent envoys seeking help from So, king of Egypt, and halted tribute payments to Assyria. Catching wind of the treason, Assyria's king arrested Hoshea and threw him into prison in chains. Assyrian armies swept over the entire land and clamped a three-year siege around Samaria. In Hoshea's ninth year, the capital fell. The king of Assyria deported Israel into exile, settling them in Halah, along the Habor River of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes (17:1–6).

Right here the narrator halts the chronicle completely to explain how a kingdom standing more than two centuries vanished off the map. This catastrophe was far more than a small state placing a bad bet on Egypt against a superpower. Its true root lay in Israel sinning against the LORD their God—the very God who had rescued them out of Egypt from beneath Pharaoh's iron fist. They feared other gods, imitated the customs of the nations God had driven out before them, and followed the corrupt practices Israel's own kings had invented. Secretly they did things that were not right against God, building high places everywhere from remote watchtowers to fortified towns, raising sacred pillars and Asherah poles atop every high hill and under every spreading tree. There they burned incense and served detestable idols God had expressly forbidden long ago (17:7–12).

Nor did disaster strike out of the blue. Generation after generation, God warned both Israel and Judah through every prophet and seer, pleading with them to turn from their evil ways and keep the law given to their ancestors. Yet they refused to listen, "stiffening their necks" just like their unbelieving fathers. Rejecting His covenant, statutes, and warnings, "they followed worthless idols and themselves became worthless." Scarcely a single form of rebellion escaped them: they cast two golden calves, built Asherah poles, bowed to the sun, moon, and starry host, served Baal, "sacrificed their sons and daughters in the fire," practiced divination and sorcery, and sold themselves to do evil provoking the LORD to anger (17:13–17).

Facing such relentless hardness, the LORD grew deeply angry and removed Israel from His presence, leaving only the tribe of Judah down south. Yet even Judah failed to keep God's commands, copying the corrupt customs Israel had introduced. So the LORD rejected all the descendants of Israel, afflicting them and handing them over to plunderers until He cast them completely out of His sight. Tracing the tragedy all the way back to its opening wound, the author points to the day Israel tore away from David's house and crowned Jeroboam son of Nebat, who drove Israel away from the LORD into massive sin. Across every generation, Israel clung stubbornly to Jeroboam's sins without letting go—until the LORD banished them from their own land into exile in Assyria, fulfilling every warning His servants the prophets had announced (17:18–23).

Samaria's towns did not sit empty long. Following standard imperial resettlement policy, Assyria's king imported foreign populations from Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim to inhabit Samaria's cities in Israel's place. Arriving in the land, these newcomers did not fear the LORD, so God sent lions among them that killed several settlers. Word went back to the Assyrian emperor that the relocated peoples "do not know the requirements of the god of the land," provoking him to send lions among them. Immediately the king ordered one of the exiled Israelite priests sent back to live at Bethel and teach the newcomers the proper rituals for the god of the land (17:24–28).

Returning to Bethel, the priest instructed them how to fear the LORD. Yet those nations had zero intention of giving up their old gods. Each group crafted images of its own homeland deities and set them right inside the hilltop shrines Samaria's former inhabitants had built: Babylonians worshiped Succoth-benoth, men of Cuthah made Nergal, people of Hamath made Ashima, Avvites made Nibhaz and Tartak, while people of Sepharvaim burned their own children in fire to Adrammelech and Anammelech. Casually appointing priests from among all ranks to staff the high places, they produced a strange religious hybrid: "So while these nations feared the LORD, they also served their carved images" just like their homeland customs (17:29–33).

The chapter closes with the historian's candid assessment. Right down to the writer's own day, those peoples clung to their mixed ways; judged by truth, "they do not fear the LORD" nor keep the statutes and commands God gave Jacob's descendants. God's covenant with Israel demanded undivided loyalty: never fear, bow down to, or sacrifice to any other god, but revere solely the LORD who brought them out of Egypt "with great power and an outstretched arm," because He alone rescues them from every enemy. Still, those populations refused to listen; mixing nominal homage to the LORD with devotion to their carved idols, they passed that blended lifestyle from parents to children and grandchildren "to this very day" (17:34–41).

## Key Takeaways

**History is never merely a political chessboard; God's justice moves behind it.** Read purely from imperial archives, Samaria's collapse in 722 BC looks like a reckless diplomatic gamble by Hoshea withholding tribute while hoping for Egyptian aid (17:3–6). Yet Scripture pulls back the curtain to reveal the deeper reality: Assyria was merely the tool, while the true cause was two full centuries of shredding God's covenant (17:7–12). No nation's fate or individual life ever unfolds outside God's righteous sight.

**God's patience stretches remarkably long—yet not infinitely.** Before letting Samaria fall, the LORD waited more than two hundred years, sending prophet after prophet begging His people to turn home (17:13). King Hoshea was not even as wicked as many predecessors (17:2), yet a cup of sin filling across generations had finally brimmed over. God's slowness to anger is an open invitation to repent, never a lifetime pass to keep stiffening our necks (17:14–18).

**People steadily become like whatever they worship.** Inside this sweeping indictment, verse 17:15 lands with chilling clarity: "they followed worthless idols and themselves became worthless." Turning their backs on the living God to chase hollow statues that cannot see, hear, or save emptied out their own souls, vigor, and identity. Whatever we enthrone at the center of our lives silently reshapes us into its own image.

**Blended, half-and-half worship is really no worship of God at all.** Samaria's new settlers never declared war on the LORD. They gladly learned His rituals to keep lions at bay while keeping every ancestral idol standing snugly inside their shrines (17:28–33). Scripture cuts cleanly through that "add God to the shelf" mentality, declaring flatly that "they do not fear the LORD" (17:34). God will never serve as an assistant deity sharing shelf space with rival idols; either He reigns alone, or we do not truly worship Him.

## Deeper Meaning

**Exile fulfills both the ancient covenant and the prophets' warnings.** Over seven centuries before Assyrian troops ringed Samaria, Deuteronomy warned unmistakably: abandon the LORD for foreign gods, and He will drive you and your king into an alien nation and scatter you among the peoples (Deuteronomy 28:36; 28:64). Closer in time, Ahijah foretold Israel would be uprooted from this good land over Jeroboam's sin (1 Kings 14:15–16). Right on the edge of collapse, Hosea compared Israel to a "silly, senseless dove" fluttering back and forth between Egypt and Assyria (Hosea 7:11), while Amos announced bluntly they would march into exile beyond Damascus (Amos 5:27). Samaria's ashes prove that God's word—whether blessing or warning—never falls empty.

**The northern kingdom breaks right along the fracture Jeroboam carved two centuries before.** Chapter 17 repeats "the sins of Jeroboam" like a haunting drumbeat summarizing northern history (17:21–23). At the kingdom's split, fearful of losing subjects to Jerusalem's temple, Jeroboam forged golden calves at Bethel and Dan and appointed non-Levites as hilltop priests (1 Kings 12:28–31; 13:33). Bitterly ironic, two hundred years later the exiled priest returned by Assyria to instruct foreign colonists settles straight back into Bethel—the old seat of compromised worship—where newcomers immediately pick random hilltop priests just as Jeroboam had done (17:28; 17:32). One initial compromise for political convenience bent the spiritual arc of generations to come.

**"Follow worthless idols and become worthless" states a spiritual law spanning all Scripture.** Verse 17:15's razor-sharp indictment reappears almost verbatim in Jeremiah's lament over Judah: their fathers wandered far from God, "followed worthless idols, and became worthless themselves" (Jeremiah 2:5). The Psalms express the exact same law vividly: idols have eyes that cannot see and ears that cannot hear, and everyone who makes or trusts them "will become like them" (Psalms 115:4–8). In the New Testament, Paul affirms the identical principle: exchanging the Creator's glory for created images plunges minds into futile thinking and darkened hearts (Romans 1:21–25). Walking away from the Giver of life inevitably hollows out human life itself.

**Samaria's divided worship mirrors the timeless choice pressed in the New Testament.** The closing covenant summary echoes the Ten Commandments at Sinai word for word: the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt's slavery, so no other gods may stand before Him (17:35–38; Exodus 20:2–5). Eight centuries later, Jesus places that identical undivided choice before crowds following Him: "No one can serve two masters" (Matthew 6:24). Modern hearts may no longer carve statues of Baal or Nergal, yet Samaria's ancient temptation lives right on: keeping enough religion for safety and peace of mind while letting money, status, or desire reign as true master.

**From Samaria's heartbreak to gospel grace beside Jacob's well.** The forced resettlements and religious blending of 17:24–33 sowed deep mistrust between Jews and Samaritans lasting centuries. When Judean exiles returned from Babylon to rebuild the temple, northern residents claiming to worship Israel's God since Assyrian days were turned away flatly over their mixed origins and worship (Ezra 4:1–3; 4:9–10). By Jesus's day, "Jews do not associate with Samaritans" (John 4:9). Yet Jesus deliberately walked straight through Samaria, sat beside Jacob's well conversing with a Samaritan woman, and announced the new hour when true worshipers are bound to neither mountain nor city, but "worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:20–24). Before ascending, He placed Samaria straight into the Great Commission, and soon afterward that formerly idol-filled city erupted with joy receiving the gospel through Philip (Acts 1:8; 8:5–8).

## Notes & Nuances

**Difficult details: child sacrifice by fire and God sending lions among new settlers.** Two elements here jolt modern readers sharply. First, Israelites sacrificing sons and daughters in fire (17:17) and Sepharvaim colonists burning children to pagan gods (17:31): as noted in chapter 16, despite older suggestions of harmless purification rites, verse 17:31's explicit language and Mosaic law leave no doubt this was literal child sacrifice fiercely condemned by Scripture (Deuteronomy 18:10–12). Second, God sending lions to kill foreign newcomers who did not yet know Him (17:25) raises understandable questions. Commentators highlight two complementary layers. Naturally, war-depopulated landscapes quickly see wild predators like lions rebound into settlements. Theologically, the narrator reads the attacks as God asserting His unbroken sovereignty: Israel's exile never meant the LORD had lost to Assyria's gods or been evicted from His land; He remained sovereign Lord there, and newcomers dwelling on His ground could not live as though He did not exist.

**Which Assyrian king captured Samaria, and who was "So, king of Egypt"?** Verse 17:3 names Shalmaneser (Shalmaneser V), who launched Samaria's three-year siege (17:5; 18:9–10), whereas 17:6 says simply "the king of Assyria" took the city and deported Israel. Excavated Assyrian annals show Sargon II—who succeeded Shalmaneser V late in 722 BC—claiming credit for seizing Samaria and deporting over twenty-seven thousand inhabitants. Historians commonly conclude either that Shalmaneser V died right as the siege closed, leaving Sargon II to finish the deportation, or that Sargon claimed credit for his predecessor's campaign; Scripture's neutral phrase "the king of Assyria" in 17:6 accommodates either cleanly. As for "So, king of Egypt" in 17:4, proposals range from an abbreviated form of Pharaoh Osorkon IV (ruling the Nile Delta amid Egyptian fragmentation), to an Egyptian general, to the Delta capital city of Sais.

**Did the "ten lost tribes" really vanish completely off the earth?** Verse 17:18's summary—"only the tribe of Judah was left"—inspired centuries of legends picturing all ten northern tribes erased without a single soul remaining. Both history and Scripture paint a fuller picture. Assyria customarily deported kings, nobles, artisans, and urban elites to cripple rebellion, leaving large rural peasant populations right on the land. Soon afterward, Judah's King Hezekiah dispatched couriers across Ephraim, Manasseh, and Zebulun inviting Israel's "remnant" to Jerusalem's Passover, and pilgrims from Asher, Manasseh, and Zebulun came down to attend (2 Chronicles 30:6; 30:10–11). Countless northern refugees also fled south and blended into Judah. Centuries later, the New Testament still identifies the elderly prophetess Anna in Jerusalem's temple as belonging to the northern tribe of Asher (Luke 2:36).

**Reading Samaritan origins fairly.** Verses 17:24–41 have sometimes been misused to paint all Samaritans one-dimensionally. Historically, the Samaritan community of Jesus's era took shape across many centuries: alongside foreign deportees (17:24; Ezra 4:2; 4:9–10), native Israelite remnants remained in the land. By New Testament times, Samaritans had completely abandoned pagan idols like Succoth-benoth and Nergal, guarding the five books of Moses fiercely and worshiping the one true God exclusively—even while disagreeing sharply with Jews over the proper temple mount (John 4:20). Chapter 17 condemns eighth- and seventh-century BC religious syncretism, never ethnic bigotry—a prejudice Jesus decisively shattered in the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:33).

**Why does verse 33 say they "feared the LORD" while verse 34 immediately says "they do not fear the LORD"?** Set back to back, the two lines look contradictory until we catch the author's biting wordplay. In 17:33, "feared" means superstitious outward caution—offering rituals to appease the LORD and keep lions away while clutching old idols. In 17:34, the writer measures by true covenant faithfulness: fear of God that ignores His law and refuses to discard rival idols counts before God as no fear of Him at all.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the first-century Jewish historian, recounts Samaria's fall and Assyria's forced resettlement as the direct historical root of the Samaritans of his day. Because most newcomers came from Cuthah, Jews routinely called them Cutheans, while in Greek they were called Samaritans. **The Babylonian Talmud**, the great rabbinic compendium, preserves a famous legal debate born directly out of the lions in 2 Kings 17. Sages divided sharply over the religious status of the Cutheans (Samaritans) settled in Israel: one view regarded them as genuine converts, while another dismissed them as "lion converts"—people who turned to the LORD purely out of terror at wild beasts rather than authentic faith. That debate shows how keenly Jewish tradition distinguished fear-driven religion from genuine surrender. *(Babylonian Talmud, tractates Kiddushin and Sanhedrin)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with the famous rabbinic category of "lion converts" (gerei arayot) linked directly to 2 Kings 17 in Talmudic debates, though exact folio numbers were not checked -->

**Augustine**, the fourth- and fifth-century North African bishop, turns straight back to 2 Kings 17 while expounding Jesus's encounter with the Samaritan woman in John's Gospel to explain the deep rift separating Jews and Samaritans. After Israel's deportation, foreign settlers learned portions of God's law while mingling it with old errors. **Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, highlights two sobering warnings in this chapter. First, seeing exile strike during the reign of Hoshea—expressly noted as less wicked than earlier monarchs—shows that God endures patiently for ages, yet once a nation's cup of guilt brims over, judgment falls when least expected. Next, on 17:33–34, Henry dubs the newcomers' mixed cult a mongrel, half-hearted religion patching God onto old idols, which Scripture strips bare as no true reverence for God whatsoever. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 2 Kings 17)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Matthew Henry's characteristic comments on King Hoshea and mixed worship in verses 33–34, though not verified verbatim on ccel.org -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, an American Old Testament scholar, treats 2 Kings 17 as the supreme theological hinge of the entire book of Kings. Pausing the brisk royal chronicle for an extended sermon (17:7–23), the narrator insists Assyria is never history's ultimate actor—Israel's covenant betrayal triggered exile, while 17:19 drops a chilling side-warning directly onto Judah's doorstep. In the chapter's second half, Brueggemann spotlights the irony of Assyria's emperor reducing the LORD to a moody local deity appeased to call off lions, clueless that Israel's God refuses to line up alongside imperial idols. *(1 & 2 Kings, 2000)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: familiar with Brueggemann's treatment of chapter 17 as the theological pivot of Kings and the emperor reducing the LORD to a territorial god; exact page numbers not verified -->
