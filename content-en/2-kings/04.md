# 2 Kings 4 — Unending Oil, a Boy Raised, and Twenty Barley Loaves

## Story

A world away from royal armies and battlefields in chapter 3, chapter 4 unfolds four deeply ordinary, human scenes around Elisha's ministry. First comes the desperate cry of a widow whose late husband belonged to the sons of the prophets. Her husband had feared the LORD, yet died leaving debts she could not pay; now the creditor had arrived to seize her two sons as slaves. Elisha asked what she still had inside her house. Nothing, she replied, except one small jar of olive oil. He told her to go borrow empty jars from every neighbor—"do not gather just a few"—then step inside, shut the door behind herself and her sons, and keep pouring from her lone flask into every empty vessel (4:1–4).

She followed every word. Her sons kept carrying jars to her, and she kept pouring until every vessel stood brimming full. When she called for one more jar, her son answered, "'There is not another jar.' Then the oil stopped flowing" (4:5–6). Running back to report to Elisha, she heard his practical counsel: sell the oil, pay off your debt, and live with your sons on everything left over (4:7).

The second episode unfolds in Shunem, where a well-to-do, warmhearted woman lived. Every time Elisha passed through town, she urged him to stop for a meal. Over time she recognized him as "a holy man of God" and suggested to her husband that they build a small roof chamber furnished with a bed, table, chair, and lamp so he would always have a quiet place to rest whenever he came by (4:8–10). Deeply touched by her thoughtfulness, Elisha sent his servant Gehazi to ask whether he could put in a good word for her with the king or army commander. Calmly she answered, "I live among my own people." When Gehazi pointed out that she had no son and her husband was old, Elisha summoned her to the doorway and promised: at this exact season next year, you will hold a son in your arms. Scarcely daring to hope, she begged him not to mislead her. Yet exactly a year later, she bore a son just as he had said (4:11–17).

The boy grew until one morning he went out to join his father among the harvesters. Suddenly he clutched his head crying, "My head! My head!" His father had a servant carry him back to his mother. The child sat on her lap until noon and then died. Making no loud commotion, the mother carried her son upstairs, laid him on Elisha's own bed, and shut the door behind her (4:18–21). Calling her husband, she asked for a servant and a donkey so she could hurry to the man of God. When her husband asked in surprise why she was going since it was neither New Moon nor Sabbath, she answered crisply, "It is well." Driving the donkey hard without slowing, she raced straight to Mount Carmel (4:22–24).

Spotting her in the distance, Elisha sent Gehazi running to ask if all was well with her, her husband, and her child; again she replied briefly, "It is well." Reaching Elisha on the hill, however, she collapsed gripping his feet. Gehazi moved to push her away, yet Elisha stopped him at once: "Her soul is in bitter distress, and the LORD has hidden it from me and has not told me." At last her agony spilled out: did I ever ask for a son? Didn't I plead with you not to raise false hopes? (4:25–28). Elisha immediately handed Gehazi his staff and told him to run ahead without stopping to greet anyone along the road and lay the staff across the boy's face. The mother refused to leave without Elisha himself, swearing the exact oath Elisha had once sworn to Elijah: "As surely as the LORD lives and as you yourself live, I will not leave you!" So Elisha got up and went with her (4:29–30).

Gehazi arrived first and laid the staff on the child's face, yet there was neither sound nor response. Arriving at the house, Elisha stepped inside, shut the door behind the two of them, and prayed to the LORD. Then he climbed onto the bed and stretched himself over the boy—mouth to mouth, eyes to eyes, hands to hands—until the child's flesh began to grow warm. Stepping down to pace back and forth across the room, he climbed up and stretched across him once more. Finally the boy sneezed seven times and opened his eyes. Elisha called the mother in and told her to pick up her son. She fell gratefully at his feet, bowed to the floor, scooped up her boy, and walked out (4:31–37).

The closing two scenes occur in Gilgal during a severe famine. Elisha instructed his servant to set a big pot on the fire to cook stew for the sons of the prophets. One student went out to gather wild herbs, mistakenly picked poisonous wild gourds, and sliced them straight into the stew. One taste sent everyone shouting in alarm, "O man of God, there is death in the pot!" Elisha called for a handful of flour, tossed it into the kettle, and every trace of poison vanished so all ate safely (4:38–41). Soon afterward, a man from Baal-shalishah arrived bringing Elisha twenty loaves of barley bread baked from firstfruits along with fresh grain. Elisha ordered him to set it before the hundred men to eat. His attendant hesitated—how could twenty small loaves possibly feed a hundred?—yet Elisha repeated the word of the LORD: "They will eat and have some left over." Sure enough, all hundred ate their fill with loaves still left behind (4:42–44).

## Key Takeaways

**God cares deeply about ordinary kitchens and quiet household grief.** Stepping away from clash of armies in chapter 3, chapter 4 turns the camera toward overlooked corners: a widow terrified of losing her boys to debt, a mother sitting silently beside her dead child, hungry seminary students foraging wild greens. God's power does not belong solely on grand battlefields; it stoops gently into struggling homes.

**God delights to begin miracles with whatever little already sits in our hands.** Elisha did not conjure oil out of empty air; he asked the widow, "What do you have in your house?" (4:2), and multiplied that single modest jar until every borrowed vessel overflowed. Given twenty modest barley loaves from one generous donor (4:42), God fed a hundred hungry men. Never dismiss the small resource resting in your hands once it is placed before God.

**Real faith pairs steady resolve with raw honesty before God.** The woman of Shunem stands among the bravest figures in the Old Testament. She welcomed God's prophet expecting nothing back (4:13). When her son died, she kept remarkable composure along the whole journey without wasting breath complaining, yet once face to face with Elisha she dropped polite religious masks and poured out her bitter anguish (4:27–28). Refusing a quick-fix proxy via Gehazi's staff, she clung to the prophet himself until life returned (4:30).

**Miracles never flow from magic objects, but from prayer to the living God.** Watching Elisha's staff rest lifelessly across the dead child's face (4:31) delivers a quiet, sharp lesson. No sacred relic works automatically; only when Elisha shuts the door, cries out to the LORD, and gives himself closely beside the child does breath return (4:33–35).

## Deeper Meaning

**Following directly in Elijah's footsteps—and echoing the promise to Sarah.** Attentive readers notice immediately that chapter 4's opening two wonders echo and expand Elijah's signature miracles for the widow of Zarephath: unending olive oil and a dead son raised after the prophet stretches over him in prayer (1 Kings 17:14–16; 17:21–23). Meanwhile, Elisha's pledge to the Shunammite—"At this time next year, you will hold a son in your arms" (4:16)—echoes almost verbatim God's promise to elderly Abraham and Sarah (Genesis 18:10–14). Centuries later, Hebrews honors "women [who] received back their dead, raised to life again" among Scripture's heroes of faith (Hebrews 11:35).

**Twenty barley loaves foreshadow Jesus multiplying five loaves.** The closing story of chapter 4 rings unmistakably familiar to New Testament readers: someone brings barley loaves, an assistant protests they cannot possibly feed such a crowd, the prophet insists on passing them out anyway, and everyone eats until full with leftovers remaining (4:42–44). Centuries later beside the Sea of Galilee, Jesus took five barley loaves amid the exact same puzzled question from His disciples and fed five thousand with twelve baskets left over (John 6:9–13). Elisha's miracles stand like early pencil sketches pointing forward to One infinitely greater than Elisha.

**God's care does not end when chapter 4 closes.** The Shunammite woman's story continues in chapter 8: right before a seven-year famine strikes, Elisha warns her to relocate her family safely, and upon her return the king restores her home, land, and seven years' produce at the exact moment Gehazi is telling the king how her boy was raised (8:1–6). Elisha folding himself over the boy on the bed also echoes later in the New Testament when Paul throws himself around young Eutychus after his fatal fall from a window (Acts 20:9–10).

## Notes & Nuances

**Creditors seizing children as debt slaves.** This grim threat reflects harsh social realities in Israel whenever the Law of Moses was ignored. Covenant law allowed an impoverished Israelite to work off debts as a hired laborer, yet strictly forbade treating fellow Israelites as permanent chattel slaves and mandated full liberation in the Year of Jubilee (Leviticus 25:39–41). Under corrupt northern kings, even devout families like this prophet's widow faced ruthless exploitation.

**Why did the Shunammite woman answer "It is well" right after her child died?** The Hebrew word she uses is *shalom*—a conventional reassuring phrase much like "all is fine, don't worry." Saying it to her husband (4:23) and to Gehazi (4:26) likely meant she refused to waste precious time explaining her grief to people who could not restore her boy, holding her anguish together until she reached the one person who could plead with God. It does not need to be read as dishonesty or denial.

**Why did Elisha's staff fail to revive the child?** The text leaves the reason unstated. Some think Gehazi lacked faith or botched his instructions; others note that Elisha himself had not yet grasped the full gravity of what had happened—candidly admitting the LORD had hidden it from him (4:27)—and tried sending an assistant first until the mother's fierce persistence drew him to come kneel and pray in person. Either way, the detail leaves no room for superstition: power over death belongs exclusively to the LORD, never to a piece of wood.

**What were the toxic wild gourds tossed into the stew?** Botanists of ancient Palestine commonly identify them as colocynth (*Citrullus colocynthis*), a wild desert gourd abundant near the Dead Sea and Jordan Valley; resembling small edible melons, they taste fiercely bitter and cause severe gastrointestinal poisoning if eaten. Ordinary flour holds no chemical antidote, so tossing flour into the pot served simply as a visible sign accompanying God's healing power—just like pouring salt into Jericho's poisoned spring (2:20–21).

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, the North African theologian writing around AD 200, appeals directly to Elisha feeding a hundred men with twenty barley loaves with leftovers to spare (4:42–44) in his refutation of Marcion. **Matthew Henry**, an English pastor who died in 1714, pauses memorably over 4:6: "'There is not another jar.' Then the oil stopped flowing."
