# 2 Kings 6 — A Floating Axe Head, Fiery Armies at Dothan, and Famine in Samaria

## Story

The community of the sons of the prophets grew so large that their meeting place became cramped. They asked Elisha's permission to head down to the Jordan to cut timber for larger quarters, inviting him to come along. While everyone was felling beams, one man's iron axe head flew off its handle and sank deep into the river. Panicked, he cried out, "Oh no, my master! It was borrowed!" Elisha asked where it fell, cut a stick, and tossed it into the water at that exact spot. The heavy iron axe head floated right up to the surface, and the student simply reached out and lifted it back (6:1–7).

The scene shifts abruptly to border warfare between Aram and Israel. Every time Aram's king secretly mapped out an ambush with his officers, Elisha sent warning ahead so Israel's king steered clear. After this happened repeatedly, the Aramean monarch grew frantic, suspecting a spy inside his own court. One adviser reassured him there was no traitor at all: it was Elisha the prophet in Israel, who "tells the king of Israel the very words you speak in your bedroom" (6:8–12).

Immediately the king of Aram tracked down where Elisha was staying and dispatched a massive strike force of horses and chariots by night to surround the town of Dothan. Rising early the next morning, Elisha's servant stepped outside and froze at seeing enemy forces ringing the town on every side. Terrified, he cried out asking what they should do. Elisha replied calmly, "Do not be afraid, for those who are with us are more than those who are with them." Then he prayed, "O LORD, please open his eyes that he may see." The LORD opened the young man's eyes, and he gasped to see the entire hillside around Elisha ablaze with horses and chariots of fire (6:13–17).

As the Arameans closed in to seize him, Elisha prayed for the LORD to strike them with blindness. God answered at once. Stepping straight up to them, Elisha told them they had the wrong road and the wrong city—just follow him and he would lead them straight to the man they were looking for. Single-handedly, he led the entire Aramean battalion right through the gates into the heart of Samaria! Once inside, Elisha prayed for God to open their eyes again; the enemy troops blinked in shock to discover themselves trapped inside Israel's capital (6:18–20).

Israel's king practically leaped with excitement, asking Elisha, "My father, shall I strike them down? Shall I strike them down?" Instantly the prophet halted him: you would not slaughter prisoners captured with your own sword and bow, much less these! Instead, "set food and water before them so that they may eat and drink and then go back to their master." So the king laid out a sumptuous banquet for the entire enemy force and sent them safely home. For a season afterward, Aramean raiding bands stopped harassing Israel's territory altogether (6:21–23).

Peace, however, did not last. Some time later, King Ben-hadad of Aram mobilized his whole army and clamped a suffocating siege around Samaria. The siege dragged on until famine grew so horrific that a donkey's head—unclean meat forbidden by law—sold for eighty shekels of silver, and a quarter-cab of dove's dung went for five shekels of silver (6:24–25). One day as Israel's king walked along the city wall, a woman cried out for help. Bitterly he snapped back, "If the LORD does not help you, where can I find help for you?" Yet hearing her story turned his blood cold: she and another woman had agreed to boil and eat their own sons to survive—yesterday they had eaten hers, and today the other mother had hidden her child away (6:26–29).

Horrified, Israel's king tore his royal robes right there on the wall, revealing sackcloth worn underneath directly against his skin. Yet instead of repenting before God, he erupted in rage and swore an oath to cut off Elisha's head that very day. Before the royal executioner ever reached his door, Elisha—sitting indoors with the elders—already knew, labeling the king "this son of a murderer" and instructing them to hold the door shut because the king himself was marching close behind his messenger. Right as the envoy arrived came the king's bitter, defiant cry: "This calamity is from the LORD. Why should I wait for the LORD any longer?" (6:30–33).

## Key Takeaways

**God cares about everything from a borrowed axe head to the fate of a nation.** Setting the recovered iron axe head right beside heavenly fiery armies at Dothan is no accident. For a poor prophetic student, losing a borrowed iron tool meant crushing debt (cf. Exodus 22:14). The Sovereign commanding heaven's chariots of fire gladly stoops to keep one humble worker out of debt.

**What human eyes see is never the whole reality.** That morning at Dothan, master and servant stood on the exact same hillside yet saw two completely different worlds: the servant saw only ringing enemy spears, while Elisha saw fiery armies of heaven surrounding them (6:15–17). Faith is never pretending danger is not real; it is asking God to open our eyes to see that He is vastly bigger than the danger.

**Mercy sometimes disarms enemies far better than swords.** With an entire hostile army dropped neatly in his lap inside Samaria, Israel's king reached instantly for slaughter. Elisha chose instead to serve them a feast and send them home untouched (6:21–23). The surprising payoff: hospitality ended the cross-border raids, proving some victories are won only by grace.

**Wearing sackcloth underneath royal robes is not the same as genuine repentance.** Confronted by Samaria's nightmare famine, King Joram wore rough sackcloth hidden beneath his tunic as a mark of grief (6:30). Yet the moment pressure peaked, his real heart surfaced: instead of examining his own idolatry and his family's sins, he blamed the prophet and lashed out at God (6:31–33).

## Deeper Meaning

**Angels encamp around those who fear the LORD.** Elisha's quiet assurance—"those who are with us are more than those who are with them" (6:16)—turns into living pictures the psalmist's promise that the angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear Him to deliver them (Psalms 34:7). In the New Testament, the apostle John echoes that very confidence to encourage a small Church inside a hostile world: greater is He who is in you than he who is in the world (1 John 4:4).

**The banquet at Samaria anticipates overcoming evil with good.** Elisha feeding hungry enemy soldiers and releasing them unharmed (6:22–23) lives out long beforehand the teaching of Proverbs and Paul: if your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat; if thirsty, give him water to drink; do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good (Proverbs 25:21–22; Romans 12:20–21). Meanwhile, God striking aggressive troops with disorienting blindness to shield His servant echoes the angels protecting Lot's household in Sodom (Genesis 19:11).

**The unspeakable famine fulfills Moses's covenant warning.** Watching two mothers bargain over eating their own sons (6:28–29) ranks among the bleakest scenes in all of Scripture. It did not strike out of thin air: centuries earlier, Moses warned in stark detail that stubborn covenant betrayal would bring sieges so desperate parents would resort to this very horror (Deuteronomy 28:53–57). Centuries later, the same nightmare replayed when Babylon starved Jerusalem into collapse (Lamentations 4:10).

## Notes & Nuances

**How precious was an iron axe head in that era?** In the ninth century BC, forged iron tools remained expensive luxury items for poor laborers—especially a frugal community of prophetic students. Mosaic law required anyone who borrowed a neighbor's property and lost or ruined it to make full restitution (Exodus 22:14). The student's distressed cry reveals both real financial anxiety and an honest conscience determined not to hurt a generous neighbor.

**Did Elisha lie to the Aramean soldiers in 6:19?** Struck with disorienting blindness (the Hebrew denotes dazzled confusion rather than total loss of sight), the troops heard Elisha say, "This is not the way, and this is not the city," before he marched them into Samaria. Many commentators view this as wartime misdirection against an armed force sent specifically to capture or kill him—and strictly speaking, Elisha's home really was in Samaria (2:25; 6:32), where he delivered them safely before Israel's king and treated them to a generous feast rather than execution.

**Was "dove's dung" in 6:25 literal bird droppings?** Scholars propose two readings. Taken literally, starving townspeople bought dried pigeon droppings either for cooking fuel or even to scavenge undigested grain seeds inside. Alternatively, "dove's dung" may have been a folk nickname for an inexpensive wild bulb or seed pod (like colloquial plant names in many cultures) ordinarily ignored yet now selling at outrageous wartime prices. Either way, the verse conveys Samaria's unimaginable extremity.

**Who speaks the final line in 6:33—the messenger or the king?** While the Hebrew simply states "he said" right after the messenger arrives, many English translations identify the speaker explicitly as the king—either speaking directly as he steps right behind his courier (6:32) or through his envoy voicing the king's exact words—since chapter 7 opens immediately with Elisha answering the king face to face. Chapter 6 breaks off mid-scene; read straight into chapter 7 for Elisha's astonishing reply.

God did not have to scramble heavenly angels down from the sky at the last second, Spurgeon noted—they already filled the hillside!
