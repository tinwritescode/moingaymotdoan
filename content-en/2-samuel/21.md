# 2 Samuel 21 — The Gibeonite Famine and Four Battles with Giants

## Story

During David's reign, a severe famine struck the land and dragged on for three consecutive years. Refusing to sit passively while the nation wasted away, David sought the face of the LORD to learn the root cause. The LORD answered that the disaster had come "because of Saul and his blood-stained house, because he put the Gibeonites to death" (21:1).

The Gibeonites were not Israelites, but a surviving remnant of the Amorites. Back when Israel first entered the Promised Land, the nation's leaders had sworn an oath by the LORD to let them live. Yet Saul, in his "zeal for the people of Israel and Judah," had trampled that covenant and tried to wipe them out. Understanding the truth, David summoned the Gibeonites and asked what he could do—what atonement he could make—so they would once again bless "the inheritance of the LORD" (21:2–3).

The Gibeonites replied plainly that they wanted no silver or gold from Saul's house, nor did they have "the right to put anyone to death in Israel." When David asked what they wished him to do, they named the crime of the man who had plotted to wipe them out of Israel's territory and stated a chilling demand: hand over seven of Saul's male descendants so they could expose them before the LORD at Gibeah, Saul's own hometown. David answered, "I will hand them over" (21:4–6).

Amid that grim reckoning, David remembered the oath he had sworn with Jonathan before the LORD and spared Mephibosheth, Jonathan's son. Instead, the king took seven other men of Saul's line: Armoni and Mephibosheth—two sons borne to Saul by his concubine Rizpah—along with five sons whom Saul's daughter had borne to Adriel of Meholah. He handed all seven over to the Gibeonites, who executed and exposed them together on the hill before the LORD. All seven died at the same time, during "the first days of the barley harvest" (21:7–9).

From that day on, a heartbreaking scene unfolded on the hillside. Rizpah daughter of Aiah took sackcloth and spread it out for herself on the rock. From the start of the barley harvest until rains finally poured down from the sky upon the bodies, day after day and night after night, that grieving mother stayed beside her dead sons and relatives: keeping birds of the air from landing on them by day and driving wild animals away by night (21:10).

When word of what Rizpah had done reached David, the king was moved. He went in person to the citizens of Jabesh-gilead—the loyal men who years before had quietly removed the bodies of Saul and Jonathan from the square of Beth-shan after the defeat on Mount Gilboa—and retrieved the bones of father and son. David also ordered the bones of the seven executed men gathered up, and buried them all honorably alongside the bones of Saul and Jonathan in the tomb of Saul's father Kish at Zela in Benjamin. Everything was carried out just as the king commanded. "And after that, God answered prayer in behalf of the land" (21:11–14).

Shifting to another chapter of David's reign, war flared up again with the Philistines. David went down with his troops to fight, yet midway through the battle the king grew "exhausted." At that exact moment, Ishbi-benob—one of the descendants of the giant Rapha, carrying a bronze spearhead weighing three hundred shekels and strapped with a new sword—charged in to finish David off. Fortunately, Abishai son of Zeruiah rushed to his rescue, struck the Philistine down, and killed him. From that day forward, David's men made him swear never to go out to battle with them again, "so that the lamp of Israel will not be extinguished" (21:15–17).

Three further clashes with Philistine giants of Rapha's line follow in quick succession. At Gob, Sibbecai the Hushathite struck down Saph. Also at Gob, Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim from Bethlehem killed Goliath the Gittite, whose spear shaft was "like a weaver's beam." And at Gath, a massive warrior with six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot—twenty-four in all—stepped out to taunt Israel, only to be struck down by Jonathan son of Shimeah, David's own nephew. All four of these giants born to Rapha in Gath fell by the hand of David and his servants (21:18–22).

## Key Takeaways

**An oath sworn in God's name never expires with the passing of time.** Israel's vow to the Gibeonites was centuries old, and Saul's betrayal lay years in the past, yet God did not forget. No nation can build lasting peace on broken promises and the blood of vulnerable people it vowed to protect (21:1–2).

**Zeal divorced from justice is still sin.** Scripture specifically notes that Saul hunted the Gibeonites out of "zeal for the people of Israel and Judah" (21:2). Patriotic passion or religious fervor can never justify tearing up a solemn promise and slaughtering others; blind zeal only brings disaster back onto the very people it claims to serve.

**Patient motherly love can awaken the conscience of an entire court.** Rizpah commanded no army, held no political leverage, and spoke not a single recorded protest before the king. She simply spread sackcloth on bare rock and kept silent vigil beside her dead children through months of sun, wind, and wild beasts (21:10). That agonizing faithfulness finally stopped David in his tracks and restored final dignity to the whole house of Saul (21:11–14).

**No one keeps the strength of youth forever—not even the giant-slayer.** David, who once felled a giant with a sling and a stone, now staggered from exhaustion in combat and nearly died before Abishai stepped into the gap (21:15–17). God never rests the fate of His people on one weary individual's shoulders; He raises up a new generation of faithful servants to share the burden with the king (21:18–22).

## Deeper Meaning

**Joshua's ancient oath echoes right into David's reign.** The opening of chapter 21 reaches all the way back to Israel's earliest days in Canaan. Back then, the Gibeonites had tricked Joshua and the elders into signing a peace treaty by pretending to be envoys from a distant country. Even after discovering the trick, Israel's leaders refused to harm them because they had sworn by the LORD God of Israel, warning that breaking the oath would bring God's wrath upon the whole nation (Joshua 9:15–20). Saul's violation turned that ancient warning into grim reality: three years of famine descended because unatoned blood defiled the land (Numbers 35:33).

**Fidelity to Jonathan's covenant stands in sharp contrast to Saul's betrayal.** Right inside an episode exposing the terrible cost of Saul's broken oath, Scripture places David's faithfulness to Mephibosheth (21:7). Years earlier, David and Jonathan had sworn before the LORD to show steadfast kindness to each other's descendants forever (1 Samuel 20:14–16; 20:42), and David had welcomed his friend's crippled son to dine permanently at the royal table (9:1–7). By shielding Mephibosheth amid the purge of Saul's household, David showed that covenant love outlasts every political storm.

**The final four chapters of 2 Samuel form a deliberate symmetrical frame.** Once the rebellion cycle wraps up in chapter 20, chapters 21–24 abandon strict chronology and arrange six blocks of material in mirrored pairs to sum up David's entire reign. Chapter 21 opens with a national calamity caused by Saul's sin and closes with God answering prayer for the land (21:14), pairing directly with chapter 24's plague caused by David's sin and its closing line that God answered prayer and halted the plague (24:25). Inside that outer shell, the giant battles at the close of chapter 21 (21:15–22) mirror the roster of mighty men in chapter 23, framing the twin poetic centerpieces of David's song in chapter 22 and his last words in early chapter 23.

**The long shadow of Mount Gilboa finally finds closure.** The bodies of Saul and Jonathan had once suffered the disgrace of hanging on the wall of Beth-shan before the brave men of Jabesh-gilead risked their lives to retrieve them and bury them temporarily under a tamarisk tree (1 Samuel 31:8–13). When David personally brings their bones home to rest alongside their seven kinsmen in the family tomb in Benjamin (21:12–14), it serves as a final gesture of reconciliation with the former dynasty. Tellingly, Scripture notes that God answered prayer for the land only *after* this compassionate burial—not immediately upon the executions.

**"The lamp of Israel" and the light of David's house.** When David's men call him "the lamp of Israel" (21:17), they invoke an image tightly bound to God's covenant promise for his dynasty. Later, even when David's descendants stumbled into sin and the kingdom split in two, God kept "a lamp" burning for David in Jerusalem (1 Kings 11:36; 15:4) and promised to prepare a lamp for His anointed (Psalms 132:17). In the very next chapter, David himself redirects that image to its true Source—"You are my lamp, O LORD" (22:29)—pointing toward the light that shines in fullness in Christ, the Son of David.

## Notes & Nuances

**Executing seven descendants of Saul (21:6–9) is one of the hardest passages in the book.** The Law of Moses clearly forbade putting children to death for their fathers' sins (Deuteronomy 24:16) and prohibited leaving an executed body hanging overnight lest it defile the land (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). Why then did this happen, and why did the famine end afterward? Commentators weigh several angles. Many point out that God told David only the *cause* of the famine (21:1); the demand to execute seven descendants came from the Gibeonites—a non-Israelite group outside Mosaic law—and Scripture never says God instructed David to grant it. Others wonder whether those adult members of Saul's "blood-stained house" (21:1) had participated directly in the earlier Gibeonite slaughter, or whether David yielded to ancient notions of collective blood-guilt while conveniently removing rivals from Saul's dynasty—which would also explain Shimei's curse calling him a "man of bloodshed" (16:7–8) if these events occurred earlier. However one reads David's motives, the text places Rizpah's heartbreaking vigil at the moral center of the scene and records God's mercy returning only after the dead receive an honorable burial (21:14).

**Why does the Hebrew text of 21:8 read "five sons of Michal" when 6:23 says she had no children?** Looking back to 1 Samuel, it was Michal's older sister Merab who was given in marriage to Adriel of Meholah (1 Samuel 18:19), while Michal died childless (6:23). Most scholars recognize an early scribal slip in the Hebrew text where the less familiar name "Merab" was accidentally copied as the much better-known "Michal" (which is why some manuscripts and modern translations—including the Berean Standard Bible—render the name "Merab" here). A classic Jewish rabbinic explanation instead suggests that Merab bore the five boys and died young, and Michal raised them as her own.

**Why does the Hebrew of 21:19 say Elhanan killed Goliath when David killed Goliath?** In 1 Samuel 17, David famously slew Goliath the Gittite, whose spear shaft was like a weaver's beam (1 Samuel 17:4–7; 17:49–51). Comparing the parallel verse in Chronicles clarifies the wording: Elhanan killed "Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite" (1 Chronicles 20:5), which translations like the Berean Standard Bible incorporate here. Many textual scholars point out that in the Hebrew of 2 Samuel 21:19, the words for "the Bethlehemite" (*beit ha-lachmi*) and "brother of" / "Lahmi" (*et lachmi*) look strikingly similar and could easily have been blurred or dropped by an early copyist. Some critical scholars propose the reverse hypothesis—that an earlier exploit by Elhanan was later transferred to the famous king David, with Chronicles harmonizing the tension by adding "the brother of." Both views are widely noted in biblical scholarship.

**When did the events of chapter 21 take place?** The Gibeonite famine most likely occurred fairly early in David's reign, prior to Absalom's revolt—which helps explain why Shimei, a kinsman of Saul, cursed David during his flight as a "man of bloodshed" guilty of the blood of Saul's house (16:7–8). By contrast, the Philistine battle in which David grew too weary to defend himself (21:15–17) clearly occurred later as the king aged. The author of Samuel gathered these episodes into the closing appendix for theological symmetry rather than strict chronology after chapter 20.

**Four giants of the line of Rapha (21:16–22).** "Rapha" (plural *Rephaim*) is an Old Testament term for unusually tall peoples who once inhabited regions of Canaan, a remnant of whom settled in the Philistine city of Gath. The twenty-four fingers and toes noted in 21:20 describe a real congenital condition known as polydactyly, regarded in the ancient world as a fearsome, uncanny mark of a giant warrior.

**Josephus**, first-century Jewish historian, recounts the three-year famine as a national crisis driving David to consult the prophets for God's mind. **The Babylonian Talmud**, classic Jewish compendium of law and exegesis compiled in the fifth and sixth centuries, examines this controversial passage in depth. The sages ask why the seven bodies were left exposed for months despite Deuteronomy's ban on leaving corpses hanging overnight. They answer that a single burial precept was temporarily set aside so God's name might be sanctified: whenever passersby saw royal princes hanging and asked why, they learned it was because their father had violated an oath to resident aliens—striking awe of God's justice into the nations. At the same time, the sages fault the Gibeonites for refusing mercy, noting they lacked Israel's three defining traits: compassion, modesty, and lovingkindness. *(Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 79a)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall the two famous arguments in Yevamot 79a regarding sanctifying God's name through the prolonged exposure and Israel's three traits, exact wording not verified -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, treats the Gibeonite affair in his sermons on 2 Samuel as a sobering lesson on the sacredness of oaths. **Matthew Henry**, English minister who died in 1714, advises that when prolonged calamities like a multi-year famine strike, the wisest first step is to seek God and ask whether unresolved sin lies underneath. Viewing David's sudden fatigue in battle and rescue by Abishai, Henry draws a humbling comfort: even God's greatest servants grow faint at times, and the Lord always provides timely helpers to uphold them when their strength runs dry. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, 2 Samuel chapter 21)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Matthew Henry's characteristic remarks on chapter 21, exact wording not verified -->

**Walter Brueggemann**, American Old Testament scholar, reads chapter 21 as the opening panel of the book's four-chapter symmetrical epilogue. While noting the troubling political ambiguity surrounding David's handover of seven Saulide heirs, Brueggemann argues that the true human center of the chapter belongs to Rizpah's silent, stubborn vigil on the rock. In his reading, that powerless mother's agonizing fidelity unsettles royal politics and compels the compassionate burial through which God's wrath finally subsides. *(First and Second Samuel, Interpretation, 1990)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Brueggemann's emphasis on Rizpah's central role and David's political ambiguity in the Interpretation commentary, exact page numbers not verified -->

**Robert Alter**, Jewish-American scholar of biblical literature, pauses over two contrasting strokes of narrative artistry here. In the first half, Rizpah's vigil is rendered in tight, unadorned prose without a single spoken lament, producing one of the most haunting images in the Old Testament. In the second half, the blunt admission that David "grew exhausted" before a giant of Rapha reveals an author who refuses to mythologize his hero: the nimble youth who once felled Goliath is now aging and frail, handing the spotlight to the warriors around him. *(The David Story, 1999)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: clearly recall Alter's literary observations on Rizpah's vigil and David's aging frailty in chapter 21 of The David Story, exact page numbers not verified -->
