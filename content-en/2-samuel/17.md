# 2 Samuel 17 — Two Counsels in the Palace and the Night Crossing of the Jordan

## Story

Having marched into Jerusalem and followed Ahithophel's advice to disgrace his father's concubines publicly on the palace roof to burn every bridge (16:20–22), Absalom sits in command while David and his exhausted company pause outside to catch their breath (16:14). Determined not to give the old king a moment to regroup, Ahithophel proposes a lightning strike: let me handpick twelve thousand men and ride out after David tonight. I will overtake him while he is "weary and discouraged," throw his band into panic so everyone flees, and strike down only the king himself. With David dead, the entire nation will return smoothly in peace to Absalom. Sharp and surgical, the plan wins instant approval from Absalom and all Israel's elders (17:1–4).

Yet Absalom hesitates just long enough to ask for a second opinion: summon Hushai the Archite—David's longtime friend who just pledged allegiance—and hear what he thinks. Listening to Ahithophel's blueprint, Hushai shakes his head: "This time the advice Ahithophel has given is not good." Masterfully he plays on Absalom's lingering dread of his father's battlefield legend: you know your father and his warriors are fierce—"as enraged as a wild bear robbed of her cubs." A seasoned combat veteran, David will never sleep out among his troops; by now he is tucked safely inside some cave or hideout. If your vanguard takes early casualties, word will flash that Absalom's men suffered slaughter, and even the bravest soldier with a lion's heart will melt in terror (17:5–10).

Having dismantled his rival's strategy, Hushai paints a far grander tableau: summon all Israel from Dan to Beersheba, vast as sand on the seashore, with Absalom himself riding heroically at their head! That colossal host will blanket David "like dew falling on the ground," leaving not one survivor; and if he ducks inside any fortified city, all Israel will wrap ropes around its walls and drag it straight down into the valley until not a pebble remains. Spellbound, Absalom and every man of Israel declare Hushai's counsel far superior to Ahithophel's. Right here, Scripture pulls back the curtain so readers glimpse the unseen hand steering everything: "For the LORD had purposed to thwart the good counsel of Ahithophel in order to bring disaster on Absalom" (17:11–14).

Even after winning the council room, Hushai takes zero chances in case Absalom shifts back. Quickly he briefs the priests Zadok and Abiathar on both counsels and urges them to rush word to David not to spend the night at the wilderness fords, but to cross over immediately lest the king and everyone with him be swallowed up (17:15–16).

The covert relay kicks into motion. The priests' two sons, Jonathan and Ahimaaz, stay hidden outside the walls at the spring of En-rogel so nobody spots them entering town; a servant girl quietly slips out to deliver the message. Unfortunately a young boy notices them and runs straight to tell Absalom. Sprinting for Bahurim, the two couriers duck into a sympathetic citizen's courtyard and drop down inside a dry well. Thinking on her feet, the homeowner's wife spreads a cloth over the well's mouth and scatters crushed grain across it to erase every trace. Moments later Absalom's pursuers burst in demanding where Ahimaaz and Jonathan went. Calmly she points them off course, saying they already crossed the brook. Sweeping the area empty-handed, the guards trudge back to Jerusalem (17:17–20).

Once the coast clears, Ahimaaz and Jonathan scramble out of the well, race to David, relay Ahithophel's strike plan, and urge him across the river immediately. Instantly David rouses his entire camp and pushes everyone across the Jordan under cover of night. By daybreak, not a single soul remains on the western bank (17:21–22).

Back inside Jerusalem, brilliant Ahithophel reads the writing on the wall instantly. Missing tonight's window loses the war; David will rally troops and crush the revolt. Seeing his counsel rejected, the famous strategist saddles his donkey, rides home to his hometown, puts his household affairs carefully in order, and hangs himself. He is buried in his father's tomb (17:23).

Thanks to that midnight crossing, David brings his entire company safely into Mahanaim east of the Jordan. Later Absalom completes his massive muster, crosses the river into Gilead, and appoints Amasa—Joab's cousin and David's nephew—as army commander in place of Joab (17:24–26). Right when David's road-weary band hits exhaustion at Mahanaim, three unexpected friends arrive bearing relief: Shobi son of Nahash the Ammonite from Rabbah, Machir of Lo-debar, and elderly Barzillai the Gileadite from Rogelim. Seeing the troops "hungry, weary, and thirsty in the wilderness," they bring beds, bowls, pottery, wheat, barley, flour, roasted grain, beans, lentils, honey, curds, sheep, and cheese from the herd (17:27–29).

## Key Takeaways

**The sharpest human brilliance cannot outmaneuver God's sovereign purpose.** People revered Ahithophel's advice like God's own oracle (16:23), and militarily his midnight strike plan was airtight. Yet one moment of second-guessing by Absalom dissolved every tactical edge because God's unseen hand steered the council chamber (17:14).

**God routinely works deliverance through quiet, unheralded people.** That night the fate of David's kingdom hinged not on fire falling from heaven, but on Hushai's steady nerve, an unnamed servant girl's bravery, two young runners dashing through the dark, and a quick-thinking housewife spreading grain across a well in Bahurim (17:17–20). In God's hands, small courageous acts reshape history.

**Vanity prefers flattering fantasy over sharp truth.** Ahithophel's plan was brisk and deadly—yet Ahithophel would lead the charge and win the glory. Hushai's plan crawled slowly with huge openings, yet starred Absalom gloriously commanding a seashore-sized host pulling whole cities down with ropes (17:11–13). Ego-hungry leaders walk willingly into traps built from their own pride.

**Kindness planted in quiet years returns as shelter in the storm.** David fled Jerusalem empty-handed, hunted by his own son and stoned along the road (16:5–13). Yet across the Jordan, an Ammonite prince, a former protector of Saul's grandson, and an elderly Gileadite spread a banquet before him (17:27–29). Right where human loyalty looks vanished, God keeps generous tables waiting.

## Deeper Meaning

**One desperate prayer flips an entire civil war.** Climbing the Mount of Olives upon learning Ahithophel defected, David managed just one breathless plea: "O LORD, please turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness" (15:31), before sending Hushai back into town (15:32–37). Chapter 17 stamps heaven's direct answer right across the page (17:14). Proverbs sums up the lesson crisply: many plans sit in a human heart, yet the LORD's purpose prevails; neither wisdom nor insight nor counsel can stand against the LORD (Proverbs 19:21; 21:30).

**The housewife of Bahurim echoes Rahab of Jericho.** A courageous woman hiding two Israelite couriers inside a well, cloaking them under spread grain, and sending royal pursuers down the wrong road (17:18–20) mirrors Rahab hiding Joshua's two spies beneath stalks of flax on her roof and misdirecting Jericho's guards (Joshua 2:4–6). Centuries apart, both scenes spotlight how God repeatedly safeguards redemptive history through quick-witted, overlooked women.

**Ahithophel casts a dark shadow toward Judas Iscariot.** Ahithophel—David's trusted inner adviser—betrays God's anointed king and ends his life by hanging once his plot unravels (17:23). A thousand years later, Judas Iscariot—intimate companion betraying Jesus, David's greater Son—likewise takes his own life by hanging (Matthew 27:3–5). From the early Church onward, readers have seen Judas foreshadowed in Ahithophel, linking both with David's anguished psalms mourning a close table friend who turned against him (Psalms 41:9; 55:12–14).

**Sleep in the middle of danger and a feast in the wilderness.** Traditional headings assign Psalm 3 directly to David's flight from Absalom. Ringed by enemies taunting that God has abandoned him, David lies down and sleeps safely knowing the LORD lifts his head as his shield (Psalms 3:1–8). Arriving spent at Mahanaim to discover three friends laying out beds, grain, honey, and sheep (17:27–29) turns that psalm's quiet confidence into living bread and shelter.

**Bonds of grace crossing old battle lines.** Look closely at those three rescuers at chapter's end. Mahanaim itself had served as Ish-bosheth's rival capital (2:8), and Machir of Lo-debar had sheltered crippled Mephibosheth before David welcomed him to Jerusalem's table (9:4–5). Shobi was the son of Nahash of Ammon—the king whose kindness David had sought to repay before Hanun's folly sparked war (10:1–2). David's past faithfulness now draws former rivals and foreigners alike to sustain him in exile.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why did Ahithophel hate David fiercely enough to urge total humiliation and swift assassination?** Scripture never spells out his inner motive directly, yet two scattered genealogical clues point straight to a stunning answer. The mighty men roster names "Eliam son of Ahithophel the Gilonite" (23:34), while chapter 11 identifies Bathsheba as "the daughter of Eliam" (11:3). If those Eliams are one person, Ahithophel was Bathsheba's grandfather—making his ruthless zeal look very much like long-simmering vengeance for David violating his granddaughter and murdering her husband Uriah.

**How should we read Ahithophel's suicide?** Verse 17:23 records one of the very few suicides in Scripture. Far from a chaotic panic, Ahithophel methodically rides home, puts his estate in order, and hangs himself—clear-eyed enough to see David will soon win and hang the chief traitor anyway. The narrator reports it with sober restraint without extra moralizing, noting he received burial in his family tomb (ancient Israel did not bar burial). Placed inside the larger arc, it stands not as an endorsement, but as the desolate end of brilliant pride pitted against God.

**Were Hushai's and the Bahurim woman's deceptions sinful?** Both Hushai and the Bahurim housewife knowingly misled enemies to save David and his messengers (17:7–13; 17:19–20). Across Christian ethics, two classic views prevail. One—championed by Augustine—maintains every falsehood remains intrinsically wrong, noting Scripture records human acts candidly without endorsing every tactic. The other distinguishes selfish lying from wartime deception protecting innocent lives from unjust killers, grouping these acts beside the Hebrew midwives in Egypt and Rahab at Jericho.

**Amasa's tangled family tree in 17:25.** Here Amasa's father is named Ithra "an Israelite," married to Abigail daughter of Nahash and sister of Zeruiah. Yet 1 Chronicles calls his father "Jether the Ishmaelite," listing Abigail and Zeruiah as David's own sisters (1 Chronicles 2:16–17). Because ancient Hebrew spellings for *Israelite* and *Ishmaelite* look virtually identical, most scholars suspect either a tiny copying slip in 2 Samuel or an Ishmaelite immigrant absorbed into Israel. As for "Nahash," he was likely either another name for Jesse or an earlier husband of David's mother—making Abigail and Zeruiah David's half-sisters. Either way, the heartbreaking point shines clear: the two opposing commanders-in-chief—Joab and Amasa—were first cousins!
