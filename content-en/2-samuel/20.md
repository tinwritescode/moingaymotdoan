# 2 Samuel 20 — Sheba's Rebellion and the Wise Woman of Abel

## Story

No sooner had the dispute between Judah and the ten tribes of Israel flared up beside the Jordan than an opportunist tossed a match into the dry grass. A wicked Benjaminite named Sheba son of Bichri stepped forward, blew a ram's horn, and shouted, "We have no share in David, no inheritance in Jesse's son! Every man to his tent, O Israel!" At the sound of that trumpet, all the men of Israel deserted David and marched off behind Sheba. Only the men of Judah stayed fiercely loyal to their king, escorting him all the way from the Jordan back to Jerusalem (20:1–2).

The moment David returned to his palace in Jerusalem, he dealt with the ten concubines he had left behind to care for the house—the women Absalom had publicly violated. The king placed them in a separate house under guard and supplied all their needs, yet never went near them again. Those ten women lived out their lives in confinement, "living as widows until the day of their death" (20:3).

David then turned to confront the fresh revolt. Summoning his newly appointed commander Amasa, he ordered him to rally the men of Judah and report back within three days. Amasa set out to muster the troops, yet stayed longer than the appointed time. Growing anxious that Sheba might seize fortified cities and inflict even worse damage than Absalom, David refused to wait any longer. He ordered Abishai—Joab's brother—to take the royal guard, including Joab's men, the Cherethites, the Pelethites, and all the mighty warriors, and march out from Jerusalem at once to pursue Sheba (20:4–7).

When the column reached the great stone at Gibeon, Amasa came out to meet them. Joab was marching in the ranks wearing his battle tunic with a strapped dagger hanging at his hip; as he strode forward, the sword slipped from its sheath. Greeting his cousin warmly—"Are you well, my brother?"—Joab reached out with his right hand to take Amasa by the beard as if to kiss him in traditional greeting. Amasa paid no attention to the blade in Joab's left hand. With one sudden thrust into the belly, Joab spilled his entrails onto the ground; Amasa died on the spot, needing no second blow. Joab and his brother Abishai pressed straight on after Sheba, leaving one of Joab's young men beside the corpse shouting, "Whoever favors Joab, and whoever is for David, let him follow Joab!" Seeing marching soldiers stop dead in their tracks at the sight of Amasa wallowing in blood on the highway, the attendant dragged the body off the road into a field and threw a garment over it. With the road cleared, the entire army marched on behind Joab (20:8–13).

Sheba passed through all the tribes of Israel northward until he took refuge inside the fortified town of Abel-beth-maacah. Joab's forces gathered and pursued him all the way there, laid tight siege to the city, piled up a siege ramp against the outer rampart, and began battering the base of the wall to bring it crashing down (20:14–15).

Just as the city faced total ruin, "a wise woman" called out from the top of the wall, asking to summon Joab close enough to talk. When the general approached and confirmed his name, she calmly cited an old proverb celebrating the town's wisdom: whenever a hard question arose, people said, "Ask counsel at Abel," and the matter was settled. Straightforwardly she challenged him: Abel was among the most peaceful and faithful cities in Israel—"a mother in Israel"—so why was he trying to swallow up "the inheritance of the LORD"? Stung by the charge, Joab protested immediately, "Far be it, far be it from me to swallow up or destroy!" He explained that he wanted only one man, Sheba from the hill country of Ephraim, who had rebelled against King David; hand over that one man and the siege would lift immediately. The woman answered firmly, "His head will be thrown to you over the wall." Turning back to the townspeople with persuasive wisdom, she convinced the whole city; they promptly cut off Sheba's head and tossed it over the wall to Joab. Joab blew the trumpet to lift the siege, the troops dispersed to their homes, and Joab returned to the king in Jerusalem (20:16–22).

The chapter closes with a roster of David's restructured administration after the turmoil: Joab once again commanded the entire army of Israel; Benaiah son of Jehoiada commanded the Cherethites and Pelethites; Adoram was placed over forced labor; Jehoshaphat served as recorder; Sheva served as secretary; Zadok and Abiathar served as priests; and Ira the Jairite served as David's personal priest and close counselor (20:23–26).

## Key Takeaways

**Internal cracks always invite opportunists to sow division.** It took only one wicked man blowing a trumpet and tapping into the simmering pride of the northern tribes to pull a huge crowd away from the king into a fresh revolt (20:1–2). When brothers refuse to mend a rift early, a single spark is enough to burn the whole house down.

**Lust for power turns kinship into camouflage for cruelty.** Joab addressed Amasa as "brother," gripped his beard as if to kiss him, and gutted his own cousin in full view of the army to reclaim his post as commander (20:9–10; 20:23). Once power becomes the only god a person worships, no moral line and no family bond can hold back their hand.

**A single wise voice can save more lives than an army of swords.** Trapped beneath a ruthless general and soldiers hammering down the walls, an unnamed woman of Abel wielded neither blade nor force—only poise, fairness, and devotion to peace—and rescued an entire city (20:16–22).

**Behind power struggles, the vulnerable often pay the longest price.** While kings and generals maneuvered to hold onto authority, ten innocent concubines paid for the crisis with the rest of their lives locked away in lonely widowhood (20:3).

## Deeper Meaning

**Joab's treacherous kiss stands as a dark emblem of hypocrisy.** The way Joab murdered Amasa repeats almost step for step how he had once drawn Abner aside privately in the gate and stabbed him in the stomach (3:27). Both times, Joab used a gesture of warm fellowship to ambush an unsuspecting rival. Across Scripture, twisting a sign of affection into an instrument of betrayal reaches its most painful climax in Judas's kiss against Jesus in Gethsemane (Luke 22:47–48).

**Joab's blood-debt is never forgotten by God's justice.** Amid the chaos, David felt powerless to stop Joab from stepping right back into supreme military command (20:23). Yet God's justice kept a clear ledger: on his deathbed, David cited those exact two murders—Abner and Amasa, shedding the blood of war in a time of peace—and charged Solomon to deal with him justly, whereupon Benaiah, the very man listed right after Joab in verse 23, carried out the sentence (1 Kings 2:5–6; 2:31–34).

**Sheba's slogan and Adoram's new office foreshadow the nation's breakup.** The secessionist cry at the chapter's opening (20:1) matches almost syllable for syllable what the ten northern tribes will shout two generations later under Rehoboam when they permanently break away from the house of David (1 Kings 12:16). Furthermore, comparing the cabinet list at the end of chapter 20 (20:23–26) with the list from David's golden years in chapter 8 (8:16–18) reveals one brand-new post: Adoram in charge of forced labor. That crushing burden of forced labor would breed such deep resentment that on the very day the kingdom split, the Israelites stoned Adoram to death (1 Kings 12:18).

**The woman of Abel awakens God's law of peace.** The Law of Moses strictly required Israel to offer terms of peace before laying siege to any city (Deuteronomy 20:10–12). Joab had brushed that command aside and begun tearing down the walls of a city belonging to God's own people. An unnamed woman courageously reminded him of "the inheritance of the LORD" (20:19) to halt a senseless massacre—vividly echoing Ecclesiastes' portrait of the humble wise person who saves a besieged city yet whose name nobody remembers (Ecclesiastes 9:14–16).

**Ten concubines and the long shadow of sin in David's house.** The quiet tragedy of ten women confined until death (20:3) ties straight back to the stain left when David fled Jerusalem, leaving them behind to keep the palace where Absalom publicly violated them in sight of all Israel (15:16; 16:21–22). Exactly as the prophet Nathan warned, sin's fallout does not vanish when the fighting stops; its scars linger for years across the lives of people caught in the blast.

## Notes & Nuances

**David's treatment of the ten concubines (20:3) is deeply troubling to read.** Modern readers rightly ache at the unfairness of ten innocent women—already victimized by Absalom as pawns in a political power play (16:21–22)—being shut away in a separate house to live as widows for the rest of their days. Under ancient Near Eastern royal custom and law, a king could neither resume relations with concubines publicly claimed by a rival nor release them to marry outsiders, since royal concubines were tied directly to claims on the throne. That David continued to house and support them rather than cast them out reflected ancient convention, yet the text narrates their lifelong confinement in a bleak, subdued tone rather than praising it, letting readers feel the heavy human cost of polygamy and civil war.

**Why did Amasa miss the three-day deadline (20:5)?** Scripture does not state the reason. Many commentators suggest that the men of Judah were exhausted from civil war and distrusted Amasa—who just days earlier had commanded Absalom's rebel army—making recruitment sluggish. Others think Amasa lacked Joab's prestige and organizational skill, or that David's three-day window was unrealistically tight. Whatever the cause, the delay cost Amasa his chance and handed Joab the opening to seize back command.

**Was David powerless to control Joab?** Looking closely at 20:6–7, David pointedly assigns the pursuit of Sheba to Abishai without even naming Joab, proving the king still wanted Joab sidelined. Yet the elite fighting corps remained fiercely tied to Joab ("Joab's men"), and the instant Joab cut down Amasa on the road, the whole army fell smoothly into line behind him (20:10–13). Seeing Joab brazenly seated back atop the army roster at the chapter's close (20:23) shows that after two consecutive revolts, David lacked the political strength to remove so formidable a general, biding his time until Solomon could settle the account (1 Kings 2:5–6).

**The rich meaning of "a mother in Israel" (20:19) and "David's priest" (20:26).** In 20:19, the Hebrew text calls Abel "a city that is a mother in Israel"—a tender image for an ancient, prominent mother-city that nurtured and sheltered surrounding daughter-villages (which some translations paraphrase simply as a "chief city"). Meanwhile, in 20:26, the Berean Standard Bible literally renders Ira the Jairite's title as "David's priest"—using the same Hebrew word applied earlier to David's own sons (8:18), which other versions render "chief minister" or "royal counselor." Many scholars understand the role as a personal royal chaplain or confidential religious adviser to the king, distinct from the official high priests Zadok and Abiathar.
