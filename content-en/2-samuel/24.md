# 2 Samuel 24 — The Census and the Threshing Floor of Araunah

## Story

The closing chapter of 2 Samuel opens under a heavy cloud: "Again the anger of the LORD burned against Israel," and He incited David against them to "go and take a census of Israel and Judah." The king summoned commander Joab and ordered him to traverse every tribe "from Dan to Beersheba" and count the fighting men so David would know the exact total. Surprisingly, it was Joab—normally iron-fisted and pragmatic—who stepped up to object. Wishing that the LORD might multiply the king's people a hundredfold before his very eyes, he asked point-blank why the king took delight in such a venture. Yet the king's word overruled Joab and the army officers; reluctantly, they set out to carry out the census (24:1–4).

The officers crossed the Jordan, starting from Aroer and Jazer, swept into Gilead, looped far north to Dan-jaan, around Sidon and the fortress of Tyre, through the towns of the Hivites and Canaanites, and finally swung south to Beersheba. After "nine months and twenty days" traversing the entire kingdom, they returned to Jerusalem. Joab handed David the tally: Israel counted "eight hundred thousand valiant men who drew the sword," and Judah counted "five hundred thousand" (24:5–9).

Yet the moment those numbers sat in his hands, David's "conscience struck him" sharply. Without waiting for any prophet to confront him, he dropped before the LORD, confessed that he had sinned greatly and "acted very foolishly," and begged God to take away the guilt of His servant (24:10).

Early the next morning, as David rose, the word of the LORD came to the prophet Gad, David's seer, instructing him to lay three punishments before the king to choose from: seven years of famine across the land, three months of fleeing before pursuing enemies, or three days of plague sweeping the nation. Gad told him to think carefully so he could bring an answer back to the One who sent him. Torn with anguish, David replied that he was in deep distress, yet asked to fall into the hands of the LORD, "for His mercies are great," rather than fall into the hands of human beings (24:11–14).

So the LORD sent a plague upon Israel from that morning until the appointed time. Across the entire land, "from Dan to Beersheba," seventy thousand people perished. Yet as the angel stretched out his hand toward Jerusalem to destroy it, the LORD relented concerning the calamity and commanded the destroying angel, "Enough! Withdraw your hand now." At that moment the angel stood right beside the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite. Seeing the angel striking down the people, David cried out to the LORD, taking all the blame upon himself: he alone had sinned and done wrong—"but these sheep, what have they done? Please let Your hand fall upon me and my father's house!" (24:15–17).

That very day, the prophet Gad returned to David with instructions to go up and build an altar to the LORD on Araunah's threshing floor. David obeyed at once. Looking up and seeing the king and his servants approaching, Araunah hurried out and bowed face down to the ground. Hearing that David wished to buy the threshing floor to erect an altar and stop the plague, Araunah generously offered to donate everything free of charge: the threshing floor, the oxen for burnt offerings, and the wooden threshing sledges and ox yokes for fuel, praying that the LORD would accept the king (24:18–23).

David firmly shook his head. Insisting on paying full silver price for every item, he spoke words that have echoed across the centuries: "I will not offer to the LORD my God burnt offerings that cost me nothing." The king purchased the threshing floor and the oxen for "fifty shekels of silver," built an altar there, and presented burnt offerings and peace offerings. "Then the LORD answered the prayers on behalf of the land, and the plague upon Israel was halted" (24:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Success's subtlest temptation is wanting to count our own strength.** After decades of sweeping victories, David wanted to see on paper how many hundreds of thousands of swords he commanded (24:2; 24:9). The sin lay not in bare arithmetic, but in a heart quietly shifting its trust from God to military manpower and imperial pride.

**Even in discipline, God's mercy runs deeper than human cruelty.** Faced with three terrible options, David did not try to dodge judgment; he chose to fall into God's hands, "for His mercies are great" (24:14). He knew well that vengeful humans never know when to stop, whereas God Himself cried "Enough!" and stayed the hand of judgment right outside Jerusalem's gate (24:16).

**A true shepherd steps forward to bear responsibility for the flock.** Grievous as David's blunder was, his finest quality shone through as his people fell: he blamed neither circumstances nor subordinates, but pleaded for judgment to strike himself and his own house rather than the sheep (24:17).

**Authentic worship is never something cheap that costs us nothing.** David could easily have accepted Araunah's generous offer and saved his own purse, yet he refused outright: he would not bring God sacrifices that demanded no sacrifice from himself (24:24). Anything that costs the heart nothing can scarcely be called a true offering.

## Deeper Meaning

**Araunah's threshing floor becomes the foundation of the Jerusalem Temple.** What looked like an ordinary Jebusite grain floor outside the city walls turns out, in 2 Chronicles, to be "Mount Moriah"—the exact ridge where Solomon later built the Temple of the LORD (2 Chronicles 3:1). Reaching even further back, Mount Moriah was the mountain where Abraham once laid his beloved son Isaac upon the altar, where the angel of the LORD likewise called a halt at the last second and provided a ram in his place, leaving the proverb "On the mountain of the LORD it will be provided" (Genesis 22:2; 22:11–14). The plague-stopping altar at the close of 2 Samuel knots a scarlet thread straight through the Old Testament: the very spot where wrath halts through sacrificial blood becomes the place where God chooses to dwell among His people.

**Negotiating with Araunah mirrors Abraham purchasing burial land.** Araunah the Jebusite's courteous offer to gift the threshing floor and David's firm insistence on paying full silver weight (24:21–24) closely reenact Abraham's ancient negotiation with Ephron the Hittite for the cave of Machpelah (Genesis 23:11–13). Both times, Israel's two most sacred plots of land—the patriarchal tomb and the Temple mount—are purchased fairly and openly from local residents rather than seized by royal force.

**Echoes of Passover and the Good Shepherd.** The destroying angel holding back his hand over Jerusalem as sacrifice rises (24:16; 24:25) recalls Passover night in Egypt, when the destroyer passed over every household marked by sacrificial blood (Exodus 12:23). Meanwhile, David's anguished plea to take the blow in place of "these sheep" (24:17) both anticipates and contrasts deeply with Jesus. David was a guilty shepherd offering himself for sheep innocent of this offense; Jesus is "the good shepherd" who "lays down His life for the sheep"—the utterly innocent Shepherd stepping voluntarily into judgment to save a wandering flock (John 10:11).

**The symmetrical frame of the book's final four chapters snaps cleanly shut.** Chapter 24 pairs point for point with chapter 21: the epilogue opened in chapter 21 with three years of famine stemming from Saul's sin (21:1) and closes here with three days of plague stemming from David's sin (24:13–15); chapter 21 ended with atonement followed by "God answered prayer in behalf of the land" (21:14), and chapter 24 seals both books of Samuel with the altar on the threshing floor and the verdict, "Then the LORD answered the prayers on behalf of the land, and the plague upon Israel was halted" (24:25). Even the prophet Gad—who had walked with David since his fugitive days in the forest of Hereth (1 Samuel 22:5)—returns here as a faithful witness closing out the king's long journey.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does 2 Samuel 24:1 say the LORD incited David, while 1 Chronicles 21:1 says Satan incited David?** This is one of the best-known textual comparisons in the entire Bible. Here, the LORD's anger burns against Israel and He incites David to take a census (24:1), whereas the parallel in Chronicles states, "Then Satan rose up against Israel and incited David to take a census of Israel" (1 Chronicles 21:1). Interpreters commonly highlight two complementary perspectives. Theologically, both verses describe the same event on two distinct levels of causation: at the ultimate level, nothing unfolds outside God's sovereign permission (as He withdraws restraining grace to judge existing sin in Israel); at the immediate level, Satan acts as malicious tempter and David freely chooses sin—which is why David instantly takes personal blame without ever faulting God (24:10). Historically, Chronicles was composed centuries later, when Israel's theological vocabulary more sharply distinguished God's overarching sovereignty from the adversary's active temptation, framing the account so readers would not imagine God directly tempting anyone into sin.

**What was so wrong with taking a census that seventy thousand people died?** Counting the people was not inherently forbidden—Moses conducted two divinely commanded censuses in Numbers. However, the Law of Moses required that whenever Israel was numbered, each person had to pay "a ransom for his life to the LORD" acknowledging that the people belonged to God rather than any human ruler, "so that no plague will come upon them while you are numbering them" (Exodus 30:11–12). Beyond likely omitting that ransom money, most commentators emphasize David's underlying motive: Joab's tally reports solely "valiant men who drew the sword" (24:9), showing David was measuring military might—for pride, taxation, or conscription—during peacetime rather than trusting God alone. That the whole nation suffered also connects directly to verse 1 (24:1): the LORD's anger was *already* burning against Israel over unspecified sins (many suspect linked to the rebellions of Absalom and Sheba), so the king's pride triggered a judgment already poised to fall.

**Numerical differences between 2 Samuel 24 and 1 Chronicles 21.** Placed side by side, three figures differ: the military tally here reads 800,000 in Israel and 500,000 in Judah (24:9) versus 1,100,000 and 470,000 in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 21:5); the proposed famine is seven years here (24:13) versus three years in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 21:12), which matches the neat rhythm of three years, three months, three days and is also supported by the ancient Greek Septuagint; and the purchase price is fifty shekels of silver here (24:24) versus six hundred shekels of gold in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 21:25). Commentators note that census totals may reflect different counting methods (such as including standing army divisions or omitting unfinished tribes), "seven years" likely arose from a copyist's slip for "three years," and fifty shekels of silver bought the immediate threshing floor and oxen while six hundred shekels of gold later covered the entire expansive hilltop site for the Temple complex.

**How should we understand "the LORD relented concerning the calamity" (24:16)?** Scripture is not suggesting that God blundered and regretted a mistake like a fallible human. In Old Testament idiom, saying the LORD "relented" expresses His deep compassion and sorrow over human suffering, moving Him to stay the hand of judgment and turn toward mercy once discipline has served its purpose.
