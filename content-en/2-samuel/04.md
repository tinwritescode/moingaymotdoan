# 2 Samuel 4 — The Murder of Ish-bosheth and David's Clean Hands

## Story

Hearing that Abner has fallen dead at Hebron, Saul's son Ish-bosheth instantly "lost courage," and all Israel plunges into alarm. Serving under Ish-bosheth are two captains of raiding bands, brothers named Baanah and Rechab, sons of Rimmon the Beerothite from the tribe of Benjamin. The narrator notes parenthetically that Beeroth is counted as part of Benjamin, even though its residents once fled to Gittaim and have lived there as resident aliens to this day (4:1–3).

Right as the northern court totters, the story pauses briefly to mention a vulnerable child in Saul's line. Jonathan son of Saul has a son crippled in both feet named Mephibosheth. He was five years old when word of Saul and Jonathan's deaths arrived from Jezreel; his nurse scooped him up and fled in panic, dropping him in her haste so that he remained lame for life (4:4).

Seeing Saul's house stripped of its defender, Rechab and Baanah decide to strike. In the blazing heat of midday while Ish-bosheth takes his noon rest inside his bedroom, the two brothers slip quietly into the center of the house as if fetching wheat. Catching him completely off guard, they stab him straight in the belly, slice off his head, and escape unseen. Racing through the Jordan valley road, they travel nonstop all night heading south (4:5–7).

Arriving in Hebron, the two traitors present Ish-bosheth's severed head before David and boast eagerly of their deed. Calling Ish-bosheth the son of Saul, David's enemy who hunted his life, they declare smoothly that today the LORD has avenged my lord the king against Saul and his offspring (4:8).

Greeting them, however, is neither gold nor promotion, but David's icy wrath. Swearing "as surely as the LORD lives, who has redeemed my life from every distress," David reminds them of the precedent back at Ziklag: when a messenger rushed in bringing word of Saul's death, "thinking he was bringing good news," David seized and executed him as the reward for his tidings. How much more now, when wicked men have murdered "a righteous man" in his own house upon his own bed! Firmly David announces: "Shall I not now require his blood from your hands and wipe you from the earth?" (4:9–11).

With a word, David orders his young men to execute Rechab and Baanah on the spot. They cut off the assassins' hands and feet and hang their bodies beside the pool in Hebron as a stark public warning. As for Ish-bosheth's head, David gives it respectful burial inside Abner's very tomb at Hebron (4:12).

## Key Takeaways

**Invoking God's name never turns a crime into righteousness.** Rechab and Baanah stabbed a sleeping man in bed and shamelessly credited the LORD with avenging David (4:8). People constantly drape religious labels over shabby, self-serving acts, yet God never needs bloody hands to deliver His promises.

**Trust in God keeps us off dirty shortcuts.** David's oath—by "the LORD who has redeemed my life from every distress" (4:9)—reveals the secret of his steadiness. Having watched God rescue him personally through every dark valley, he needed no traitor's dagger to climb onto the throne.

**True justice never bends for political convenience.** Ish-bosheth had sat on a rival throne and ruled feebly for two years. Yet facing cowardly murder, David openly honored him as "a righteous man" and gave him dignified burial (4:11–12). Upholding fairness even for a fallen rival proves a ruler's true character.

**Power struggles wound the most innocent bystanders deepest.** Amid endless palace intrigue, one brief verse about five-year-old Mephibosheth dropped in terror and lamed for life (4:4) tugs straight at the heart. Kings battle over crowns while an innocent child carries lifelong scars.

## Deeper Meaning

**An ancient grievance buried in Beeroth's history.** Why does the narrator pause to note that the two killers hailed from Beeroth and had fled to Gittaim (4:2–3)? In Joshua, Beeroth was one of four Gibeonite towns bound to Israel by a solemn peace treaty (Joshua 9:17). Later 2 Samuel reveals that Saul in misplaced zeal violated that oath and slaughtered the Gibeonites (2 Samuel 21:1–2). Many commentators connect the dots: Saul's purge may have driven Beeroth's residents into exile at Gittaim, planting a deep grudge that drove Rechab and Baanah to take savage payback on Saul's son.

**Planting the cornerstone for a coming story of grace.** Dropping Mephibosheth's story in 4:4 right inside Ish-bosheth's murder looks like an interruption at first glance, yet serves a masterly design. With Ish-bosheth slain, Saul's royal line virtually ends, clearing David's way. Quietly the text notes one surviving heir of Jonathan—lame and no threat to any throne, yet poised to receive David's full covenant kindness honoring his old oath to Jonathan (1 Samuel 20:14–16; 2 Samuel 9:6–7).

**Three straight refusals to buy the throne with blood.** Rechab and Baanah claiming God avenged David's enemy (4:8) echo both David's soldiers urging him to kill Saul in En-gedi's cave (1 Samuel 24:4–7) and the Amalekite runner opening this book (4:10; cf. 1:10). From damp desert cave to the doorstep of unified kingship, David held one unwavering line: taking the LORD's name in vain to whitewash murder is something God will never tolerate (Exodus 20:7).

**Purging innocent blood from the land.** Declaring he must require Ish-bosheth's blood and wipe the killers from the earth (4:11), David enforces God's foundational justice from Noah through Moses: whoever sheds innocent blood must answer so blood-guilt does not pollute the land (Genesis 9:5–6; Numbers 35:33). Unlike chapter 3, where David felt too weak to punish Joab (3:39), here he executes swift, decisive justice immediately.

**A lifelong confession carried to David's final days.** The solemn vow in 4:9—"As surely as the LORD lives, who has redeemed my life from every distress"—was no rhetorical flourish. Decades later, frail on his deathbed amid Adonijah's coup attempt, David repeats virtually those exact words to testify to God's unchanging faithfulness across his entire life (1 Kings 1:29).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was severing the assassins' hands and feet overly brutal (4:12)?** Hanging mutilated corpses beside Hebron's pool shocks modern readers. Across the ancient Near East, this served as starkly symbolic public deterrence: cutting off the hands that stabbed their sleeping master and the feet that raced all night chasing reward, displayed beside the public water source so every citizen saw the incoming king utterly repudiated treason. Under Moses' law, executed bodies displayed publicly had to be taken down and buried before nightfall lest they defile the land (Deuteronomy 21:22–23).

**Why does Ish-bosheth's murder seem told twice across verses 6 and 7?** Reading 4:6–7 closely, verse 6 already states the brothers entered, stabbed Ish-bosheth in the belly, and escaped, while verse 7 repeats their entering the bedroom, killing him, and taking his head. Hebrew narrative frequently summarizes an event first before zooming back in to give vivid detail. Notably, the ancient Greek Septuagint preserves a helpful detail in verse 6: the female doorkeeper cleaning wheat grew drowsy in the midday heat and fell asleep, explaining how Rechab and Baanah slipped past unseen.

**Mephibosheth and "Merib-baal."** Just like his uncle Ish-bosheth, Jonathan's son appears under his original name "Merib-baal" in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 8:34). Later scribes copying Samuel avoided pronouncing the idol *Baal* by swapping the ending to *bosheth* ("shame").

**Was David truly spotless or simply lucky in politics?** Skeptical modern critics sometimes eye chapters 1–4 suspiciously: every rival blocking David's path falls conveniently by someone else's blade while David collects the prize with clean hands. Remember, however, that this exact same narrator does not hide a shred of David's repulsive adultery and murder of Uriah just seven chapters later (11:1–27). An author candid enough to expose his hero's ugliest sin had zero motive to cover up assassinations here.
