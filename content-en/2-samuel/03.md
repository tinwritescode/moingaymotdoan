# 2 Samuel 3 — Abner's Defection and Murder at Hebron's Gate

## Story

The civil war between the house of David and the house of Saul drags on. Yet the momentum tilts steadily one way: "David grew stronger and stronger, while the house of Saul grew weaker and weaker." During his years based in Hebron, David's household expands rapidly. Six sons are born to him by six different wives: firstborn Amnon by Ahinoam of Jezreel; second, Chileab by Abigail, widow of Nabal; third, Absalom by Maacah, daughter of Talmai king of Geshur; fourth, Adonijah by Haggith; fifth, Shephatiah by Abital; and sixth, Ithream by his wife Eglah (3:1–5).

Throughout those years of conflict, Abner remains the pillar propping up Saul's fragile dynasty. Then the northern court cracks wide open over a single accusation. Saul had left behind a concubine named Rizpah daughter of Aiah, and Ish-bosheth challenges Abner: why have you slept with my father's concubine? Abner erupts in fury: "Am I a dog's head on Judah's side?" After spending every ounce of loyalty guarding Saul's house and keeping Ish-bosheth out of David's grip, how dare the king charge him with an offense over a woman! Boiling with rage, Abner swears a solemn oath to fulfill everything the LORD promised David: transferring the kingdom from Saul's house to establish David's throne over Israel and Judah, "from Dan to Beersheba." Terrified, Ish-bosheth cannot utter another word (3:6–11).

True to his threat, Abner immediately sends envoys to David proposing a covenant to bring all Israel over to his side. David accepts—on one non-negotiable condition: Abner must not appear before him without bringing Saul's daughter Michal along. At the same time, David sends messengers straight to Ish-bosheth demanding back his wife Michal, whom he had betrothed for a hundred Philistine foreskins. Ish-bosheth complies and has her taken away from her second husband, Paltiel. The parting is heartbreaking: Paltiel walks behind her weeping all the way to Bahurim until Abner snaps at him, "Go back!" and he turns home alone (3:12–16).

Before traveling down to David, Abner confers with Israel's elders. He reminds them they have long wanted David as king and urges action now, citing the LORD's promise to deliver Israel through David from the Philistines and all their enemies. He also speaks personally with Benjamin—Saul's own tribe. With support lined up, Abner brings twenty men down to Hebron to report. David hosts a lavish feast for Abner and his companions. Abner pledges to go gather all Israel to make a covenant so David may reign over everything his heart desires. David sends him off, and Abner departs in peace (3:17–21).

Scarcely has Abner left town when Joab and David's troops return from a raid laden with plunder. Learning that Abner just visited the king and walked away untouched, Joab storms in and upbraids David angrily, insisting Abner only came to deceive him and spy out every move he makes. Stepping out of David's presence, Joab secretly sends messengers to bring Abner back from the cistern of Sirah without letting the king know a thing. Once Abner returns to Hebron, Joab pulls him aside inside the gateway as if to talk privately, then plunges a dagger straight into his belly to avenge the blood of his brother Asahel (3:22–27).

Shocked by the news, David immediately declares himself and his kingdom forever innocent before the LORD regarding Abner's blood. He pronounces a blistering curse on Joab and his father's house, praying that Joab's line never lack someone with a discharge, a skin disease, one leaning on a crutch, one falling by the sword, or one lacking bread. The narrator clarifies the motive plainly: Joab and his brother Abishai murdered Abner because Abner had killed their brother Asahel in battle at Gibeon (3:28–30).

David orders Joab himself and all the people to tear their clothes, put on sackcloth, and walk mourning ahead of Abner's bier, while the king personally follows behind. At Abner's burial in Hebron, David lifts his voice and weeps openly beside the tomb, and all the people weep with him. The king chants a poignant lament for the fallen commander: "Should Abner die as a fool dies?" His hands were not bound, nor his feet shackled—yet he fell as one falls before wicked men. The crowd weeps again and begs the king to eat bread while day remains, yet David swears not to taste a morsel before sunset. Watching closely, the entire nation approves and knows beyond doubt that the king played zero part in killing Abner. Privately to his aides, David sighs that a prince and great man has fallen in Israel today. Candidly he admits that though anointed king, "today I am weak," while these sons of Zeruiah are too ruthless for him—leaving it to the LORD to repay the evildoer according to his wickedness (3:31–39).

## Key Takeaways

**Human self-interest cannot stop God's plan, yet it lays bare human hearts.** Abner knew all along that the LORD had promised the throne to David (3:9–10; 3:18). Yet as long as he held power, he propped up Saul's house against God's word; only when his ego got bruised did he suddenly quote God's promise to switch sides. God still steered the nation toward unity, while exposing Abner's true motives completely.

**Private grudges can wreck an entire nation's peace.** Abner had already persuaded Israel's elders and was days from handing eleven northern tribes peacefully to David without another drop of blood (3:17–21). Driven by family vendetta—and likely fear of losing his own post as top general—Joab stabbed the king's peace partner right inside the gate (3:24–27).

**Power bargains frequently crush ordinary, helpless people.** Amid the political chess match among David, Abner, and Ish-bosheth, Michal is pulled out of a settled home like a bargaining chip, while heartbroken Paltiel walks weeping behind her to Bahurim until barked home by a general (3:13–16). Scripture records the moment in just a few words, ensuring unseen pain never goes unnoticed.

**Wearing the crown does not guarantee mastery over every force around it.** David handled the political crisis shrewdly, winning national trust through public mourning and sincere lament (3:31–37). Yet admitting weakness before Zeruiah's sons (3:39) reveals a painful crack: when a ruler cannot enforce justice upon his own right-hand general, tolerated ruthlessness casts a dark shadow for decades.

## Deeper Meaning

**Six wives at Hebron sow seeds of royal heartbreak.** The chapter opens listing six sons born in Hebron as outward proof of David's rising strength (3:2–5). Read across all Scripture, however, it reads like an ominous warning. Moses' law explicitly forbade Israel's king from multiplying wives lest his heart turn astray (Deuteronomy 17:17). Tragically, three sons on this short roster will rip David's house apart: Amnon falls murdered by Absalom (2 Samuel 13:28–29), Absalom rebels to seize his father's throne (2 Samuel 16:21–22), and Adonijah grabs for power while David lies dying (1 Kings 1:5–6).

**A deceased king's harem tied directly to royal succession.** Why did Ish-bosheth explode over Abner approaching Saul's concubine Rizpah (3:7)? Across the ancient Near East, taking a former monarch's concubine functioned as a covert claim to his throne. Scripture echoes that unspoken rule repeatedly: Reuben sleeping with Jacob's concubine (Genesis 35:22), Absalom publicly taking David's concubines on the palace roof (2 Samuel 16:21–22), and Adonijah asking for Abishag, prompting Solomon to order his execution for treason (1 Kings 2:17–22). As for Rizpah herself, she reappears later in an unforgettable tableau of maternal devotion, spreading sackcloth on a rock to guard the bodies of her slain sons for months (2 Samuel 21:8–10).

**Murder inside a city of refuge.** Joab lured Abner to his death inside the gateway of Hebron (3:27). Making the crime doubly heinous, under Moses' law and Joshua, Hebron stood as one of Israel's six designated cities of refuge shielding anyone guilty of unintentional bloodshed from avengers (Numbers 35:11–12; Joshua 20:7). Worse still, Abner had killed Asahel in open battlefield self-defense, never by ambush (2:19–23). Joab trampled brazenly over God's law protecting human life.

**A blood debt never forgotten.** Unable then to discipline Joab, David entrusted retribution to the LORD (3:39). Yet justice merely waited. Nearly forty years later on his deathbed, David charged Solomon not to let Joab's gray head go down to the grave in peace, naming explicitly how Joab "shed the blood of war in a time of peace" against Abner and Amasa (1 Kings 2:5–6; 1 Kings 2:31–33). David's appeal to heaven's justice resonates with Paul's teaching in the New Testament: vengeance is Mine, I will repay, says the Lord (Romans 12:19).

**Reclaiming Michal reopens an unhealable wound.** David originally won Michal through Saul's deadly Philistine snare (1 Samuel 18:25–27) before Saul gave her to Paltiel during David's exile (1 Samuel 25:44). Bringing her back restored David's legal standing as Saul's son-in-law to smooth northern unity, yet whatever love once lived between them had died long ago. Shortly afterward, watching David dance before the ark, Michal will despise him from her window, ending their marriage in permanent estrangement (2 Samuel 6:20–23).

## Notes & Nuances

**Did reclaiming Michal violate the Law of Moses?** Deuteronomy 24:1–4 forbids a husband from remarrying a former wife whom he formally divorced after she remarried another man. Most commentators explain that David's situation differed fundamentally: David never divorced Michal—Saul arbitrarily seized her and gave her to Paltiel (1 Samuel 25:44)—so the original marriage remained legally intact. Still, few readers watch Paltiel weeping down the road without feeling genuine grief and wondering whether David reclaimed her out of affection or sharp political calculus.

**Did Abner actually sleep with Rizpah?** The text never explicitly confirms whether Ish-bosheth's charge was true or false (3:7–8). Some readers think Abner genuinely overstepped out of contempt for the weak puppet king he had installed. Others think Ish-bosheth acted on rumor or sought an excuse to rein in an overbearing general, accidentally shattering the sole pillar holding up his regime. Either way, the quarrel exposes a dynasty imploding from within.

**How should we understand David's harsh curse on Joab's house?** Modern readers often recoil hearing David invoke chronic illness, lameness, violent death, and poverty not just on Joab but across his descendants (3:29). In ancient Israelite legal theology, unpunished murder inside a royal city left blood-guilt resting over the throne and the whole land. This public imprecation formally severed the crown from any complicity, placing full judicial liability squarely back on the killer's house—a debt finally settled under Solomon (1 Kings 2:31–33).

**Chileab or Daniel?** David's second son by Abigail is named Chileab in 3:3, yet listed as Daniel in 1 Chronicles 3:1. Commentators usually conclude either he bore two names or a slight scribal variation occurred early in copying. Since he disappears completely from later succession struggles, many suspect he died young.
