# 2 Samuel 5 — King over All Israel, Capturing Jerusalem, and Defeating the Philistines

## Story

With Saul's dynasty left leaderless after Ish-bosheth's death, all the tribes of Israel gather to David at Hebron. They voice three compelling reasons for crowning him: first, shared blood—"we are your own flesh and blood"; second, proven leadership even while Saul lived, when David led Israel out and brought them safely home; and above all, the LORD's own word to him, "You will shepherd My people Israel, and you will become their ruler." Before the LORD, King David makes a formal covenant with all Israel's elders, and they anoint him king over the united nation (5:1–3).

The narrator pauses briefly to summarize David's full reign: he is thirty years old when he becomes king and reigns for forty years in total—seven years and six months over Judah in Hebron, followed by thirty-three years over all Israel and Judah in Jerusalem (5:4–5).

The new king's bold opening move is marching against Jerusalem, still held by the Jebusites. Banking on steep mountain slopes and thick walls, the Jebusites mock David that he will never break in—"even the blind and the lame can ward you off!" Yet David captures the fortress of Zion, henceforth known as the City of David. On the day of the assault, David offers top command to whoever strikes the Jebusites by climbing through the water shaft to bring down those arrogant defenders whom he playfully echoes as "the lame and the blind." From this exploit springs a popular saying: "The blind and the lame shall not enter the house" (5:6–8).

David takes up residence inside the fortress, renaming it the City of David, and builds up the city inward from the supporting terraces (the Millo). Steadily he grows greater and greater, because the LORD God of Hosts is with him. Watching David's rising stature, King Hiram of Tyre sends envoys with cedar timber, carpenters, and stonemasons to build David a royal palace. Amid this flood of success, David discerns with humble clarity that the LORD has established him as king over Israel and exalted his kingdom "for the sake of His people Israel" (5:9–12).

After moving his capital from Hebron to Jerusalem, David takes still more concubines and wives there and fathers more sons and daughters. Scripture records the names of eleven sons born in Jerusalem—most notably Nathan and Solomon (5:13–16).

No sooner do the Philistines hear that David has been anointed king over all Israel than they march up in full force to crush the newly united realm. Alerted, David goes down to the stronghold while Philistine troops spread across the Valley of Rephaim. Refusing to charge blindly, David inquires of the LORD whether to march up and whether God will hand them over. Receiving the LORD's clear promise, he breaks through them at a site he names Baal-perazim, declaring, "Like a bursting flood, the LORD has broken through my enemies before me." Fleeing in panic, the Philistines abandon their idols right on the field, and David and his men carry them away (5:17–21).

Unwilling to accept defeat, the Philistines regroup and spread across the exact same Valley of Rephaim a second time. Rather than coasting on yesterday's triumph, David inquires of the LORD once more. This time God gives a wholly different plan: do not march straight up; circle behind them opposite the balsam trees. Wait until you hear "the sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees," then strike swiftly—for at that instant the LORD has gone out ahead of you to strike the Philistine army. Obeying every syllable, David strikes down the Philistines all the way from Geba to Gezer (5:22–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Godly kingship looks like shepherding, not autocratic domination.** Israel's elders recalled God calling David to "shepherd My people" (5:2), and David entered a formal covenant with them before the LORD rather than ruling by brute force (5:3). Real leadership carries accountability and commitment, watching over and defending the flock entrusted to it.

**A leader's elevation exists for the people's good, not personal glory.** Verse 5:12 stands among the noblest lines written about David: surrounded by victories, foreign embassies, and a cedar palace, he recognized clearly that God exalted his kingdom "for the sake of His people Israel." Every gift, platform, or resource God grants anyone aims ultimately at blessing others.

**Yesterday's victory never replaces seeking God today.** Both Philistine clashes occurred on the exact same battlefield against the exact same enemy (5:18; 5:22). Yet David refused to copy-paste last week's strategy. He inquired of God both times and received opposite battle plans: once a frontal assault, next a flanking ambush waiting on heaven's signal (5:19; 5:23–24). Mature faith never runs on autopilot; it keeps listening step by step.

**Seeds of future decline quietly sprout right at peak prosperity.** Tucked inside a chapter overflowing with triumphs sit two quiet verses noting David taking additional wives and concubines in Jerusalem (5:13–14). Right when life runs smoothest, hearts most easily drop their guard and adopt the habits of the surrounding world.

## Deeper Meaning

**Three anointings and a patient school of leadership.** Here David receives his third anointing—first privately among his brothers in Bethlehem as a shepherd boy (1 Samuel 16:13), second publicly at Hebron over Judah (2:4), and third over all twelve tribes here (5:3). Over a decade of flight, waiting, and refining separated the initial promise from full coronation. Strikingly, David began reigning at age thirty (5:4)—the very age at which Jesus launched His public ministry (Luke 3:23).

**The shepherd-king foreshadows the Messiah.** Here for the first time in the Old Testament, an Israelite monarch is directly commissioned as shepherd of God's people (5:2). Later Psalms praise God for bringing David from the sheepfolds to shepherd Israel with integrity of heart and skillful hands (Psalms 78:70–72). Centuries onward, after faithless kings failed, Ezekiel promised God would place "one shepherd," His servant David, over them (Ezekiel 34:23)—reaching glorious climax when Jesus declared Himself the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11).

**Jerusalem and Mount Zion step to the heart of redemption's story.** For centuries since the conquest, neither Judah nor Benjamin managed to dislodge the Jebusites from Jerusalem (Joshua 15:63; Judges 1:21). Sitting neatly on the border between northern tribes and southern Judah—belonging exclusively to neither—Jerusalem proved a stroke of genius for unifying all twelve tribes. Deeper still, ancient Salem of priest-king Melchizedek (Genesis 14:18) now becomes Zion, the dwelling the LORD chose forever (Psalms 132:13–14).

**Total reversal over Philistine idols.** Decades earlier, the Philistines captured Israel's ark and carried it into Dagon's temple, only to find Dagon smashed facedown before the LORD (1 Samuel 4:11; 5:2–4). Now at Baal-perazim, Philistine troops haul their idols to war hoping for luck, only to abandon their gods in the dirt for David's men to seize and burn (5:21; 1 Chronicles 14:12). That stunning breakthrough echoed for centuries—Isaiah later citing Mount Perazim as a timeless emblem of God's unstoppable power (Isaiah 28:21).

**The house of God ultimately opens wide to the blind and the lame.** The saying at the close of 5:8—"The blind and the lame shall not enter the house"—later tied into strict priestly regulations barring physical blemishes from altar service (Leviticus 21:18). Yet when the Son of David enters Jerusalem's temple during His final week, Matthew deliberately highlights a radiant reversal: "The blind and the lame came to Him at the temple, and He healed them," while children cheered "Hosanna to the Son of David!" (Matthew 21:14–15). Doors closed in David's fortress swing wide open through the Messiah's mercy.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does 5:8 discriminate against people with disabilities?** In Hebrew, 5:8 ranks among the trickiest verses in Samuel. The clearest reading tracks directly from verse 6: overconfident Jebusites boasted that even blind and lame defenders could repel David, so during the assault David simply turned their own taunt back onto their smug garrison. Some scholars think the Jebusites invoked an ancient curse threatening blindness and lameness on attackers, while the proverb at verse's end reflected later temple access restrictions (Leviticus 21:18). David clearly held zero personal prejudice against disability: just four chapters later he welcomes Mephibosheth—crippled in both feet—to live in Jerusalem and dine at the king's table forever (9:13).

**What were the "water shaft" in verse 8 and "the Millo" in verse 9?** The rare Hebrew word translated "water shaft" is widely understood by scholars and archaeologists as an underground conduit or shaft bringing water from the Gihon spring outside into Jerusalem's heart. First Chronicles adds that Joab led the climb up first and won appointment as commander-in-chief (1 Chronicles 11:6), though some translators interpret the obscure term as a grappling weapon or windpipe. "The Millo" (literally "filling") refers to stepped stone terracing reinforcing the steep eastern slope of the City of David to expand building space and walls (1 Chronicles 11:8).

**David's expanding harem in Jerusalem.** Verse 5:13 matter-of-factly notes David taking more wives and concubines in Jerusalem. Across the ancient Near East, large harems and abundant heirs displayed royal prestige and sealed diplomatic alliances—much like David's earlier marriage to the princess of Geshur at Hebron (3:3). Scripture never applauds the practice: Moses' law explicitly forbade kings from multiplying wives lest their hearts turn away (Deuteronomy 17:17). The author drops this quiet note here and lets the devastating sibling rivalries, incest, and coups ahead render the verdict.

**Are the events of chapter 5 arranged topically rather than strictly chronologically?** On a quick read, it looks as though the Philistines attacked only after Hiram built David's palace and eleven sons were born (5:11–17). Yet 5:17 says explicitly they marched right when they heard David had been anointed king over all Israel. Most commentators conclude the twin Rephaim battles occurred early in David's reign—likely well before Hiram's cedar palace, since Hiram was still alive decades later during Solomon's reign (1 Kings 5:1–6). The author grouped capital establishment, palace construction, and royal family together cleanly before shifting to foreign wars.

No ruler is ever elevated for private gain, Calvin stressed; the instant authority forgets it exists for the people's welfare, it mutates into tyranny.
