# Proverbs 11 — Honesty, Humility, and Generosity

## Story

Chapter 11 continues Solomon's series of contrasting proverbs while widening the lens to marketplace commerce, civic life, and our relationship with money. The sayings gather naturally around three central themes.

The first theme is honesty, humility, and uprightness before God. The chapter opens right in the marketplace with scales and weights: "Dishonest scales are an abomination to the LORD, but an accurate weight is His delight." Next comes a lesson in posture of heart: "When pride comes, disgrace follows, but with humility comes wisdom." Integrity guides the upright, while duplicity destroys the treacherous. On the day of wrath wealth cannot save anyone, but righteousness delivers from death; when the wicked die their hope perishes, whereas the righteous are rescued from trouble. The one who sows righteousness reaps a sure reward, while the wicked earn deceptive wages; the LORD detests perverse hearts yet delights in those whose ways are blameless. The fruit of the righteous is a tree of life, and the wise person wins souls; if the righteous receive their due right here on earth, how much more the ungodly and the sinner (11:1–8; 11:18–21; 11:23; 11:27; 11:30–31).

The second theme turns outward to community, speech, and family life. With his mouth the godless person destroys his neighbor, while the blessing of the upright lifts up an entire city; that is why a city rejoices when the righteous prosper and shouts for joy when the wicked perish. A person of understanding holds his tongue instead of belittling his neighbor, and keeps a confidence faithfully rather than going around gossiping. In public affairs, where there is no wise guidance a people falls, "but in an abundance of counselors there is safety." A gracious woman gains honor, a kind person benefits his own soul, while a cruel person brings harm upon himself. The poet also offers two unforgettable sketches of character and home: "Like a gold ring in a pig's snout is a beautiful woman who lacks discretion," and whoever brings trouble on his own household will end up inheriting only the wind (11:9–14; 11:16–17; 11:22; 11:29).

The third theme tackles money through a thought-provoking paradox. After a brief reminder not to recklessly put up security for a stranger, the chapter unfolds the principle of generosity: "One gives freely, yet gains even more; another withholds what is right, only to become poor." A generous soul will prosper, and "he who refreshes others will himself be refreshed." People curse a merchant who hoards grain during a shortage, while blessing crowns the one willing to sell it to feed the public. In short, whoever trusts in his riches will fall, while the righteous will thrive like a green leaf (11:15; 11:24–26; 11:28).

## Key Takeaways

**God cares about the smallest business transaction of everyday life.** Placing dishonest scales right in verse 1 makes clear that worship is not confined to the temple; shaving a fraction off a weight in the market is already detestable before the LORD.

**One person's way of life directly shapes the health of the whole community.** Upright people do not merely do well for themselves; they lift an entire city and bring it joy (11:10–11). Conversely, gossips, grain hoarders, and troublemakers at home fracture the community around them (11:13; 11:26; 11:29).

**Generosity is the paradox of true wealth.** By selfish math, clutching everything tightly is how you keep what you have; yet verses 24–25 point to the opposite law: those who give freely and refresh others are the very ones who grow rich and refreshed themselves.

**Outward beauty cannot compensate for a lack of inward wisdom.** The picture of a gold ring stuck in a pig's snout (11:22) is both humorous and sharp: charm or talent without character and discretion looks painfully out of place and ends up spoiling its own worth.

## Deeper Meaning

**The paradox that sharing increases abundance (11:24–25) is strongly reaffirmed in the New Testament.** Jesus teaches, give and it will be given to you—a good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over (Luke 6:38). The apostle Paul invokes the same principle when encouraging the Corinthian church to give generously to the poor: whoever sows sparingly will reap sparingly, whoever sows generously will reap generously, for God loves a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:6–11).

**Verse 31 is quoted directly in 1 Peter.** Encouraging believers under trial, Peter cites Proverbs 11:31 via the ancient Greek translation: if it is hard for the righteous to be saved, what will become of the ungodly and the sinner (1 Peter 4:18)?

**The warning against trusting in riches (11:28) leads straight to Jesus' parable of the rich fool.** Wealth cannot secure life on the day of judgment (11:4; 11:28), just like the rich man who built bigger barns in Jesus' parable only to have his soul demanded of him that very night (Luke 12:16–21; 1 Timothy 6:17).

## Notes & Nuances

**The phrase "he who wins souls is wise" (11:30) goes beyond evangelism alone.** In Hebrew, the expression carries a broader sense: through a righteous life and wisdom that acts like a tree of life, a wise person wins people over and draws them toward goodness and life. Christians have naturally applied this verse to sharing the gospel—an entirely fitting extension—yet its original meaning embraces wholesome, life-giving influence in every relationship.

**Verse 16 shows a slight difference between the Hebrew text and the ancient Greek translation.** The Hebrew is concise and punchy—a gracious woman attains honor, while ruthless (or forceful) men attain wealth—contrasting two very different measures of value (lasting honor versus grasping wealth). The ancient Greek translation (Septuagint) inserts two middle lines to make the contrast between honor and greed even more explicit.
