# Proverbs 23 — Do Not Chase Quick Riches and Guard Against Drunkenness

## Story

Continuing *the words of the wise* begun in chapter 22, chapter 23 heads straight into down-to-earth traps around banquets, money, and powerful circles. When dining with a ruler, watch carefully who sits before you; if you have a big appetite, restrain yourself fiercely as if putting a knife to your throat, and do not crave deceptive delicacies. Do not wear yourself out chasing riches by your own cleverness, for wealth disappears at a glance—sprouting wings and flying skyward like an eagle. Do not eat the bread of a stingy, grudging host: his mouth warmly urges you to eat and drink while his heart calculates every bite, until you feel sick enough to vomit up what you ate. Do not waste wise words on a fool who will only scorn them, and never move an ancient boundary stone or encroach on orphans' fields, because their Redeemer is mighty and will take up their case against you (23:1–11).

The middle of the chapter shifts into the warm voice of a father mentoring his child. Apply your heart to instruction and do not withhold discipline from a child so as to rescue their soul from death. A father's heart leaps with joy whenever a child grows wise and speaks what is right. Do not envy sinners, but walk in the fear of the LORD all day long, for surely there is a future and your hope will not be cut off. Direct your heart on the right way and steer clear of heavy drinkers and gluttons, who slide into poverty and rags; listen to the father who gave you life and do not despise your mother when she grows old. Buy truth, wisdom, discipline, and understanding—and never sell them—so that both father and mother rejoice (23:12–25).

The final two sections deliver two of the sharpest, most vivid warnings in Proverbs. First, the father pleads for his son's whole heart and steady eyes along right paths, warning him off sexual immorality—for a prostitute is a deep pit and an adulteress a narrow well, lying in wait like a bandit and multiplying the unfaithful (23:26–28).

The chapter closes with a masterfully biting poem on alcoholism. Opening with six rapid-fire questions—who has woe, who has sorrow, who has strife, who has complaints, who has needless bruises, who has bloodshot eyes—the sage points straight at those who linger over mixed wine. He urges readers not to gaze wistfully at red wine sparkling and sliding down smoothly in the cup, because in the end it bites like a venomous snake: eyes stare at strange sights, the mind utters twisted things, and the body pitches like someone lying out on the high seas or atop a ship's mast. Most chilling of all is the closing line capturing addiction to the bone: struck and beaten yet feeling no pain, waking up only to look for another drink (23:29–35).

## Key Takeaways

**Money sprouts wings fast—do not trade your life for it.** Verses 23:4–5 never forbid hard work, yet they expose the obsession with getting rich at all costs. Wealth is so fleeting that the moment you glance at it, it "makes wings for itself and flies like an eagle to the sky" (23:5).

**See past generous invitations into people's real motives.** Both the ruler's feast (23:1–3) and the stingy host's table (23:6–8) call for clear-eyed self-control. Some tempting perks carry hidden strings that cost our freedom and leave bitter regret.

**Never sell out the truth.** "Buy the truth and do not sell it—wisdom, instruction, and understanding" (23:23). Truth is worth paying for with time, effort, and sacrifice, yet nothing in the world is worth trading truth away to obtain.

**Addiction numbs both pain and reason.** Verses 23:29–35 stand among the sharpest psychological portraits of addiction in all ancient literature. The peak of the tragedy is not merely lost balance or fresh bruises, but the final muttered line: "When will I wake up so I can seek another drink?" (23:35).

## Deeper Meaning

**The Redeemer of the fatherless.** In 23:11, *Redeemer* translates the Hebrew concept of the kinsman-redeemer—the nearest relative responsible to buy back family land and shield vulnerable kin (Leviticus 25:25; Ruth 4:4). God claims the role of closest family protector for orphans. In the New Testament, Jesus Himself becomes our Redeemer and elder brother, sharing our humanity to rescue the helpless (Hebrews 2:11–15).

**Truth worth buying at any price.** The call to buy truth in 23:23 echoes in Jesus' parable of the pearl of great price: a merchant finds one priceless pearl and sells everything he owns to purchase it (Matthew 13:45–46).

**A parent's joy at seeing children walk in truth.** The father's overflowing gladness in 23:15–16 and 23:24–25 mirrors the apostle John's words to the church: he has no greater joy than to hear that his children are walking in the truth (3 John 1:4).

**Watchfulness against drunkenness and excess.** The sobering portrait in 23:29–35 speaks with the same voice as the New Testament call to live alertly and with self-control, turning away from carousing, drunkenness, and debauchery (Romans 13:13; Ephesians 5:18).

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 23:7 and a popular out-of-context quote.** The traditional phrasing—"For as he thinks in his heart, so is he"—is frequently lifted on its own as a self-help motto about positive thinking. Yet in the actual flow of 23:6–8, the line describes a miserly host (which the BSB clarifies as *for he is keeping track of the cost*): his mouth urges guests to eat, while inside he tallies every morsel, revealing that his real nature lies in his calculating stinginess rather than his polite invitation.

**Verses 23:13–14 do not endorse child abuse.** In the ancient world, *the rod* functioned as shorthand for firm family discipline. The stated goal here is to "save his soul from Sheol"—rescuing a child from growing up undisciplined and wrecking his life. Read within the whole of Scripture, correction must always be paired with tenderness and parental self-restraint (19:18; Colossians 3:21).

**Parallels with "The Instruction of Amenemope."** Several vivid images in chapter 23—wealth sprouting wings like wild geese or an eagle (23:5), refusing to dine before a greedy man (23:6–8), and not encroaching on an orphan's field (23:10)—also share striking similarities with this ancient Egyptian text, showing how Israel's sages used familiar imagery from their wider world to teach the fear of the LORD.
