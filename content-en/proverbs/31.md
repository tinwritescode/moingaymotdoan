# Proverbs 31 — King Lemuel's Mother and the Song of the Noble Wife

## Story

The closing chapter of Proverbs holds two radiant sections, both honoring the voice and strength of a wise woman. The opening part (31:1–9) preserves the counsel King Lemuel's mother gave her son. Calling out to him tenderly three times—*my son*, *son of my womb*, *son of my vows*—she warns the young ruler against two snares that ruin leaders: giving his vigor over to women and drowning his judgment in heavy drink. Her reasoning is crystal clear: kings who drink forget the law and twist justice for the afflicted; leave strong drink to the dying or the bitterly distressed, while a ruler must stay clear-headed for the noblest duty of royal office—opening his mouth for those who have no voice, defending the destitute, and judging fairly for the poor and needy (31:1–9).

The second half (31:10–31) is the celebrated alphabetic acrostic poem praising *a wife of noble character*. It opens with a rhetorical question: who can find a woman of noble character, since her worth surpasses rubies? Her husband's heart trusts her completely, and every day of her life she brings him good and never harm (31:10–12).

The verses that follow paint a dynamic, resourceful, courageous woman active in both household stewardship and commerce. She gladly selects wool and flax and works with eager hands; like merchant ships she brings food from afar; she rises before dawn to provide for her household and assign tasks to her servant women. She evaluates a field, buys it on her own judgment, and plants a vineyard out of her earnings; she wraps herself in strength, works ably into the night with her lamp burning, and handles spindle and distaff with skill. Far from focusing only inward, she opens her palms to the poor and reaches out her hands to the needy (31:13–20).

Thanks to her foresight, her household fears no winter snow because everyone is warmly clothed in scarlet wool; she weaves coverings and dresses in fine linen, and also crafts linen garments and sashes to supply merchants. Her husband is respected as he sits among the town elders at the city gate. Clothed in strength and dignity, she smiles confidently at the future; she opens her mouth with wisdom, faithful instruction rests on her tongue, she watches attentively over her household, and never eats the bread of idleness (31:21–27).

Both the poem and the entire book of Proverbs culminate in overflowing praise. Her children rise up and call her blessed, and her husband praises her above all other noble women. Verse 31:30 sets the theological crown atop the whole book: charm is deceptive and beauty fades away, yet a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised; give her the reward her own hands have earned, and let her works praise her publicly at the city gate (31:28–31).

## Key Takeaways

**Authority exists to shield the vulnerable, not to chase self-indulgence.** King Lemuel's mother gives a timeless definition of leadership: practice strict self-discipline around desire and drink (31:3–5) so you stay clear-headed to "open your mouth for those with no voice" and secure justice for the poor and needy (31:8–9).

**A noble woman is neither weak nor passive, but capable and compassionate.** The portrait in 31:10–31 shatters cramped stereotypes: she runs a household, evaluates real estate, plants a vineyard, trades textiles, stretches out generous hands to the poor, and speaks with wisdom and kindness.

**The bedrock of real beauty is the fear of the LORD.** Verse 31:30 loops straight back to the opening motto of the whole book (1:7): "Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised." Outward looks fade with the years, while character rooted in reverence for God grows brighter with time.

**Wisdom walks out into everyday life.** Proverbs begins with *Lady Wisdom* calling in the streets (1:20–21) and ends with a flesh-and-blood woman living that wisdom out across ordinary daily tasks (31:10–31).

## Deeper Meaning

**A righteous king championing the poor.** The counsel of Lemuel's mother regarding a monarch who defends the needy (31:8–9) mirrors Isaiah's prophecy of the coming Messiah judging the poor with righteousness (Isaiah 11:4). In the New Testament, Jesus proclaims good news to the poor, sight to the blind, and freedom for the oppressed (Luke 4:18–19).

**Unfading inner beauty.** Verse 31:30 resonates unmistakably in 1 Peter, where the apostle encourages Christian women not to rely merely on outward adornment, but to cultivate the unfading inner beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit—which is deeply precious in God's sight (1 Peter 3:3–4).

**The church—the noble bride of Christ.** Across the centuries, many Christian interpreters have also glimpsed in this poem a luminous portrait of the faithful church: loving, diligent in good works, and welcoming the future with joy as she awaits Christ the Bridegroom (Ephesians 5:25–27; Revelation 19:7–8).

**Praise spoken by one's own deeds.** The closing verse (31:31)—letting her own works praise her at the city gate—recalls Jesus' call to believers to let their light shine before others, so that people may see their good deeds and glorify their Father in heaven (Matthew 5:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**An alphabetic acrostic poem (31:10–31).** The twenty-two verses from 31:10 to 31:31 form a complete Hebrew alphabetic acrostic: verse 10 starts with the first letter (*Alef*), verse 11 with the second (*Bet*), continuing letter by letter through verse 31 with the twenty-second letter (*Tav*). Writing from A to Z both aided memorization and symbolized comprehensive praise for the character of a wise woman.

**Reading 31:10–31 as a song of praise, not a crushing to-do list.** The woman depicted here oversees household servants, likely reflecting a well-resourced household of antiquity or a composite portrait of noble qualities expressed across a whole lifetime. Read as a checklist demanding every woman accomplish everything in a single day, the passage leaves readers exhausted; read in its true genre—a celebratory anthem of wisdom and reverence for God, traditionally recited in Jewish homes on Friday evenings as husbands and children honor wives and mothers—it becomes a deeply warm affirmation.

**Verses 31:6–7 on strong drink for the suffering.** Lemuel's mother is not recommending drunkenness as a cure for sadness, a trap Proverbs has already denounced forcefully (23:29–35). In an era without painkillers, strong drink was occasionally used to numb agony for the dying; she uses that stark contrast to underscore how utterly unfit drunkenness is for rulers charged with upholding justice (31:4–5).

**The phrase "noble character" in 31:10.** The underlying Hebrew word (*chayil*) carries remarkable vigor—strength, valor, competence—the same term often used for mighty warriors in the Old Testament and applied to Ruth by Boaz (Ruth 3:11). A *wife of noble character* is not merely gentle, but resilient, capable, and spiritually strong.
