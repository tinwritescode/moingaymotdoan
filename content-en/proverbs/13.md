# Proverbs 13 — Choosing Friends, Guarding the Mouth, and Growing Little by Little

## Story

Chapter 13 gathers twenty-five concise proverbs of Solomon that press deep into practical lessons about raising children, choosing companions, tending hope, and building lasting wealth.

The first group deals with listening to instruction, picking companions, and training within the family. A wise son heeds his father's discipline, whereas a mocker refuses every rebuke; arrogance breeds only quarrels, while wisdom stays with those who take advice. Whoever scorns instruction meets poverty, disgrace, and ruin, while those who respect counsel are rewarded and honored because the teaching of the wise is a fountain of life that steers people away from the snares of death. A prudent person acts with knowledge, whereas a fool puts folly on display. One famous saying highlights the shaping power of our circle: "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed." Within the home, "He who spares the rod hates his son, but he who loves him disciplines him diligently" (13:1; 13:10; 13:13–14; 13:16; 13:18; 13:20; 13:24).

The second group explores speech, faithfulness, and the ache of waiting in the human heart. "He who guards his mouth protects his life," whereas the one who speaks recklessly brings on ruin; the righteous enjoy good from wholesome speech and hate falsehood, their light shines brightly while the lamp of the wicked is snuffed out and disaster hunts them down. A wicked messenger falls into trouble, while a trustworthy envoy brings healing. Good understanding wins favor, whereas the way of the unfaithful is hard. In particular, two deeply perceptive observations about human psychology note that "Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but desire fulfilled is a tree of life," sweet to the soul (13:2–3; 13:5–6; 13:9; 13:12; 13:15; 13:17; 13:19; 13:21).

The third group looks squarely at work, money, and justice. The sluggard craves and gets nothing, while the diligent are fully satisfied. Society exhibits two telling extremes: one pretends to be rich yet has nothing, while another acts poor yet holds great wealth; riches may ransom a wealthy person's life, while a poor person hears no threats of extortion. Wealth won hastily through dishonesty or windfall soon dwindles, whereas whoever gathers little by little through steady work sees it grow. A good person leaves an inheritance to grandchildren and eats to satisfaction. Yet the writer never closes his eyes to injustice: a poor person's field may produce abundant food, only to be swept away because society lacks justice (13:4; 13:7–8; 13:11; 13:22–23; 13:25).

## Key Takeaways

**The company we keep quietly shapes who we become.** Verse 13:20 needs no elaborate commentary: walk with the wise and you gradually grow wise; tie yourself to fools and sooner or later you share the fallout. Choosing our community is one of life's most consequential decisions.

**Steady accumulation outlasts money that comes too fast.** Verse 13:11 exposes a real economic and psychological pattern: easy money usually slips through the fingers just as fast, while patient saving and labor little by little build both assets and the character to steward them.

**Proverbs recognizes both the ache of waiting and the reality of social injustice.** Verse 13:12 understands how draining it feels when hope keeps getting delayed. And verse 13:23 proves the author is never naive enough to claim that everyone poor is lazy: many poor people work hard on their land yet lose everything to injustice.

**Genuine love for children includes serious discipline.** Verse 13:24 poses a paradox that startles many parents: indulging children and shrinking from correction is not love, but abandoning them to harm.

## Deeper Meaning

**The warning about choosing companions (13:20) is echoed by the apostle Paul in the New Testament.** Urging the Corinthian church to avoid corrupting influences, Paul cites a well-known maxim: bad company corrupts good character (1 Corinthians 15:33).

**Parents' loving discipline mirrors the way God trains His children.** The principle that love requires diligent correction (13:24) links to Proverbs 3:12 and is expanded in Hebrews: no discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful; later on, however, it produces a harvest of righteousness and peace (Hebrews 12:7–11).

**Concern for the oppressed poor (13:23) runs throughout Scripture.** From the Mosaic Law safeguarding the vulnerable to the prophets condemning those who seize the fields of the poor (Isaiah 5:8; Amos 5:11–12), God consistently stands on the side of justice for the voiceless.

## Notes & Nuances

**The "rod" in 13:24 is not a justification for violence or child abuse.** In the vocabulary of Proverbs, the *rod* is a shorthand image for parental discipline, boundaries, and moral guidance—contrasted with neglectful permissiveness that leaves a child to drift. Scripture as a whole strictly forbids parents from provoking, embittering, or dealing cruelly with their children (Ephesians 6:4; Colossians 3:21). The heart of this verse is that real love has the courage to guide and correct a child early.

**Verse 13:7 satirizes both flaunting wealth and hiding it.** The one who *pretends to be rich yet has nothing* lives beyond his means to keep up appearances; the one who *pretends to be poor yet has great wealth* may be living with quiet simplicity, or may be a miser concealing assets to avoid generosity. The brief proverb invites readers to examine their own honesty toward money.

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor of the early 18th century, noted on 13:11 that wealth gathered little by little through honest labor puts down lasting roots and carries God's blessing, whereas money gained by fraud or gambling melts away like snow in the sun.
