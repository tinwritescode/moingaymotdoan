# Proverbs 9 — Two Banquets and Two Invitations

## Story

Chapter 9 brings the long opening section of Proverbs (chapters 1–9) to a close with a striking symmetrical tableau: two women—Lady Wisdom and Lady Folly—both stand on the heights of the city, both host a meal, and both call out with the exact same opening invitation, placing the reader at life's decisive crossroads.

The first six verses unveil Wisdom's stately, welcoming home: "Wisdom has built her house; she has carved out her seven pillars." She slaughters her meat, mixes her fine wine, sets her table with care, and sends out her servant girls to the highest points of the city to invite guests. Those she calls are not a self-satisfied elite, but the naive and those lacking sense: turn in here, eat my bread, drink the wine I have mixed, leave your simple ways behind, and walk in the path of insight so you may live (9:1–6).

Positioned right between the two invitations (9:7–12) is a brief interlude contrasting how two kinds of people respond to correction—which also explains why some accept Wisdom's call while others walk away. Whoever corrects a mocker invites insult and abuse. By contrast, "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you; rebuke a wise man, and he will love you"—instruct the wise and they grow wiser still; teach the righteous and they add to their learning. Then the author repeats the great motto of the entire book like a clasp closing the first nine chapters: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and knowledge of the Holy One is understanding." Whoever chooses wisdom gains benefit and added years; whoever chooses mockery bears the cost alone (9:7–12).

The final six verses (9:13–18) present the dark mirror image of the first six: Lady Folly. She is loud, undisciplined, and knows nothing. Instead of working to build a house or prepare a feast, she merely sits in the doorway on the city's high place trying to lure passersby off their straight paths. She repeats Wisdom's opening invitation word for word—calling out to the simple and those lacking sense to turn in—yet the menu she promises is completely different: "Stolen water is sweet, and bread eaten in secret is delicious!" What the guests fail to realize is chilling: her house is full of the dead, and her previous guests already lie deep in the depths of Sheol (9:13–18).

## Key Takeaways

**Life ultimately comes down to one choice: whose table will we sit at?** The nine-chapter introduction to Proverbs concludes with a crisp contrast that leaves no neutral middle ground: either step into Wisdom's house and live, or step into Folly's house and descend toward death.

**Wisdom takes patient preparation, while folly lives off stolen goods.** Lady Wisdom builds a seven-pillared house, prepares meat, mixes wine, and sets a banquet (9:1–2). Lady Folly prepares nothing at all, sitting at her door peddling stolen water and secret bread (9:14; 9:17). A wise life requires labor and discipline, whereas temptation always markets cheap, furtive thrills without building anything real.

**Our reaction to correction is the clearest measure of our character.** Verses 7–9 offer a very practical test: wise people feel gratitude when someone points out a flaw because they want to grow; mockers flare up in anger and strike back because they love their ego more than the truth.

**Each person bears personal responsibility for their choice.** Verse 12 puts it bluntly: if you are wise, your wisdom rewards you; if you are a mocker, you alone will suffer for it. Nobody can live wisely on someone else's behalf.

## Deeper Meaning

**Wisdom's banquet echoes in Jesus' parables of the kingdom wedding feast.** Just as Wisdom prepares a banquet of bread and wine and sends servants out through the city to invite guests (9:1–5), Jesus tells of a king who prepares a wedding feast for his son and sends servants out to the crossroads to summon people in (Matthew 22:1–10; Luke 14:15–24). And in John's Gospel, Jesus Himself declares that He is the bread of life given for the world (John 6:35).

**The warning about mockers connects with Jesus' teaching on discernment in sharing truth.** Verses 7–8 advise against wasting correction on determined scoffers only to invite abuse. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus states the same principle of wise discernment: do not throw your pearls before pigs, lest they trample them and turn to tear you to pieces (Matthew 7:6).

**The opening and closing frame of 1:7 and 9:10 keeps the entire book anchored in God.** The first nine chapters open with "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7) and close with "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (9:10). Thanks to that frame, none of the down-to-earth proverbs from chapter 10 onward are ever detached from relationship with the Holy One.

## Notes & Nuances

**The seven pillars of Wisdom's house (9:1) symbolize stability and completeness.** In Scripture, the number seven often denotes fullness and perfection; a house supported by seven pillars conjures up a spacious, grand, enduring hall able to welcome many guests—the opposite of Folly's shallow, empty lure. Early interpreters sometimes linked the seven pillars to the sevenfold gifts of the Spirit or the pillars of the cosmos, yet the simplest sense fitting the poem is a solid, generous, welcoming home.

**Why does verse 17 say *stolen water is sweet, and bread eaten in secret is delicious*?** The imagery picks up the cistern and well imagery for marital love in chapter 5 (5:15–18). Stolen water and secret bread point to the dark allure of forbidden pleasures, especially adultery and recklessness: secrecy makes the thrill feel sharper for a moment, yet in reality it is poison leading straight to death (9:18).
