# Proverbs 6 — Four Practical Warnings and the Fire of Adultery

## Story

Chapter 6 divides into two major parts. The first half (6:1–19) delivers four crisp, down-to-earth warnings about common traps of daily life: reckless cosigning of debts, laziness, deceitful troublemaking, and seven things the LORD hates. The second half (6:20–35) returns to the father's sustained lecture warning his son about the irreversible fallout of adultery.

The first warning addresses putting up security for a neighbor's or stranger's debt. Whoever shakes hands to guarantee someone else's loan has snared himself by his own spoken pledge. The father urges immediate action: do not delay, do not sleep on it, but humble yourself and plead urgently to be released from the commitment—escaping as fast as a gazelle from a hunter's hand or a bird from a trapper's snare (6:1–5).

The second warning speaks straight to the sluggard, assigning him a tiny instructor: "Go to the ant, O sluggard; consider her ways and be wise!" Without any chief, officer, or overseer driving her on, the ant diligently stores up provisions in summer and gathers food at harvest. Meanwhile the sluggard lounges around muttering, "A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest"—and suddenly poverty bursts in like a robber and scarcity like an armed man, leaving no chance to recover (6:6–11).

The third warning sketches a portrait of a worthless, scheming troublemaker: lying lips, winking eyes, shuffling feet, gesturing fingers, a heart constantly plotting evil and sowing discord. Calamity will crash down on him suddenly, breaking him beyond repair (6:12–15). Right on its heels comes the famous numbered proverb: "There are six things that the LORD hates, seven that are detestable to Him"—haughty eyes, a lying tongue, hands that shed innocent blood, a heart that devises wicked schemes, feet quick to run to evil, a false witness who pours out lies, and as the seventh climax, one who stirs up conflict among brothers (6:16–19).

From verse 20 to the end of the chapter, the father resumes his warm, urgent voice. Keep a father's command and a mother's teaching bound deep in the heart, for they will guide you when you walk, watch over you when you sleep, and speak with you when you wake; after all, "this commandment is a lamp, this teaching is a light" that guards you from the smooth tongue of an adulterous woman (6:20–24). Do not lust after her beauty or be captured by her eyes. The father asks two rhetorical questions whose answers everyone knows: "Can a man embrace fire and his clothes not be burned?" And can anyone walk on hot coals without scorching his feet? Touching another man's wife is just the same (6:25–29).

Finally, he compares a thief with an adulterer. People can understand to some degree why a starving man steals to fill his stomach, and even if caught he can repay the loss with money. An adulterer, however, destroys his own soul: his wounds and disgrace will never be wiped away, and the furious jealousy of a betrayed husband will accept no compensation or bribe (6:30–35).

## Key Takeaways

**Wise living begins with taking responsibility for our promises and daily work.** Proverbs refuses to separate spirituality from money and labor. Recklessly guaranteeing loans (6:1–5) and putting off work little by little (6:6–11) are both ways people walk themselves into ruin.

**A sluggard's poverty comes not from one dramatic choice, but from repeating the word *a little*.** The lazy person in verse 10 never announces that he is quitting work forever; he only asks for a little more sleep, a little more folding of the hands. Tiny daily indulgences pile up until collapse arrives seemingly out of nowhere.

**God deeply hates stirring up division among brothers.** In the list of seven things the LORD hates (6:16–19), the climactic seventh spot is held not by some exotic crime, but by the person who poisons harmony inside a family or community.

**Adultery is like holding fire against your chest: you cannot play with it without getting burned.** The pictures of embracing fire and walking on hot coals (6:27–28) shatter the illusion that *if we keep it secret, nobody gets hurt*. Adultery destroys trust, honor, and life's most precious bonds in ways money can never repair (6:32–35).

## Deeper Meaning

**God's Word is a lamp lighting the believer's way.** Verse 23 declares that the commandment is a lamp and the teaching is a light, directly echoing the famous line in the Psalms: Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path (Psalms 119:105). When the heart is filled with that light, the shadows of temptation lose their power to deceive.

**The call to diligent work carries straight into the New Testament.** The ant's self-motivated industry without an overseer (6:6–8) parallels the apostle Paul's charge to believers to work quietly and earn their own bread, warning that anyone unwilling to work should not eat (2 Thessalonians 3:10–12).

**God loves unity and hates division within His people.** The seventh item in the list of what God detests—sowing conflict among brothers (6:19)—explains why the New Testament repeatedly urges believers to maintain the unity of the Spirit (Ephesians 4:3) and to reject divisive people in the church (Titus 3:10).

## Notes & Nuances

**The warning against guaranteeing loans (6:1–5) does not forbid helping the poor.** Scripture consistently commands generous lending and compassion toward people in need (19:17; Deuteronomy 15:7–8). What 6:1–5 warns against is naively signing away legal responsibility for debts beyond your means—especially for strangers or risky ventures—putting your own household in danger of losing everything.

**The pattern *six things... seven things* (6:16) is a classic poetic device in the Old Testament.** This *X, even X+1* numerical pattern (cf. 30:15–31; Amos 1:3) does not mean the poet lost count; it creates poetic rhythm and spotlights the final item (here, the sower of discord) as the climax of the whole list.

**Sevenfold repayment (6:31) expresses the fullest possible penalty.** Under the Law of Moses, a thief typically repaid double, fourfold, or fivefold depending on what was stolen (Exodus 22:1–4). Here the number seven symbolizes *complete repayment, even up to everything in his house*—underscoring that even the heaviest penalty for theft can still be settled with property, unlike the ruin of adultery.
