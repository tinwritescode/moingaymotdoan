# Proverbs 30 — The Words of Agur and the Numerical Proverbs

## Story

Chapter 30 introduces a new voice beyond Solomon: "the words of Agur son of Jakeh," addressed to Ithiel and Ucal. He opens with startling humility, confessing that he feels more ignorant than anyone else, lacking human insight and full knowledge of the Holy One. Then he unleashes a majestic sequence of questions echoing the book of Job: who has ascended to heaven and come down, who has gathered the wind in his fists, who has wrapped the waters in a cloak, who has established all the ends of the earth—what is his name, and what is the name of his son? Set directly over against human smallness stands the flawless reliability of God's word: every word of God is tested and pure, He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him, so never add anything to His words lest He rebuke you and expose you as a liar (30:1–6).

Next comes the only prayer in the entire book of Proverbs—brief yet remarkably profound. Agur asks God for two gifts before he dies: first, keep falsehood and deceit far away; second, give him neither poverty nor riches, feeding him only the bread that is his daily portion. He explains both dangers clearly: with too much wealth he might grow full and deny God, asking *Who is the LORD?*; with grinding poverty he might steal and bring disgrace upon the name of his God (30:7–9).

From verse 10 onward, the chapter unfolds a dazzling chain of observations, most framed as *numerical proverbs* grouped in fours. After a brief warning not to slander a servant to a master, Agur sketches four troubling types of people: those who curse father and fail to bless mother, those pure in their own eyes yet unwashed from their filth, those with haughty eyes and lifted brows, and those whose teeth are swords and knives devouring the poor. Next appears the leech with two daughters crying only *Give! Give!*, opening into four insatiable things: Sheol, a barren womb, parched land thirsty for water, and fire. Tucked in between sits a chilling warning that ravens and young eagles will peck out the eye that mocks a father and scorns obeying a mother (30:10–17).

The final four numerical sets sweep from wonders of creation to human society. Four marvels exceed comprehension: the path of an eagle through the sky, a snake slithering across a rock, a ship's wake through open sea, and the way of a man with a young woman—immediately contrasted with the brazen adulteress who wipes her mouth and claims she has done nothing wrong. Four social upheavals make the earth tremble: a servant becoming king, a fool gorged with food, an unloved woman finally married, and a maidservant displacing her mistress. Conversely, four tiny creatures display extraordinary wisdom: frail ants storing summer food, feeble rock badgers making homes in the cliffs, leaderless locusts marching in orderly ranks, and small lizards slipping right into royal palaces. Four figures stride with stately dignity: a lion retreating before nothing, a strutting rooster or warhorse, a male goat leading the herd, and a king with his army around him. The chapter closes with practical advice on checking anger: if you have foolishly exalted yourself or plotted harm, clap your hand over your mouth—for churning milk yields butter, twisting a nose draws blood, and stirring up anger surely sparks strife (30:18–33).

## Key Takeaways

**Knowing how much we do not know is the doorway to true wisdom.** Agur never parades his brilliance; he starts by admitting the smallness of human intellect before the Creator (30:2–4), turning from there to cling to God's tested, flawless word (30:5–6).

**Living with "just enough" protects a whole heart before God.** The prayer in 30:7–9 is a model of spiritual realism: extreme wealth and extreme poverty each carry real hazards for faith. Asking for *enough* reflects someone who knows their own limits honestly.

**Small creatures can be great teachers of wisdom.** Agur looks not only up to the heavens, but down at ants, rock badgers, locusts, and lizards (30:24–28). Raw power is not the only key to thriving; foresight, choosing sturdy shelter, teamwork, and steady persistence are genuine wisdom.

**Clap a hand over your mouth before stirring up strife.** The closing verses (30:32–33) are wonderfully direct: the instant you catch pride or provocation rising inside, cover your mouth before anger gets churned into full-blown conflict.

## Deeper Meaning

**Who has ascended to heaven and come down—and what is His Son's name?** The questions in Proverbs 30:4 originally highlight how utterly incomparable God is. In the New Testament, Jesus uses remarkably close language when speaking to Nicodemus: no one has ascended into heaven except the One who descended from heaven—the Son of Man (John 3:13). The question *what is his son's name?* also leads Christian readers to Christ, the Son of God, who commands wind and sea (Mark 4:39–41) and makes the Father known to humanity (John 1:18).

**Daily bread for each day.** Agur's plea "feed me with the bread that is my portion" (30:8) rings directly in the Lord's Prayer Jesus taught His disciples: give us this day our daily bread (Matthew 6:11), as well as Paul's reminder that godliness with contentment is great gain (1 Timothy 6:6–8).

**God's pure word, neither added to nor taken away from.** Verses 30:5–6 link back to Moses' charge in Deuteronomy not to add to or subtract from what God commands (Deuteronomy 4:2), and echo again in the closing lines of the Bible in Revelation (Revelation 22:18–19).

**Religious self-satisfaction.** The portrait in 30:12—"those who are pure in their own eyes and yet unwashed of their filth"—mirrors the exact condition of the Pharisees in the Gospels, who scrubbed the outside of cup and dish while staying full of greed and wickedness inside (Matthew 23:25–26).

## Notes & Nuances

**Who was Agur?** Scripture gives us no further biographical detail about Agur son of Jakeh or his listeners Ithiel and Ucal (30:1). He may have been a sage outside Solomon's direct circle who shared Israel's faith in the LORD, included in the closing chapters of Proverbs for his deep insight. Because Hebrew manuscripts were originally written without vowels, some modern translations read the consonants behind *Ithiel and Ucal* as a weary exclamation—*"I am weary, O God, I am weary and worn out."*

**The numerical proverb pattern ("three… even four…").** Chapter 30 repeatedly employs a classic ancient Near Eastern poetic device: *there are three things… even four…* (30:15; 30:18; 30:21; 30:29), alongside the simpler *four things* in 30:24. Stepping up by one is not a math correction, but a rhythmic literary technique that aids memory and puts climactic emphasis on the fourth item.

**A couple of creatures in 30:26–31.** The animal older translations render as *coney* or *rock rabbit* (BSB: *rock badgers*) in 30:26 is actually the rock hyrax, a small guinea-pig-like mammal living in rocky crevices across the Middle East. And in 30:31, a rare Hebrew phrase meaning *girt in the loins* is rendered variously across translations as a *strutting rooster* (BSB), a *warhorse*, or a *greyhound*.

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor of the 17th and 18th centuries, regarded Agur's prayer in 30:7–9 as one of the wisest prayers in Scripture.
