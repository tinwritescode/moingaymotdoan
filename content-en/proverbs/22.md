# Proverbs 22 — A Good Name and the Words of the Wise

## Story

The first half of chapter 22 brings Solomon's main collection of proverbs to a close with crisp sayings on true value and social fairness. A good name is more desirable than great riches, and favor is better than silver and gold; though the rich and the poor stand at opposite ends of society, the LORD is the Maker of them all. Humility and the fear of the LORD bring wealth, honor, and life; whoever has a generous eye and shares bread with the poor will be blessed. By contrast, debt turns the borrower into the lender's slave, whoever sows injustice reaps calamity, and anyone who oppresses the poor or bribes the rich ends up in poverty (22:1–2; 22:4; 22:7–9; 22:16).

This opening half also gathers counsel on foresight, parenting, and upright conduct. The prudent see danger ahead and take refuge, while the simple keep going and pay the penalty; the path of the crooked is lined with thorns and snares. Parents are urged to train up a child in the way they should go so that even in old age they will not depart from it, paired with loving correction because folly is bound up in a child's heart. Drive out a mocker and strife vanishes; whoever loves a pure heart and speaks with grace will have the king as a friend. The eyes of the LORD guard knowledge while overturning the words of the unfaithful; meanwhile, the sluggard invents a lion in the street to dodge work, and the mouth of an adulteress is a deep pit of ruin (22:3; 22:5–6; 22:10–15).

At verse 22:17, Proverbs shifts into a new section titled *the words of the wise*, running through the end of chapter 24. Instead of standalone one-line contrasts, the voice turns into a teacher addressing a student directly as *I* and *you*: incline your ear and store these words in your heart so that your trust may be in the LORD, equipping you to grasp reliable truth and bring back a clear answer to the one who sent you (22:17–21).

The opening lessons of this collection are strikingly concrete and practical. Do not exploit the poor or crush the needy at the city gate, because the LORD Himself will plead their case. Do not make friends with a hot-tempered, angry person lest you pick up their ways; do not foolishly pledge security for another's debt or your very bed may be taken from under you. Do not move an ancient boundary stone set by your ancestors to steal land. Finally, anyone skilled and diligent in their work will serve before kings rather than stand before obscure people (22:22–29).

## Key Takeaways

**Rich and poor differ in wallet size, yet stand equal before the Creator.** "The rich and the poor have this in common: The LORD is Maker of them all" (22:2). That single truth strips away the arrogance of wealth and upholds the dignity of the poor.

**Reputation outweighs wealth, and freedom outweighs debt.** "A good name is more desirable than great riches" (22:1), while "the borrower is slave to the lender" (22:7). Keeping your word and living within your means preserve both liberty and self-respect.

**God is the advocate of the vulnerable.** Verses 22:22–23 confront bullying the poor at the city gate—the courtroom of ancient towns. The poor may not afford a defender, yet the LORD steps in personally to argue their case.

**Mastery of craft opens doors to high service.** Verse 22:29 honors diligence and professional excellence. Faith and wisdom never stand apart from competence; doing our work thoroughly is one way we serve both God and our community.

## Deeper Meaning

**God loves a cheerful giver.** Verse 22:9 pronounces blessing on the generous person who shares food with the poor. The ancient Greek translation adds a line immediately before this verse that Paul echoes in 2 Corinthians when urging the church to give freely, because God loves a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:7).

**God defends the oppressed.** The picture of the LORD taking up the cause of the poor in 22:22–23 carries straight into the New Testament. James condemns wealthy landowners who withhold harvesters' wages and declares that their cries have reached the ears of the Lord (James 5:4).

**Beware hot-tempered companions and corrupting environments.** The counsel not to associate with an angry person lest you learn their ways (22:24–25) is summed up by Paul as a principle for the church: bad company corrupts good character (1 Corinthians 15:33).

**Every word of wisdom aims at trust in God.** Verse 22:19 states the goal of *the words of the wise* plainly: "So that your trust may be in the LORD." In the same way, all Scripture is given to lead people into saving faith in God and in Christ (2 Timothy 3:15).

## Notes & Nuances

**Reading 22:6 on raising children rightly.** "Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it." Many parents read this as an ironclad guarantee, and then grieve in crushing self-blame if a grown child chooses a different road. Like other proverbs throughout the book, this states a general principle about the lasting power of early formation, not a mechanical formula that overrides an adult child's free will.

**The collection from 22:17 to 24:34 and the Egyptian "Instruction of Amenemope."** Since the 20th century, scholars have noticed clear parallels between Proverbs 22:17 through 24:22 and an ancient Egyptian wisdom work called *The Instruction of Amenemope*—including warnings against robbing the poor, befriending hot-tempered people, and moving ancient boundary stones, along with the number thirty, since the Hebrew word in 22:20 can be rendered *thirty sayings* (as in the BSB and many modern translations) or *noble things* (as in older traditional versions). Stating the views fairly, most scholars think the Israelite compiler knew this Egyptian wisdom tradition and adapted it selectively; a smaller group of more conservative scholars suggest both drew from a shared ancient Near Eastern wisdom heritage, or that Amenemope drew from Israelite material. Most important of all, Proverbs never copies blindly—it anchors every saying in trust and reverence for the LORD (22:19; 22:23).

**City gates and ancient boundary stones.** In ancient Israel, the city gate was where town elders sat to hear legal disputes (22:22). *Boundary stones* (22:28) marked ancestral family plots; secretly shifting those stones meant stealing a neighbor's livelihood, an act strictly forbidden by the Law of Moses (Deuteronomy 19:14).

## What Commentators Say

**Matthew Henry**, an English pastor of the 17th and 18th centuries, viewed 22:6 as an urgent parental responsibility: bending a sapling while it is tender is far easier than trying to straighten a hardened tree. On 22:2, he stressed that economic differences give the rich an arena to practice generosity and the poor an arena for patience, since both stand before the same Maker. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, Proverbs chapter 22)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: these expositions of 22:2 and 22:6 are very characteristic of Matthew Henry; exact wording not verified -->

**Derek Kidner**, a 20th-century British Old Testament scholar, discussed the connection between Proverbs 22:17 through 24:22 and *The Instruction of Amenemope* thoughtfully. He observed fairly that even if Israelite sages drew upon Egyptian material, they purified it and set it in the light of the fear of the LORD, because all truth everywhere belongs to God. *(Proverbs, Tyndale Old Testament Commentaries, Introduction and chapter 22 verses 17–21)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Kidner addresses Amenemope by acknowledging parallels while emphasizing Israel's theological transformation of the material; page number not verified -->

**Bruce K. Waltke**, an American Old Testament scholar, noted that 22:6 is a proverb rather than an unconditional covenant promise, and analyzed the *thirty sayings* structure introduced in 22:17–21. In his view, aiming the whole collection at "so that your trust may be in the LORD" (22:19) proves biblical wisdom always joins everyday ethics to a living relationship with God. *(The Book of Proverbs, NICOT, vol. 2, chapter 22)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: reading 22:6 as a general principle and 22:20 as thirty sayings is prominent in Waltke's NICOT commentary; exact text not verified -->

**Charles Bridges**, a 19th-century Anglican minister, paused at 22:22–23 to warn against abusing power over the weak: the poor may have no voice in earthly courts, yet God Himself steps forward as their advocate. *(An Exposition of the Book of Proverbs, chapter 22 verses 22–23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: pastoral emphasis on protecting the poor in 22:22–23 aligns closely with Bridges; exact wording not verified -->
