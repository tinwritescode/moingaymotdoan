# Ezekiel 17 — Two Eagles and the Tender Cedar Sprig

## Story

At the close of chapter 16, God rebuked Jerusalem for despising her oath and breaking His covenant (16:59). In chapter 17, God shifts His manner of speaking. Addressing Ezekiel once again as "son of man," He commands him to pose a "riddle" and tell a "parable" to the house of Israel. The story opens on Mount Lebanon with "a great eagle with great wings and long pinions, full of feathers of many colors." It swoops down, breaks off the topmost shoot of a cedar tree, carries that young twig away to "a land of merchants," and sets it down in "a city of traders" (17:1–4).

Next, the eagle takes a seed of the land and plants it in fertile soil beside abundant waters, like a willow tree. The seed sprouts into a low, spreading grapevine; its branches turn toward the eagle while its roots stay beneath it. Putting forth shoots and sending out branches, it thrives quietly in its own planted bed (17:5–6).

Then a second great eagle appears, likewise with broad wings and dense plumage. Even though the vine was already planted in rich soil beside plentiful water to grow into a noble vine, it bends its roots and stretches its branches toward this new eagle, hoping to be watered by it. God asks directly: will that vine prosper? Will the first eagle not return to rip up its roots, strip off its fruit, and wither all its fresh foliage? Pulling it up will take neither a mighty arm nor a huge crowd; the instant the scorching east wind hits it, it will shrivel right on the bed where it grew (17:7–10).

Having finished the fable, God tells Ezekiel to ask the "rebellious house" whether they understand the riddle—and then He interprets it Himself. The first eagle is the king of Babylon: he came to Jerusalem, captured its king and officials, and carried them off to Babylon. Then he selected someone from the royal line, entered into a covenant with him, put him under a binding oath of loyalty, and deported the leading men of the land so Judah would remain a humble kingdom unable to exalt itself—yet guaranteed to survive so long as it kept the covenant (17:11–14).

That new king, Zedekiah, rebelled against Babylon's king anyway, sending envoys to Egypt—the second eagle—begging for horses and a large army. God challenges him: can a man who breaks a covenant expect to escape? God swears by His own life that Zedekiah will die right in Babylon, in the capital of the very king who placed him on the throne. Pharaoh and his mighty host will prove powerless to save him when Babylonian troops build ramps and siege works around the city. Zedekiah "gave his hand in pledge" and still broke faith, so he certainly will not escape (17:15–18).

At this point the indictment intensifies sharply. God no longer calls it merely an oath to Babylon; He calls it "My oath" and "My covenant." The LORD Himself will bring that guilt squarely down on Zedekiah's head: "I will spread My net over him, and he will be caught in My snare," bringing him to Babylon to stand trial for his treachery against God. His fleeing troops will fall by the sword and the survivors will be scattered to every wind, so the people will know that the LORD Himself has spoken (17:19–21).

Right amid all this uprooting and withering, the chapter suddenly pivots into a radiant promise. The LORD Himself will take a shoot from the very top of the lofty cedar, "break off a tender sprig," and plant it with His own hand upon a high and lofty mountain of Israel. That tender sprig will put out boughs, bear fruit, and grow into a majestic cedar so wide that "birds of every kind will nest in it." Then every tree of the field will know that the LORD is the One who "brings down the tall tree and exalts the low tree," who "dries up the green tree and makes the dry tree flourish" (17:22–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Keeping an oath matters vitally before God, even when sworn to a pagan ruler.** Judeans easily told themselves that Nebuchadnezzar was an invader, so breaking an oath to him carried no sin. God rejects that reasoning outright. Zedekiah had sworn his oath in the name of the LORD (2 Chronicles 36:13); breaking it insulted God's own name (17:19).

**Political scheming cannot rescue anyone fighting God's discipline.** The vine already sat in fertile soil beside abundant water—meaning Judah still had a path to survival if it accepted humility and kept its pledge (17:8; 17:14). Turning to Egypt for rescue only accelerated disaster (17:17).

**Behind the clash of empires stands the sovereign hand of God.** To human eyes, Jerusalem fell simply because Babylon outweighed Egypt. Ezekiel pulls back the curtain: the hunter throwing the net over Zedekiah is not merely the Chaldean army, but the LORD Himself (17:20).

**Hope does not sprout from human strategy, but from the tender sprig God plants Himself.** Every scheme of Jerusalem's royal court dries up and dies. Into that dead end, God declares that "I Myself" will plant a new sprig on Israel's high mountain, overturning every measure of earthly power (17:22–24).

## Deeper Meaning

**An allegory matching the exact history of Judah's final kings.** In 597 BC, the king of Babylon besieged Jerusalem, deported King Jehoiachin (the cedar's crown) along with the nobility to Babylon, and installed Jehoiachin's uncle Mattaniah as king, renaming him Zedekiah (2 Kings 24:12–17). Chronicles explicitly records that Nebuchadnezzar made Zedekiah "swear an oath in God's name," yet Zedekiah stiffened his neck and rebelled (2 Chronicles 36:13). Sure enough, Pharaoh's army marched out briefly and then retreated home (Jeremiah 37:5–8); Zedekiah was captured, blinded, and hauled to Babylon—fulfilling both the net imagery here and the earlier prophecy that he would die in Babylon without ever seeing it (12:13; 2 Kings 25:6–7).

**Integrity before God crosses national boundaries.** The principle holding Zedekiah to his pledge to a foreign monarch runs right through the Old Testament. In Joshua's day, even after the Gibeonites tricked Israel into signing a peace treaty, Israel's leaders refused to attack them because they had sworn in the name of the LORD (Joshua 9:18–20). Likewise, the psalmist describes the person welcome in God's presence as one who "keeps his oath even when it hurts" (Psalms 15:4).

**The tender cedar sprig points to the Messiah.** God does not salvage Zedekiah's faithless vine; instead, He breaks a "tender sprig" off the lofty cedar crown carried away to Babylon—that is, the exiled Davidic line of Jehoiachin (17:22). A tiny shoot sprouting from a felled tree is a classic messianic emblem: "a shoot will spring up from the stump of Jesse" (Isaiah 11:1), growing up "like a tender shoot" out of dry ground (Isaiah 53:2), and "a righteous Branch" raised up for David (Jeremiah 23:5).

**A sheltering tree for every bird and the Kingdom of Heaven.** God's planted cedar grows so vast that "birds of every kind will nest in it" (17:23)—picturing the nations finding refuge beneath God's kingdom. In the New Testament, Jesus borrows this exact image in the parable of the tiny mustard seed growing into a tree "so that the birds of the air come and nest in its branches" (Matthew 13:31–32). Christ's kingdom begins unobtrusively yet spreads wide to shelter all peoples.

**The God who reverses earthly power.** The closing declaration that God brings down the tall tree and lifts up the lowly tree (17:24) resonates directly in Mary's song of praise upon conceiving Jesus: "He has brought down rulers from their thrones, but has exalted the humble" (Luke 1:52).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does God seem to take the side of Babylonian invaders?** This chapter never praises Babylon's empire; rather, it reminds Judah that Babylon's yoke was divine discipline for generations of idolatry and injustice. Under those conditions, keeping the oath to Babylon meant submitting humbly to God's discipline and honoring God's name, whereas courting Egypt meant running back to an earthly crutch God had repeatedly forbidden.

**Cedar and vine represent two contrasting destinies.** The cedar of Lebanon represents the royal house of David in its former majesty (with Jehoiachin as its topmost shoot), while the low grapevine represents Zedekiah's vassal reign. At the chapter's close, God does not revive that faithless vine, but plants a fresh tender sprig drawn straight from the royal cedar itself (17:22).

**What does "the east wind" symbolize?** To Israelites, the east wind was the scorching hot desert blast that parched crops and vegetation almost overnight. In Ezekiel, the east wind frequently pictures Babylon's army sweeping down on Judah with devastating heat (17:10).

**The link binding chapters 16, 17, and 18.** Chapter 16 indicted Jerusalem for breaking covenant in worship; chapter 17 exposes the exact same covenant-breaking disease in the king's political oath. And the exiles' bitter frustration over watching their entire nation ruined by royal folly opens directly into chapter 18's frank conversation about individual responsibility.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, read chapter 17 on two sequential levels. He expounded the first portion strictly historically: the two eagles are Babylon and Egypt, the cedar's crown is Jehoiachin, and the vine is Zedekiah. In the closing verses, he saw a prophecy of Christ springing from David's house, with the lofty cedar on the high mountain representing the Church where all nations—the birds of the air—find shade. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 17)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's combination of historical exposition in the first section and Christological reading in the closing verses of chapter 17 is well remembered; exact book number among his fourteen books not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, dwelled at length on Zedekiah's perjury in verses 15–19. Even though Nebuchadnezzar was a pagan king and Zedekiah swore under duress, Calvin argued that once God's name was invoked, the oath bound his conscience; violating it dishonored God Himself. On the final three verses, Calvin saw the tender sprig as Christ's kingdom, starting small yet growing to shelter the nations. *(Lectures on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of Ezekiel, on chapter 17)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's reasoning on the binding force of an oath sworn to a pagan king and his reading of the tender sprig in chapter 17 are well remembered; specific lecture number not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew a clear takeaway from God calling Zedekiah's treaty "My oath": God keeps close watch over treaties among rulers, and no political advantage ever excuses breaking faith. Viewing the cedar at the chapter's close, he saw a clear promise of the Messiah spreading the shelter of His kingdom to welcome Gentiles. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 17)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar points on oath-breaking and the messianic cedar in chapter 17 are well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, renowned Jewish Old Testament scholar, noticed how Ezekiel uses a botanical riddle in the first ten verses to withhold the names of the two eagles and the vine. By doing so, he draws listeners into judging for themselves how absurd it was for a well-watered vine in good soil to twist away toward a second eagle—before abruptly revealing their own identity and tying straight back to chapter 16's covenant betrayal. *(Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's structural analysis of the riddle and its narrative bond with chapter 16 is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, American Old Testament professor, placed verse 19 squarely in the world of ancient Near Eastern vassal treaties: the deity invoked in a treaty oath stood as both witness and enforcer whenever a vassal broke faith. Because Zedekiah swore by the LORD, the LORD Himself stepped in to execute sentence—ensuring that Babylon's triumph never diminished the supremacy of Israel's God. Block also stressed that God plants a shoot from the cedar (Jehoiachin's line) rather than the vine (Zedekiah) to honor His enduring promise to David's house. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 1–24, NICOT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's ancient Near Eastern vassal-treaty background and contrast between cedar and vine are well remembered; exact page numbers not verified -->
