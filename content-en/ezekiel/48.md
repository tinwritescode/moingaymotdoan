# Ezekiel 48 — Allotting the Land to Twelve Tribes and the City's New Name

## Story

Chapter 48 brings the entire book of Ezekiel to a majestic close with a detailed territorial map for all twelve tribes of Israel and a glorious brand-new name crowning the central city. Completely unlike Joshua's ancient patchwork of uneven hills and mismatched shares, the whole promised land now divides into neat, level east-to-west bands spanning from eastern border to western coast. Starting at the far northern edge, seven tribes line up southward in order: Dan bordering Hamath and Damascus, followed by Asher, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, Reuben, and Judah (48:1–7).

Flush against Judah's southern edge sits a dedicated central district offered to the LORD—a crisp square measuring twenty-five thousand cubits per side, partitioned into three parallel strips. The northernmost band (twenty-five thousand by ten thousand) forms the "sacred portion" allotted to the Zadokite priests—those who stood faithful when Israel and other Levites strayed—with "the sanctuary of the LORD" centered right in its heart (48:8–12). Directly below runs the second band (likewise twenty-five thousand by ten thousand) reserved for the Levites, strictly barring them from selling, trading, or transferring the land's choice produce because it is set apart as holy to the LORD (48:13–14).

The third strip along the district's southern rim (twenty-five thousand by five thousand) serves as "common property" housing the city, dwellings, and pastureland. Centered neatly inside stands the city itself—a flawless square measuring four thousand five hundred cubits per side, ringed all around by two-hundred-and-fifty-cubit greenbelt pastures. Flanking the city on both east and west stretch ten-thousand-cubit farmlands worked by "the workers of the city, from all the tribes of Israel," supplying food for every resident (48:15–20). Meanwhile, the remaining stretches flanking east and west of the entire central square all the way to the country's eastern and western borders form "the portion of the prince," framed cleanly between Judah on the north and Benjamin on the south (48:21–22).

South of that central heartland, the remaining five tribes receive matching east-to-west strips marching southward: Benjamin, Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun, and finally Gad reaching down to the southern border at Tamar, Meribath-kadesh, and the Wadi of Egypt. God confirms these are the exact inheritances to be allotted to Israel's tribes (48:23–29).

The book's closing six verses bring our eyes to rest on the square walls of the new city. Twelve gates pierce its perimeter—three gates along every four-thousand-five-hundred-cubit side bearing the names of all twelve tribes of Israel: on the north, the gates of Reuben, Judah, and Levi; on the east, Joseph, Benjamin, and Dan; on the south, Simeon, Issachar, and Zebulun; on the west, Gad, Asher, and Naphtali (48:30–34). The city's total circumference measures eighteen thousand cubits. Then the final sentence of Ezekiel rings out like a triumphant bell crowning a forty-eight-chapter journey from exile's grief to eternal restoration: from that day onward, the city sheds its old name to bear a brand-new title—*The LORD Is There* (48:35).

## Key Takeaways

**Inside God's restored kingdom, historic divisions heal in complete equality.** Chapter 48's map gives every tribe a parallel east-to-west band of equal dignity, sweeping away centuries of land disputes and rivalry (48:1–7; 48:23–28). Most movingly, all twelve tribes—including the ten northern tribes scattered long ago under Assyria—receive full shares and send citizens side by side to serve the shared central city (48:19).

**God's living presence stands as the center uniting the whole nation.** Chapter 48's entire geography orbits one single axis: "the sanctuary of the LORD will be in the middle of it" (48:8; 48:10; 48:21). God's house neither sits pushed to the margins nor shares a cramped wall with a royal palace where kings abuse power; instead, the prince's holdings flank the edges to shield the center while every tribe faces inward toward God.

**The new city belongs to every tribe, never to one privileged faction.** Historically, Jerusalem straddled Judah and Benjamin's border; now the renewed city sits inside shared territory, opens twelve gates carrying all twelve tribal names (including Levi and Joseph), and welcomes citizens from every tribe to work and live within it (48:18–19; 48:31–34). Three gates face every point of the compass so nobody ever feels shut out at the fringe.

**Scripture's ultimate hope lies not in towering city walls, but in God dwelling with His people.** Across nine detailed chapters packed with cubits, courts, and boundaries, the entire masterpiece converges onto four closing words in 48:35: *The LORD Is There* (Hebrew *YHWH Shammah*). A holy city's true glory never shines from dressed stone or gold leaf, but from the living God abiding forever in the midst of His people.

## Deeper Meaning

**Ezekiel's grand arc completed: from departing glory to *The LORD Is There*.** Setting 48:35 right beside chapter 10 reveals the breathtaking sweep of the entire book. Back in chapters 8–11, Ezekiel watched heartbroken as the LORD's glory withdrew step by slow step from the Most Holy Place to the threshold, out the east gate, and clear off the sinful city (10:18–19). In chapter 43, he beheld that same glory blaze back through the east gate filling the temple with the promise, "Here I will dwell among the Israelites forever" (43:2–5; 43:7). Now on the final line of chapter 48, that promise becomes the city's permanent name: *The LORD Is There* (48:35).

**The square twelve-gated city leading straight into Revelation's New Jerusalem.** Describing the Holy City descending out of heaven at the close of the New Testament, the apostle John draws his canvas directly from Ezekiel 48: a flawlessly symmetrical square "with twelve gates," inscribed with "the names of the twelve tribes of Israel: three gates on the east, three on the north, three on the south, and three on the west" (Revelation 21:12–16). Yet Revelation adds one crowning step beyond Ezekiel: whereas Ezekiel 48 still sets the civic city slightly south of the sanctuary, John marvels, "I saw no temple in the city, because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" (Revelation 21:22).

**From *YHWH Shammah* to *Immanuel*—God with us.** The closing title of 48:35 (*The LORD Is There*) finds its living fulfillment in Jesus Christ. Opening Matthew's Gospel, the angel announces that the newborn child will be called "Immanuel—which means, 'God with us'" (Matthew 1:23). Closing Matthew's Gospel, the risen Jesus pledges, "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (Matthew 28:20). And on the Bible's next-to-last chapter, a loud voice from the throne seals Ezekiel 48:35 forever: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man, and He will dwell with them. They will be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God" (Revelation 21:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**The meaning of the city's closing name *The LORD Is There* (48:35).** In Hebrew, the new name reads *YHWH Shammah* ("the LORD is there"). Remarkably, *Shammah* chimes closely in sound with the old name *Jerusalem* (*Yerushalayim*), creating a deliberate poetic renaming: rather than keeping an old name scarred by centuries of rebellion, the renewed city takes its identity straight from God's unbroken presence.

**A fascinating family design hides inside the tribal arrangement of chapter 48.** Comparing the land order in 48:1–7 and 48:23–28 with Jacob's four wives (free wives Leah and Rachel, plus handmaids Bilhah and Zilpah) reveals careful symmetry: the honored spots hugging both borders of the central sacred district go to two sons of free wives—**Judah** to the north (Leah's son) and **Benjamin** to the south (Rachel's son)—even swapping Judah and Benjamin's historical north-south positions to signal a brand-new order! Placed along the outer northern and southern frontiers are sons of the handmaids (Dan, Asher, Naphtali far north; Gad far south). Turning to the twelve city gates (48:31–34), because gates honor Jacob's twelve original sons rather than land allotments, Levi receives his own northern gate (48:31) while Ephraim and Manasseh reunite under their father's name at "the gate of Joseph" (48:32).

**Are the units in chapter 48 *rods* or *cubits*?** Some older translations supply "rods" across 48:8–21 and 48:30–35—where each six-cubit rod spans slightly over three meters (making a twenty-five-thousand-rod sacred block stretch over seventy-five kilometers, wider than Israel from Jordan to Mediterranean). In Hebrew, chapter 48 leaves the unit unstated beside bare figures like "twenty-five thousand," and most modern scholars and translations—including the BSB—supply *cubits* (roughly half a meter, yielding a practical 12.5-kilometer sacred square and a 2.25-kilometer-per-side city). Under either scale, chapter 48's immaculate geometry speaks far more as a theological vision than a surveyor's deed map.

**Why do temple and city sit slightly apart in chapter 48?** Historically, King Solomon built his palace flush against Jerusalem's temple wall, inviting royal tombs and pagan shrines right beside God's altar (43:7–8). Here in chapter 48, the civic city occupies the "common" southern strip (48:15), separated from the sanctuary by priestly and Levitical strips. Yet the stunning climax arrives at 48:35: the name *The LORD Is There* crowns the everyday **city** where people live and work—not merely the temple enclosure—foreshadowing God's holiness radiating out across all human life!

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, read the symmetrical twelve-tribe grid and twelve-gated city of chapter 48 as a radiant figure of the universal Church gathering peoples from east, west, north, and south. Closing on 48:35's title *The LORD Is There*, Jerome noted that this seals the promise of Christ's unbroken presence among His people to the end of the age, bringing every temple vision since chapter 40 to perfection. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 48)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's ecclesiological reading of chapter 48 and Christ's abiding presence in 48:35 are well remembered; exact book number and Latin text not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, lingered tenderly over the city's final name in 48:35—*The LORD Is There*. The highest glory of any city, church, or household, he wrote, never lies in thick walls or abundant wealth, but in God's living presence in their midst—for where God dwells, peace, holiness, and true joy abide. Henry likewise celebrated the twelve gates opening toward all four compass points (48:31–34) as a warm pledge that God's gates of grace stand wide open welcoming believers from every corner of earth. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 48)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral emphasis on the closing name in verse 35 and the twelve outward-facing gates in verses 31–34 is well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the 19th-century English preacher, preached on *Jehovah-Shammah* in 48:35 as a glowing summary of both Christian living and heavenly hope. Without God's presence, he declared, even the grandest sanctuary shrinks to an empty room; conversely, wherever *The LORD Is There*, believers walking through earthly trials discover strength, shelter, and a real foretaste of the New Jerusalem's eternal joy. *(Sermons on Ezekiel 48; specific sermon number unverified)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's sermons on Jehovah-Shammah in 48:35 linking God's presence in the Church and heaven are well remembered; exact sermon number not verified -->

**Daniel I. Block**, Canadian-American Old Testament professor, showed how chapter 48's hyper-symmetrical geography deliberately elevates equity and justice above rugged natural terrain. Twelve parallel bands wipe out north-south rivalries; fixed royal tracts prevent future oppression; and conferring *The LORD Is There* in 48:35 specifically upon the everyday civic city—not merely the sanctuary—completes Ezekiel's sweeping theological arc from glory fleeing Jerusalem in chapter 10 to God's presence saturating His covenant people's entire life. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 25–48, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Block's insights on equal tribal strips overcoming division, civic-city naming, and Ezekiel's overarching departure-to-presence arc at 48:35 are hallmarks of NICOT volume 2 -->
