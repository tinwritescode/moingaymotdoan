# Ezekiel 26 — Judgment on the City of Tyre

## Story

After pronouncing judgment in turn on Judah's four small neighbors in chapter 25—Ammon, Moab, Edom, and Philistia—the word of the LORD swings northwest to target one of the wealthiest maritime powers of the ancient world: the city of Tyre. This oracle reaches Ezekiel in "the eleventh year, on the first day of the month" of exile, right as Jerusalem is dying or has just fallen (26:1).

Tyre's guilt surfaces immediately through her own calculating cheer as Jerusalem collapses: "Aha! The gate to the nations is broken; it has swung open to me; now that she lies in ruins I will be filled." Seeing a rival along regional trade routes crumble, Tyre celebrates because profits will now funnel straight into her own pockets. God steps forward at once: "Behold, I am against you, O Tyre." He will raise up many nations against her, crashing in wave after wave "as the sea brings up its waves." Her strong walls will be demolished and her towers toppled; God will even scrape away her very soil until Tyre is nothing but "a bare rock," a place in the midst of the sea for spreading fishermen's nets and plunder for the nations. Her "daughters on the mainland"—Tyre's dependent coastal towns and villages—will fall by the sword, so they will know He is the LORD (26:2–6).

Moving from sweeping overview to battlefield specifics, the prophecy names the exact instrument: God will bring Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, titled "king of kings," marching from the north with horses, chariots, horsemen, and a massive army against Tyre (26:7). First he will put the mainland towns to the sword; then he will build siege works, raise ramps, lift shields against her, pound her walls with battering rams, and smash her towers with axes (26:8–9).

The Babylonian assault bursts alive with noise and dust: horses so numerous their dust blankets the city; walls shuddering at the rumble of horsemen, wagons, and chariots rolling through her gates like troops storming a breached fortress (26:10). Hooves trample every street, swords cut down citizens, and "your mighty pillars"—the proud monuments and stone columns symbolizing her grandeur—crash to the ground (26:11). Invaders loot her riches and merchandise, tear down walls and luxurious mansions, and heave stones, timber, and rubble straight into the sea. The lively sound of her songs and harps falls silent forever. Once again the iron sentence repeats: Tyre will become a bare rock for spreading nets and "will never be rebuilt" (26:12–14).

The next oracle sweeps its gaze across the Mediterranean coast to capture neighboring reactions. When the crash of Tyre's fall echoes alongside the groaning of the wounded and slaughter inside her walls, won't the coastlands tremble? Every prince of the sea steps down from his throne, lays aside royal robes and embroidered garments, clothes himself in terror, and sits on the bare ground like a mourner. Shaking moment by moment, they lift a lament over Tyre: "How you have perished, O city of renown, inhabited by seafaring men—she who was mighty on the sea, she and her inhabitants, who imposed their terror on all who lived there!" The distant coastlands shiver in dread on the day she vanishes (26:15–18).

The closing scene carries Tyre's fate past military defeat straight into the underworld. God declares He will make Tyre desolate like uninhabited towns, bringing up the waters of the deep to cover her completely (26:19). He will cast her down with "those who go down to the Pit"—the realm of the dead—to dwell among "the people of antiquity" in the lowest depths of the earth, amid ancient ruins, never to be inhabited again. Set starkly against that pitch-black abyss shines one short, radiant line: "and I will set glory in the land of the living." Tyre herself will become a horror, vanishing so utterly that though people hunt for her, she will never be found again (26:20–21).

## Key Takeaways

**Profiting off someone else's tragedy is a grave sin before God.** Tyre did not march armies against Jerusalem like Babylon did, yet her heart leaped when Jerusalem fell because she calculated all that trade would pour into her own ledgers (26:2). Scripture calls it plainly: cold indifference and profiteering from a neighbor's disaster are not "sharp business instincts," but callous greed that provokes God Himself to step forward as an adversary (26:3).

**Wealth and natural fortresses never make anyone untouchable.** Tyre boasted vast riches and an offshore island fortress ringed with high walls and towers that awed the Mediterranean (26:17). Yet under God's judgment, walls, palaces, cargo, and music shrink to a smooth rock good only for drying fishing nets (26:12–14). No economy or fortress can safeguard itself forever.

**Earthly empires remain under God's sovereign direction.** Nebuchadnezzar was an ambitious pagan conqueror, yet God declares that He Himself "will bring" him from the north as an instrument of judgment (26:7). Even geopolitical upheavals that appear utterly chaotic stay inside the grasp of history's true Ruler.

**When a hub of wealth crashes, panic ripples through everyone relying on it.** Coastal princes hurry off their thrones, strip off embroidered robes, and sit trembling on the dirt (26:16). That scene exposes how brittle alliances built on money and profit really are: onlookers weep for Tyre mostly because they see their own security and revenue collapsing alongside her.

## Deeper Meaning

**From historic friendship to coldhearted betrayal.** Hearing Tyre's gloating words in 26:2, Israelites could not help recalling an older story. Back in David and Solomon's day, King Hiram of Tyre had been Israel's warm friend, generously supplying Lebanon cedars and skilled craftsmen to build the Jerusalem temple and sealing a treaty of peace with Solomon (1 Kings 5:1–12). Over a century before Ezekiel, Amos already rebuked Tyre for "not remembering the covenant of brotherhood" and selling captive communities to Edom (Amos 1:9–10), while Isaiah too pronounced judgment on this boastful harbor city (Isaiah 23:1–8). By chapter 26, greed had erased every trace of former friendship.

**A prophecy candidly updated by Ezekiel himself sixteen years later.** One of the Old Testament's most remarkably honest details links chapter 26 directly with chapter 29. After Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for thirteen grueling years without winning spoils equal to his labor, God spoke again to Ezekiel in the twenty-seventh year: Babylon's troops worked so hard that every head was rubbed bare and every shoulder worn raw, yet he "received no wages from Tyre," so God would hand him Egypt as alternative compensation (29:17–20). Keeping both oracles side by side proves Ezekiel never doctored his record to look neat, faithfully preserving how God steers history stage by stage.

**Tyre's shadow over the fall of Babylon in Revelation.** Depicting the fall of Babylon the Great—emblem of arrogant global commerce and power defying God—the apostle John draws heavily from Ezekiel 26 and 27. Earthly kings, merchants, and seafarers stand far off weeping, throwing dust on their heads, and mourning a city that enriched the world yet crashed into ruin in a single hour (Revelation 18:9–19). Sixth-century BC Tyre becomes the prototype of every empire that puts profit above God.

**A brief glint of "the land of the living" and gospel mercy.** Right inside the funeral gloom of Tyre sinking into the Pit, verse 26:20 flashes a sudden promise—"I will set glory in the land of the living"—quietly hinting that once proud powers are laid low, God will restore life and glory to His own people. And the most startling sequel arrives in the Gospels: Jesus invokes Tyre itself while rebuking hardhearted Judean towns, declaring that if His miracles had been performed in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes (Matthew 11:21–22). Not even a city once under the sternest oracle lies outside the reach of grace.

## Notes & Nuances

**How was the prophecy concerning Nebuchadnezzar fulfilled in history?** This is the question readers ask most often in chapter 26. Ancient records confirm that Nebuchadnezzar really did besiege Tyre for thirteen years (roughly 585–572 BC). He devastated the mainland city, yet the fortified offshore island held out and eventually submitted to Babylonian overlordship by treaty rather than being razed completely—matching Ezekiel's own later confirmation that Babylon gained no real wages there (29:18). Over two centuries later, in 332 BC, Alexander the Great scraped stones, timber, and rubble from the mainland ruins into the sea to build a long causeway out to the island (cf. 26:12), storming and conquering island Tyre completely. Interpreters highlight two complementary views: one points to 26:3 ("many nations... as the sea brings up its waves"), seeing Nebuchadnezzar as the mighty opening wave followed by Alexander and later conquerors completing her desolation; the other notes that ancient prophetic judgment uses vivid poetic hyperbole, and when events unfolded somewhat differently, Ezekiel honestly appended chapter 29's follow-up oracle.

**Why does 26:14 say "you will never be rebuilt" when a city of Tyre still exists today?** Modern southern Lebanon does contain a coastal town named Sur (Tyre). Commentators regularly point out two facts. First, ancient mainland Tyre genuinely remained a ruin, and the famed Phoenician maritime empire that once ruled Mediterranean commerce fell permanently and never regained its former might. Second, lines like "you will never be rebuilt" and "though you are sought, you will never be found again" (26:14; 26:21) are standard emphatic idioms in Old Testament doom oracles stressing an irreversible fall—not a geographical pledge that no modest town would ever exist near the site.

**Why does 26:1 omit the name of the month?** The Hebrew text states simply "in the eleventh year, on the first day of the month," leaving the month unspecified. Some ancient Greek manuscripts supply "the first month," though many scholars think it fell in the latter half of the eleventh year (after Jerusalem's walls were breached in the fourth month), since 26:2 already quotes Tyre cheering that Jerusalem "is broken." Whichever month of that eleventh year (around 587–586 BC), the oracle ties directly to Jerusalem's collapse.

**The grim war imagery of chapter 26.** Scenes of Babylonian swords slaughtering mainland towns, warhorses trampling streets, and the wounded groaning (26:8–15) weigh heavily on modern readers. Remember that Scripture never celebrates Nebuchadnezzar's brutality—other prophets fiercely indict Babylon itself for cruelty and arrogance. This passage enforces a stern moral reality: wealth and power never shield a nation that grows callous and cashes in on another's bloodshed.

**Josephus**, the 1st-century Jewish historian, preserved vital extra-biblical testimony confirming the siege foretold in chapter 26. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, stressed that Tyre's offense in verse 2 represents an all-too-common commercial sin: celebrating a competitor's ruin to increase one's own gain. Whenever a city or person builds prosperity on a neighbor's wreckage and mocks God's people in distress, Henry noted, that very greed summons divine judgment. He also saw the trembling sea princes of verse 16 as a vivid reminder of how swiftly earthly riches vanish, leaving none safe who trust in them. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 26)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral emphasis on commercial greed in verse 2 and unstable wealth in chapter 26 is well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, Jewish biblical scholar at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, pointed out the dramatic stylistic pivot inside chapter 26: the middle verses portray siege warfare in sharp military realism, while the closing verses shift into ancient Near Eastern cosmic-mythic imagery as the "deep" floods Tyre and plunges her into the "Pit" among "the people of antiquity." **Daniel I. Block**, Canadian-American Old Testament professor, regarded verse 3's image of "many nations" rolling in "as the sea brings up its waves" as the interpretive key to Tyre's oracle. Nebuchadnezzar stands out as the mighty opening wave named explicitly, while subsequent waves—culminating in Alexander the Great's conquest two centuries later—sealed the decline of this commercial empire. Block also underlined the brief phrase "and I will set glory in the land of the living" in verse 20 as a purposeful ray of contrast: even as proud power sinks into death, God opens the way for life and glory to return to His people. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 25–48, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's wave metaphor from verse 3 and the contrast in verse 20 from NICOT volume 2 are well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->
