# Ezekiel 5 — The Barber's Sword and the Sentence on Jerusalem

## Story

Picking up immediately from the days of lying on his side besieging the clay brick in chapter 4, the LORD gives Ezekiel the final silent drama in this sequence of signs. God instructs him to take a sharp sword, use it like a barber's razor to shave off all the hair from his head and beard, and divide the hair into three equal parts on a balance scale. When the days of the siege are complete, he is to burn one third in the fire right inside the model city; strike another third with the sword all around the city; and scatter the final third to the wind while God declares that He Himself will unsheathe a sword to pursue them. From what remains, God tells him to gather a few hairs and tuck them tightly into the folds of his robe—yet even from that small tuft seeming to be safe, he must pull out a few more strands and throw them into the fire to be burned, for from there fire will break out and spread to the whole house of Israel (5:1–4).

From silent drama, the chapter moves straight into direct prophecy from the Lord GOD. Pointing to Jerusalem, God declares that He Himself placed her in the center of the nations, with countries all around her. Yet Jerusalem rebelled against His ordinances, acting more wickedly than the nations and violating His statutes more than the surrounding countries; indeed, they have grown more unruly than those peoples and "have not even conformed to the ordinances of the nations around you" (5:5–7).

Because of this guilt, God utters a chilling declaration: "Behold, I Myself am against you." He will execute judgment right in the midst of Jerusalem in full view of the nations—a disaster unlike "anything I have ever done or will ever do again." Most horrifying of all, in the city's extremity, "fathers will eat their sons, and sons will eat their fathers," while every survivor will be scattered to all the winds (5:8–10).

The Lord GOD swears by His own life: because the people have defiled His sanctuary with all their detestable idols and abominations, His eye will not spare them and He will show no pity. Then He spells out clearly the meaning of the three portions of hair Ezekiel just divided: a third of the city's people will die by plague and waste away by famine inside the walls; a third will fall by the sword outside around the city; and a third will be scattered to every wind with a sword drawn in pursuit behind them. Only when His wrath has spent itself upon them will His anger subside, and He declares, "I will be appeased"—then they will know that the LORD Himself has spoken "in My zeal" (5:11–13).

From an honored place among the nations, Jerusalem will be turned into a ruin and an object of scorn before the eyes of every passerby. When God executes judgment in anger and furious rebuke, the once-holy city will become a reproach, a warning, and a horror to the surrounding nations (5:14–15).

The oracle closes with four relentless blows falling upon the unfaithful city. God will shoot deadly arrows of famine against them and "break their staff of bread"; He will send wild beasts to rob them of their children; plague and bloodshed will sweep through the streets; and finally He will bring the sword upon them. The entire chapter ends with an immovable seal: "I, the LORD, have spoken!" (5:16–17).

## Key Takeaways

**Greater privilege brings heavier accountability.** God placed Jerusalem at the center of the nations not so she could boast of special status, but so she might live by His just laws as a witness to the world (5:5–6). When the chosen city betrays God's law more grievously than nations that never received the covenant, a glorious history or central position offers no shelter for sin. Where the brightest light has been given, judgment falls most strictly.

**The temple cannot protect a people who defile it with their own hands.** Jerusalem's citizens clung to the belief that God would never allow His own temple to be destroyed. The oath in 5:11 shatters that illusion: they themselves had already brought detestable things inside to defile His sanctuary. Once a place of worship is turned into a site of betrayal, stone walls can no longer keep God inside.

**The greatest danger is not Babylon, but God Himself turning against you.** The centerpiece of the chapter is the blunt statement: "Behold, I Myself am against you" (5:8). Babylon's army is merely the barber's sword in the Judge's hand. When God's people stubbornly walk away from the covenant, the deepest tragedy is not the strength of enemies outside the gate, but losing the One who used to stand as their defender.

**Even in total devastation, a small tuft is preserved.** While all three portions of hair are burned, hacked apart, or blown away on the wind, God still tells Ezekiel to tie a few hairs securely in the folds of his garment (5:3). Even though a few strands from that small remnant are still cast into purifying fire (5:4), the detail of the garment fold shows that judgment does not erase the covenant people forever. Israel's survival no longer rests on its own strength, but solely on God's hand preserving a tiny remnant.

## Deeper Meaning

**Shaving head and beard: a priest's humiliation.** In the ancient world, shaving off hair and beard signaled deep mourning or the disgrace of captives. For Ezekiel specifically, this command carried extra sting because Mosaic law strictly forbade priests from shaving their heads bald or trimming the edges of their beards (Leviticus 21:5). Just as with the defiled cooking fuel in chapter 4, God requires the priest to enact the stripping away of dignity, foreshadowing the humiliation of both the priesthood and Jerusalem. Over a century earlier, Isaiah had used the very same image when warning that the LORD would use "a razor hired from beyond the Euphrates"—the Assyrian empire—to shave the head and beard of Judah (Isaiah 7:20).

**A city set among the nations and a calling turned upside down.** Verse 5:5 recalls the very reason Israel was chosen. In Deuteronomy, Moses taught that if the people kept God's righteous statutes, the surrounding nations would marvel at such a wise people who had God so near to them (Deuteronomy 4:6–8). Instead, Jerusalem ruined that witness until she became a laughingstock—just as Jeremiah lamented that no pagan nation ever traded away its gods even though they were not real gods, yet God's people traded their Glory for worthless idols (Jeremiah 2:10–11). In the New Testament, Jesus sums up this principle of justice cleanly: "From everyone who has been given much, much will be required" (Luke 12:48).

**Four plagues read straight from the ancient covenant.** The grim images in the chapter's second half—wild beasts bereaving parents, the sword pursuing scattered fugitives, and even parents eating their children during siege (5:10; 5:12; 5:17)—are not improvised threats invented by Ezekiel. He is quoting and applying the exact covenant warnings written centuries earlier in Leviticus and Deuteronomy (Leviticus 26:22; 26:29; 26:33; Deuteronomy 28:53). God does not punish His people out of arbitrary rage; He carries out the very terms both parties pledged.

**Fire breaking out from the preserved tuft.** Pulling a few extra strands from the hairs tucked inside the robe and throwing them into the fire (5:3–4) found tragic fulfillment right after Jerusalem fell. When Babylon left a small remnant of poor citizens in the land under Governor Gedaliah, that remnant seemed safe for a moment; yet Ishmael of Judah's royal line assassinated Gedaliah, triggering a panicked flight to Egypt and dragging fresh disaster after it (2 Kings 25:25–26).

**Distress unequaled in history.** The declaration of judgment unlike "anything I have ever done or will ever do again" (5:9) became a solemn biblical idiom marking watershed moments of divine reckoning. More than six hundred years later, Jesus echoed the very same cadence when foretelling Jerusalem's second downfall and the great tribulation to come (Matthew 24:21).

## Notes & Nuances

**How should we read "fathers will eat their sons, and sons will eat their fathers" (5:10)?** This is one of the most horrifying verses in the Old Testament. Scripture never condones or minimizes cannibalism; rather, it lays bare the unthinkable depths to which prolonged warfare and famine drive human beings. Brutal ancient sieges really did push trapped populations to such desperation, and Lamentations—written immediately after 586 BC—confirms that even compassionate mothers fell into that nightmare (Lamentations 4:10). The text names the full horror of sin and war instead of hiding it behind polite euphemisms.

**The phrase "I will be appeased" once wrath is spent (5:13) can make God sound cruel.** Heard casually in modern English, saying God is "comforted" or "appeased" after pouring out disaster might sound as though He delights in human pain. In Hebrew, however, the verb conveys relief and release—setting down a heavy burden of anguish once a prolonged betrayal has finally been addressed decisively. "My zeal" immediately afterward denotes the covenant jealousy of a faithful husband whose marriage vow has been trampled inside His own house (5:11; 5:13). God's anger is not senseless cruelty; once justice has run its course, wrath subsides to make room for the mercy and restoration promised in the book's second half.

**The knot in 5:7: why does it say Judah "has not even conformed to the ordinances of the nations around you"?** At first this sounds puzzling—surely God forbade Israel from following pagan ways, so why fault them for not doing so? Two main explanations exist. Reading the standard Hebrew text with the word "not" intact, the charge drips with painful irony: pagan nations at least maintain basic standards and stay loyal to their gods, whereas Jerusalem has abandoned God's law without even matching the minimum decency of her neighbors. On the other hand, several ancient Hebrew manuscripts and the Syriac version omit the word "not," reading instead that Judah *did* follow the wicked practices of surrounding nations—matching the tone found elsewhere in Ezekiel. Either way, the verdict is identical: Jerusalem has completely forfeited her character as God's covenant people.

**The three portions of hair are not a rigid demographic census.** Dividing the population evenly into a third by famine and plague, a third by sword, and a third by exile (5:12) uses dramatic symbolic language to express all-encompassing disaster: stay inside the walls and starve or catch plague, flee outside and meet the sword, escape abroad and still face pursuit. One should not press the equal thirds into strict historical percentages.

## What Commentators Say

**Origen**, the brilliant 3rd-century theologian and biblical interpreter in Alexandria, read the shaving of hair with a sharp sword in chapter 5 through the lens of spiritual purification in his homilies on Ezekiel. In his view, hair growing outward from the body represents dead, superfluous attachments and sin clinging to the lives of God's people; the sharp sword shaves it off not out of meaningless destruction, but to separate and discipline—just as God's Word trims away impurity from the soul. *(Homilies on Ezekiel, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Origen's characteristic spiritual-purification reading of the shaved hair and punishments in chapter 5 is well remembered; specific homily number and Jerome's Latin text not checked -->

**Jerome**, the 4th–5th century biblical scholar who translated Scripture into Latin, lingered extensively over 5:5 where God says He placed Jerusalem in the center of the nations. In his commentary on Ezekiel, Jerome took this not only spiritually but geographically: Jerusalem stood at the center of the inhabited ancient world, surrounded by Asia, Europe, and Africa. This famous reading profoundly shaped medieval European world maps (*mappae mundi*), which routinely placed Jerusalem right at the center. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jerome's geographical reading of Ezekiel 5:5 placing Jerusalem at the center of the world in Commentary on Ezekiel and its influence on medieval maps are widely cited in interpretive history -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, examined closely the accusation in 5:6–7 that Judah acted worse than pagan nations. According to Calvin, God's people carried heavier guilt not necessarily because every individual act was cruder than pagan conduct, but because they sinned deliberately in the clear light of God's revealed law. Addressing "I will be appeased" in 5:13, he reminded readers that Scripture borrows the language of human emotion to underscore the gravity of divine justice, never attributing petty temper or vindictive pleasure to God. *(Commentaries on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of the Prophet Ezekiel, on chapter 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's classic points about greater guilt under brighter light of the Word and anthropomorphic language in 5:13 are well remembered; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, paid special attention to the tiny cluster of hairs tucked into the folds of Ezekiel's robe in 5:3. There he saw a picture of the faithful remnant whom God quietly preserves amid national ruin; yet immediately afterward, 5:4 still draws a few hairs from that very fold to throw into the fire, reminding believers that even those spared from great calamity must continue walking in holy fear and submitting to God's refining work. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 5)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral reading of the remnant tucked in the robe and refining fire in chapter 5; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, highlighted the tight narrative bond between chapters 4 and 5: chapter 4 portrays the prolonged siege and starvation inside the walls, while chapter 5 moves immediately to the aftermath once the city falls. He also underscored how shocking the command to shave head and beard in 5:1 was for a priest barred by Leviticus from doing so: with his own shorn head, Ezekiel embodied the complete stripping away of holiness from both the temple and Jerusalem. *(Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's analysis of the continuity between chapters 4 and 5 and the violation of priestly purity in 5:1 is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->
