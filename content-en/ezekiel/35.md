# Ezekiel 35 — Judgment on Mount Seir

## Story

In chapter 34, God promised to gather Israel's scattered flock back onto their homeland's mountains under a covenant of peace. Yet before those mountains of Israel bloom back to life in chapter 36, a hostile shadow encroaching from the south must be cleared away. The LORD instructs Ezekiel to set his face toward Mount Seir—the mountainous homeland of Edom, descendants of Esau—and prophesy against it. The verdict opens short and razor-sharp: "Behold, I am against you, O Mount Seir!" God will stretch out His hand to reduce its cities to ruins and turn the entire range into an eerie wasteland, so it will know that He is the LORD (35:1–4).

God states Edom's first offense: it "harbored an ancient hostility" from generation to generation, and in "the time of their calamity, the time of their final punishment"—when Babylon tore Jerusalem apart—Edom delivered the Israelites over to the sword. God swears that because Edom delighted in bloodshed, He will hand it over to bloodshed: "Since you did not hate bloodshed, bloodshed will pursue you!" Mount Seir will lie so empty that nobody passes through or returns; every hill, valley, and ravine will fill with sword-slain corpses. The country will become a "perpetual desolation," its towns uninhabited, so they know He is the LORD (35:5–9).

Next God names Edom's second offense: bottomless greed paired with brazen contempt for God Himself. Seeing both northern Israel and southern Judah fallen and exiled, Edom rushed to claim the land: "These two nations and countries will be ours, and we will take possession of them"—ignoring the decisive truth, "even though the LORD was there." God swears to treat Edom with the exact fury and jealousy it poured onto Israel. Not a single scornful boast escaped God's ears as Edom gazed over Israel's mountains gloating, "They are desolate; they have been given to us to devour." Those arrogant sneers did not target Israel alone—they boasted straight against God Himself. God states flatly: "I have heard them!" (35:10–13).

The oracle closes with exact poetic justice for Edom's third sin—gloating over a brother's misery: "As the whole earth rejoices, I will make you desolate." Just as Edom celebrated watching the inheritance of the house of Israel turn to rubble, so Mount Seir and every corner of Edom will become the exact same wasteland—forcing them at last to recognize He is the LORD (35:14–15).

## Key Takeaways

**A nursed grudge eats an entire people alive.** God does not condemn Edom for a momentary flash of temper, but because it "harbored an ancient hostility" (35:5). From the rift between twin brothers Esau and Jacob over a thousand years earlier, resentment was fed from parent to child until it hardened into cold cruelty the very day their brother fell into crisis.

**God hears every sneer aimed at suffering people.** Edom assumed that once Israel went into exile, nobody was left to stop them from mocking and carving up their land. Yet 35:12–13 declares God never misses a word: whoever claps over someone undergoing God's discipline opens his mouth directly against God Himself.

**God's discipline never means God walked out.** Edom's fatal blind spot sits inside five quiet words in 35:10: plotting to grab Israel's land "even though the LORD was there." Outwardly, the temple stood burned and the people marched off in chains, looking as if God had vacated the premises. Yet the true Owner never left, and He never surrenders His people's inheritance to vultures.

**Judgment matches the crime measure for measure.** Chapter 35 echoes God's exacting justice from start to finish: whoever refuses to hate bloodshed gets hunted down by bloodshed (35:6); whoever deals out wrath and envy receives wrath and envy in return (35:11); whoever rejoices over another's desolation becomes desolate while the whole earth celebrates (35:14–15).

## Deeper Meaning

**A thousand-year wound traced back to Esau and Jacob.** Mount Seir was the territory where Esau—that is, Edom—settled (Genesis 36:8). Tension between the two peoples began inside Rebekah's womb and erupted when Esau hated Jacob over the stolen blessing (Genesis 25:23–26; 27:41). When Israel marched out of Egypt asking politely to walk straight along Edom's royal highway without touching a single field, Edom marched out armed to block their road (Numbers 20:14–21). Even so, Mosaic law still instructed Israel: "Do not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). That very blood kinship made Edom's betrayal on Jerusalem's darkest day cut all the deeper.

**Voices of outrage from the day Jerusalem fell.** Grief over Edom's treachery echoes right across the Old Testament. Weeping beside Babylon's rivers, the psalmist pleaded with God to remember Edom's cries on the day the city fell: "Tear it down! Tear it down to its foundations!" (Psalms 137:7). The whole prophecy of Obadiah indicts the exact same crime: Edom stood aloof watching its brother looted, then posted troops at crossroads to cut down fleeing Judeans and hand survivors over to Babylon (Obadiah 1:10–15). Proverbs had long warned against gloating over an enemy's collapse (Proverbs 24:17–18)—how much worse against one's own brother.

**A contrasting diptych with chapter 36 and a preview of the book's final name.** Reading chapter 35 straight into chapter 36 reveals two paired oracles standing like shadow and sunrise: chapter 35 turns toward *Mount Seir* pronouncing desolation, while chapter 36 turns toward *the mountains of Israel* pronouncing rebirth—beginning right in its opening verses by recalling the mocking boast of enemies and Edom (36:1–5). Even the phrase "even though the LORD was there" in 35:10 uses the exact Hebrew words *YHWH shammah* ("the LORD is there") that will crown the restored holy city as its brand-new name in the final verse of the book (48:35).

**Fulfillment in history and the book of Malachi.** Over a century after Ezekiel, Malachi pointed to Edom's hills turned into a wasteland for desert jackals, warning that even if they tried rebuilding, God would tear it back down (Malachi 1:2–4). Historically, Arab Nabataean tribes gradually pushed Edomites out of their ancient Mount Seir homeland; migrating into southern Judah (later called Idumea), they were eventually conquered by the Hasmoneans and vanished as a distinct nation.

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does Edom receive a whole extra chapter here after already being judged in chapter 25?** Chapters 25–32 already judged seven foreign nations, including a brief oracle on Edom (25:12–14). Edom's return in chapter 35 is no accidental duplication. Once Jerusalem fell and Judah went into exile, Edomites actively migrated northward to seize vacated towns in southern Judah. Before promising to hand the land back to Israel in chapter 36, God must formally assert His ownership and expel Edom's land grab. Here Mount Seir also comes to stand as an emblem for every hostile force seeking to swallow God's people (cf. 36:5).

**The hidden Hebrew wordplay between "Edom" and "blood" in 35:6.** In English we simply read, "Since you did not hate bloodshed, bloodshed will pursue you!"—yet ancient Hebrew listeners immediately heard the chilling pun: the name *Edom* (meaning "red," tied to Esau's ruddy skin and red stew) sounds nearly identical to *dam*, the Hebrew word for "blood." The nation's very name locked onto the bloody judgment it called down upon itself.

**Does sentencing Edom to "perpetual desolation" sound overly cruel?** Prophetic judgment language against ruthless oppressors regularly uses sweeping, absolute terms to guarantee that evil will never defeat God's plan. Notice that Scripture never encourages ethnic hatred against individual Edomites—elsewhere Scripture keeps the door open for a remnant of Edom and all nations to bear God's name—while here judgment strikes squarely at the spirit of violent cruelty, brotherly betrayal, and arrogance against God.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar, explained chapter 35's striking placement immediately after chapter 34's Good Shepherd promise: historically, Mount Seir denotes the Edomites who betrayed their brothers when Jerusalem collapsed; spiritually, Jerome read *Edom* (meaning "red" and "earthly") as representing every cruel worldly power gloating over trials hitting the Church. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, paused thoughtfully over five words in 35:10: "even though the LORD was there." The greatest miscalculation made by opponents of God's people, Henry noted, is assuming from outward ruins that God has vacated the premises. Even when Israel lay devastated under discipline, God's ownership and presence never departed; anyone greedily rushing in to carve up God's inheritance finds the rightful Landlord waiting to collect. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 35)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic remark on "even though the LORD was there" in 35:10 is well remembered; exact text not checked -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, Jewish biblical scholar, demonstrated that chapters 35 and 36 were composed as an inseparable diptych: on one side stands hate-filled *Mount Seir* turned desolate; on the other stand once-desolate *mountains of Israel* bursting back to life. He also highlighted the sharp Hebrew wordplay in 35:6 linking *Edom* with *dam* ("blood"), illustrating Ezekiel's biting literary craft matching each sentence directly to the offense. *(Ezekiel 21–37, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the paired structure of chapters 35–36 and the Edom/blood wordplay in 35:6 are well-known signature analyses of Greenberg's Anchor Bible commentary -->

**Daniel I.
