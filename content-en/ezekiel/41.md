# Ezekiel 41 — The Most Holy Place, Two-Faced Cherubim, and the Table Before the LORD

## Story

Leaving the temple portico at the close of chapter 40, the bronze-like guide leads Ezekiel across the threshold straight into the main hall of the temple—the Holy Place. He measures the entry jambs at six cubits wide on each side, matching "the width of the ancient tabernacle" (or side walls), with a doorway ten cubits across. The main sanctuary nave measures forty cubits long by twenty cubits wide, matching Solomon's temple footprint exactly. At the doorway to the innermost chamber, however, Ezekiel must stop short. Only the messenger "went inside," measuring the doorposts at two cubits, the doorway height at six cubits, and its flanking walls at seven cubits, then measuring the inner chamber itself: a perfect square, twenty cubits long and twenty cubits wide. Stepping back out, he tells the prophet waiting outside: "This is the Most Holy Place" (41:1–4).

Next, the guide steps outside to measure the structure wrapping around the temple building. The temple's main wall stands six cubits thick. Pressing along three outer faces of the temple runs a ring of side chambers four cubits wide, stacked three stories high with thirty rooms per floor—ninety small chambers in all. Ingeniously, the main temple wall steps inward as it rises, creating external ledges so the wooden floor beams of the side chambers rest on those projections and "do not enter the wall of the temple itself"—not a single beam is notched into the holy masonry. Because the main wall narrows upward, the upper stories actually grow "wider as they go up," linked by a winding stairway ascending from the bottom story through the middle to the top. The entire temple and its three-story annex sit atop a raised stone platform a full measuring rod high—"six long cubits"—framed by a five-cubit outer wall, a five-cubit walkway opening northward and southward, and an open twenty-cubit strip beyond separating the main temple block cleanly from adjacent chambers (41:5–11).

Walking around behind the temple to the west, Ezekiel sees a massive building facing the restricted yard. Its interior measures seventy cubits wide by ninety cubits long inside walls five cubits thick—making an eighty-by-one-hundred-cubit block—though the guide offers zero explanation of its function. Then he measures the overall footprint of the central complex: the temple's full length is one hundred cubits; the restricted courtyard plus the western building and its walls also span one hundred cubits; the temple's eastern facade and adjacent courtyard measure one hundred cubits across; and the western building with its side galleries measures an exact one hundred cubits too. Viewed from above, the sanctuary core locks into crisp, exact hundred-cubit squares (41:12–15).

Turning back to examine the interior finish, Ezekiel sees that the sanctuary walls, vestibules, thresholds, recessed windows, and surrounding galleries are paneled smoothly in wood from floor level up to the windows and over the doorways. What grips the prophet's gaze most is the carved relief running continuously around the walls from floor to above the doors: alternating figures of cherubim and palm trees spaced evenly—"a palm tree between every two cherubim." Unlike the four-faced living cherubim he saw by the Kebar River in chapters 1 and 10, each cherub carved into the temple paneling displays two faces: "the face of a man facing the palm tree on one side, and the face of a young lion facing the palm tree on the other side" (41:15–20).

Finally, the messenger points out the square doorframes and the sole piece of sanctuary furniture mentioned in the entire main hall. Standing right in front of the Most Holy Place is "an altar of wood," three cubits high and two cubits square—corners, top, and sides crafted entirely of wood, with no mention of gold overlay. The guide names it with warm simplicity: "This is the table that is before the LORD." Both the outer sanctuary and the Most Holy Place feature double doors, each leaf hinged with two folding panels carved with the exact same cherubim and palm trees as the walls. Across the front of the portico extends a wooden canopy or awning, while recessed windows and carved palms line the side walls of the portico and side chambers (41:21–26).

## Key Takeaways

**Even a prophet of priestly lineage halts at the threshold of the Most Holy Place.** Throughout chapter 40 and the opening of chapter 41, wherever the guide walks, Ezekiel walks right behind. Yet right at the entrance to the Most Holy Place, verse 41:3 shifts quietly: "Then he went inside"—only the heavenly messenger enters to measure, while Ezekiel waits outside hearing the declaration, "This is the Most Holy Place" (41:3–4). Born into the priesthood (1:3), Ezekiel knows Mosaic law well: nobody strolls casually into God's innermost presence (Leviticus 16:2). That quiet pause teaches enduring reverence: intimacy with God never erases the awe between Creator and creature.

**Holiness is guarded down to every timber beam and open strip of ground.** Builders ordinarily notch beams straight into load-bearing walls for support, yet here the ninety side rooms may never pierce the temple's sacred wall (41:6). Around the house stretches a twenty-cubit buffer zone, and the whole sanctuary rises atop a six-long-cubit stone foundation (41:8–10). Every structural feature declares one message: God's dwelling must stay wholly set apart, never blurred with common routines or stained by defilement.

**The sanctuary highlights fellowship and welcome rather than glittering gold.** Reading Solomon's temple description in 1 Kings nearly dazzles the eye with solid gold sheeting walls, floors, doors, and giant cherubim (1 Kings 6:20; 6:32). Here in chapter 41, the word "gold" never appears once—only sturdy stone, warm wood paneling, palm trees, and cherubim. Most remarkably, the only furnishing inside the main hall is neither ark nor golden lampstand, but a wooden altar explicitly called "the table that is before the LORD" (41:22). Right at the heart of a towering, tightly guarded holy complex stands a table—the universal sign of welcome, worship, and communion before God.

**Spatial progression draws the heart inward toward the center.** While chapter 40 led us past outer walls, outer court, and inner court, chapter 41 brings us straight to the beating heart of the whole complex: the forty-by-twenty Holy Place and the twenty-by-twenty square Most Holy Place (41:2–4). Every thick wall, corridor, and ninety side rooms exists solely to support and orient every eye toward that central cube—where God's glory is about to return in chapter 43.

## Deeper Meaning

**The new temple recalls the Garden of Eden.** Covering every wooden wall and door panel from floor to ceiling are just two motifs repeated in rhythm: cherubim and palm trees (41:18–20; 41:25). In Genesis, after humanity fell, God stationed cherubim east of Eden to guard the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24), and later woven cherubim adorned the curtain veiling the Most Holy Place (Exodus 26:31). Now on these sanctuary walls, cherubim stand peacefully beside lush palm trees—emblems of life and God's garden—foreshadowing God's dwelling as Eden restored. On Scripture's closing page, that imagery reaches fullness as the tree of life flourishes along both banks of the river flowing from God's throne (Revelation 22:1–2).

**Continuing Solomon's temple while surpassing it.** The forty-by-twenty Holy Place, the twenty-by-twenty Most Holy Place, three tiers of side chambers resting on wall ledges, and double folding doors carved with cherubim and palms echo Solomon's sanctuary closely (1 Kings 6:2; 6:5–6; 6:16–17; 6:20; 6:29; 6:31–35). Yet every dimension here is scaled up in long royal cubits, the complex expands into flawless hundred-cubit squares, and the wooden altar-table towers larger than the old tabernacle incense altar (Exodus 30:1–3). The vision assures exiles that God is not merely patching broken ruins; He is renewing everything on a grander scale.

**The missing ark of the covenant and Jeremiah's prophecy.** Attentive readers instantly notice something astonishing in 41:4: no ark of the covenant is ever mentioned inside the Most Holy Place. Ezekiel's contemporary Jeremiah had already foretold a day when God's people would no longer think of, miss, or craft another ark of the covenant, because all Jerusalem itself would be called the throne of the LORD (Jeremiah 3:16–17). Right ahead in chapter 43, God Himself will call this entire temple "the place of My throne and the place for the soles of My feet" (43:7). Once God's living presence dwells directly with His people, old furniture-symbols are no longer needed.

**From Ezekiel's closed threshold to the open road in Christ.** Throughout the Old Testament, even Ezekiel waited outside the Most Holy Place (41:3–4), and Hebrews explains that because the high priest alone entered once a year behind the veil, the Holy Spirit was signaling that the way into the Most Holy Place was not yet open (Hebrews 9:7–8). In the New Testament everything transforms: through Jesus' blood and His flesh torn like the temple curtain, believers now have "confidence to enter the Most Holy Place" (Hebrews 10:19–20). Meanwhile, the flawless twenty-by-twenty square of 41:4 expands in Revelation into the entire New Jerusalem—a colossal cube equal in length, width, and height, meaning the whole city has become the Most Holy Place (Revelation 21:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why is the wooden altar in 41:22 called "the table"?** In the tabernacle and Solomon's temple, two items stood side by side inside the Holy Place: the gold-overlaid wooden altar of incense (Exodus 30:1–3) and the table of the Bread of the Presence (Exodus 25:23; 25:30). In Ezekiel 41:22, the guide describes "an altar of wood" (three cubits high by two wide—larger than the old incense altar) and instantly calls it "the table that is before the LORD." In Old Testament idiom, *altar* and *table of the LORD* sometimes interchange (Malachi 1:7). Many commentators understand this piece as merging both functions: a place where prayers ascend like fragrant incense and a fellowship table symbolizing warm communion between God and His people.

**Why do these cherubim display two faces instead of four?** In the living visions by the Kebar River and in chapter 10, each cherub had four faces—man, lion, ox, and eagle (1:10; 10:14). On the temple walls of 41:18–19, each cherub shows just two faces: a man and a young lion. The simplest and best explanation is artistic: in flat two-dimensional wall relief carving, only two profile faces can look left and right toward the flanking palm trees. Choosing man (wisdom and stewardship) and lion (royal majesty) perfectly fits guardians of the King of kings' palace.

**What was the purpose of the western building in 41:12?** Scripture records exact dimensions for the large structure behind the temple—seventy cubits wide by ninety long with five-cubit walls—yet says not a word about its use. Scholars propose reasonable guesses: storage for temple utensils and sacrificial refuse, a rear service block shielding the back of the Most Holy Place from the west, or simply an architectural counterbalance completing the symmetrical hundred-cubit squares. Where Scripture stays silent, speculation should stay modest.

**Rare Hebrew architectural vocabulary makes parts of chapter 41 tough to translate.** Chapter 41 packs more obscure building terms than almost any chapter in the Old Testament, producing slight variations across English versions. For instance, 41:1 mentions "the width of the tabernacle" in Hebrew (some modern translations follow the Greek Septuagint reading "the side walls"); 41:3 gives dimensions around six and seven cubits (differently divided between doorway width and flanking walls); and 41:7 describes the ascending stairway in highly compressed syntax. None of these technical translation nuances affects the chapter's clear theological flow.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the foundational Jewish oral law compilation edited around AD 200, cited Ezekiel 41:7 directly in its tractate detailing Second Temple architecture when describing the three-story side chambers ringing the sanctuary. The sages explained that because the main wall narrowed as it ascended, each upper chamber widened over the one below, linked by a gently sloping ramp circling from bottom to middle to top—showing post-exilic Jews read Ezekiel's architectural measurements closely as a touchstone for Jerusalem's actual temple. *(Mishnah, Middot, chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Middot chapter 4's discussion of the three-tier side chambers citing 41:7 on widening upward and the ascending walkway is well remembered; exact section number inside chapter 4 not verified -->

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, delighted in the unusual architectural touch of 41:7 where the temple's side rooms grow wider on higher stories. Reading spiritually, he saw a radiant picture of Christian growth in the Church: hearts clinging close to earth remain cramped and narrow, yet the higher we climb in grace and the knowledge of God, the wider, freer, and more generous our souls become. He likewise read the cherubim and palm trees carpeting the walls as heavenly wisdom joined with eternal victory. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 41)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's classic spiritual reading of 41:7 (growing wider as one ascends spiritually) in his Ezekiel commentary is well remembered; exact book number and Latin text not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew two practical lessons from chapter 41 for church life. First, from side chambers widening as they climb (41:7), he encouraged believers that growing closer to heaven always enlarges the heart rather than shrinking it. Second, seeing the wooden altar called "the table that is before the LORD" in 41:22, Henry noted that in gospel days the blood-stained altar gives way to the Lord's Table—where believers draw near for joyful communion with God in Christ. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 41)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral points on the widening upper chambers in 41:7 and the Lord's Table in 41:22 are well remembered; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Daniel I. Block**, Canadian-American Old Testament professor, highlighted two decisive theological features in chapter 41. First, Ezekiel halting outside while the guide alone enters to measure the Most Holy Place (41:3–4) underscores that even a Zadokite priest must honor God's holy boundaries absolutely. Second, Block cataloged everything strikingly *absent* here—no ark, no golden lampstand, no ornate veil, no lavish gold plating—drawing every eye onto Eden restored through cherubim and palms alongside the lone wooden altar-table anticipating the direct return of God's glory. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 25–48, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Ezekiel's pause outside the Most Holy Place in 41:3–4 and the theological significance of the missing furnishings are major signature emphases in Block's NICOT volume 2 -->
