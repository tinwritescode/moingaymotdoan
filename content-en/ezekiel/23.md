# Ezekiel 23 — The Two Sisters Oholah and Oholibah

## Story

Right after chapter 22's itemized indictment of Jerusalem, the word of the LORD delivers an even harsher, more jarring allegory tracing the unfaithful history of both kingdoms—Israel and Judah. There were two women, "daughters of the same mother." Even in their youth in Egypt, both gave themselves to prostitution and lost their purity. The older sister was named Oholah, and the younger Oholibah. Both later became wives of the LORD and bore Him sons and daughters. God identifies them right up front without leaving anyone guessing: "Oholah is Samaria, and Oholibah is Jerusalem" (23:1–4).

The story starts with the older sister, Oholah—capital of the northern kingdom of Israel. Though she belonged to the LORD, she lusted after new lovers, the Assyrians—governors and commanders dressed in purple, handsome young warriors riding proudly on horseback. She defiled herself with them and with all their idols, while never letting go of the prostitution habits she brought out of Egypt. So God handed her over to the very Assyrians she had craved. They stripped her naked, seized her sons and daughters, and killed her with the sword—recounting Assyria's obliteration of Samaria. Oholah became "a byword among women," a tragic warning held up before the world (23:5–10).

Her younger sister Oholibah—Jerusalem—watched her sister's horrifying fate with her own eyes. Yet she felt zero fear; instead, she grew even more depraved than her sister. First, she too lusted after richly dressed Assyrian governors and commanders riding magnificently on horseback, marching straight down Oholah's path (23:11–13). Next, spotting paintings of Chaldean officials etched in bright vermilion on the wall—wearing sashes around their waists and flowing turbans, bearing the stately look of Babylonian officers—she fell infatuated at first glance and hurried messengers off to Chaldea to invite them over. The Babylonians arrived and defiled her; then her own heart turned away from them in disgust—and at that exact moment, the LORD's soul turned away from her in disgust, just as it had from her sister (23:14–18).

Not stopping there, Oholibah plunged deeper into her earliest obsession: she grew nostalgic for the days of her youth in Egypt. Using some of the coarsest, most explicit language in all of Scripture, God lays bare Jerusalem's degrading infatuation with the brute worldly power of Egypt—lovers "whose flesh was like that of donkeys and whose passion was like that of stallions"—reviving the exact youthful lust she had carried out of that land (23:19–21).

Sentence now crashes down on Oholibah, heavy and final. The very Babylonian lovers from whom she turned away—Babylonians, all the Chaldeans, men of Pekod, Shoa, and Koa, along with all the Assyrians marching beside them—the governors, officers, and horsemen who once dazzled her will now be roused by God to surround her on every side with chariots, wagons, bucklers, shields, and helmets. God hands judgment over to them to punish her by their own brutal customs: mutilating her body, slaying her survivors by the sword, dragging off her sons and daughters, burning what remains, and stripping away her fine clothes and jewels. Handed over to those she hates, stripped of all her toil and left bare, she will finally stop lifting her eyes back toward Egypt (23:22–30).

Next follows a short, chilling poem about "your sister's cup." Because Jerusalem walked straight down Samaria's road, God places in her hand the deep, wide cup her sister had to drink—"a cup of horror and desolation," brimming with drunkenness, sorrow, and derision. She must drain it down to the very dregs, gnawing even the broken shards and tearing at her own body in madness. The reason stands sealed plainly: "Because you have forgotten Me and cast Me behind your back" (23:31–35).

The chapter's final section opens a joint trial for both sisters together. God urges Ezekiel to judge Oholah and Oholibah and name their two gravest crimes: adultery and "blood on their hands." They committed adultery with idols, even passing the very children they had borne to the LORD through fire as food for false gods. Worse still—an outrage beyond belief—"on that very same day," right after slaughtering their children for idols, they walked casually straight into the LORD's sanctuary, defiling His temple and profaning His Sabbaths (23:36–39).

The portrait expands even further, showing the sisters sending messengers to invite men from afar—bathing, painting their eyes, putting on jewelry, and reclining on an opulent couch before a table set with the LORD's own sacred incense and oil, throwing loud banquets amid a carefree crowd and drunkards brought in from the desert (23:40–44). Finally, sentence is delivered before "righteous men" as the verdict for adulteresses and women who shed blood: an assembly will march up to stone them, hack them down with swords, kill their sons and daughters, and burn their houses—ending lewdness in the land so they bear the full weight of their idolatrous sins and know that He is the Lord GOD (23:45–49).

## Key Takeaways

**Leaning on worldly empires instead of God is covenant betrayal.** Chapter 23's shocking sexual allegory directly addresses the foreign alliances and religious compromises of Israel and Judah. Turning to Assyria, then to Babylon, then glancing back toward Egypt, both capitals grew dazzled by the glitter of foreign armies while forgetting they belonged to the LORD (23:5–21; 23:35).

**Watching someone else fall and refusing to learn multiplies guilt.** Samaria's destruction by Assyria stood as a living warning right across Judah's border. Yet Jerusalem watched every detail unfold and marched down the exact same road—even outdoing her older sister (23:11–13). Knowing the consequences and charging ahead anyway is inexcusable hardness of heart.

**The worldly crutches we chase usually turn around and devour us.** Both Samaria and Jerusalem were destroyed by the very empires they had courted—Assyria crushed Samaria, and Babylon crushed Jerusalem (23:9–10; 23:22–29). When people trade away faithfulness to buy protection from earthly powers, the protector quickly turns executioner the moment interests shift.

**Outward religious ritual cannot wash away bloodstained hands.** The chilling detail in 23:38–39 shows both sisters sacrificing their own children to idols and strolling straight into God's temple that very same day—even laying God's incense and oil on idolatrous banquet tables (23:41). Going through the motions of worship when the heart belongs elsewhere only compounds defilement before God.

## Deeper Meaning

**The two-sisters allegory continues Jeremiah's prophecy and Ezekiel's own chapter 16.** Before Ezekiel ever prophesied in Babylon, Jeremiah in Jerusalem had already portrayed Israel and Judah as two sisters: faithless Israel was sent away for adultery, yet treacherous sister Judah watched without fear, making Israel appear more righteous than Judah (Jeremiah 3:6–11). Ezekiel himself had likewise paired sister Samaria and sister Sodom to show Jerusalem sinned worse than both (16:46–52). Earlier prophets such as Hosea and Isaiah repeatedly exposed the folly of chasing superpowers: Israel acted like a silly dove calling first to Egypt and then fluttering to Assyria, while Judah schemed to shelter under Pharaoh's shadow only to harvest shame (Hosea 7:11; Isaiah 30:1–3).

**Behind painted portraits and envoys stand real pages of Judah's royal history.** Oholibah's infatuation with Assyrian governors directly recalls King Ahaz of Judah: he raided gold and silver from God's temple to bribe Assyria's king, called himself his servant and son, and traveled up to Damascus to copy a pagan altar pattern and erect it right in Jerusalem (2 Kings 16:7–10). Likewise, Samaria's fall to Assyria after a three-year siege stands recorded in Kings (2 Kings 17:5–8). Reading prophecy alongside history clarifies why Ezekiel merges political alliances with idolatry: in the ancient world, signing a vassal treaty with an empire formally required acknowledging and honoring that empire's gods.

**The "cup" of judgment stretches across Scripture to Gethsemane.** The haunting poem about the deep, wide cup driving drinkers to stupor, madness, and ruin (23:31–34) belongs to a great prophetic stream: God hands the cup of His wrath to Jerusalem and the nations to drink down to the dregs (Isaiah 51:17; Jeremiah 25:15–17). Six centuries later, on the night before His crucifixion in the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus fell facedown and prayed, "My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me. Yet not as I will, but as You will" (Matthew 26:39). The cup of judgment unfaithful people deserved to drain to the bottom, the Son of God willingly took and drank in their place on the cross.

**Destruction by former lovers returns in Revelation's portrait of Babylon.** Chapter 23's startling sentence—foreign powers once courted by Oholibah turning to hate her, strip her naked, and burn her with fire (23:25–29)—is echoed almost verbatim by the apostle John when describing the fall of Babylon the Great: the ten horns and the beast will hate the prostitute, leave her desolate and naked, devour her flesh, and burn her up with fire (Revelation 17:16). On the level of personal discipleship, James likewise borrows this exact language of spiritual adultery to wake up the Church: "You adulteresses! Do you not know that friendship with the world is hostility toward God?" (James 4:4).

## Notes & Nuances

**The hardest element to read: coarse sexual imagery and violence against women's bodies (23:3; 23:20–21; 23:25–29; 23:47).** Chapter 23 stands among the most jarring and painful texts in Scripture for modern readers. Alongside raw, graphic sexual descriptions, the chapter portrays God turning an unfaithful wife over to former lovers to mutilate her face, strip her in public, stone her, and hack her down. Facing this tension honestly requires holding two major perspectives side by side. First, within classical historical and rhetorical reading, Ezekiel is not targeting individual women or endorsing domestic abuse; the two "women" represent two capital cities led by male kings and elites frantically courting imperial armies and even burning children to idols. Deliberately crude language strips the glamour off ancient power politics to jolt exiles awake, while mutilation and stoning reflect actual Assyrian-Babylonian war atrocities and ancient adultery penalties (Leviticus 20:10). Second, many modern scholars and feminist interpreters rightly caution that even as metaphor, casting God as a furious husband handing His wife to a mob for torture and public shaming carries real danger: throughout history, such imagery has been misused to justify victim-blaming or domestic violence against women. Reading chapter 23 with maturity means holding fast to its theological core—the deadly seriousness of covenant betrayal and oppression—while clearly distinguishing that message from harsh ancient cultural conventions.

**The meaning of Oholah and Oholibah (23:4) and the question of marrying two sisters.** In Hebrew, *Oholah* means "her own tent"—pointing to northern Samaria erecting its own unauthorized shrines and altars outside God's chosen dwelling. *Oholibah* means "My tent is in her"—pointing straight to Jerusalem, where God's temple stood in the city's midst. Having God dwell right inside her made Jerusalem's betrayal all the more inexcusable. Some readers wonder why God is pictured marrying two sisters when Mosaic law forbade a man from marrying sisters simultaneously (Leviticus 18:18). Remember that this is not a marriage manual, but a political allegory telling the story of one single covenant nation that split into two sibling kingdoms—the exact figure Jeremiah had used before him (Jeremiah 3:6–11).

**Unlike chapter 16, chapter 23 closes in total darkness.** In chapter 16, after an equally long and scorching allegory of Jerusalem's unfaithfulness, God still opened a breathtaking grace-filled ending: He would remember His youth-covenant, establish an everlasting covenant, and forgive her (16:60–63). Here in chapter 23, the oracle stops abruptly amid stoning, swords, and burning houses without a single ray of hope (23:47–49). The book's flow explains why: chapter 23 stands as the final closing indictment right before Babylon formally locks its siege around Jerusalem at the start of chapter 24.

**Pekod, Shoa, and Koa (23:23) and cutting off nose and ears (23:25).** Pekod, Shoa, and Koa were real tribes living east of Babylon who supplied troops to the Chaldean empire; at the same time, *Pekod* sounds like the Hebrew word for "visitation" or "punishment," slipping an ironic pun right into the roster of advancing enemies. As for "cutting off your nose and your ears" in 23:25, that reflects a well-documented Assyrian and Babylonian legal penalty for treason and adultery, permanently branding a convicted traitor's face.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, began his exposition of chapter 23 by translating the Hebrew names Oholah ("her own tent") and Oholibah ("My tent is in her") to highlight the sharp contrast between Samaria's self-invented shrines and Jerusalem's true temple. Facing the chapter's graphic sexual details, Jerome urged readers not to get stuck on the carnal surface, but to discern the spiritual reality beneath: a portrait of spiritual adultery in which God's chosen people abandon Him for the idols and glitter of pagan empires. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's etymology of the two sisters' names and spiritual reading of chapter 23's graphic language are well remembered; exact book number and Latin wording not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, stressed the sobering warning of 23:11: Oholibah's guilt far exceeded her older sister's because she watched Samaria suffer judgment yet refused to turn back. Henry expressed particular horror at the brazen hypocrisy of verses 38–39—slaughtering children to idols and walking straight into God's house that very same day—observing that divine justice often turns the very partners we sinned to please into the harshest rods of punishment. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 23)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar points on verse 11, same-day hypocrisy in verses 38–39, and poetic justice via former lovers in chapter 23 are well remembered; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, Jewish biblical scholar at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, drew a sharp line between Ezekiel's two grand allegories: whereas chapter 16 focuses chiefly on Jerusalem's religious idolatry and covenant history, chapter 23 delivers a blistering indictment aimed directly at the reckless foreign policy and military alliances of both kingdoms with Assyria, Babylon, and Egypt. Greenberg connected the vermilion-painted Chaldean officers on walls (23:14–15) with ancient Mesopotamian palace reliefs, while documenting the historical reality of harsh punishments like severing nose and ears in the ancient Near East. *(Ezekiel 21–37, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's contrast between the political focus of chapter 23 and chapter 16 along with Mesopotamian wall-relief parallels in Anchor Bible volume 2 are well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, Canadian-American Old Testament professor, argued that chapter 23's crude, shocking language serves a calculated rhetorical purpose: stripping away every shred of prestige from imperial diplomacy. Judah's kings viewed courting Babylon or running to Egypt as savvy survival politics, so Ezekiel deliberately paints those deals in the most repulsive terms imaginable to make the exiles recoil in disgust. Block also observed that chapter 23 intentionally ends with zero word of hope—unlike chapter 16—because chapter 24 immediately sounds the drumbeat of Jerusalem's siege. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 1–24, NICOT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's view of the shock rhetoric deflating royal diplomacy and the deliberate omission of hope right before chapter 24 in NICOT volume 1 are well remembered; exact page numbers not checked -->

**Renita J. Weems**, American Old Testament scholar, examined the metaphors of marriage and violence across Hosea, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. She noted that Ezekiel deployed his fiercest sexual and punitive imagery in chapters 16 and 23 to shock an ancient male audience—forcing them to confront their own political and religious treachery as unbearable disgrace. Yet Weems urged modern readers to engage these texts with critical care, never allowing an ancient literary metaphor to excuse real-world violence or degradation directed against women. *(Battered Love: Marriage, Sex, and Violence in the Hebrew Prophets, 1995)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: this is the well-known central thesis of Weems's Battered Love analyzing Ezekiel 16 and 23; specific chapters and page numbers not checked -->
