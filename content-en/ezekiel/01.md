# Ezekiel 1 — The Chariot of Glory by the Kebar River

## Story

The story opens far from the homeland of God's people. It is the thirtieth year, on the fifth day of the fourth month—also the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin of Judah to Babylon. The priest Ezekiel, son of Buzi, is living among the exiles by the Kebar River in the land of the Chaldeans. Suddenly, "the heavens were opened," the word of the LORD came directly to him, and the hand of the LORD was upon him, opening visions of God (1:1–3).

Looking up, Ezekiel sees a windstorm sweeping in from the north. At the heart of the storm is a massive cloud with flashing fire and brilliant light all around it, and from the center of the fire shines something like gleaming amber (1:4).

Out of the midst of the fire appear the forms of four living creatures. Their overall appearance is human, yet each has four faces and four wings. Their legs are straight, their feet like those of a calf, gleaming like burnished bronze; beneath their wings on all four sides are human hands. Each creature's four faces point in four directions: a human face, a lion's face on the right, an ox's face on the left, and also an eagle's face. Above, two wings of each creature stretch out to touch the wings of another, while two wings cover their bodies. Whenever they move, they go straight ahead without turning, going wherever the Spirit leads them. They look like burning coals of fire and glowing torches; fire moves back and forth among them, radiant and flashing with lightning, while the creatures dart back and forth like bolts of lightning (1:5–14).

As Ezekiel gazes intently at the four living creatures, he sees wheels on the ground beside each of them. All four wheels look identical, gleaming like beryl, with an astonishing structure like "a wheel within a wheel," allowing them to roll in any of the four directions without turning. Their rims are high and awesome, and all four rims are full of eyes all around. The wheels and the living creatures move as a single unified whole: wherever the creatures go, the wheels go; when the creatures stand still, the wheels stand still; when the creatures rise from the earth, the wheels rise beside them, "for the spirit of the living creatures was in the wheels" (1:15–21).

Spread out above the heads of the four living creatures is an expanse sparkling like awesome crystal. Under this expanse, their wings are stretched out straight toward one another, while two wings still cover their bodies. When they move, the sound of their wings roars like the roar of rushing waters, like the voice of the Almighty, loud as the tumult of an army. When they come to a halt and lower their wings, a voice resounds from above the expanse over their heads (1:22–25).

Looking higher above the expanse, Ezekiel sees something like a throne with the appearance of sapphire, and seated upon the throne is "a figure like that of a man." From the waist up, the figure glows like gleaming metal filled with fire all around; from the waist down, it blazes like bright fire radiating brilliance. Surrounding all that radiance is a halo of colors like the appearance of a rainbow in the clouds on a rainy day. Ezekiel carefully calls everything he has just witnessed "the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD." Overwhelmed by such majesty, he falls facedown on the ground and hears the voice of One speaking to him (1:26–28).

## Key Takeaways

**God is not locked inside the temple in Jerusalem.** For a young priest deported more than a thousand kilometers away to pagan Babylon, the deepest shock was not merely losing his home—it was thinking he had lost the place where God could be met. This opening vision shatters that despair. The throne of the LORD has wheels; it is not bolted to a building or confined by borders. God leaves the familiar land, crosses the desert, and comes all the way to the Kebar River in the midst of an exile camp to seek His people (1:1; 1:15–21).

**God's glory surpasses all human language.** Throughout this chapter, Ezekiel hesitates again and again, repeating words like "like," "likeness," and "appearance." Even at the summit of the vision, he does not dare say he simply saw God directly; instead, he carefully calls it "the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD" (1:26–28). That reverent restraint teaches readers holy awe: God truly reveals Himself so people can encounter Him, yet He never shrinks down to fit inside our frames or imagination.

**Absolute sovereignty in the midst of ruin.** To human eyes, the Babylonian empire appears completely victorious, carrying off Judah's king and leading citizens into captivity. Yet the storm sweeping in from the north—the very direction from which Babylon's armies marched—carries the throne of the LORD (1:4). The One steering history is not the king of Babylon; God sits enthroned above every earthly power, moves freely in every direction with nothing able to hinder Him, and sees all things through rims full of eyes (1:17–18).

**A genuine encounter with God brings people facedown.** There is no smug pride or casual curiosity when the heavens open. Ezekiel's only response is to fall facedown on the ground in silent readiness to listen (1:28). Before receiving the bitter and lonely mission of the chapters ahead, he first has to see with his own eyes just how great the One sending him truly is.

## Deeper Meaning

**A mobile temple continuing Mount Sinai and the ark of the covenant.** The great cloud, blazing fire, lightning, and thunderous sound immediately recall the day the LORD descended upon Mount Sinai (Exodus 19:16–18). The gleaming sapphire-like expanse above the creatures recalls the moment Moses and the seventy elders saw under God's feet something like a pavement of sapphire, as clear as the sky itself (Exodus 24:9–10). And the four living creatures bearing up the throne—whom Ezekiel later recognizes as cherubim (10:15; 10:20)—echo both the two winged cherubim over the mercy seat of the ark and the psalmist's image of the LORD riding upon a cherub and flying (Exodus 25:18–22; Psalms 18:10). The God who once dwelled in the Jerusalem temple now rides a living throne-chariot all the way into exile.

**A rainbow surrounding the fire of judgment.** Around the blazing throne of fire appears a radiance like a rainbow in the clouds on a rainy day (1:28). In Genesis, the rainbow is the sign by which God remembers His everlasting covenant with the earth never again to wipe out all living things (Genesis 9:13–16). Although the storm from the north foreshadows fierce words of judgment soon to fall on Jerusalem (cf. Jeremiah 1:13–15), the rainbow around the throne reminds the reader that God's faithfulness and mercy still encircle His wrath.

**The thirtieth year and a priestly ministry cut short.** Under the law of Moses, Levites entered active service at the tent of meeting at age thirty (Numbers 4:3). Many readers understand "the thirtieth year" in the opening verse as Ezekiel's own age: in the very year he should have stepped into the courts of the Jerusalem temple for the first time as a priest, he is sitting as a captive beside a Babylonian canal following the deportation with King Jehoiachin (2 Kings 24:12–16; cf. Psalms 137:1). God does not allow his life to become meaningless; when the stone temple lies far out of reach, God Himself opens the heavens in a foreign land to call him as a prophet.

**Powerful echoes in the book of Revelation.** More than six hundred years later, when the apostle John is likewise living in exile on the island of Patmos, he receives a vision strikingly close to Ezekiel 1: a heavenly throne glowing like precious stones, encircled by a rainbow, before it a sea of glass clear as crystal, and four living creatures with faces like a lion, an ox, a man, and a flying eagle, covered with eyes (Revelation 4:2–8). The vision of the risen Jesus with feet like burnished bronze and a voice like rushing waters, causing John to fall at His feet as though dead, also points straight back to this chapter (Revelation 1:13–17). Both times, heaven opens to an exile to declare that the throne of God has never been vacant.

**The heavens were opened.** The phrase "the heavens were opened" occurs rarely in the Old Testament, marking a decisive turning point when God Himself parts the veil between heaven and earth (1:1). At the opening of the New Testament, at the exact moment Jesus is baptized in the Jordan River—also at about thirty years of age—the heavens open once again and the Spirit of God descends upon Him (Matthew 3:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**The number "the thirtieth year" in 1:1 has several interpretations.** The most widespread view today takes it as Ezekiel's age, matching the age at which a priest began his service according to Numbers 4:3—which also explains why 1:2 immediately adds the public historical marker of the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's exile, around 593 BC. On the other hand, several ancient Jewish sources (such as the Aramaic *Targum* and the commentator Rashi) count thirty years from the discovery of the Book of the Law in the temple during King Josiah's reign around 622 BC, or connect it with the cycle of the Jubilee year.

**Do not turn the chariot of glory into a mechanical blueprint or a UFO.** Details like "a wheel within a wheel," metallic radiance, and roaring noise have tempted many modern readers to sketch an engineered machine or even speculate about flying saucers. Yet Ezekiel's dense cluster of comparisons shows that he is drawing on visionary language and imagery familiar across the ancient Near East—where winged composite creatures supporting divine thrones were common in Babylonian and Assyrian sculpture—to convey a spiritual reality beyond ordinary sight. The point is not the mechanics of a vehicle, but the truth it declares: God moves freely and is wholly present everywhere.

**The four faces of the living creatures receive no direct explanation in the text.** Ezekiel simply reports what he sees without assigning labels to the faces of the man, lion, ox, and eagle (1:10). According to a widely held view among biblical scholars and ancient Jewish tradition, these four faces represent the four pinnacles of creaturely life: wise humanity, the mighty lion among wild beasts, the strong ox among domestic livestock, and the swift eagle among the birds of the air—all serving beneath the Creator's throne. In early Christian art and tradition, those same four figures famously became symbols of the four Gospels.

**The vision does not end at the close of chapter 1.** At the end of 1:28, Ezekiel has just fallen facedown and begun to hear a voice speaking. The entire call connected with this vision by the Kebar River continues seamlessly through chapter 2 and halfway through chapter 3 (3:15), so the chapters should be read together to grasp the real purpose of this awe-inspiring scene.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah and Babylonian Talmud**, foundational Jewish legal and interpretive works from the 2nd to 6th centuries, regard Ezekiel 1 as one of the most awe-inspiring and perilous passages in Scripture, calling it the Work of the Chariot (*Ma'aseh Merkabah*). The *Mishnah* forbids expounding this chapter publicly, even to a single student, unless that person is already wise enough to understand it independently. The *Talmud* even relates that the book of Ezekiel once risked being withdrawn from public reading until Rabbi Hananiah ben Hezekiah stayed awake through many nights burning hundreds of jars of lamp oil to reconcile the book's difficult passages with the Law of Moses. *(Mishnah, Hagigah chapter 2, section 1; Babylonian Talmud, Hagigah 13a–13b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the restriction against expounding Ma'aseh Merkabah in Mishnah Hagigah 2.1 and the account of Rabbi Hananiah ben Hezekiah in Babylonian Talmud Hagigah 13b are two very well-known Jewish sources on Ezekiel 1 -->

**Jerome**, the 4th–5th century biblical scholar who translated Scripture into Latin, wrote his commentary on Ezekiel just after Rome had been sacked in AD 410, when the world felt as shaken as that of the ancient exiles. On chapter 1, he followed early Church tradition in linking the four faces of the living creatures with the four Gospels—man for Matthew, lion for Mark, ox for Luke, and eagle for John—an arrangement that became standard across the West. Alongside this, he also read the first three figures as the three parts of the human soul in classical philosophy (reason, will, and appetite), with the soaring eagle symbolizing the spark of conscience (*synderesis*) that sin cannot completely extinguish. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, Book 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Jerome's pairing of the four faces in Ezekiel 1 with the four Gospels and his interpretation of the eagle as synderesis in Book 1 of Commentary on Ezekiel are widely cited -->

**Gregory the Great**, 6th-century Bishop of Rome, preached a series of sermons on Ezekiel in AD 593 while Rome itself was under siege by the Lombards. Looking at "a wheel within a wheel" in 1:16, he explained it as the bond between the two Testaments: the New Testament lies hidden within the Old like a smaller wheel inside a larger one, and what the Old Testament promises, the New brings into clear light. From the wheels rising whenever the living creatures rose, he drew his famous observation that Scripture grows along with its reader: the deeper a person walks in the knowledge of God, the more profoundly the text unfolds before their eyes. *(Homilies on Ezekiel, Book 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: explaining a wheel within a wheel as the New Testament within the Old and the remark that Scripture grows with its reader are two classic passages in Book 1 of Gregory the Great's Homilies on Ezekiel -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, devoted the final months of his life in 1563–1564 to lecturing on the first twenty chapters of Ezekiel. Approaching chapter 1, he urged restraint, warning readers not to indulge in fanciful speculation over every detail of the wheels or wings, but to grasp the practical purpose of the vision: to display the boundless power of God so as to confirm the young prophet's authority and keep the exiles from despair while cut off from the temple. *(Commentaries on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of the Prophet Ezekiel, on chapter 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's restrained hermeneutic avoiding elaborate speculation and emphasizing consolation for the exiles in chapter 1 is well remembered; exact wording not checked against ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, read chapter 1 holistically rather than slicing the text into disconnected editorial fragments. He pointed out that the long, halting sentences and dense chains of comparisons authentically reflect an eyewitness straining to describe something beyond the reach of human speech, while safeguarding the majesty of the invisible God in a world filled with Babylonian idols. *(Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's holistic approach and analysis of the cautious comparative language in chapter 1 are signature features of his Anchor Bible commentary; specific page numbers not verified -->
