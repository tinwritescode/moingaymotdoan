# Ezekiel 8 — Abominations in the Temple

## Story

Chapter 8 launches a sweeping four-chapter vision stretching through chapter 11, dated to "the sixth year, on the fifth day of the sixth month"—about fourteen months after Ezekiel's call by the Kebar River, around September 592 BC. Ezekiel is sitting in his house in Babylonian exile with the elders of Judah seated before him to hear God's word when the hand of the Lord GOD suddenly falls upon him. Before his eyes appears a magnificent figure resembling fire: from the waist down it blazes like fire, and from the waist up it shines brightly "like gleaming amber." The figure stretches out the form of a hand and grasps Ezekiel by a lock of his hair. The Spirit lifts him up between earth and heaven and carries him "in visions of God" more than a thousand kilometers back to Jerusalem, setting him down at the entrance of the north gate leading into the inner court of the temple—where stands "the idol of jealousy that provokes to jealousy." Right there, the glory of the God of Israel still rests, just as Ezekiel had seen it earlier on the plain (8:1–4).

God leads him through four successive scenes of sin, each graver and deeper inside the temple than the last. In the first scene, God tells him to look northward. Just north of the altar gate, right at the entrance, stands "the idol of jealousy." God asks Ezekiel whether he sees the great abominations the house of Israel is committing right here—"things that will drive Me far from My sanctuary"—for their own betrayal is forcing Him out of His house. Yet God immediately warns him, "You will see even greater abominations than these!" (8:5–6).

God brings him to the entrance of the courtyard. Looking closely, Ezekiel spots a small hole in the wall. God tells him to dig through the wall; once he digs through, a hidden doorway appears, and God tells him to step inside and look at the wicked abominations being practiced within. What meets Ezekiel's eyes is chilling: all around the walls are engraved "every kind of crawling creature and detestable beast, and all the idols of the house of Israel." Standing before those murals are seventy elders of the house of Israel, and right in their midst stands Jaazaniah son of Shaphan. Each man holds a censer in his hand, sending up a thick cloud of fragrant incense. God exposes what these elders tell themselves in "the dark, each at the shrine of his own idol": "The LORD does not see us; the LORD has forsaken the land!" Then God warns again, "You will see them committing even greater abominations!" (8:7–13).

In the third scene, God brings him to the entrance of the north gate of the LORD's house itself. Seated right at the temple gateway are women weeping for Tammuz—a foreign pagan god of vegetation and harvests. God asks, "Do you see this, son of man?" and leads him one final step deeper: "You will see even greater abominations than these!" (8:14–15).

The fourth scene unfolds at the very heart of the temple. God brings Ezekiel into the inner court, standing directly "between the portico and the altar"—the sacred zone reserved for priests interceding for the nation. There, about twenty-five men are committing an act of supreme contempt: with their "backs toward the temple of the LORD and their faces toward the east," they bow down eastward worshiping the sun (8:16).

The chapter closes with a stern verdict. God asks whether the house of Judah considers the abominations just seen a trivial matter, when besides corrupting His sanctuary they have filled the land with violence and continually provoked Him to anger—even "putting the branch to their nose!" Facing such brazen mockery, God resolves to act in wrath; His eye will not spare them, He will show no pity, and "even though they shout loudly in My ears, I will not listen to them" (8:17–18).

## Key Takeaways

**God sees straight through what people think they have hidden.** The scene of seventy elders tucked behind a sealed wall, burning incense in the dark while comforting themselves that "the LORD does not see us" (8:12), is a vivid portrait of hypocrisy. People routinely fear the eyes of neighbors far more than the gaze of God. Yet God's hand pierces the thickest wall and calls out those inside by name (8:8; 8:11).

**God did not abandon His people first; their own sin drove Him away.** The elders use the excuse "the LORD has forsaken the land" to justify seeking other gods (8:12). The truth is the exact opposite: verse 8:4 shows God's glory still present in the temple, and 8:6 declares that the abominations they commit there are what "drive Me far from My sanctuary." People frequently blame God's supposed absence to rationalize their own unfaithfulness.

**Corrupt worship always produces violence and injustice in society.** Delivering His sentence in 8:17, God does not cite temple idols alone; He links them directly to the fact that they have "filled the land with violence." False religion never stays neatly inside shrine walls; once hearts turn away from the righteous God, public life inevitably slides into cruelty and oppression.

**The failure of leaders corrupts the entire nation.** Those standing in the dark chamber are not ignorant commoners, but seventy elders representing the whole nation—including even a son of Shaphan the scribe, from a family once renowned for faithfulness (8:11). In the final scene, twenty-five men standing inside the priestly court deliberately turn their backs on the Most Holy Place to bow to the sun (8:16). When leaders betray God in secret, the moral foundation of the whole country cracks alongside them.

## Deeper Meaning

**Four escalating sins breaking Moses' prohibitions item by item.** Opening Moses' parting instructions in Deuteronomy yields a startling match: the list of forbidden practices mirrors the four scenes of chapter 8 almost point for point—do not make a carved image of male or female form, do not fashion likenesses of beasts on earth or crawling creatures, and do not look up to the sky to bow down to the sun (Deuteronomy 4:16–19). The very temple built to house and honor that law had become a gallery displaying everything the law most strictly forbade.

**The painful contrast of the seventy elders and Shaphan's family.** The phrase "seventy elders" in 8:11 immediately calls to mind the radiant scene on Mount Sinai at Israel's birth, when seventy elders accompanied Moses and Aaron up the mountain and "saw the God of Israel" above a pavement of clear sapphire (Exodus 24:1; 24:9–10). From the dazzling light of Sinai, the seventy elders of Ezekiel's day had sunk into a dark room worshiping crawling beasts. Even sadder, the name Jaazaniah son of Shaphan recalls Shaphan the scribe—the faithful official who read the newly rediscovered Book of the Law aloud before King Josiah, sparking a great reform only thirty years earlier (2 Kings 22:8–10). A godly heritage never automatically guarantees the faithfulness of the next generation.

**The sacred space "between the portico and the altar" betrayed.** The spot where twenty-five men turn their backs on the temple in 8:16 was no ordinary strip of pavement. In the book of Joel, the space "between the portico and the altar" is the exact place where priests are summoned to weep and plead with God to spare His people (Joel 2:17). In that most sacred place of intercession, they turned their backs to the LORD to bow before the sun—reviving the very solar worship King Josiah had worked so hard to abolish (2 Kings 23:11).

**The timeless self-deception of sinners.** The elders' whisper—"The LORD does not see us" (8:12)—echoes the exact words of the wicked in the Psalms: "The LORD does not see; the God of Jacob pays no heed," prompting the psalmist's piercing reply: "He who fashioned the ear, does He not hear? He who formed the eye, does He not see?" (Psalms 94:7–9). When sinners pretend God cannot see, the day comes when disaster hits and they cry out loudly, only to find God will not listen—just as Isaiah warned a generation earlier (8:18; Isaiah 1:15).

**Zeal for God's house in the New Testament.** Viewed from the New Testament, God's holy jealousy over His defiled house resounds again when Jesus enters the Jerusalem temple, driving out merchants because "zeal for Your house will consume Me" (John 2:16–17). Furthermore, the apostle Paul teaches that the Church and the bodies of believers are now the temple of the living God, raising a pointed question straight in line with chapter 8: "What agreement can exist between the temple of God and idols?" (2 Corinthians 6:16).

## Notes & Nuances

**Was Ezekiel physically transported to Jerusalem or taken in a vision?** The detail of a hand grasping a lock of his hair and lifting him between earth and sky sounds strikingly vivid (8:3), making many readers wonder whether his body physically traveled. Yet verse 8:3 states clearly that he was brought to Jerusalem "in visions of God," and when the vision closes at the end of chapter 11, the Spirit brings him back to Chaldea "in the vision," whereupon he immediately recounts everything he saw to the exiles—likely the very elders still sitting before him from the start of chapter 8 (8:1; 11:24–25). Most commentators therefore understand this as a visionary transport through which God allowed the prophet to witness real sins taking place in Jerusalem. One also readily recognizes that the radiant figure matches the One enthroned at the book's opening (8:2; 1:27), while "the vision I had seen in the plain" refers back to chapter 3 (8:4; 3:22–23).

**What were "the idol of jealousy" (8:3; 8:5) and the god "Tammuz" (8:14)?** Scripture does not name "the idol of jealousy" specifically, though many scholars connect it with the Asherah pole King Manasseh once set up inside the temple (2 Kings 21:7). It bears that label because it directly provokes the LORD's covenant jealousy when His people worship a rival inside His own house (Exodus 20:5). Tammuz (originally Mesopotamian Dumuzi) was a widely worshiped vegetation and fertility deity across Babylon and the ancient Near East. According to ancient myth, each summer's scorching heat killed Tammuz and sent him to the underworld, leaving the earth parched; women gathered to weep for him in hopes of coaxing him back to life along with rain and fertile crops.

**What does the enigmatic phrase "putting the branch to their nose" (8:17) mean?** This is one of the most debated phrases in Ezekiel, with two primary lines of interpretation. The first sees it as a pagan ritual gesture—such as holding a twig near the mouth and nose while praying to the sun god in certain ancient Near Eastern cults. The second draws on a famous ancient Jewish scribal tradition (*tiqqune soferim*) stating that the original text read "putting the branch to *My* nose"—that is, God's nose, an idiom for an insulting, offensive gesture thrust right in God's face—and that early copyists out of reverence altered "My" to "their" to avoid phrasing that seemed disrespectful toward God. Under either explanation, the core point stands: they insulted the LORD with utter brazenness.

**Did all four scenes occur on a single day in 592 BC?** Some scholars read chapter 8 as four actual rites taking place simultaneously during King Zedekiah's reign, while others understand it as a composite visionary panorama gathering the various syncretistic cults that had infiltrated the temple from Manasseh's era right down to the eve of 586 BC. Either way, the four-stage progression moving steadily from the outer gate straight into the inner sanctuary proves an undeniable truth: the holy temple had been corrupted from its perimeter to its core.

## What Commentators Say

**The Mishnah**, the foundational compilation of Jewish oral law edited around AD 200, quotes verse 16 of this chapter directly in describing the joyous water-drawing ceremony of the Feast of Tabernacles at the Second Temple. When the priests reached the temple's eastern gate, they paused, turned westward to face the sanctuary directly, and proclaimed aloud that their ancestors in this very spot had turned their backs on the temple of the LORD and faced east to worship the sun, whereas now their eyes were fixed on the LORD alone. *(Mishnah, tractate Sukkah, chapter 5, section 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the priests' declaration at the eastern gate in Mishnah Sukkah 5.4 quoting Ezekiel 8:16 directly is a very famous and widely cited Jewish liturgical source -->

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, explained the women weeping for Tammuz in verse 14 by identifying Hebrew and Syriac Tammuz with Adonis of Greek mythology—the young lover of the goddess of love, wounded to death each year and mourned by women in midsummer rites. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, paused at length over the seventy elders' dark-room excuse in verse 12: "The LORD does not see us; the LORD has forsaken the land!" In Calvin's eyes, this is the cradle of all practical atheism and hypocrisy: people rarely dare state outright that God does not exist, yet they push Him out of everyday reality by imagining He pays no attention to earthly affairs, freeing themselves to commit evil behind closed walls. *(Commentaries on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of the Prophet Ezekiel, on chapter 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's classic analysis of hypocrisy and practical unbelief in the elders' words at 8:12 is well remembered; exact lecture wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, used the elders' hidden "chambers of imagery" behind the wall in verses 10–12 as a mirror for the human heart. Many people maintain a devout exterior, he observed, while keeping secret dark rooms in their imagination lined with selfish idols and lusts they assume nobody sees. He also noted with sadness the name Jaazaniah son of Shaphan, reminding readers that grace does not pass down through bloodlines: the son of a godly father can still stand at the forefront of betrayal. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral reflections on the heart's hidden chambers of imagery and the son of Shaphan in chapter 8; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, traced the tight spatial progression of chapter 8 as the four scenes move step by step from the outer gate straight into the space between the portico and the altar. He also carefully discussed the ancient Jewish scribal tradition of reverential emendation on verse 17, explaining why many scholars believe the original wording pictured the people thrusting the branch to God's nose to provoke Him before scribes softened it to "their nose." *(Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's spatial analysis of the four temple scenes and discussion of the scribal tradition on 8:17 are well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, American Old Testament professor, read chapter 8 as a courtroom indictment answering the agonizing question of Babylonian exiles about how God could bear to let Jerusalem fall. Each escalating scene proves that the LORD was neither unfaithful nor powerless; rather, four layers of sin inside His own house compelled Him to abandon His sanctuary, directly setting up the purge of chapter 9 and the departure of glory in chapter 10. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 1–24, NICOT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's courtroom-indictment reading justifying God's departure from the temple in chapter 8 is well remembered; exact page numbers not verified -->
