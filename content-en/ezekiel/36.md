# Ezekiel 36 — A New Heart and a New Spirit

## Story

No sooner has the LORD sentenced Edom's Mount Seir to desolation in chapter 35 than He tells Ezekiel to turn around and prophesy directly to the mountains of Israel. Surrounding neighbors and Edom alike had gloated over the country's wreckage, crowing, "Aha! Even the ancient heights have become our possession!" Israel's mountains, hills, ravines, valleys, and ruined towns had become easy prey and the butt of Gentile jokes. Yet in burning jealousy, God lifts His hand in oath that those very neighboring nations will now bear their own disgrace (36:1–7).

Turning His face warmly toward the mountains of Israel, God speaks pure favor. He tells mountain orchards to sprout branches and yield fruit because His people are coming home soon. Abandoned farmland will be plowed and sown again, ruined towns rebuilt, people and livestock multiplied far beyond former days—God will bless the land "more than at your beginnings." Once people cursed that soil, taunting, "You devour people and bereave your nation of its children." Now God promises the land will never again devour its residents, rob them of children, or endure the taunts of foreign nations (36:8–15).

Moving into the heart of the chapter, God explains candidly the real reason He is stepping in to save His people. He does not gloss over history: back in their land, the house of Israel defiled the soil with bloodshed and idols until His wrath had to scatter them among the nations. Yet once exiled among Gentiles, they went right on profaning His name, as watching peoples jeered, "These are the people of the LORD, yet they had to leave His land!" God therefore speaks bluntly so Israel holds zero illusions: "It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of My holy name." He will vindicate the holiness of His great name right before the nations through what He does in Israel (36:16–23).

Next God unveils the chapter's crowning promise—an inside-out renewal unfolding step by step. First, He will gather them out of every nation and bring them home to their own land. Second, He will wash away their defiled past: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean." Third comes miraculous heart surgery: "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you; I will remove your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh." Going even further, He pledges: "I will put My Spirit within you and cause you to walk in My statutes." Then they will live securely in the land given to their fathers, restoring the ancient covenant whole: "You will be My people, and I will be your God" (36:24–28).

Once hearts are made new, fields outside burst into bloom as well. Grain, fruit, and harvests will overflow so famine and shame among the nations vanish forever. Astonishingly, right amid all that overflowing grace, Israel will look back on its former wicked ways and loathe its own past sins. God reminds them once more: He is not saving them for their merit, so let them bow in humble shame over their past (36:29–32).

The chapter closes with a garden brought back from the dead. On the day God cleanses all their guilt, ruined towns will stand walled and inhabited and neglected fields will turn green again until astonished passersby cry out, "This land that was desolate has become like the garden of Eden!" Surrounding nations will be forced to see that the LORD Himself rebuilt what was torn down and replanted what lay bare. And right after guaranteeing every promise as settled fact, God adds an unexpected invitation: "I will also let the house of Israel inquire of Me to do this for them." He will multiply their people like vast flocks of festival sheep streaming toward Jerusalem on appointed feasts (36:33–38).

## Key Takeaways

**God's grace flows from His own character and honor, never from human merit.** Twice in one chapter God tells Israel plainly that He saves them not because they earned it, but for the sake of His holy name (36:22; 36:32). At first that might sound blunt, yet it is actually the firmest ground any sinner could stand on: if salvation depended on our shaky goodness, nobody would stand a chance; because it rests on God's own faithfulness and honor, hope holds unshakable.

**Humanity's root problem sits inside the heart, so true renewal must start from within.** Bringing exiles home and rebuilding stone walls fixes only the shell; if their hearts stay hard as granite, they will repeat the exact same rebellion. So God promises both to wash past stains with clean water and to replace their stone hearts with tender living hearts, placing His own Spirit inside them so obedience springs naturally from within (36:25–27).

**The deepest repentance blossoms after encountering grace.** Usually we assume people must feel sorry enough first to earn God's pardon. Here God flips that order completely: He cleanses them first, gives a new heart first, pours out blessing first—and right then they glance back and truly recoil in sorrow at their old sin (36:31). A heart of stone feels nothing over sin; only a God-given heart of flesh truly aches over grieving the One who loves us.

**God's certain promises never make prayer pointless.** Even right after declaring, "I, the LORD, have spoken, and I will do it" (36:36), God adds immediately that He wants Israel to "inquire of Me to do this for them" (36:37). Prayer is never arm-twisting a reluctant Deity; it is how God's people open their hands to receive the good He has already resolved to give.

## Deeper Meaning

**Total reversal of chapter 6's verdict.** Early in the book, God directed Ezekiel to face *the mountains of Israel* and announce the sword smashing every idol altar across them (6:1–4). Now in chapter 36, the exact same address—*the mountains of Israel*—hears the promise of blooming boughs, abundant fruit, and a land reborn like the garden of Eden (36:1; 36:8; 36:35; cf. Isaiah 51:3). Even the ancient slander of the ten fearful wilderness spies branding Canaan "a land that devours its inhabitants" (Numbers 13:32) is wiped clean away (36:13–14).

**From chapter 18's command to chapter 36's gift.** Earlier God commanded the people, "Fashion for yourselves a new heart and a new spirit" (18:31), and in chapter 11 He first glimpsed His promise to supply a heart of flesh Himself (11:19–20). Here in chapter 36 that pledge shines at full noon, standing arm in arm with Jeremiah's *new covenant*, where God inscribes His law straight onto His people's hearts (Jeremiah 31:31–34). What the law rightly demands yet humans cannot produce, God steps in to create and give.

**"Born of water and the Spirit" in Jesus' conversation with Nicodemus.** When Jesus told Israel's teacher Nicodemus that nobody can enter God's kingdom unless he is "born of water and the Spirit," expressing surprise that a teacher of Israel missed this (John 3:3–5), He was pointing directly back to Ezekiel 36:25–27! Clean water sprinkled to remove defilement—rooted in Mosaic cleansing rites (Numbers 19:17–19)—paired with God's Spirit placed within forms the Old Testament bedrock for the new birth echoed throughout the New Testament (Titus 3:5; Hebrews 10:22).

**Tablets of stone versus tablets of human hearts in Paul.** Writing to the Corinthian church, Paul weaves Ezekiel 36:26 together with Jeremiah 31:33 to describe the Holy Spirit's work: believers themselves are Christ's letter, written "not on tablets of stone, but on tablets of human hearts" (2 Corinthians 3:3).

**The grief of God's name profaned among the nations.** The heartbreak of 36:20—God's people behaving so badly that outsiders look down on God Himself—had already burdened Moses interceding on Mount Sinai (Exodus 32:12), and Paul later quotes this exact principle when rebuking those who boast in the law while breaking it, causing God's name to be blasphemed among the Gentiles (Romans 2:24).

## Notes & Nuances

**Is acting "for the sake of My holy name" selfish?** Hearing God say He acts not for Israel's sake but for His own name (36:22) can strike modern ears as cold. Yet in Scripture, God's *name* never means vanity or ego—it embodies His entire truthful, righteous, compassionate character. When watching nations saw exiled Israel and concluded the LORD was weak or indifferent (36:20), they misread the only God able to give humanity life. Defending His holy name means defending the truth about who God really is so every nation may come to know Him (36:23).

**Ritual impurity imagery in 36:17.** Under Mosaic law, a woman's menstrual cycle placed her temporarily in a state of *ceremonial uncleanness* restricting temple access—a ritual regulation, never a moral sin. As a priest, Ezekiel draws on that familiar image of ritual separation to picture how thoroughly bloodshed and idolatry had distanced the nation from God's holiness.

**What do "heart of stone" and "heart of flesh" mean?** In biblical Hebrew, *heart* denotes far more than emotion—it is the core seat of thought, will, and moral choice. A *heart of stone* is calloused, dead, unresponsive to God's voice and numb to compassion. A *heart of flesh* does not mean "sinful flesh" as in some Pauline letters, but a warm, living, tender, responsive heart alive to God.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, 4th–5th century Bishop of Hippo, treated Ezekiel 36:26–27 as a cornerstone passage in his debates with Pelagius over grace and free will. Setting the command of 18:31 ("Fashion for yourselves a new heart") alongside the promise of 36:26 ("I will give you a new heart"), Augustine explained that God commands so we recognize our duty and helplessness, driving us to pray to the only One who can renew the heart by His Spirit. *(On the Spirit and the Letter)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's use of Ezekiel 36:26–27 alongside Jeremiah 31 to contrast law's demand with the Spirit's renewing grace is the famous core thesis of On the Spirit and the Letter -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, analyzed the twin verbs "remove the heart of stone" and "give you a heart of flesh" closely in his landmark theology. God does not merely assist a human heart that already possesses a little good will, Calvin argued; He performs complete recreation—extracting our stubborn rebellious nature and generating a brand-new will that truly delights to walk in His ways. *(Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 2, Chapters 3 and 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Institutes Book 2 Chapter 3 section 6 and Chapter 5 section 15 directly cite and expound Ezekiel 36:26–27 on regeneration and the bondage of the fallen will -->

**Charles Spurgeon**, the 19th-century English preacher, preached repeatedly and powerfully on chapter 36, especially verses 36:26 and 36:37. Expounding 36:37 ("I will also let the house of Israel inquire of Me to do this for them"), Spurgeon insisted that God's guaranteed promises never render prayer redundant; quite the opposite, every promise is a key unlocking bold prayer so the Church asks for and welcomes the blessings God has prepared. *(Sermons on Ezekiel 36; specific sermon number unverified)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Spurgeon's famous point connecting assured divine promises with the duty of prayer in 36:37 is well remembered; exact sermon title and number not verified -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, Jewish biblical scholar, highlighted the piercing psychological insight of 36:31–32: in Ezekiel's theology, shame and revulsion over sin do not precede salvation as an entry fee, but surge up *after* God has cleansed and restored His people unconditionally. Unmerited grace awakens a real conscience, Greenberg noted, while acting "for the sake of My holy name" reflects the deep Old Testament link between Israel's fate and how the nations perceive God. *(Ezekiel 21–37, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's observation on shame following restoration in 36:31 and the international visibility of God's name is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Christopher J. H. Wright**, Northern Irish Old Testament scholar, built a pillar of biblical mission theology straight on Ezekiel 36:22–23. The ultimate motive driving God's redemption and renewal of Israel, Wright showed, is that all nations across the earth may come to know who the LORD truly is—proving God's passion for the glory of His name walks hand in hand with His mercy toward the world. *(The Message of Ezekiel, The Bible Speaks Today, 2001; The Mission of God, 2006)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Wright's missional reading of Ezekiel 36:22–23 is a celebrated central pillar across both The Mission of God and The Message of Ezekiel -->
