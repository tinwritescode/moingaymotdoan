# Ezekiel 37 — The Valley of Dry Bones and Two Sticks Joined as One

## Story

Right after chapter 36's pledge of a new heart and a new Spirit, God lets Ezekiel witness the life-giving power of that Spirit firsthand in two of the book's most unforgettable scenes. In the first vision, the hand of the LORD comes upon Ezekiel, and the Spirit carries him out and sets him down in the middle of a valley littered with bones. God leads him back and forth around them so he can take in every detail: countless bones blanket the valley floor, and they are bone-dry. Then God asks a question no mortal dares answer lightly: "Son of man, can these bones live?" Ezekiel answers simply and humbly: "O Lord GOD, only You know!" (37:1–3).

God then gives him an astonishing command: stand in a graveyard and preach God's word. He tells him to prophesy: "Dry bones, hear the word of the LORD!" God promises to lay sinews upon them, put flesh over them, cover them with skin, and breathe breath into them so they live. The instant Ezekiel obeys and speaks, a rattling noise breaks out and the earth shakes: bones click back together bone to bone, sinews and flesh form over them, and skin stretches across them. Yet the whole valley stays dead silent, because there is still no breath inside those bodies (37:4–8).

God directs him to prophesy a second time—no longer addressing bones, but summoning the wind itself: "Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe into these slain, that they may live!" The moment he speaks, breath rushes into the bodies; they spring to life, rise onto their feet, and stand as "a vast army" (37:9–10).

Immediately afterward, God Himself interprets the vision so nobody has to guess. These dry bones represent the whole house of Israel sighing in despair in exile: "Our bones are dried up, and our hope has perished; we are cut off!" God sends Ezekiel to proclaim His promise: "I will open your graves," lift them out of the grave of Babylonian exile, and bring them home to Israel. Then He seals it: "I will put My Spirit in you and you will live." Then the people will know that what the LORD speaks, He surely performs (37:11–14).

In the chapter's second half, God assigns Ezekiel a visible sign-act before the crowd. He must take one wooden stick and write on it: "For Judah and for the Israelites associated with him." Taking a second stick, he writes: "For Joseph, the stick of Ephraim, and all the house of Israel associated with him." Then he presses the two sticks end to end inside his hand until they fuse into one single stick (37:15–17).

When curious onlookers ask what this means, Ezekiel announces the LORD's message: God will gather the Israelites from every direction back to their homeland and reunite two halves split apart for centuries into one single nation ruled by one single king. No longer divided into two kingdoms, never again defiling themselves with idols, they will experience chapter 34's promise even more vividly: "My servant David will be king over them," and they will have "one shepherd." They and their children forever will dwell safely in ancestor Jacob's land under an everlasting covenant of peace. Crowning the entire chapter stands the promise opening the door to the book's final nine chapters: "My dwelling place will be with them; I will be their God, and they will be My people." Seeing His sanctuary set forever in their midst, all nations will know that the LORD makes Israel holy (37:18–28).

## Key Takeaways

**No situation lies too dry or dead for God's life-giving power.** The vision deliberately stresses that these bones are not newly dead, but sun-bleached and "very dry" (37:2)—zero trace of life left. To human eyes, exiled Israel was finished (37:11). Ezekiel's reply in 37:3 sets a model for faith: neither glib optimism based on wishful thinking nor cynical despair based on sight, simply placing the impossible right back into God's hands—"O Lord GOD, only You know!"

**True life requires both God's Word and God's Spirit.** The valley miracle unfolds in two clear stages. At Ezekiel's first proclamation of God's word, skeletons assemble, sinews bind, and flesh and skin cover them—yet what lies before him is still merely a field of corpses (37:7–8). Only when breath—God's Spirit—rushes in at the second call do bodies leap up into a living army (37:9–10). Polished structures and correct outward forms without God's Spirit remain lifeless shells.

**God not only revives life; He heals divisions thought permanent.** Ever since King Solomon's reign over three centuries earlier, God's people had fractured into rival northern (Israel/Ephraim) and southern (Judah) kingdoms, battling repeatedly until both collapsed. Two sticks fusing into one inside God's hand proves His restoration mends even history's deepest wounds (37:19–22).

**The ultimate goal of restoration is God dwelling right among His people.** Returning home, shaking off foreign rule, and reuniting the nation are all precious, yet the true summit shines in 37:26–27: God's sanctuary and dwelling place established forever in their midst. Without God's presence, even the homeland would be ordinary soil.

## Deeper Meaning

**A second creation echoing the Garden of Eden.** Bodies formed with sinews, flesh, and skin yet waiting lifeless without breath in 37:8—followed by breath entering to impart life in 37:10—replays the exact two-step creation of Adam in Genesis: God shaped dust into a human form first, then breathed the breath of life into his nostrils so man became a living being (Genesis 2:7). Moreover, a single Hebrew word (*ruach*) carries all three meanings—*wind*, *breath*, and *Spirit*—weaving all three seamlessly across 37:1–14. When the risen Jesus appears, breathes on His disciples, and says, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22), that new creation breaks into living reality.

**Dead in sin, made alive in Christ.** Ephesians draws straight on resurrection imagery to describe saving grace: while humanity lay dead in trespasses, God rich in mercy made us alive together with Christ (Ephesians 2:1–5). Revelation likewise echoes this valley scene when the breath of life from God enters the two slain witnesses and they stand upright on their feet (Revelation 11:11). And speaking of Israel's future turning back in grace, Paul calls it nothing short of "life from the dead" (Romans 11:15).

**Healing the schism from Rehoboam's day and expanding to all nations.** The twin sticks of Judah and Joseph (Ephraim) recall the painful rift when the ten northern tribes broke away from David's house (1 Kings 12:16–19). Jesus echoed 37:24's promise of "one shepherd" when declaring He had other sheep outside this fold whom He would bring in so there would be "one flock and one shepherd" (John 10:16). Paul saw that unity achieved at the cross, where Christ tore down the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile to create one new unified body (Ephesians 2:14–16).

**God's dwelling place among humanity.** Verse 37:27 echoes Sinai's covenant pledge (Leviticus 26:11–12), swings open the door directly into Ezekiel's majestic closing temple vision (chapters 40–48), and finally rings out on the next-to-last page of Scripture: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man!" (Revelation 21:3).

## Notes & Nuances

**Does the valley of dry bones refer to Israel's national restoration or final bodily resurrection?** Read closely, 37:11–14 gives God's plain primary meaning: in Ezekiel's immediate setting, dry bones symbolize exiled Israel languishing in the "graves" of Babylon, and coming alive promises bringing them home and renewing them by God's Spirit. Yet across the wider arc of Scripture—connecting with Isaiah 26:19, Daniel 12:2, and supremely Jesus' resurrection in the New Testament—both Jewish and Christian interpreters very early recognized here a luminous foundation and preview of real bodily resurrection on the last day. The two meanings never compete; national rebirth opens the horizon toward eternal resurrection.

**One Hebrew word behind *wind*, *breath*, and *Spirit* in 37:1–14.** English readers notice three different nouns shifting across these verses—*Spirit* (37:1; 37:14), *breath* (37:5; 37:9–10), and *winds* (37:9). In Hebrew, every single one translates the same word: *ruach*. That deliberate wordplay blends the wind sweeping the valley, the breath filling human lungs, and God's own life-giving Spirit into one single stream of divine power.

**Why does the second stick carry the names Joseph and Ephraim?** After the kingdom split, Ephraim—son of Joseph—was the largest and dominant northern tribe, so "Joseph" or "Ephraim" routinely stood for all ten northern tribes deported by Assyria more than a century before Ezekiel (722 BC). Joining Joseph's stick with Judah's stick promises that God has forgotten not a single tribe of His people.

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, North African theologian in Carthage writing around AD 200, tackled head-on the classic question about the dry bones: if 37:11 explicitly says the bones represent Israel's national restoration, can this passage still prove bodily resurrection? Tertullian replied with razor logic: nobody illustrates a guaranteed promise using an impossible fiction—the metaphor of dry bones rising works precisely because bodily resurrection is an authentic reality within God's power. *(On the Resurrection of the Flesh, Chapters 29–30)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Tertullian's famous argument in De Resurrectione Carnis chapters 29–30 connecting the dry-bones metaphor to genuine bodily resurrection is widely cited -->

**Irenaeus**, 2nd-century Bishop of Lyon in Gaul, placed 37:12–14 ("I will open your graves") straight beside Adam's creation from dust to refute Gnostic heretics who despised physical bodies. The exact same hand of God that sculpted bones and flesh at creation and breathed life into humanity, he insisted, will open tombs and pour His Spirit into believers' bodies to raise them forever. *(Against Heresies, Book 5, Chapter 15)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Irenaeus directly cites Ezekiel 37:12–14 in Book 5 Chapter 15 to defend bodily resurrection -->

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the monumental collection of ancient Jewish rabbinic debates, records a lively disagreement over the valley of dry bones: one rabbi viewed it purely as a prophetic parable, while several others held it happened as a literal historical miracle—some even claiming those revived men returned to Israel, married, and raised families, whereupon a rabbi present stood up claiming descent from them. The debate demonstrates how early Jewish tradition placed Ezekiel 37 at the core of resurrection hope. *(Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 92b)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the rabbinic debate over whether Ezekiel 37's dry bones were literal or parabolic in Sanhedrin 92b is a very famous Talmudic passage -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew special notice to the two distinct stages of the miracle in 37:7–10: preaching to the bones assembled complete frames, yet they stayed dead still until Ezekiel summoned breath from the four winds. Henry applied this straight to gospel ministry: preaching can form outward order and mental understanding, yet without earnest prayer calling on God's Spirit to breathe life, human hearts never truly awake. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 37)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's classic pastoral application of the two-step preaching and Spirit-breathing miracle in 37:7–10 is well remembered; exact text not checked -->

**N. T. Wright**, New Testament scholar and former Anglican bishop, traced carefully Ezekiel 37's pivotal place in the development of resurrection belief. While agreeing that 37:11–14 originally addressed Israel's national return from exile rather than individual life after death, Wright showed how its daring portrait of graves opening and God's Spirit breathing into dry bones became one of the primary engines fueling genuine bodily resurrection hope across Second Temple Judaism and early Christianity. *(The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analyzing Ezekiel 37's dual role in national return and emerging bodily resurrection hope is a landmark section in the Old Testament chapter of Wright's The Resurrection of the Son of God -->
