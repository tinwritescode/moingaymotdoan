# Ezekiel 10 — Burning Coals Over the City and Glory Leaving the Temple

## Story

The vision inside the Jerusalem temple picks up right after the bloody purge of chapter 9. The man clothed in linen with the writing kit has just returned to report that he has marked the foreheads of everyone grieving over the city's sins, while the six armed executioners are sweeping through the streets carrying out the sentence (9:4–11). At that exact moment, Ezekiel looks up above the expanse over the heads of the cherubim and sees something "like a sapphire stone" resembling a throne (10:1).

From that throne, God calls out to the man clothed in linen, telling him to step in among "the whirling wheels" beneath the cherubim, fill both hands with burning coals from among them, and scatter the coals over Jerusalem. Right before Ezekiel's eyes, the man steps inside to obey (10:2).

As the man in linen enters, the cherubim are standing on the south side of the temple, while a cloud fills the inner court. Then the glory of the LORD rises from above the cherubim and moves to the threshold of the temple. Instantly the cloud fills the house, and the courtyard glows with the radiance of the LORD's glory. The sound of the cherubim's wings beating carries all the way out to the outer court, roaring like the voice of God Almighty when He speaks (10:3–5).

Zooming in closer on the moment the fire is taken, Ezekiel watches the man in linen go in and stand beside one of the wheels. One of the cherubim reaches out from among them into the blazing fire in their midst, scoops up coals, and places them into the hands of the man in linen, who takes them and walks out. Through that gesture, Ezekiel sees clearly the form of a human hand beneath the cherubim's wings (10:6–8).

The prophet's eyes linger over the extraordinary throne-chariot standing beside the temple. Next to each cherub is a wheel—four identical wheels gleaming like beryl, structured like "a wheel within a wheel." Because of that design, they can travel straight in any of the four directions without pivoting; whichever way the front face points, the entire assembly follows. The whole bodies of the cherubim—their backs, hands, and wings, as well as all four wheels—are covered all around with eyes. Ezekiel even hears the wheels called "the whirling wheels." Each cherub has four faces: the first is "the face of a cherub," the second a human face, the third a lion's face, and the fourth an eagle's face (10:9–14).

Then the cherubim rise into the air. At that instant Ezekiel recognizes them beyond doubt: these are the very living creatures he encountered by the Kebar River in Babylon. Cherubim and wheels move in complete unison: when the cherubim move, the wheels roll beside them; when the cherubim lift their wings to rise off the earth, the wheels rise right alongside; when they pause, the wheels stand still, "for the spirit of the living creatures was in the wheels" (10:15–17).

Now arrives the most heartbreaking moment of the chapter. The glory of the LORD departs from the threshold of the temple and moves to stand over the cherubim. Before Ezekiel's eyes, the cherubim spread their wings and lift off the ground, the wheels rising beside them. The entire chariot of glory leaves the main sanctuary area and pauses at the entrance of the east gate of the LORD's house, with the glory of the God of Israel hovering above them (10:18–19).

Closing the chapter, Ezekiel confirms once more so no uncertainty remains: the creatures supporting the throne of God whom he sees in Jerusalem today are the exact living creatures he saw by the Kebar River, and now he knows for certain they are cherubim. Each has four faces and four wings with the form of human hands underneath; their faces are the very faces he saw in exile, and all four move straight ahead (10:20–22).

## Key Takeaways

**A temple cannot rescue a city that has turned its back on God.** Judeans felt sure Jerusalem was invincible because the house of the LORD stood in its center. Chapter 10 delivers a jarring wake-up call: once the sanctuary is polluted by idols and violence, God Himself leaves His own house (10:18–19). A gold-lined stone building without God dwelling inside is merely an empty shell.

**The fire destroying Jerusalem comes from God's own throne, not merely from Babylon's torches.** Five years after this vision, the captain of Babylon's royal guard would march in and burn the temple, the palace, and every house in Jerusalem (2 Kings 25:8–9). Before any earthly flame is lit, Ezekiel sees the real fire already drawn from among the cherubim beneath God's throne (10:2; 10:6–7). Babylon's victory never proves its gods are stronger; it is a righteous verdict permitted by the Holy One of Israel.

**God departs slowly, step by step, rather than walking away in a flash.** Tracing the movement of God's glory reveals a deliberate, unhurried pace: from the Most Holy Place to the temple threshold (9:3; 10:4), from the threshold onto the cherubim (10:18), pausing at the entrance of the east gate (10:19), and only at the end of chapter 11 leaving the city altogether to rest on the mountain to the east (11:22–23). Each pause speaks of the heavy reluctance of a God who takes no pleasure in abandoning His people.

**The One departing Jerusalem is the very One who already came seeking the exiles.** Twice Ezekiel stresses that the cherubim lifting God's throne out of the temple are the exact living creatures he saw by the Kebar River (10:15; 10:20). That seemingly repetitive detail holds deep comfort amid devastation: God has not vanished from the world; He leaves a defiled building in Jerusalem and heads eastward—straight toward Babylonian exile, where His chariot already appeared to the captives back in chapter 1.

## Deeper Meaning

**Reversing the dedication of Solomon's temple.** When King Solomon dedicated the temple, the cloud and the glory of the LORD filled the house so densely that the priests could not even stand to minister (1 Kings 8:10–11). In Ezekiel 10, the cloud and brilliant radiance once again fill the temple and courtyard (10:4)—yet this time not to take up residence, but to bid farewell. The scene also recalls the earlier grief when the ark was captured and Eli's daughter-in-law named her newborn Ichabod, saying, "The glory has departed from Israel" (1 Samuel 4:21–22).

**The Day of Atonement ritual turned completely upside down.** In Leviticus, the high priest dressed in linen entered the Most Holy Place carrying a censer full of glowing coals from the altar so incense smoke would shield the mercy seat between the two cherubim and make atonement for the people (Leviticus 16:12–13; Exodus 25:18–22). Here again a man dressed in linen steps between the cherubim to take glowing coals—not to bring incense inside pleading for forgiveness, but to carry coals outward and scatter fire across the city (10:2; 10:7). Sadder still, while a glowing coal from the altar once touched Isaiah's lips to cleanse his sin (Isaiah 6:6–7), for a city refusing to repent, that very holy fire becomes consuming judgment.

**Departing through the east gate, and returning through the east gate.** At the end of chapter 10, God's glory halts at the east gate entrance before leaving the city altogether in chapter 11 (10:19; 11:23). More than thirty chapters later, when Ezekiel sees the vision of the new temple, that very same glory—identical to the visions by the Kebar River and at the city's destruction—returns from the east, enters through the exact same eastern gateway, and fills the house once more (43:1–5). Departure is not the final period of salvation history.

**Echoes in Revelation and the incarnation of Christ.** In the New Testament, the angel filling his censer with fire from the altar and hurling it onto the earth amid thunder and earthquake connects directly to the linen-clad figure scattering coals here (Revelation 8:5), while the four living creatures covered with eyes around God's throne echo these eye-covered cherubim (10:12; Revelation 4:6–8). Still more profoundly, the promise of God's glory returning to dwell among His people finds ultimate fulfillment in Jesus: the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us, and His disciples beheld His glory (John 1:14).

## Notes & Nuances

**Why does "the face of an ox" from chapter 1 become "the face of a cherub" in 10:14?** In chapter 1, each living creature has the faces of a man, a lion, an ox, and an eagle (1:10). In 10:14, however, the ox's place is listed as "the face of a cherub," even though 10:22 insists these are the exact same faces seen by the Kebar River. Two familiar explanations exist. Modern scholars often point to ancient Near Eastern art—where temple-guarding cherubim typically took the form of winged bulls—or to a possible early scribal slip. Ancient Jewish tradition offers a deeply moving theological explanation: the ox's face brought to mind Israel's sin with the golden calf at Mount Sinai, so God changed it lest anything remind Him of that old disgrace at the very moment Jerusalem stood on trial.

**Why does the man in linen take the coals and walk out without a scene showing him scattering them?** Verse 10:7 simply notes that he received the fire and went out, never narrating flames falling on the rooftops. That omission is deliberate storytelling: chapter 10's spotlight has shifted away from the mechanics of physical destruction (already depicted starkly in chapter 9) onto a far deeper tragedy—the departure of the glory of God from the temple. Moreover, once the decree is placed into the messenger's hands, Jerusalem's fiery doom is already sealed beyond reversal.

**Why does chapter 10 repeat so many details already described in chapter 1?** Modern readers can find chapter 10 repetitive or slightly interrupted where the action of taking coals pauses for a description of wheels, wings, and eyes (10:8–17). For exiles listening to Ezekiel, however, this repetition was essential: he needed to establish unmistakably that the God who appeared by the Kebar River in pagan Babylon is the exact same God of the Jerusalem temple, and that those wondrous living creatures are the cherubim attending His throne (10:15; 10:20). In addition, whereas in chapter 1 only the wheel rims had eyes, here the cherubim's backs, hands, and wings are covered with eyes as well (10:12)—underscoring that not a single hidden sin in the city escaped God's sight.

**The departure is not yet finished at the end of chapter 10.** God's glory has paused at the east gate of the temple (10:19). Right at that same eastern gate, chapter 11 opens with twenty-five leaders plotting evil, and only at the close of chapter 11 does God's glory finally rise clear of the city onto the mountain to the east (11:22–23). Reading both chapters together preserves the full drama.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic Jewish interpretive work compiled between the 3rd and 6th centuries, paid close attention to the change from "the face of an ox" in chapter 1 to "the face of a cherub" in verse 14. The sages explained that Ezekiel pleaded for mercy so the ox's face would be changed to a cherub's face (understood via Aramaic as a childlike face), because the ox recalled the sin of the golden calf; an accuser reminding heaven of past guilt could not serve as an advocate lifting up the throne. *(Babylonian Talmud, Hagigah 13b)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the shift from ox face to cherub face in Hagigah 13b is well remembered, though whether the golden-calf reasoning appears directly in the Talmud text or in Rashi's gloss on it warrants verification -->

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, emphasized in his comments on the linen-clad figure stepping among the wheels for coals that the fire consuming Jerusalem came straight from between the cherubim—the very dwelling of holiness. This proved that the city's ruin was no random accident of war, but a just sentence decreed by God, while the withdrawal of His glory showed that a sanctuary retains its value only so long as the people remain faithful. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's points on the holy source of the fire and the departure of glory in chapter 10 are well remembered; exact book number among his fourteen books not checked -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, pointed out that the direct aim of this vision was to shatter the Judeans' blind pride in a building of stone. They assumed that as long as the temple stood, God was obligated to protect them regardless of their sins; therefore God let Ezekiel watch His glory walk out of the sanctuary before the city burned. Calvin also maintained his customary restraint when reading the wheels and eyes, steering clear of fanciful speculation so readers stay focused on God's wise, all-seeing rule. *(Commentaries on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of the Prophet Ezekiel, on chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's argument against presumption on the temple and restrained approach to the vision in chapter 10 are well remembered; individual lectures not verified on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, lingered tenderly over the slow, measured steps of God's departing glory. He observed that God did not abandon His house abruptly, but withdrew by short stages—from the Most Holy Place to the threshold, from the threshold onto the cherubim, and then pausing at the east gate—like a reluctant father glancing back before leaving a beloved home, displaying His long-suffering patience and sorrow at being forced to depart. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 10)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the observation on the reluctant, step-by-step departure of glory is a signature feature of Matthew Henry's notes on chapter 10; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, examined how deliberately chapter 10 weaves back into chapter 1 and identifies the creatures from the Kebar River as the temple cherubim. Linking the two visions, he argued, delivered the vital message the exiles needed: the heavenly throne-chariot leaving Jerusalem's temple had already chosen to appear among them in Babylon, proving that God's presence and power did not die with the sanctuary. *(Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's analysis of the link between chapters 1 and 10 and its consolation for the exiles is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, American Old Testament professor, highlighted the bitter reversal of the Day of Atonement rite from Leviticus 16. The linen-clad priestly figure who once took glowing coals from the altar to carry incense inward and plead for mercy now receives burning coals from between the cherubim to scatter outward and ignite the city.
