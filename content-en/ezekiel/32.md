# Ezekiel 32 — Lament Over Pharaoh and the Graveyard of Empires in Sheol

## Story

Chapter 32 brings the entire eight-chapter cycle of foreign-nation oracles (chapters 25–32) to a close with two of the heaviest, most visually gripping funeral dirges in the book of Ezekiel. The first lament reaches the prophet in "the twelfth year, in the twelfth month, on the first day"—early March 585 BC, shortly after news of Jerusalem's fall reached the Babylonian exiles (cf. 33:21). God tells Ezekiel to lift up a "lamentation for Pharaoh king of Egypt": he fancies himself majestic "like a lion among the nations," yet in truth he is merely "like a monster in the seas," thrashing clumsily through rivers, "churning up the waters with your feet and muddying the streams"—a troublemaker stirring turmoil across the region (32:1–2).

God steps in like a giant hunter casting a net: He spreads His net over Pharaoh with an assembly of many peoples, hauls the river monster ashore, and flings him onto open ground so birds of the sky perch on him and wild beasts of the whole earth eat their fill. His flesh piles high across mountains and valleys, and his blood drenches the land across which he once swam (32:3–6).

The scene then expands to cosmic scale as God announces the extinguishing of Pharaoh's brilliance: "When I extinguish you, I will cover the heavens and darken their stars. I will cover the sun with a cloud, and the moon will not give its light." Pitch darkness blankets all Egypt. Word of her ruin ripples out to distant lands, troubling many peoples and making kings shudder in dread as God brandishes His sword—a sword placed directly into the hands of "the king of Babylon" alongside troops "the most ruthless of the nations" to humble Egypt's pride. Every riverside beast is wiped out with biting irony: from now on the Nile's waters will settle clear and "run like oil" simply because no human foot or animal hoof remains alive to muddy them! The daughters of the nations will sing this dirge mourning over Egypt (32:7–16).

Exactly two weeks later—"in the twelfth year, on the fifteenth day of the month" (mid-March 585 BC)—God's word arrives with a second funeral chant, crafting a chilling tour through a vast subterranean graveyard of empires inside Sheol. God commands Ezekiel to wail over Egypt's hordes and cast them down to the underworld alongside those descending to the Pit. A mocking question rings out: "Whom do you surpass in beauty? Go down and be laid to rest with the uncircumcised!" From the depths of Sheol, mighty warriors of old rise to greet sword-slain Pharaoh arriving to join their ranks (32:17–21).

Slowly the dirge calls the roll of five renowned powers that once terrorized the earth, all now stretched out flat inside deep rows of graves. First comes **Assyria** ringed by her slaughtered armies—warriors who once "spread terror in the land of the living," all fallen by the sword (32:22–23). Second stands **Elam** with all her hordes, lying shamed among the uncircumcised (32:24–25). Third come the northern warrior peoples **Meshech and Tubal**, denied even the honorable burial of ancient heroes laid to rest with swords beneath their heads, bearing their guilt straight upon their bones (32:26–28). Fourth lies **Edom** with all her kings and mighty princes (32:29). Fifth appear "all the princes of the north and all the Sidonians"—once spreading dread everywhere, now lying in disgrace among the slain (32:30).

Closing the chapter is a haunting blend of gloom and bitter irony: gazing around this massive cemetery crammed with shattered empires, Pharaoh is "consoled" over his ruined army—simply seeing he is not the only giant brought down. God stamps the final seal across the entire Egypt cycle: though he once spread terror across the land of the living, Pharaoh and all his hordes end up laid to rest among the uncircumcised, slain by the sword (32:31–32).

## Key Takeaways

**What calls itself a noble lion is often just a monster muddying the waters of peace.** Pharaoh prided himself on the royal image of "a lion among the nations" (32:2), yet God's clear eyes saw Egypt's meddling and schemes as a crocodile thrashing mud through every nearby river. What human propaganda paints as heroism, God calls sheer disruption.

**When a superpower collapses, it feels like the end of the world to those trusting in it.** Clouded sun, darkened moon, and dimmed stars (32:7–8) capture the seismic shock when antiquity's oldest, proudest civilization crashes. To Egyptians worshiping the sun god Ra as supreme deity, blacking out the sky declares every guardian idol extinguished before the LORD.

**Terror built on violence never buys immortality for any empire.** The refrain "spread terror in the land of the living" tolls six times across the chapter's second half (32:23; 32:24; 32:25; 32:26; 32:27; 32:32) like a funeral bell ringing over each tomb—Assyria, Elam, Meshech, Tubal, Edom, Sidon, and Egypt. Every regime that rules by fear eventually lies motionless underground.

**The grave levels every human illusion of superiority.** The taunt "Whom do you surpass in beauty?" (32:19) slices straight through national vanity and royal rank. Under God's verdict, famous Pharaohs with towering pyramids and elaborate embalming rites wind up sharing the exact same pit with all the sword-slain (32:31–32).

## Deeper Meaning

**Darkened sun, moon, and stars connecting the prophets to the Gospels.** Blacking out the heavens at Egypt's downfall (32:7–8) uses classic prophetic-apocalyptic language whenever God's judgment overturns world powers. Joel described the day of the LORD identically: "The sun and moon grow dark, and the stars lose their brightness" (Joel 2:10; 2:31). In the New Testament, Jesus draws on that very prophetic idiom when announcing vast judgment in His Olivet Discourse (Matthew 24:29), and Revelation opens the sixth seal with the sun turning black as sackcloth and the moon turning like blood (Revelation 6:12).

**Bookending the sea-monster motif from chapter 29 through chapter 32.** The four chapters against Egypt (chapters 29–32) lock tightly together: chapter 29 opened with Pharaoh as "the great monster lying in the midst of his rivers" hooked out onto the desert for beasts and birds (29:3–5); chapter 32 closes with that same water monster netted ashore, its carcass heaped over hills and valleys (32:2–6). Meanwhile, underworld powers welcoming a newly arrived tyrant (32:21–31) directly extend chapter 31's underworld Eden trees and Isaiah 14:9–15's underworld taunt over Babylon's king.

**Closing foreign judgment to usher in restoration starting in chapter 33.** Notice the timestamp of chapter 32: twelfth year, twelfth month (32:1; 32:17)—barely two months after the fugitive arrived from Jerusalem in the tenth month reporting the city's fall (33:21). Ending the seven Egypt oracles in an eerie silent graveyard of dead empires right before the book's final third (chapters 33–48) sends an unmistakable message to the exiles: while every earthly superpower marches one by one into graves never to rise again, in chapter 37 God will breathe His Spirit across Israel's valley of dry bones and raise His people back up out of their graves!

## Notes & Nuances

**Do "the fallen mighty warriors" in 32:27 echo the ancient *Nephilim*?** In 32:27, the Hebrew uses *gibborim nofelim* ("fallen warriors")—echoing closely the famed ancient giants or mighty men (*Nephilim*) of Genesis 6:4. Translators and scholars note two readings of 32:27: the standard Hebrew text reads that Meshech and Tubal do *not* lie alongside ancient heroes given honorable burial with swords beneath their heads (because their crimes were too grievous); the ancient Greek Septuagint omits "not," reading that they *do* lie right alongside those fallen warriors. Under either reading, the irony holds firm: all martial glory shrinks down to cold bones in the Pit.

**The Old Testament imagery of "Sheol" in chapter 32.** Chapter 32 is not sketching a detailed New Testament map of heaven and hell. Ezekiel draws on standard Old Testament poetic imagery of *Sheol* ("the Pit" or realm of the dead)—the shadowy underworld where all mortals descend—to dramatize the humiliating collapse of proud empires. The point is never afterlife geography, but theological reality: military might and grand royal tombs rescue nobody from God's verdict.

**Why does "the uncircumcised" ring out ten times across the second half of chapter 32?** As noted in chapter 31, ancient Egyptians practiced circumcision and regarded uncircumcised outsiders (as well as battlefield dead denied proper mummification and burial) as defiled and stripped of afterlife honor. Hammering the refrain "laid to rest with the uncircumcised" and "uncircumcised slain by the sword" (32:19; 32:21; 32:24; 32:28; 32:30; 32:32) delivers a devastating psychological blow straight at Pharaoh's deepest cultural pride.

**Minor textual and translation quirks in chapter 32.** Older printed editions contain a few minor typographical or archaic-phrasing slips across verses 32:9, 32:15, 32:26, and 32:30 (touching the spread of ruin, the emptying of the land, the graves, and spreading terror freely), yet the underlying Hebrew poetic flow remains steady and unmistakable across both laments.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, clarified that the darkened heavens of 32:7–8 did not denote the literal end of the physical cosmos right then, but forceful prophetic imagery expressing overwhelming national mourning when an empire collapses—as though sun, moon, and stars went out before defeated eyes. Looking across the graveyard roll call (32:22–30), Jerome noted that God places Assyria, Elam, Edom, and Sidon beside Egypt as an enduring warning for every era: no power leaning on the sword stands forever. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 32)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's figurative reading of the darkened skies in verses 7–8 and timeless lesson from the underworld empires in chapter 32 are well remembered; exact Latin wording not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew a vivid reflection from 32:13–14—where Egypt's rivers settle clear and "run like oil" only once the mud-churning monster is removed and the land falls quiet. Similarly, Henry observed, when God removes proud troublemakers from society, surrounding lands finally recover peace. At 32:31, he labeled Pharaoh's "consolation" at seeing other dead tyrants beside him the bleakest comfort imaginable—the wretched solace of despairing company in misery. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 32)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic notes on the calm clear waters of verse 14 and Pharaoh's wretched consolation in verse 31 are well remembered; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Walther Zimmerli**, Swiss Old Testament scholar, viewed chapter 32 as a somber, thunderous finale closing the entire foreign-nation block (chapters 25–32). He analyzed the heavy funeral cadence of 32:17–32 with its tolling refrains—"uncircumcised," "slain by the sword," "spread terror in the land of the living"—sounding like a slow dirge escorting empire after empire into permanent silence, clearing the historical stage so God can begin rebuilding His people from chapter 33 onward. *(Ezekiel 2, Hermeneia, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Zimmerli's analysis of the funeral cadence and structural closure of chapters 25–32 in Hermeneia volume 2 is well remembered; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, spotlighted the deft shift of metaphor in 32:2: Pharaoh loves picturing himself as a royal land lion, yet the prophet instantly deflates that image into a thrashing Nile crocodile clouding the water with mud. Greenberg also noted the striking symmetry dividing chapter 32: the first lament (32:1–16) closes with an utterly empty earth above, whereas the second lament (32:17–32) opens an overcrowded underworld packed with fallen armies below—proving no empire can endure outside the LORD's sovereignty. *(Ezekiel 21–37, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's analysis of the lion-to-crocodile shift in verse 2 and the empty-earth versus crowded-Sheol contrast in Anchor Bible volume 2 is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, Canadian-American Old Testament professor, pointed to the most intriguing omission in the underworld cemetery list of 32:22–30: Assyria, Elam, Meshech, Tubal, Edom, Sidon, and Egypt all appear, yet **Babylon**—the reigning superpower of that very moment—is conspicuously absent! The reason is clear, Block explained: in 585 BC, Babylon still served as the sword in God's hand executing judgment on Egypt (32:11); nevertheless, the universal principle hammered home across chapter 32—that everyone who "spreads terror in the land of the living" eventually descends to the Pit—quietly seals the unavoidable doom of every violent empire to come. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 25–48, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's sharp observation on Babylon's deliberate absence from chapter 32's Sheol roster in NICOT volume 2 is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->
