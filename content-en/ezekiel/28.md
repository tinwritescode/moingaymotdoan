# Ezekiel 28 — The King of Tyre, the Fallen Cherub, and Sidon

## Story

After two chapters pronouncing doom on the port city of Tyre as a whole, the word of the LORD comes to Ezekiel to zoom in squarely on the man at its helm: the ruler of Tyre. God lays bare the sky-high arrogance swelling inside his heart, daring to boast: "I am a god; I sit in the seat of gods in the heart of the seas." Perched on an impregnable offshore island, this monarch fancies himself divine, yet God confronts him with plain reality: though he sets his mind like the mind of a god, "you are a man and not a god." With razor-sharp sarcasm, God mocks his pretensions: "Behold, you are wiser than Daniel; no secret is hidden from you!" Through shrewdness, skill, and vast trade he has piled gold and silver into his treasuries—and that mounting fortune has inflated his heart with pride (28:1–5).

Sentence falls swiftly and conclusively. Because Tyre's ruler considers his mind equal to God's, God will summon the most ruthless of foreign nations against him to draw their swords against his dazzling wisdom and defile his splendor. They will thrust him down into the Pit to die a violent death in the heart of the seas. God asks a question that punctures every illusion: "Will you still say, 'I am a god,' in the presence of those who slay you?" Helpless in the hands of those who wound him, he will stand exposed as a frail mortal, dying at the hands of foreigners "the death of the uncircumcised"—a shameful end cast into defilement (28:6–10).

The word of the LORD comes a second time, commanding Ezekiel to lift a funeral lament over the king of Tyre painted in radiant imagery transcending everyday earthly terms. The dirge recalls his dazzling original splendor: "You were the seal of perfection, full of wisdom and perfect in beauty." He dwelled in "Eden, the garden of God," adorned with nine precious stones—ruby, topaz, diamond, beryl, onyx, jasper, sapphire, turquoise, and emerald—mounted in gold, with tambourines and pipes (or intricate gold settings) prepared for him on the very day he was created (28:11–13).

His initial station was breathtakingly high: "You were anointed as a guardian cherub, for I had ordained you. You were on the holy mountain of God; you walked among the fiery stones." His ways were blameless from the day he was created—until iniquity was found right inside him (28:14–15).

Then comes the fall. "Through the abundance of your trade, you were filled with violence, and you sinned." God drove him in disgrace like a profaned thing off the mountain of God and banished him from among the fiery stones. His own beauty made his heart grow proud, and his splendor corrupted his wisdom. God hurled him to the earth and laid him exposed as a spectacle before earthly kings. Because he profaned his sanctuaries through countless iniquities and dishonest trade, God declares: "I brought forth fire from within you, and it consumed you," reducing him to ashes on the ground before everyone's eyes. All nations who knew him gasp in horror; he has come to a dreadful end and will be no more forever (28:16–19).

Rounding off the four-part sequence on Tyre, the word of the LORD directs Ezekiel to set his face toward Sidon—the ancient Phoenician harbor city just north of Tyre—and prophesy against her. God declares He will display His glory and show Himself holy through judgment upon Sidon: plague will sweep in, blood will flow through her streets, and the sword will strike from every side until the wounded fall right inside her walls, forcing them to know He is the LORD (28:20–23).

Suddenly, right after these heavy verdicts, the sky breaks open with a tender promise for the exiles. God declares that from then on, the house of Israel will never again suffer "a painful brier or a sharp thorn" from any surrounding neighbors who treated them with contempt. When God gathers the house of Israel from the peoples among whom they were scattered and displays His holiness in them before the nations, they will return to live securely in the land He gave His servant Jacob. They will build houses, plant vineyards, and dwell in safety once God has judged all who despised them—and they will know that He is the LORD their God (28:24–26).

## Key Takeaways

**Playing God is the summit of human arrogance.** Tyre's king imagined that an unconquerable island and overflowing vaults lifted him onto a divine throne (28:2). Scripture brings him crashing back to earth with one cool question: with an executioner's blade pressed to his throat, will he still call himself a god? (28:9). Every human fantasy of absolute power shatters against mortality.

**Talent, beauty, and success can corrupt a once-noble heart.** The lament never denies that Tyre's king once possessed wisdom, splendor, and extraordinary gifts (28:12–15). The tragedy is that flourishing trade, beauty, and glory fed his pride until he twisted his wisdom and filled himself with violence (28:16–17). Success is not evil in itself, yet it serves as the fiercest test of the human heart.

**Sin ignites its own destruction from within.** In 28:18, the fire consuming Tyre's king does not merely strike from outside—it comes "forth from within you." Greed, dishonesty, and corrupt trade rot an empire silently from the inside until the day it erupts into ashes before the nations.

**Judgment on proud superpowers clears the path for God's people to live in peace.** The chapter does not end in the blood and ashes of Tyre and Sidon, but with Israelite families returning home to build houses, plant vineyards, and dwell safely (28:25–26). God brings down self-proclaimed gods not out of bloodlust, but to clear painful thorns away from His people and vindicate His holiness before the world.

## Deeper Meaning

**Echoes of the Garden of Eden and humanity's primal fall.** The dirge across 28:12–19 brims with motifs from Scripture's opening pages: "Eden, the garden of God," a "cherub," blamelessness from the day of creation, sin entering, and expulsion from the holy presence (Genesis 2:8; 3:24). Though spoken over Tyre's king, Ezekiel frames him inside humanity's universal tragedy: fashioned in glory to walk before God, reaching to become like God, and tumbling down into dust and ash.

**Nine gemstones echoing the high priest's breastpiece.** The catalog of nine precious stones covering this figure in 28:13 matches almost item for item three of the four rows set into Israel's high priestly breastpiece (Exodus 28:17–20). That link illuminates "profaning your sanctuaries" in 28:18: across the ancient world, monarchs regularly functioned as priest-kings claiming to represent deity inside royal temples. Tyre's king turned sacred office into a racket for greed and deceit.

**Paralleling the taunt against Babylon's king and the shadow of Satan.** Watching someone who exalted himself to God's level get hurled down to earth (28:2; 28:16–17), Bible readers immediately recall Isaiah's taunt over Babylon's king: "How you have fallen from heaven, O day star, son of the dawn!"—who vowed to "make myself like the Most High" only to plunge into Sheol (Isaiah 14:12–15). Both passages expose the same proud spirit animating earthly tyrants who play god, rooted in God's unseen adversary—echoed when Jesus declared, "I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (Luke 10:18).

**From Tyre's throne to King Herod and the "man of lawlessness" in the New Testament.** History replays the temptation of self-deification again and again. Centuries later, when King Herod took his throne in royal robes and gave an address, the crowd cheered, "This is the voice of a god, not a man!"—and an angel of the Lord struck him down immediately because he refused to give glory to God (Acts 12:21–23). Paul likewise foretold a final "man of lawlessness" echoing Tyre's boast verbatim as he "takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God" (2 Thessalonians 2:4).

**Clearing away "thorns" to fulfill the promise of peaceful rest.** Promising that neighboring nations will no longer be "a painful brier or a sharp thorn" to Israel (28:24) directly recalls Moses' ancient warning in Numbers: if Israel left hostile pagan nations unaddressed, they would become "barbs in your eyes and thorns in your sides" (Numbers 33:55). Closing chapter 28 amid exile's darkness, God plants the seed of the grand restoration chapters to come (34:25–28; 36:24–28): a day when His people will come home to build and dwell in genuine safety.

## Notes & Nuances

**Is the figure in 28:12–19 the king of Tyre, Adam, or Satan?** This ranks among the most intensely debated questions in the Old Testament, with three major approaches. The first stays close to the immediate historical context: the lament is explicitly titled "concerning the king of Tyre" (28:12), citing "abundance of trade," "profaned sanctuaries," and exposure "before kings" (28:16–18); Eden and the holy mountain serve as heightened poetic-mythic language magnifying a human monarch's loftiness and catastrophic crash. The second—widespread from early Church Fathers down to many Christians today—looks through Tyre's king to catch sight of Satan: descriptions like "in Eden," "anointed guardian cherub," and "blameless from the day you were created" soar past any mortal ruler, simultaneously addressing the earthly king while lifting the veil on the fallen angelic power fueling his pride. The third draws on the ancient Greek Septuagint in 28:14 and 28:16: because unvocalized Hebrew consonants for "you were" (*att*) can also be read "with" (*et*), the Greek renders that he was placed *with* the guardian cherub on God's holy mountain—picturing an Adam-like first human dwelling in Eden beside the cherub before sinning and being expelled. Holding all three perspectives lets readers honor the clear historical setting while appreciating the deeper cosmic horizon the text evokes.

**Who is "Daniel" in 28:3?** Just as in 14:14 where Daniel appears alongside Noah and Job, scholars divide into two main views. Many identify him with the biblical prophet Daniel, Ezekiel's contemporary deported to Babylon several years earlier who had already gained royal fame for unraveling mysteries. Others point out that Ezekiel spells the name slightly differently in Hebrew from the book of Daniel, matching *Danel*, a famed wise and righteous ancient Canaanite king celebrated in Ugaritic epics well known to a Phoenician ruler of Tyre. Either way, the irony cuts cleanly: Tyre's king imagines himself wiser than history's wisest sage, yet acts like the biggest fool alive by thinking he is God.

**Two obscure phrases: "the death of the uncircumcised" (28:10) and "tambourines and pipes" (28:13).** For Israelites—and according to ancient records, also for several Phoenician and Egyptian groups who practiced circumcision—dying "the death of the uncircumcised" meant dying disgracefully on the battlefield, denied honorable burial and tossed into an unclean pit in the underworld, a motif recurring heavily in chapter 32. As for "tambourines and pipes" in 28:13, this renders two very rare Hebrew terms: older translations understand them as musical instruments evoking royal coronation festivities, whereas many modern versions—including the BSB—follow the immediately preceding catalog of nine gemstones and translate them as gold "settings and mountings" crafted for jewels.

**The brief yet fierce oracle against Sidon (28:20–23).** Compared with three full chapters devoted to Tyre, Sidon's verdict spans just four verses filled with plague, blood, and sword. Sidon was the ancient sister port just north of Tyre, often allied with Tyre and repeatedly pressuring or scorning Israel. Crucially, the passage does not stop at Sidon's bloodshed, moving straight into the ultimate aim of every judgment here: displaying God's holiness and securing a peaceful, unthreatened home for His people (28:24–26).

## What Commentators Say

**Tertullian**, the theologian of Carthage (North Africa) writing around AD 200, was among the earliest Christian voices to read Ezekiel 28's dirge as unveiling the devil's fall. Debating the heretic Marcion—who blamed the Creator for evil—Tertullian appealed to the blameless cherub in Eden in whom iniquity later arose, insisting God originally created that angel wholly good; evil emerged solely when he freely abused his will and turned against God. *(Against Marcion, Book 2, Chapter 10)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Against Marcion Book 2 Chapter 10 is famous for citing Ezekiel 28 directly to explain the origin of evil and Satan's fall -->

**Origen**, the great 3rd-century Alexandrian interpreter, likewise discerned an invisible spiritual power behind the human prince of Tyre in chapter 28. Expressions like dwelling in Eden, walking among fiery stones, and standing as a cherub on God's holy mountain cannot apply purely to an ordinary mortal king, he observed; thus through Tyre's monarch, Scripture simultaneously exposes the fallen angelic ruler hurled down from heaven by pride. *(On First Principles, Book 1; Homilies on Ezekiel, Homily 13)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Origen's reading of Ezekiel 28 concerning the demonic power behind Tyre's prince in On First Principles Book 1 and Homilies on Ezekiel Homily 13 is well remembered; exact wording not checked -->

**Augustine**, 4th–5th century Bishop of Hippo in North Africa, also noted while discussing the fall of evil angels how widely interpreters applied Ezekiel 28's lament over Tyre's king to the devil. While cautious about pressing every poetic detail literally, Augustine warmly endorsed the theological truth exposed here: God created no evil nature from the start; every fall springs from pride, when a creature grows infatuated with its own beauty and deserts its Creator. *(The City of God, Book 11)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Augustine's reference to Ezekiel 28 when discussing the fall of angels in Book 11 of The City of God is well remembered; exact chapter number and Latin wording not verified -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, stayed grounded in the historical king of Tyre, underlining the piercing irony of verse 9: sitting enthroned amid island wealth, a ruler easily imagines himself divine, yet one brush with an attacker's sword shatters the delusion instantaneously. Turning to the close of the chapter, Henry lingered over the sweet comfort of verses 25–26: right inside stern oracles against foreign powers, God deliberately weaves a loving word so Judean exiles will not despair, knowing every judgment serves their peaceful return. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 28)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar emphasis on the irony of verse 9 and the restoration comfort ending chapter 28 is well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, Jewish biblical scholar at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, closely analyzed the textual divergence between the Hebrew ("you were a cherub") and Septuagint ("with the cherub") in verses 14 and 16. In his view, the dirge draws on ancient traditions of Eden and primal humanity placed in splendor and expelled for pride to craft a biting caricature of Tyre's king: fancying himself an immortal demigod in radiant temples, he ends up profaning his own sanctuaries through commercial greed and crumbling into ash. *(Ezekiel 21–37, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's Hebrew-vs-Septuagint textual analysis and Eden motif study on chapter 28 in Anchor Bible volume 2 are well remembered; exact page numbers not verified -->
