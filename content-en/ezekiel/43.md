# Ezekiel 43 — The Glory of the LORD Returns to the Temple

## Story

After three full chapters measuring every wall, gatehouse, and chamber of the new temple, the bronze-like messenger finished measuring the vast square wall dividing holy from common at the end of chapter 42 (42:15–20). Everything stands spotless and ready, yet the house remains empty of its Owner. As chapter 43 opens, the guide brings Ezekiel back to the east gate—the exact gateway through which, more than a decade earlier, he had watched in agony as God's glory walked out (43:1).

From the east sweeps an overwhelming sight: the glory of the God of Israel is coming back. His voice resounds "like the roar of many waters," and the whole earth blazes with His radiance. Instantly Ezekiel recognizes the appearance: it is the very vision he encountered beside the Kebar River in Babylon, and the very vision he saw when sent to announce Jerusalem's destruction. Overcome by such majesty, he collapses "facedown" on the ground (43:2–3).

The glory of the LORD marches straight into the temple through the gate facing east. At that exact moment, the Spirit lifts Ezekiel up and carries him into the inner court. Before his eyes unfolds the moment every exile longed for above all else: "the glory of the LORD filled the temple" (43:4–5).

From inside the temple a voice speaks directly to Ezekiel, while the bronze-like guide stands quietly beside him. The voice proclaims solemnly: "Son of man, this is the place of My throne and the place for the soles of My feet, where I will dwell among the Israelites forever." Then God names bluntly the old stain that once drove Him out: Israel and their kings defiled His holy name through idolatry—called straight out "prostitution"—and through "the corpses of their kings at their high places" (or royal burials). They brazenly set their own thresholds and doorposts right alongside God's sanctuary until "only a wall" separated Him from their abominations. Those outrages ignited His wrath to consume them. Now He draws a clean, permanent line: put away idolatry and royal tombs far from Me, and I will dwell among them forever (43:6–9).

God next hands Ezekiel an extraordinary pastoral assignment for his fellow exiles: describe this temple's design to the house of Israel, "so that they will be ashamed of their iniquities." Once they feel genuine shame for everything they have done, he is to lay out the complete design before their eyes—layout, exits, entrances, every statute and law—and write it down clearly so they may guard and obey it. The entire vast blueprint condenses into one solemn summary sentence: "This is the law of the temple: All its surrounding territory on top of the mountain shall be most holy" (43:10–12).

Now that God's glory occupies the house, the guide turns directly to the center of the inner court to measure the altar of burnt offering—the exact place where God's people meet Him through atonement. Measured in long royal cubits (a cubit plus a handbreadth), the altar rises in stepped tiers narrowing upward: at the bottom rests a base one cubit high and one cubit wide rimmed by a one-span gutter; above it rises a lower ledge two cubits high; then a larger ledge four cubits high measuring fourteen cubits square; crowning the top sits the altar hearth—a twelve-cubit square rising four cubits high, with four horns projecting from its four corners. On the eastern side steps lead up for priests to minister (43:13–17).

Even newly built, the altar cannot be used immediately; it must undergo seven full days of purification and consecration. The Lord GOD assigns this rite to "the Levitical priests who are descendants of Zadok," those privileged to draw near to serve Him. On day one, Ezekiel must take a spotless young bull as a sin offering, smear its blood on the altar's four horns, the four corners of the ledge, and all around the rim to purify and atone for it, burning the bull's carcass at the appointed spot outside the sanctuary. On day two, he presents a spotless male goat to purify the altar further, followed by a spotless young bull and ram seasoned with salt as burnt offerings. Day after day for seven consecutive days, a sin-offering goat plus a bull and ram for burnt offerings consecrate the altar completely. Only from the eighth day onward, once seven days of cleansing are complete, do priests begin presenting the people's burnt offerings and peace offerings—and God gives His assured promise: "I will accept you" (43:18–27).

## Key Takeaways

**No matter how magnificent a temple looks, it is an empty shell without God's presence.** Across chapters 40–42 we walked through hundreds of meticulous measurements of gates, courts, and chambers, yet everything stood silent. Only in 43:2–5, when God's glory marches in from the east to fill the house, does the entire sanctuary come alive. Outward buildings, rituals, and church programs mean nothing unless God Himself dwells at the center.

**God's holiness refuses to share quarters with sin.** God speaks plainly about why He once left: Israel and their kings dragged idols and royal tombs right up against temple masonry until "only a wall" separated the holy from the defiled (43:8). In the new sanctuary, the entire mountaintop territory is "most holy" (43:12). God never settles for one tidy corner alongside rival idols in our lives; He claims the whole house.

**The beauty of God's grace moves human hearts to honest repentance.** Amazingly, in 43:10–11 God does not tell Ezekiel to wave a long list of charges to scold the exiles; He tells him to show them the dazzling design of His restored house "so that they will be ashamed of their iniquities." Beholding God's purity, order, and faithfulness—ready to come live among them again—awakens honest humility before Him. Deep repentance springs not merely from fear of judgment, but from seeing God's radiant kindness.

**Approaching the Holy One always passes through cleansing and atonement.** Even a brand-new visionary altar untainted by past idolatry still requires seven consecutive days of blood purification before God accepts the people's offerings on day eight (43:18–27). Scripture underscores one unchanging truth: sinners never waltz casually up to a holy throne; the road home always passes through God's appointed altar of atonement.

## Deeper Meaning

**A return reversing chapter 10 and 11's heartbreaking departure frame by frame.** Back in chapters 10 and 11, Ezekiel watched step by agonizing step as God's glory lifted off the cherubim, paused at the threshold, moved to the east gate, and rose clear onto the mountain east of the city (10:18–19; 11:22–23). Here in chapter 43, the reel plays completely in reverse: glory returns from the east, roaring like rushing waters just as beside the Kebar River (1:24; 1:28), sweeps straight back through the east gate, and fills the temple full (43:2–5). Everything sin lost, grace restores—now carrying the pledge to dwell "forever" (43:7).

**Continuing the two great fillings of Israel's history.** Seeing the LORD's glory fill the temple in 43:5 directly echoes Israel's two highest sacred milestones: the day Moses finished erecting the wilderness tabernacle (Exodus 40:34–35) and the day King Solomon dedicated the first temple in Jerusalem (1 Kings 8:10–11). With both structures gone, Ezekiel's vision forecasts an even greater arrival—the moment the apostle John announces came true in flesh and blood: the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and "we have seen His glory" (John 1:14).

**The whole mountaintop "most holy"—holiness expanding outward.** Formerly, inside tabernacle and Solomon's temple, only the innermost room behind the thick curtain bore the name Most Holy Place. Now "the law of the temple" in 43:12 declares the entire mountaintop compound "most holy." Later Zechariah saw that same widening when even horse bells and kitchen pots across Jerusalem bear "HOLY TO THE LORD" (Zechariah 14:20–21). On Scripture's closing page, the entire New Jerusalem takes the cubic proportions of the Most Holy Place, needing zero stone sanctuary because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple and His glory illuminates everything (Revelation 21:3; 21:22–23). Even the glory's voice "like the roar of many waters" in 43:2 matches the risen Christ's voice heard by John (Revelation 1:15).

**From seven days of altar purification to one sacrifice for all time.** Seven days dabbing blood onto the altar horns before regular ministry starts on day eight (43:18–27) mirrors Moses' consecration of the tabernacle altar (Exodus 29:36–37; Leviticus 8:14–15; 9:1), complete with salt symbolizing an enduring covenant (Leviticus 2:13). Yet daily repetition also pointed beyond itself. Hebrews looks back across every Old Testament offering to lift our eyes to Jesus: needing no daily repetition of bulls and goats, He "offered for all time one sacrifice for sins," perfecting forever those being made holy (Hebrews 10:10–14).

**God's throne and footstool.** Naming the temple "the place of My throne and the place for the soles of My feet" (43:7) echoes beloved temple worship psalms (Psalms 99:5), while Isaiah reminds us no earthly building ever walls in the Creator, since "Heaven is My throne, and earth is My footstool" (Isaiah 66:1). Chapter 43 never shrinks God inside stone walls; it displays the astonishing humility of the Most High willing to rest His footstool right amid a formerly faithless people.

## Notes & Nuances

**What were "the corpses of their kings" in 43:7 and 43:9?** This startling detail reflects historical geography in Jerusalem: the royal palace sat directly flush against the temple precinct, and late Judean kings like Manasseh and Amon were buried in the palace garden practically touching temple walls (2 Kings 21:18)—on top of installing pagan altars and idols inside temple courts (2 Kings 21:4–7). Under purity law, corpses and graves caused peak ritual defilement, making royal tombs adjacent to the sanctuary a direct intrusion on God's holiness. Some modern scholars also suggest the Hebrew word may encompass memorial stelae or ancient Near Eastern royal ancestor-cult monuments.

**Why does this altar have steps (43:17) when Exodus 20:26 forbade altar steps?** At Sinai, God barred steps up to an altar so a priest's nakedness would not be exposed while climbing (Exodus 20:26). Yet Ezekiel 43:17 explicitly shows steps ascending the eastern side of the towering altar. Commentators point out a straightforward resolution: Exodus 20 preceded the requirement that priests wear linen undergarments beneath their robes—a statute explicitly enforced right in the next chapter (44:18); furthermore, chapters 40–48 intentionally introduce a renewed visionary order differing in several specifics from Sinai's codes.

**Will animal sacrifices and a physical altar literally resume?** This remains one of the liveliest interpretive questions across chapters 40–48, dividing readers into three main approaches. Literal-future readers (often dispensational premillennialists) anticipate a real millennial temple with animal offerings functioning strictly as memorials looking back to the cross, analogous to Communion today. Christ-centered symbolic readers (spanning early Church Fathers, Reformers, and many modern scholars) recognize that God spoke to a 6th-century BC exiled priest in the worship vocabulary he and his community knew best, picturing eternal spiritual realities—God's permanent presence and Christ's complete atonement, which ended animal blood sacrifices forever (Hebrews 10:10–14). A third view regards it as an ideal conditional blueprint offered to returning exiles had they repented fully, though in practice they built a far more modest Second Temple. Across every school of thought, chapter 43's core remains crystal clear: the holy God returns to dwell among His people on the foundation of atonement.

**Why does 43:3 say "when I came to destroy the city"?** Read quickly, it almost sounds as though Ezekiel himself swung a sword to smash Jerusalem. In ancient Hebrew prophetic idiom, declaring God's unshakable verdict is often compressed as performing it—just as God appointed Jeremiah to uproot and tear down nations. Modern versions like the BSB clarify smoothly: "when He came to destroy the city" (or "when I came to announce the city's destruction"), recalling Ezekiel's judgment vision in chapters 8–11.

## What Commentators Say

**The Babylonian Talmud**, the classic compilation of ancient Jewish rabbinic debates, candidly noted real tension reading chapters 43–46's altar and sacrifice statutes: several details—like the sequence of bull and goat cleansing offerings and festival quantities—differ noticeably from Moses' rules in Leviticus and Numbers. **Jerome**, the famed 4th–5th century biblical scholar who translated Scripture into Latin, read God's glory sweeping in from the east through the east gate (43:1–5) as a radiant prophecy of Christ—the Rising Sun from on high—entering to indwell His Church. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, noted that without the arrival opening chapter 43, the grand sanctuary measured across three preceding chapters would be no better than a lifeless corpse, since God's presence alone gives worship its soul. Pausing carefully at 43:10–11, where God uses the temple blueprint to make Israel ashamed of their sins, Henry remarked that nothing wakes deeper sorrow for sin than glimpsing the generous goodness God holds out to us despite our past ingratitude—and only humbled, repentant hearts gain eyes to grasp the laws of God's house. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 43)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral points on glory as the temple's soul and grace-awakened shame in 43:10–11 are well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

Wright**, Northern Irish Old Testament scholar, underscored the startling grace embedded in 43:10–11: instead of flogging the people first before offering blessing, God lets them behold the breathtaking house where He returns to live among them so that sheer grace melts stony hearts into repentance.
