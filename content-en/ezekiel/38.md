# Ezekiel 38 — Gog of Magog Marches Against Israel

## Story

Chapter 37 just closed with a reunited Israel living peacefully at home with God's sanctuary standing right in their midst. Yet chapter 38 immediately confronts that peace with an unprecedented terrifying test. The word of the LORD comes to Ezekiel, instructing him to set his face toward "Gog, of the land of Magog, the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal" (rendered in some translations "prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal") and declare bluntly: the Lord GOD Himself is against him (38:1–3).

Although Gog commands a colossal coalition, God makes clear that He holds complete control over every step Gog takes: "I will turn you around, put hooks in your jaws," and haul him and his entire cavalry army—fully outfitted in armor, bucklers, shields, and swords—straight out of his territory. Marching with him come armies from the farthest horizons: Persia, Cush, and Put armed with shields and helmets, joined by Gomer and Beth-togarmah from the far north. God tells Gog to prepare well and serve as commander over that vast horde. After many days, "in the latter years," he will invade the mountains of Israel—a land long ruined yet now repopulated with gathered exiles dwelling safely—advancing like a storm and "like a cloud covering the land" (38:4–9).

God then lays bare the dark scheme hatching inside Gog's mind on that day. Spotting Israel's quiet "unwalled villages," where peaceful people live securely "without walls, bars, or gates," Gog hatches an evil plot. Seeing a restored nation wealthy with livestock and goods, "living at the center of the earth" yet completely unfortified, Gog thinks he has stumbled onto effortless plunder and launches his raid. Merchants of Sheba, Dedan, and traders of Tarshish watch from the sidelines, asking Gog openly whether he mobilized this vast host purely to cart off silver, gold, cattle, and massive loot (38:10–13).

God commands Ezekiel to prophesy directly to Gog again: on that day when Israel lives securely, Gog will sweep down from the uttermost north with massive cavalry like a dark cloud blanketing the land. Yet behind Gog's greed stands God's sovereign purpose: God Himself brings Gog against His land "in the latter days" so that by displaying His holiness through crushing Gog before the watching world, every nation will come to know who the LORD truly is (38:14–16).

Pausing a beat, God asks Gog a striking historical question: long ago across many years, through His servants the prophets of Israel, didn't God foretell the day He would bring a dread adversary against His people—aren't you the very one they spoke of? (38:17).

Suddenly the scene erupts in the blazing wrath of the Almighty. The moment Gog sets foot on Israel's soil, God's jealous fury surges up. A colossal earthquake shakes the land of Israel so violently that fish in the sea, birds of the sky, wild beasts, creeping creatures, and every human on earth tremble in terror; mountains crash down, cliffs shatter, and every wall collapses to the ground. Across His mountains, God summons a sword against Gog, throwing the allied invaders into panic until they hack each other to pieces: "Every man's sword will be against his brother." Simultaneously, God strikes Gog and his armies with plague, bloodshed, torrential rain, hailstones, fire, and burning sulfur. Through a lopsided battle in which Israel never needs to fire a single arrow, God magnifies Himself and reveals His holiness before many nations, driving home one unmistakable truth: "Then they will know that I am the LORD" (38:18–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Even when the fiercest enemy thinks he acts freely, he remains inside God's grip.** This chapter holds two seemingly opposite perspectives side by side. In 38:10–12, Gog willingly schemes out of sheer greed for loot, bearing complete moral guilt for his aggression. Yet in 38:4 and 38:16, God declares He hooks Gog's jaws and drags him onto the field to execute judgment and display His glory. Evil never breaks loose from God's sovereign hand, and God turns an adversary's arrogance into the very stage of His deliverance.

**True safety for God's people rests not on high walls, but on God's presence.** Again and again this chapter emphasizes that Israel dwells in villages without walls, bars, or gates (38:11). To Gog's military eye, that looks like fatal vulnerability; in God's story, it marks a people trusting wholeheartedly in Him. When an army vast as a storm cloud rolls in, Israel never even lifts a sword—the LORD Himself serves as their wall of defense.

**Evil turns on itself once God lifts restraint.** A massive coalition assembled from north, east, and south appears unstoppable, yet in a single instant God turns brother's sword against brother (38:21). Wicked powers may link arms briefly to attack the righteous, yet chaos and self-destruction lie at evil's core.

**Judgment's final goal is not showing off power, but revealing God to the nations.** Chapter 38 both opens and closes on the same mission: God reveals His holiness before many peoples so they come to know He is the LORD (38:16; 38:23). Even amid earthquakes and fire, Scripture keeps its eyes fixed on the day all nations recognize history's Creator and King.

## Deeper Meaning

**A world map tracing straight back to Genesis' Table of Nations.** Unfamiliar names like Magog, Meshech, Tubal, Gomer, and Togarmah are not random labels; they come directly from the farthest northern branches of Japheth's descendants after the Flood (Genesis 10:2–3), while Cush, Put, Sheba, and Dedan come from Ham's southernmost lines (Genesis 10:6–7). Gathering peoples from the outermost rims of the ancient map, Ezekiel paints a symbolic coalition representing hostile forces from the ends of the earth converging on the heart of the promised land.

**Continuing the prophetic motif of "the foe from the north."** When God asks in 38:17 whether earlier prophets foretold this invader, Old Testament readers immediately remember Jeremiah announcing disaster and a great nation stirring from the north (Jeremiah 1:14–15; 6:22). Earlier, God used a northern enemy—Babylon—to discipline faithless Israel; now, after Israel's restoration into a covenant of peace (34:25–28), the ultimate northern adversary marches down—only this time God no longer sides with the invader, stepping in to crush him completely in defense of His people.

**Hooks in the jaws and memories of Israel's greatest rescues.** Putting "hooks in your jaws" in 38:4 repeats the exact threat God spoke against proud Assyrian King Sennacherib (2 Kings 19:28) and Pharaoh of Egypt (29:4), picturing a hunter hauling a wild beast helplessly along. Meanwhile, the judgments of 38:21–22 gather together Scripture's greatest miracles of deliverance: enemies turning swords on one another as in Gideon's victory over Midian (Judges 7:22) and Jehoshaphat's deliverance (2 Chronicles 20:23); hail mixed with fire echoing the Egyptian plague (Exodus 9:23–24); and fire and sulfur recalling Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis 19:24).

**Unwalled villages guarded by a wall of fire.** Dwelling safely in "unwalled villages" in 38:11 resonates directly with Zechariah's post-exilic promise: Jerusalem will overflow like "villages without walls," because the LORD Himself will be "a wall of fire around her and the glory within her" (Zechariah 2:4–5). Against every conspiracy of earthly rulers (Psalms 2:1–4), the LORD Himself remains the strongest wall.

**Gog and Magog in Revelation's final battle.** Over six hundred years after Ezekiel, the Bible's closing book borrows these exact names to depict evil's final uprising at history's close: when the thousand years end, Satan goes out to deceive "Gog and Magog" from the four corners of the earth, rallying them to surround the camp of the saints and the beloved city until fire drops from heaven and consumes them (Revelation 20:7–9). The New Testament thus reads Ezekiel's drama as the grand prototype of the final clash between hostile world powers and God's kingdom.

## Notes & Nuances

**Who is Gog, and where are the lands listed in 38:2–6?** This is one of Ezekiel's most debated riddles. In ancient history, many scholars note that *Gog* closely resembles King Gyges of Lydia in modern Turkey (7th century BC), while *Meshech*, *Tubal*, *Gomer*, and *Togarmah* were real ancient peoples in Asia Minor and the Caucasus; *Persia* corresponds to Iran, *Cush* to upper Nile regions (Sudan/Ethiopia), and *Put* to Libya in North Africa. Yet most scholars point out that no historical figure named Gog ever led such a sweeping alliance stretching from North Africa through Iran to Anatolia—suggesting Gog serves primarily as a symbolic archetype combining distant powers arrayed against God's people.

**Does *Rosh* in 38:2 mean Russia?** Certain popular modern prophecy books try linking *Rosh* with Russia, *Meshech* with Moscow, and *Tubal* with Tobolsk. The vast majority of biblical scholars and linguists firmly reject this equation as modern phonetic guesswork: the name Russia arose more than a millennium after Ezekiel (from medieval Rus peoples), whereas *Meshech* and *Tubal* were Anatolian tribes already recorded in Genesis. Furthermore, the Hebrew word *rosh* is simply the ordinary noun meaning "chief" or "head," which is why many major English translations—including the BSB—render 38:2 as "chief prince of Meshech and Tubal" rather than treating *Rosh* as a nation's name.

**When is this prophecy fulfilled?** Readers take several major interpretive paths. Some read chapters 38–39 literally as a future military invasion of Israel just before or at the end of Christ's thousand-year reign. Others see partial foreshadowings in post-exilic persecutions (such as Antiochus Epiphanes in the 2nd century BC) finding ultimate fulfillment in the final global showdown between hostile world powers and God's people as pictured in Revelation (Revelation 20:7–9). Above all, rather than turning this passage into a daily geopolitical crystal ball, we should keep Ezekiel's true comfort to exiles front and center: no hostile force can ever undo the peace God secures for His people.

**Fierce storm and judgment imagery at the chapter's close.** Verses 38:18–22 depict earthquakes, swords, plague, fire, and sulfur in startling force—verse 38:18 literally says in Hebrew "My fury will rise in My nostrils," an ancient idiom for hot indignant breath. Keep the context clear: this never glorifies Israelite aggression; Israel stands completely unarmed and unwalled while Gog marches an enormous army to plunder innocent people. God's judgment acts as righteous defense of the vulnerable against savage aggression—and God Himself steps in rather than placing a vengeful sword in human hands.

**Chapters 38 and 39 form one continuous oracle.** Both chapters recount Gog's invasion and catastrophic ruin on Israel's mountains. Chapter 38 narrates the advance and sudden routing of Gog's alliance, while chapter 39 deepens the portrait of total defeat, the feast for wild birds and beasts, and the seven-month burial cleansing the land. Reading both chapters together delivers the full picture.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, 4th–5th century Bishop of Hippo in North Africa, likewise firmly rejected narrowing *Gog and Magog*—linked from Ezekiel to Revelation—to one specific barbarian tribe in a single corner of the earth (such as the Getae or Massagetae). Because Scripture places them at "the four corners of the earth," Augustine argued, Gog and Magog symbolize all hostile nations worldwide uniting against God's holy city at history's close, while God's people rest secure not behind stone walls but under God's protection. *(The City of God, Book 20, Chapter 11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's refutation of equating Gog and Magog with the Getae/Massagetae in favor of a global symbolic reading appears explicitly in The City of God Book 20 Chapter 11 -->

Enemies of the Church imagine they act freely on their own ambition and clever plots, Henry observed, yet in reality they are wild beasts led by God's hook straight to the spot appointed for their downfall.
