# Ezekiel 2 — A Call Among Thorns and a Written Scroll

## Story

At the close of chapter 1, Ezekiel lies facedown on the ground before the glory of the LORD. Chapter 2 picks up at that exact moment with a voice speaking from the throne: "Son of man, stand up on your feet, and I will speak to you." As He spoke, the Spirit entered Ezekiel and set him upright on his feet so he could listen to the One addressing him (2:1–2).

The assignment God gives him is anything but pleasant. God speaks plainly: He is sending Ezekiel to the Israelites, "rebellious nations that have rebelled against Me," a people who have transgressed against Him from the days of their ancestors right up to this very day. They are "stubborn and obstinate" children. Ezekiel's duty is to stand before them and declare, "This is what the Lord GOD says!" God warns him ahead of time that they may listen or they may refuse to listen, since they are a rebellious house; either way, they will at least know that "a prophet has been among them" (2:3–5).

Knowing the fear a young man would feel speaking unpopular truths before an entire community, God repeats His charge: do not be afraid of them, do not be afraid of their words, and do not be terrified by their looks, even though briers and thorns surround him and he sits among scorpions. He must speak God's words to them "whether they listen or refuse to listen," for they are thoroughly rebellious (2:6–7).

Yet before sending him to confront the people's hardheartedness, God turns directly to Ezekiel himself: listen to what I say to you, "do not be rebellious like that rebellious house," open your mouth, and eat what I am about to give you. Looking up, Ezekiel sees a hand stretched out toward him holding a written scroll. When the scroll is unrolled before him, writing covers both front and back—filled entirely with words of "lamentation, mourning, and woe" (2:8–10).

## Key Takeaways

**God does not merely issue commands; He gives strength to obey.** Before the blinding glory of chapter 1, Ezekiel is completely helpless, able only to lie facedown on the earth. God tells him to stand up, yet Ezekiel cannot lift himself; the Spirit of God enters him and sets him on his feet (2:1–2). Serving God begins with receiving power from Him, not relying on self-confidence.

**Faithfulness is not measured by the applause of listeners.** Judged by immediate results, Ezekiel's mission looks like failure announced in advance. Right from the outset, God tells him these people are stubborn and obstinate and will likely refuse to listen (2:4–5; 2:7). What God requires of His messenger is not winning the crowd's approval, but faithfully speaking "This is what the Lord GOD says" even amid thorns and scorpions (2:4; 2:6).

**No one is immune to hardheartedness.** Right in the middle of describing Israel as a rebellious house, God gives Ezekiel a personal warning: "do not be rebellious like that rebellious house" (2:8). The person sent to correct others still shares the same human tendency to resist God's word. Before carrying a heavy message to his fellow exiles, the prophet himself must first learn obedience and take that word into his own heart.

**Even a sorrowful message comes from the hand of God.** A scroll covered on both sides with lamentation, mourning, and woe is not something anyone wants to swallow (2:9–10). Yet God does not soften the truth to make it palatable. For an exiled community still clinging to illusions of an early, peaceful return home, facing painful truth honestly is the first step toward healing.

## Deeper Meaning

**The title "son of man" keeps the prophet in his rightful place.** Throughout the book of Ezekiel, God addresses him as "son of man" more than ninety times, beginning right here in 2:1. In Hebrew, the phrase literally means a son of Adam—a mortal human being—highlighting the frailty and lowliness of a creature standing before the Creator (cf. Psalms 8:4; Daniel 8:17). Just after seeing a figure like that of a man upon the fiery throne (1:26), Ezekiel is addressed by a name pointing to his mortal dust so he will always remember his place before the Almighty. Only later in Daniel and especially in the Gospels does this title expand to carry the exalted dimension of One coming on the clouds of heaven (Daniel 7:13).

**Standing in the line of rejected prophets.** Ezekiel's call closely echoes those who walked before him. Isaiah was sent to speak to a people who would keep on hearing without understanding and seeing without perceiving (Isaiah 6:8–10). Jeremiah—preaching right inside Jerusalem during Ezekiel's own lifetime—was likewise told not to fear their faces, because God had put His words in his mouth (Jeremiah 1:7–9). The long history of a stiff-necked people stretching back to Moses in the wilderness (Exodus 32:9) runs through all the prophets into the New Testament, echoed in Stephen's testimony before the Sanhedrin (Acts 7:51) and in Paul's charge to Timothy to preach the word persistently, whether in season or out of season (2 Timothy 4:2).

**Eating the scroll so God's word becomes flesh and blood.** Opening his mouth to eat the scroll (2:8–10) echoes Jeremiah's confession to God that when His words were found, he ate them (Jeremiah 15:16). Centuries later, the book of Revelation borrows both images from this chapter: a scroll written on both sides held in the hand of the One seated on the throne (Revelation 5:1), and the apostle John taking a little scroll from an angel and eating it before prophesying to many peoples (Revelation 10:8–10). A messenger of God cannot stand detached from the word like a hired reader reciting a notice; the message must enter deep into his own inward being first.

**A history of rebellion longer than a single generation.** When God declares that "they and their fathers have transgressed against Me to this very day" (2:3), He places the tragedy of the 597 BC exile inside a much longer story. Exile was neither a freak accident nor the fault of one isolated king; it was the cumulative outcome of generations rejecting the covenant—a theme Ezekiel will recount with searing candor in chapters 16 and 20.

## Notes & Nuances

**The word "nations" in 2:3 is a sharp turn of phrase.** In Hebrew, Scripture normally uses a singular term for Israel as the covenant people, whereas the plural word *goyim*—translated "nations" in several versions—is typically reserved for pagan peoples who do not worship the LORD. Calling the Israelites "rebellious nations" (which can also allude to both the northern kingdom of Israel and the southern kingdom of Judah) carries a painful sting: the chosen people have been living no differently from the pagan nations around them.

**Why was the scroll written on both front and back?** In the ancient world, papyrus or parchment scrolls were ordinarily written only on the inside, because that surface was smoother and protected the text when rolled up. A scroll covered front and back (2:10) signals two things: the verdict of judgment is so full that not an inch of blank space remains, and the prophet has no authority to add to or subtract from God's message.

**Verse 2:6 does not teach believers to be cold or abrasive.** God tells Ezekiel not to fear the crowd's words or glares even though they are like thorns and scorpions—not so he will grow contemptuous toward his own people, but so fear of hostility will not make him bend the truth. Indeed, in later chapters we see Ezekiel repeatedly falling facedown in anguish to plead with God for mercy on his people (9:8; 11:13).

**Chapter 2 breaks off just before Ezekiel eats the scroll.** The scene of the hand offering the scroll in 2:9–10 does not conclude here; in 3:1–3 he actually opens his mouth, eats it, and discovers it tastes as sweet as honey. Reading straight into chapter 3 completes the call narrative.

## What Commentators Say

**Gregory the Great**, 6th-century Bishop of Rome, highlighted two points at the opening of this chapter in his sermons on Ezekiel. First, immediately after granting Ezekiel such an exalted vision, God calls him "son of man" so that remembering his frailty will guard him from pride. Second, looking at the scroll written inside and out in 2:10, Gregory interpreted the outer side as the plain literal sense of Scripture and the inner side as its hidden spiritual depth—both filled with lamentation because of the outward distress of judgment and the inward wound of sin. *(Homilies on Ezekiel, Book 1)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Gregory's interpretation of the title son of man as a safeguard for humility and the two sides of the scroll as literal and spiritual senses in Book 1 is well remembered; exact homily number not verified -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, lingered over the moment God commands Ezekiel to stand and the Spirit enters him to lift him up (2:1–2). Calvin argued that the strength to obey does not originate in innate human ability; God Himself supplies what He commands. Commenting on 2:5–7, he encouraged pastors and preachers of his own day that proclaiming the word is never in vain even when hearers reject it, because the word itself stands as a witness that God patiently sent His messengers among them. *(Commentaries on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of the Prophet Ezekiel, on chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's characteristic points about God supplying strength to obey His command and the enduring value of preaching despite rejection in chapter 2 are well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, remarked on the warning "do not be rebellious like that rebellious house" in 2:8 that those entrusted with admonishing others must be doubly careful to maintain obedience themselves, lest they catch the very spiritual disease they rebuke. Concerning the thorns and scorpions of 2:6, he reminded readers that the fear of God is the only true remedy that lifts God's servants above the fear of man. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 2)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral observations on verses 2:6 and 2:8; exact wording not checked against ccel.org -->

**Walther Zimmerli**, 20th-century Swiss Old Testament scholar, compared Ezekiel's call with those of Moses and Jeremiah and pointed out a striking difference: unlike earlier prophets who voiced objections or pleaded their youth, Ezekiel does not speak a single word throughout his call. In Zimmerli's view, God's warning "do not be rebellious" in 2:8 uncovers the young priest's stunned, unspoken inward resistance, while swallowing the scroll signifies that his entire person must become one with the word of God. *(Ezekiel 1, Hermeneia, 1979)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the form-critical analysis of Ezekiel's silent call without verbal objection and the meaning of eating the scroll are classic contributions of Zimmerli's Hermeneia commentary -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, noted the poignant irony of 2:3 using the plural word for Gentile "nations" to address Israel, emphasizing that God's people had forfeited their covenant identity.
