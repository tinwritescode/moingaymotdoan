# Ezekiel 20 — A History of Rebellion and the Holy Name of God

## Story

After the heavy funeral lament over Judah's kings in chapter 19, the scene shifts back to Babylonian exile. In "the seventh year, in the fifth month, on the tenth day"—around mid-591 BC—certain elders of Israel come to sit before Ezekiel to inquire of the LORD. Yet God's word to His prophet is an emphatic refusal: "As surely as I live, I will not be inquired of by you." Instead of answering whatever they hoped to hear, God urges Ezekiel to judge them and confront them with the detestable deeds their ancestors committed from the very beginning (20:1–4).

The sweeping historical review begins right in Egypt. God recalls the day He chose Israel, revealed Himself to Jacob's descendants, and raised His hand in oath to bring them out of slavery into a land "flowing with milk and honey, the most glorious of all lands." In return, He asked one thing: throw away the detestable images before your eyes and do not defile yourselves with Egypt's idols. Yet even there in Egypt, they rebelled, refused to listen, and clung to their idols. God resolved to pour out His wrath and destroy them right in Egypt—yet held His hand back "for the sake of My name," lest His name be profaned before the surrounding nations in whose sight He had just revealed Himself (20:5–9).

God led them out of Egypt into the wilderness, giving them His statutes and ordinances—"by which a man will live if he practices them"—along with His Sabbaths as a special sign between Him and them so they would know it is the LORD who sanctifies them. Yet the whole house of Israel rebelled in the wilderness, despised His decrees, and badly profaned His Sabbaths. Again God thought to pour out wrath and wipe them out, and again He held back "for the sake of My name" before the nations. He swore that generation would never set foot in the promised land, yet His eye spared them from total annihilation in the desert (20:10–17).

Turning to the younger generation growing up in the wilderness, God earnestly urged them not to walk in their fathers' rules or idols, but to keep His decrees and keep His Sabbaths holy. Yet the children imitated their fathers' rebellion down to the letter, keeping their eyes fixed on ancestral idols. Once more God drew His hand back "for the sake of My name," yet swore He would scatter them among the nations. Alongside that oath comes a jarring verdict: He handed them over to "statutes that were not good and ordinances by which they could not live," letting them defile themselves through their very offerings by passing every firstborn through the fire—so that they might be horrified and know He is the LORD (20:18–26).

Once God brought their ancestors into the very land He had sworn to give them, old habits never changed. Wherever they spotted a high hill or leafy tree, they offered provoking sacrifices, burned fragrant incense, and poured out drink offerings; those high places bear the mocking name *Bamah* to this day. At this point God turns directly on the elders seated in front of Ezekiel: you are still defiling yourselves just like your ancestors, still passing your sons through fire for idols right up to today—and you dare come inquire of Me? The sworn refusal rings out a second time, cold and absolute (20:27–31).

God even reads the unspoken thought brewing inside the exiles' minds. They tell one another they want to become "like the nations, like the tribes of the countries, serving wood and stone" and be done with it. God declares that what enters their minds will never happen. With "a mighty hand and an outstretched arm, and with outpoured wrath," He Himself will reign over them as King. Like a second Exodus, He will gather them out of the nations into "the wilderness of the peoples," confront them face to face in judgment, make them "pass under the rod" into the bond of the covenant, and purge out all rebels—those rebels will never enter the land of Israel (20:32–38).

To those still stubbornly clinging to idolatry, God speaks with stinging irony: go ahead and serve your idols if you insist—yet afterward you will surely listen to Me and no longer profane My holy name with divided worship. For one day, on Israel's high holy mountain, the entire nation will serve Him wholeheartedly and present offerings He accepts as a pleasing aroma. When He brings them back from the nations into the land sworn to their fathers, they will remember all their defiled ways and "loathe yourselves in your own sight," knowing that He is the LORD—who deals with them "for the sake of My name," not according to their corrupt conduct (20:39–44).

The chapter's final five verses launch a brief, blazing new oracle. God tells Ezekiel to set his face toward the south—looking from Babylon toward Judah—and prophesy against the forest of the southern land. God will kindle an unquenchable fire that devours "both the green tree and the dry tree," scorching every face from south to north until all flesh sees that the LORD Himself lit the blaze. Hearing that veiled metaphor, Ezekiel sighs to God that the people already dismiss him as "just speaking in parables"—setting up chapter 21's blunt explanation using the image of a sword (20:45–49).

## Key Takeaways

**No one can clutch idols with one hand and inquire of God with the other.** The elders sit before the prophet looking respectful, yet their lives still cling to ancestral abominations (20:1–3; 20:30–31). God is not a consultant people visit in emergencies before walking straight back into self-will; He demands a genuine turning of the heart before giving guidance.

**Israel's rebellion was not a recent dip spanning a few generations.** Exiles naturally comforted themselves that their nation's past had been noble and only recently slipped, so exile would end quickly. God reviews four consecutive chapters of history—in Egypt, the parents in the wilderness, the children in the wilderness, and life inside the promised land—exposing how deep unfaithfulness ran from day one (20:5–29).

**God's people survive not by their own merit, but for the sake of His name.** Three times across history Israel deserved total extinction, yet three times God stayed His hand "for the sake of My name" (20:9; 20:14; 20:22; 20:44). That preservation both crushes human pride and provides our surest anchor of hope: the future of God's people rests not on their shaky loyalty, but on God's steadfast faithfulness to His own character.

**A covenant people cannot simply give up and melt into surrounding cultures.** In pagan Babylon, the fiercest temptation was tossing aside their distinctive identity to live like everyone else and "serve wood and stone" in peace (20:32). Yet God insists He remains their King and will take them through searching discipline to preserve a purified worshiping community (20:33–44).

## Deeper Meaning

**God's laws and Sabbaths were meant to give life.** Three times God emphasizes that His statutes are rules "by which a man will live if he practices them" (20:11; 20:13; 20:21), linking straight back to Sinai's covenant (Leviticus 18:5), while His Sabbaths stood as a covenant sign declaring that He sets His people apart (Exodus 31:13). Centuries later, the apostle Paul cited this exact legal formula to show that God's commandments are good and promise life, yet because unfaithful human hearts fail to keep them fully, we need the righteousness that comes through faith in Christ (Romans 10:5; Galatians 3:12).

**The refrain "for the sake of My name" links Moses' intercession to the promise of a new heart.** When Israel forged the golden calf and grumbled in the desert, Moses pleaded with God to turn aside His wrath so Egypt and the nations would not mock the LORD as unable to bring His people home (Exodus 32:11–14; Numbers 14:13–16). Ezekiel develops that theological thread across his book, culminating in God's pledge to restore Israel and give them a new heart not because of anything they earned, but "for the sake of My holy name" (36:22–23).

**A second Exodus to refine God's people.** Phrases like "a mighty hand and an outstretched arm," leading Israel out from the nations into the wilderness and home to the holy mountain (20:33–42), deliberately echo the language of deliverance from Egypt (Exodus 6:6). Earlier, Hosea too foretold that God would allure Israel back into the wilderness to speak tenderly and renew the covenant (Hosea 2:14–15). Passing "under the rod" borrows the image of a shepherd counting sheep one by one beneath his staff to set apart the LORD's portion (Leviticus 27:32): the coming restoration brings both gracious gathering and genuine sifting.

**The deepest repentance blooms after tasting grace.** Ordinarily we imagine people must feel sorry enough before God forgives them, yet this chapter reverses that sequence. Only *after* God acts for His name's sake to bring them home and welcome them on His holy mountain do they remember their filthy past and "loathe yourselves in your own sight" (20:42–44). Grace never makes sin look cheap; unmerited love is the only power strong enough to break our defenses and awaken honest remorse.

**"Both the green tree and the dry tree" echoes along Jesus' road to the cross.** At the chapter's end, southern judgment fire devours "both the green tree and the dry tree" (20:47), foreshadowing an all-encompassing calamity sweeping up righteous and wicked alike across the land. Six centuries later, carrying His cross past weeping women of Jerusalem, Jesus borrowed this very proverb to warn of the city's coming doom: "If they do these things while the tree is green, what will happen when it is dry?" (Luke 23:31). If even the innocent One suffered so brutally, a hardhearted city rejecting Him would never escape judgment's fire.

## Notes & Nuances

**The hardest passage in the chapter: God giving "statutes that were not good" (20:25–26).** How can the holy God say He gave Israel bad statutes by which they could not live and led them to burn firstborn children, when through Jeremiah He explicitly declared that child sacrifice was something "I did not command, nor did it ever enter My mind" (Jeremiah 7:31)? Commentators propose three major views. The first—most widespread from the early Church through the Reformation and among many scholars today—reads this as judicial abandonment: because Israel obstinately rejected God's life-giving law, He let go and handed them over to the brutal customs of pagan deities, much as the New Testament says God "gave them over in the desires of their hearts to impurity" or "gave them over to worship the host of heaven" (Romans 1:24; Acts 7:42). The second suggests Israel willfully twisted God's law dedicating the firstborn—a law explicitly requiring every firstborn son to be redeemed with a lamb rather than sacrificed (Exodus 13:12–13)—and God left them to reap the horror of their perversion. The third, held by several modern scholars, reads the wording more starkly: Ezekiel pushes divine sovereignty to its most radical edge, insisting nothing—not even self-destructive delusion under judgment—falls outside God's rule. Whichever angle one adopts, the context makes clear that sin sprang from Israel's persistent stubbornness, and the most dreadful penalty is God letting people live out the destructive path they insist on choosing.

**Does Israel's idolatry in Egypt conflict with Exodus?** While Exodus rarely mentions Israelite idolatry during slavery explicitly, Joshua states plainly that their ancestors served other gods in Egypt (Joshua 24:14), and making the golden calf right beneath Mount Sinai shows that old habits had never been washed away. Ezekiel underscores this history to dismantle the exiles' myth of a pristine golden age.

**The opening date foreshadows the day the temple burned.** "The seventh year, fifth month, tenth day" falls around August 591 BC—exactly five years before Jerusalem went up in flames. Chillingly, on the tenth day of the fifth month five years later, Babylon's captain of the guard entered the city and set fire to the temple (Jeremiah 52:12). The scene of elders sitting before Ezekiel and being turned away also echoes their earlier visit when God exposed the idols hidden in their hearts (14:1–3).

**The final five verses belong to chapter 21 in the Hebrew Bible.** English translations place the oracle against the southern forest at the close of chapter 20 (20:45–49), whereas the original Hebrew text prints those five verses as the opening five verses of chapter 21. Knowing this makes the flow seamless: Ezekiel's sigh that people dismiss him as "just speaking in parables" (20:49) receives God's immediate reply at the top of chapter 21 in a crystal-clear prophecy about the sword descending on Jerusalem. As for "Bamah" in 20:29, the Hebrew weaves a stinging wordplay linking *bamah* ("high place" for idol worship) with the mocking question *"where are you going?"*

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, examined 20:25 closely, as it perplexed readers even in antiquity. God never originates evil or commands child sacrifice, he emphasized; when Israel persisted in spurning His good, life-giving commandments, God withdrew His restraining hand and surrendered them to the cruel practices of pagan idolatry. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, had lectured right up to chapter 20 of Ezekiel when final illness overtook him and he died in 1564. Explaining 20:25, Calvin rejected any claim that Mosaic law itself was bad; giving "statutes that were not good" was a righteous judgment in which God gave rebellious people over to blindness so they bound themselves to corrupt practices. On the refrain "for the sake of My name," Calvin underscored that every hope for the Church's survival and renewal rests purely on God's free grace, never on a scrap of human merit. *(Lectures on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of Ezekiel, on chapter 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: it is certain Calvin's lectures ended at chapter 20 before his death and his interpretation of 20:25 and "for the sake of My name" is well remembered; exact lecture number not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew a practical warning from the chapter's opening encounter: many keep up the outward habit of sitting before God's servants as if seeking His guidance while hugging idols in their hearts, only to find heaven silent. At 20:37's "pass under the rod," Henry pictured a shepherd counting and disciplining his flock to keep those who are his within the covenant bond—painful discipline, yet proof God has not abandoned His own. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 20)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar observations on hypocritical inquiry at the chapter's opening and the covenant rod in verse 37 are well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, renowned Jewish Old Testament scholar, traced the tightly balanced four-stage design of chapter 20's historical survey, each era repeating the exact same cycle: gracious election, human rebellion, threatened annihilation, and divine restraint for His name's sake. On 20:25–26, Greenberg noted Ezekiel's startlingly bold theology in placing even child sacrifice under God's active judicial decree—a stark contrast with Jeremiah's outright denial—while observing how deeply ancient Jewish interpreters wrestled with this verse. *(Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's four-stage historical outline and his discussion of 20:25–26 compared with Jeremiah and ancient Jewish exegesis are well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, American Old Testament professor, described chapter 20 as the bleakest summary of Israel's history in the entire Old Testament, because even the eras of Egypt and the wilderness—often romanticized as Israel's faithful honeymoon—are exposed here as soaked in rebellion. According to Block, 20:32—longing to be "like the nations... serving wood and stone"—exposes the exiles' deepest identity crisis: they wanted to throw off the burden of being God's covenant people, yet God asserts His absolute kingship and promises a second Exodus to refine them and restore true worship. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 1–24, NICOT, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's view of chapter 20 as the bleakest historical retrospect, the assimilation crisis in verse 32, and the second Exodus in verses 33–44 are well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->
