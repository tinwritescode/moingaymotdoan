# Ezekiel 25 — Judgment on Four Neighbors Who Gloated Over Judah's Fall

## Story

Chapter 24 closed the first half of Ezekiel with word that the siege of Jerusalem had begun and with the heartbreaking death of the prophet's wife. Turning into chapter 25, the prophetic camera pivots sharply away from Judah toward foreign nations, launching an eight-chapter sequence of oracles against seven Gentile peoples stretching all the way through chapter 32. Right here in chapter 25, the LORD pronounces judgment in turn upon four small neighbors encircling Judah clockwise from northeast to west: Ammon, Moab, Edom, and Philistia.

The first oracle targets "the Ammonites" northeast of the Jordan River. Their crime is stated right up front: when the sanctuary of the LORD was profaned, the land of Israel laid waste, and the house of Judah marched into exile, the Ammonites cheered with malicious glee: "Aha!" Not stopping at verbal jeers, they "clapped your hands and stomped your feet and rejoiced with all the malice of your heart against the land of Israel." Because of that cruel gloating, God declares He will hand their territory over to "the people of the East"—nomadic Arabian desert tribes—who will pitch encampments, settle among them, eat their fruit, and drink their milk. Their prosperous capital Rabbah will turn into "a pasture for camels," and all of Ammon into a resting place for flocks; they will become plunder for the nations and be cut off from among the peoples, so that "you will know that I am the LORD" (25:1–7).

The second oracle moves southeast to Moab and Mount Seir. Unlike Ammon's loud cheering, Moab's sin carries a sharp theological sneer: watching Jerusalem fall, they mocked, "Look, the house of Judah is like all the other nations"—claiming Judah held nothing special and that its God was just as powerless as the gods of every defeated kingdom. For that arrogant taunt, God declares He will tear open Moab's fertile frontier—the proud glory of the land, including the cities of Beth-jeshimoth, Baal-meon, and Kiriathaim—and hand both Moab and Ammon over to the people of the East as a possession, wiping out Ammon's memory among the nations so Moab too will know that He is the LORD (25:8–11).

The third oracle sweeps southward to Edom. Here hostility went far past mockery into direct violence: Edom "acted vengefully against the house of Judah and incurred grievous guilt by taking revenge on them." God stretches out His hand against Edom, cutting off both man and beast and turning the whole country from Teman in the south all the way to Dedan into wasteland by the sword. Strikingly, unlike Ammon and Moab handed over to desert tribes, God announces He will execute His vengeance on Edom "by the hand of My people Israel," forcing the Edomites to recognize the weight of His righteous retribution (25:12–14).

The fourth oracle closes the circle along the western coast against the Philistines—Israel's longtime foes since the days of the Judges and King David. The Philistines likewise seized on Judah's weakness to "act vengefully and take vengeance with malice in their hearts to destroy in ancient hostility." God declares He will stretch out His hand to cut off the "Kerethites" (a coastal branch of the Philistines), destroy the remnant along the seacoast, and execute furious rebukes upon them, so they too will know that He is the LORD (25:15–17).

## Key Takeaways

**Gloating over another's misery is a serious sin before God.** Ammon and Moab did not personally raze Jerusalem—Babylon did—yet simply shouting "Aha!", clapping and stomping in glee, and sneering when their neighbor fell brought God's direct judgment upon them (25:3; 25:6; 25:8). Even when a person or nation faces deserved discipline for its own sins, bystanders who laugh at their pain never stand innocent.

**The downfall of God's people never means God Himself has been defeated.** When Moab taunted that "the house of Judah is like all the other nations" (25:8), they fell into the classic blunder of proud powers: assuming that because Jerusalem fell, the LORD must have lost. Four times across chapter 25, the refrain "know that I am the LORD" rings out over all four foreign nations (25:5; 25:7; 25:11; 25:17), proving He is no small local deity of Judah, but the righteous Judge over every nation on earth.

**Nursing ancient grudges and kicking the fallen invites God's payback.** Both Edom and Philistia stand indicted with the same phrase: "acting vengefully" (25:12; 25:15). Philistia's motive is exposed even more sharply as "ancient hostility"—bitterness cultivated over centuries. Whenever people seize vengeance into their own hands to feed hatred, they call God's own reckoning straight down on themselves.

**God's discipline of His people never means He has abandoned them forever.** Right in the darkest hour of Jerusalem's siege, chapter 25 hands the exiles a surprising spark of comfort: God still calls the profaned temple "My sanctuary" (25:3) and still calls exiled Israel "My people Israel" (25:14). Anyone who imagines God's people are cast off and fair game for trampling will answer to their Defender.

## Deeper Meaning

**Painful kinship and the united voice of the prophets against these four neighbors.** Both Ammon and Moab descended from Lot, close kin to Abraham (Genesis 19:37–38), while Edom descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother. That ancient family bond made their treachery when Jerusalem fell all the more inexcusable. Other prophets condemned the exact same sins in unison: Proverbs already taught, "Do not gloat when your enemy falls, and do not let your heart rejoice when he stumbles" (Proverbs 24:17); Amos denounced Edom for "cherishing his fury forever" and Ammon for brutally grabbing territory (Amos 1:11–15); Jeremiah challenged Ammon for seizing Gad's inheritance (Jeremiah 49:1–2); Zephaniah condemned Moab and Ammon for taunting God's people (Zephaniah 2:8–10); and Obadiah alongside Psalm 137 preserved the raw grief of Edom shouting, "Tear it down! Tear it down to its foundations!" while blocking crossroads to hand over fleeing Judeans on the day Jerusalem fell (Obadiah 1:10–14; Psalms 137:7).

**"Vengeance is Mine" running from Moses' Song into the New Testament.** The heavy repetition of "vengeance" throughout the chapter's second half (25:12–17) underscores a central biblical principle: Edom and Philistia sinned gravely by feeding hatred and inflicting cruel retaliation, whereas righteous retribution belongs exclusively to God. Back in Deuteronomy, God declared, "Vengeance is Mine; I will repay" (Deuteronomy 32:35). In the New Testament, Paul quotes that exact line to free believers from cycles of retaliation: "Do not avenge yourselves, beloved, but leave room for God's wrath. For it is written: 'Vengeance is Mine; I will repay, says the Lord'" (Romans 12:19).

**The oracles of chapters 25–32 form the bridge from judging Judah to restoring Israel.** Looking across Ezekiel's grand design reveals an intentional pattern shared with Isaiah and Zephaniah: chapters 1–24 pronounce judgment on Jerusalem; chapters 25–32 pronounce judgment on seven foreign nations (four neighbors in chapter 25, plus Tyre, Sidon, and Egypt in chapters 26–32); and chapters 33–48 proclaim Israel's restoration. God clears away hostile surrounding powers before bringing His people home to rebuild.

## Notes & Nuances

**God's statement "My vengeance on Edom by the hand of My people Israel" (25:14) is challenging to read.** Modern readers rightly pause over fierce language of vengeance and especially seeing Israel named as the instrument executing wrath on Edom (25:14). Two lenses help keep balance. Historically, in the 2nd century BC (the Maccabean/Hasmonean era), Judean ruler John Hyrcanus did defeat and subdue Idumea (ancient Edom), while Ammon and Moab were gradually overrun and absorbed by eastern Arab tribes (such as the Nabataeans) just as 25:4 and 25:10 foretold. Theologically, Old Testament "vengeance" is never petty spite, but the *covenant-protecting justice* of the supreme King answering violent oppression against His people—and unlike Edom and Philistia's bitter grudges, final judgment always belongs to God. In the New Testament, Jesus and the apostles make clear that God's people no longer take up earthly swords for retaliation, entrusting all judgment to God (Romans 12:19).

**Why do these four neighbors lack date stamps, unlike Tyre and Egypt in the chapters ahead?** Within the foreign-nation oracles of chapters 25–32, the prophecies on Tyre (26:1) and Egypt (29:1; 30:20; 31:1; 32:1) all carry exact dates, while chapter 25 has none. Even so, verse 25:3 ("My sanctuary when it was profaned... the house of Judah when they went into exile") shows these oracles were spoken right after Jerusalem and the temple fell in 586 BC—at the exact moment surrounding neighbors who had once plotted an anti-Babylon coalition with Judah (Jeremiah 27:3) flipped sides to grab land and cheer the capital's ruin.

**Key place names and peoples in chapter 25.** **Rabbah** (25:5) was Ammon's capital—modern-day Amman, capital of Jordan. **Beth-jeshimoth**, **Baal-meon**, and **Kiriathaim** (25:9) were prosperous cities lining Moab's northern plateau near the Jordan. **Teman** and **Dedan** (25:13) marked the northern and southern edges of Edom's sphere—much like saying "from Dan to Beersheba" to encompass the entire country. Finally, **the Kerethites** (25:16) create a sharp Hebrew pun right beside the preceding verb "cut off" (*karat* / *Keretim*): they were a coastal Philistine subgroup originating from Crete.

## What Commentators Say

**Josephus**, the 1st-century Jewish historian, recorded a historical campaign occurring about five years after Jerusalem's fall: King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon marched out to subdue Syria, Ammon, and Moab before advancing on Egypt—helping break the independence of those small kingdoms that had gloated over Judah's ruin just as Ezekiel 25 foretold. *(Antiquities of the Jews, Book 10, Chapter 9)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Josephus's report of Nebuchadnezzar's campaign against Ammon and Moab in the fifth year after Jerusalem's fall in Antiquities Book 10 Chapter 9 is well remembered; exact section number not checked -->

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, explained carefully why these neighboring peoples open the foreign-nation oracles in chapter 25: even though God uses foreign powers to discipline His people for their sin, He never excuses outsiders who mock and seize the chance to humiliate them. Jerome also noted the Hebrew wordplay in 25:16 linking the Kerethites with being "cut off," reading the warnings against all four nations as a timeless lesson against anyone proudly gloating over a brother's fall. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's comments on God punishing those who mock His disciplined people and his note on the Kerethites in chapter 25 are well remembered; exact Latin wording not checked -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, saw Ammon's sin in 25:3 and 25:6—cheering "Aha!", clapping hands, and stomping feet when the sanctuary was profaned—as a timeless warning: never treat another's disaster as entertainment, and above all never gloat when the Church or God's work suffers hardship. Dwelling on Moab's taunt in 25:8 ("the house of Judah is like all the other nations"), Henry reaffirmed that even under severe discipline, God's people remain His covenant possession, and anyone who scorns God's name over their temporary defeat will soon encounter His righteous hand. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral themes on gloating over the sanctuary in verses 3 and 6 and Moab's taunt in verse 8; exact wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, highlighted the crisp, symmetrical legal structure uniting all four oracles in chapter 25: each opens with a "because..." clause naming a specific act of hostility toward Judah, moves to "therefore..." pronouncing matching judgment, and concludes with the recognition refrain "they will know that I am the LORD." Greenberg observed that all four indictments center squarely on how these neighbors behaved during Jerusalem's collapse in 586 BC—from Ammon's gleeful shouts to the bloody revenge of Edom and Philistia. *(Ezekiel 21–37, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's structural outline of the four symmetrical short oracles in Anchor Bible volume 2 is well remembered; exact page numbers not verified -->

**Daniel I. Block**, Canadian-American Old Testament professor, emphasized the comforting pivot chapter 25 provides right after chapter 24's crushing grief. Across the first twenty-four chapters, the exiles heard almost relentless condemnation aimed at Jerusalem; now chapter 25 assures them that disciplining His people never turns God into their enemy or gives neighbors license to trample them. Block particularly highlighted "My people Israel" in 25:14 as the first spark of hope glimmering within the foreign-nation oracles: Israel will not vanish, but will be restored to bear witness to the LORD's sovereignty. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 25–48, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's analysis of chapter 25's transitional comfort and the significance of verse 14 in NICOT volume 2 is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->
