# Ezekiel 39 — The Fall of Gog and the Promise Never to Hide God's Face Again

## Story

The oracle against Gog from chapter 38 rolls straight forward into chapter 39 as the LORD tells Ezekiel to face Gog, chief prince of Meshech and Tubal, and pronounce once more: God Himself brings him up from the uttermost north onto the mountains of Israel, only to strike him flat right there. God will "strike the bow from your left hand and dash down the arrows from your right hand," dropping Gog, all his troops, and his allied nations dead across Israel's mountains and open fields as food for birds of prey and wild beasts. Nor does judgment stop at the invading army: God hurls fire down onto Magog itself and onto coastal peoples living carelessly far away. God vows to make His holy name known among Israel and never let His name be profaned again: the appointed day is coming and will surely happen (39:1–8).

After that lopsided rout, residents of Israel's towns march out across the battlefield—not to stockpile weapons, but to burn every single one. Large shields, small bucklers, bows, arrows, clubs, and spears lie so thick that they "use them for fuel for seven years," needing neither to gather sticks in the fields nor chop firewood from the forests. The tables turn completely: those who marched down expecting to plunder are themselves stripped bare by the once-vulnerable nation (39:9–10).

Yet victory still leaves one immense task: cleansing the land. God assigns Gog a burial ground inside Israel, in the Valley of the Travelers east of the Sea (the Dead Sea), where the vast graveyard blocks travelers' paths and receives the name "the Valley of Hamon-gog" ("the multitude of Gog"). All Israel spends seven full months burying corpses "in order to cleanse the land," turning the day into an occasion of honor when the LORD is glorified. Even after seven months, dedicated search teams patrol the country nonstop; whenever someone spots a stray human bone, he "sets up a marker beside it" until the burial crews carry it off to the Valley of Hamon-gog. Even a nearby town takes the name *Hamonah* ("Multitude") to commemorate the total purification of the land (39:11–16).

Abruptly the scene swings back to the battlefield with an image both majestic and chilling. God instructs Ezekiel to summon every bird of the sky and every wild animal from every side to attend "a great sacrifice on the mountains of Israel" spread out by God Himself. Instead of rams, goats, or fattened bulls of Bashan, the feast lays out the flesh of mighty warriors, the blood of earth's princes, warhorses, charioteers, and fighting men: birds and wild beasts will "eat fat until you are satisfied and drink blood until you are drunk" at the LORD's banquet table (39:17–20).

Out of that stark battlefield imagery, the chapter lifts into the clear light of history's meaning. The LORD declares He will display His glory among the nations so all peoples witness His judging hand, and from that day forward Israel will know beyond doubt that He is the LORD their God. Crucially, the agonizing misunderstanding that shadowed the entire exile is cleared away forever before the world: the nations will finally grasp that Israel went into captivity not because the LORD was weak, but because of Israel's own sin, uncleanness, and rebellion, forcing God to hide His face and hand them over to enemies (39:21–24).

Finally, God rounds off the whole restoration section with a warm, comprehensive promise. He will restore the fortunes of Jacob, show compassion to the whole house of Israel, and burn with zeal for His holy name. Living safely home in their land with nobody to make them afraid, they will bear (or forget) the shame of all their past unfaithfulness. God will gather them out of every foreign land without leaving a single person behind, so they know He is the God who both sent them into exile and brought them safely home. The chapter's closing verse flings wide the gate into the new temple vision starting in chapter 40: "I will no longer hide My face from them, for I will pour out My Spirit on the house of Israel" (39:25–29).

## Key Takeaways

**True peace arrives when weapons of war turn into kitchen firewood.** In earthly politics, victors routinely collect defeated armies' weapons to enlarge their own arsenals. Yet in chapter 39, Israel keeps not a single bow or shield, burning every weapon for cooking fuel across seven years (39:9–10). Once God Himself becomes Defender, warfare ends at the root and tools of slaughter shrink down to everyday hearth fires.

**The holiness of God's dwelling land matters down to the tiniest stray bone.** It is no accident that such a large stretch of chapter 39 details seven months of burial followed by search crews flagging stray bones (39:12–16). Ezekiel is a priest: he knows that before the new temple rises and God's glory returns in chapters 40–48, the land must be scrubbed spotless of every trace of death and violence.

**God clears His name through justice and mercy standing together.** Throughout exile, watching nations mistook Israel's defeat for the LORD losing to Babylon's gods (36:20). Now two acts placed side by side—crushing Gog and bringing Israel home—show the world both halves of God's character: earlier He permitted exile because He is holy and never winks at sin (39:23–24); now He rescues and guards them because He is mighty and full of mercy (39:25–28).

**Pouring out God's Spirit is the eternal guarantee that He will never hide His face again.** In the Old Testament, God "hiding His face" was exile's sharpest pain. Verse 39:29 binds two promises inseparably together: God will never turn His face away again, *because* He pours out His Spirit upon the house of Israel. With God's own Spirit dwelling within His people, the covenant is no longer fragile.

## Deeper Meaning

**Breaking bows and burning weapons in messianic peace.** God knocking bows and arrows from Gog's grip while citizens burn shields and spears for seven years (39:3; 39:9–10) directly echoes the psalm of unshakable trust: God makes wars cease to the ends of the earth, "He breaks the bow and shatters the spear; He burns the shields with fire" (Psalms 46:9), alongside Isaiah's prophecy of the newborn King when every soldier's boot and blood-rolled cloak become fuel for the fire (Isaiah 9:5).

**Cleansing the land from corpses under Mosaic law.** The meticulous seven-month burial and bone-patrol sweep in 39:12–16 grow straight out of Old Testament purity legislation. Numbers ruled that anyone in an open field touching a corpse, human bone, or grave became ceremonially unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:16), while Deuteronomy strictly forbade letting dead bodies defile the land the LORD gave as an inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). Sweeping away every scar of battle clears the sacred ground right before chapter 40 unveils the new temple.

**The birds' banquet and the victory scene of Revelation.** God telling Ezekiel to invite birds and wild beasts to "a great sacrifice" feasting on princes, horses, and warriors in 39:17–20 (cf. Isaiah 34:6) is lifted nearly whole into Revelation. As the Rider on the white horse rides out to shatter the beast and earthly kings, an angel standing in the sun cries aloud to every bird of the sky to gather for "the great supper of God," to "eat the flesh of kings, commanders, and mighty men, of horses and riders" (Revelation 19:17–18). Startling though it feels to modern ears, this apocalyptic emblem seals the total defeat of brutal imperial tyranny before God's justice.

**From hiding His face to making His face shine—and Pentecost.** In Aaron's famed priestly blessing from Numbers, the highest good is the LORD making His face shine upon His people in grace (Numbers 6:24–26). Having God hide His face because of sin was exile's darkest grief (39:23–24). Consequently, the chapter's closing pledge—"I will no longer hide My face from them, for I will pour out My Spirit on the house of Israel" (39:29; cf. 37:14)—harmonizes directly with Joel's promise "I will pour out My Spirit on all people" (Joel 2:28–29), which the apostle Peter announced had begun to come true on the day of Pentecost (Acts 2:16–17).

## Notes & Nuances

**The flesh-and-blood banquet of 39:17–20 feels deeply jarring.** There is no need to gloss over the shock of these four verses: picturing raptors and wild beasts gorging on fat and drinking the blood of mighty warriors ranks among the starkest images in the Old Testament. Yet it must be read through the sharp priestly irony of ancient prophecy. Under Mosaic law, Israelites were strictly forbidden ever to "eat any fat or any blood" (Leviticus 3:17; 17:10–11), since fat and blood belonged solely to God on the altar. Here God never invites humans to do this; He invites scavengers to an inverted "sacrifice": arrogant conquerors who marched down like fattened bulls of Bashan expecting to devour defenseless villagers end up becoming the slaughtered sacrifice themselves. It is a fierce symbolic portrait announcing the humiliating collapse of imperial violence, never human cruelty gloating over enemies.

**How should we understand *seven years* and *seven months*?** Throughout Scripture, *seven* signals completeness and perfection. Some readers interpret seven years of burning weapons (39:9) and seven months of burying corpses (39:12) as literal time spans in a future war—even debating how wooden bows, shields, and spears fit modern armaments. Many other commentators read both numbers as vivid apocalyptic hyperbole: the invading horde is unimaginably vast, yet God's victory and land purification prove utterly thorough right down to the very last bone.

**Why place "the Valley of Hamon-gog" east of the Dead Sea?** On ancient maps, the land east of the Dead Sea lay outside the core boundary of Canaan (ancient Moab). Several scholars note that locating Gog's mass grave along the eastern fringe keeps the sacred heartland completely clean and undefiled right ahead of chapter 40's new temple. In Hebrew, the valley's name *Hamon-gog* ("noisy horde of Gog") and the town *Hamonah* ("Multitude") in 39:16 stand like a quiet gravestone reminding future generations that earth's loudest invading host ends up silenced underground.

**Why does 39:26 say they will "bear their shame" (or "forget their shame") while living safely at home?** The Hebrew verb in 39:26 turns on a single tiny consonant nuance: some translations (like the ESV) render "bear their shame," whereas others (including the BSB) read "forget their disgrace." Either reading fits Ezekiel's theology smoothly: as in 36:31, dwelling securely inside grace wakes humble remorse over past betrayal—or God lifts away exile's disgrace so His people rest securely in peace.

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew two rich practical lessons from chapter 39's cleanup. Watching Israel use enemy weapons as kitchen fuel for seven years (39:9–10), he noted how often God turns schemes and weapons forged to destroy the Church into fuel serving His people's warmth and good. At the closing verse (39:29)—"I will no longer hide My face from them, for I will pour out My Spirit"—Henry underscored that the Spirit's outpouring serves as the unbreakable seal guaranteeing God's lasting favor. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 39)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral applications on burning weapons and verse 29 are well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->
