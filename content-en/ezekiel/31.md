# Ezekiel 31 — The Cedar of Lebanon and the Lesson of Assyria

## Story

The fifth oracle in Ezekiel's cycle against Egypt arrives in "the eleventh year, in the third month, on the first day"—late June 587 BC, just weeks before Babylon breaches Jerusalem's walls. Right while Judeans still glance wistfully toward Egypt's might, the LORD sends His prophet to put a bold challenge straight to Pharaoh and all his hordes: "Whom are you like in your greatness?" (31:1–2).

To answer that question, God skips abstract theory and points straight to a towering precedent that had crashed less than thirty years earlier: the Assyrian empire. Once upon a time, Assyria stood like "a cedar in Lebanon" of unmatched splendor—fair boughs, dense forest shade, soaring trunk, "its top among the clouds." Deep underground springs nourished its roots until it towered above every other tree of the field. Its sprawling canopy spread so wide that every bird of the air nested in its branches, every wild beast bore young beneath its boughs, and "all the great nations lived in its shade." Neither cypress nor plane tree matched its beauty—not even the trees in "the garden of God" in Eden rivaled it, and all of them envied it. Right at verse 9, however, the LORD drops the decisive reminder: "I made it beautiful with its multitude of branches"—every bit of that grandeur came strictly as God's own gift (31:3–9).

From that dazzling peak, ruin broke out from a single sickness inside: "because it towered on high, setting its top among the clouds, and its heart grew proud of its height." Because of that arrogance and wickedness, God drove it away and handed it over to "the ruler of the nations" and "the most ruthless of the nations" to hack it down. Shattered limbs littered mountains, valleys, and ravines; all the nations that once sheltered under its shade quickly scattered, leaving birds of the sky and beasts of the field to roost atop its fallen trunk. The lesson of that earth-shaking fall is stated plainly: never again may any well-watered tree lift its crest proudly into the clouds, for all of them are mere mortals consigned to death and the depths of the earth among the sons of men (31:10–14).

The poem abruptly widens into a cosmic funeral spanning earth and the underworld. On the day that mighty tree plunged into Sheol, God covered the deep in mourning, held back the rivers, draped Lebanon in gloom, and caused every tree of the field to faint. The thunder of its fall shook the nations. Down in the depths of the earth, every prized tree of Eden and Lebanon that had already fallen before it felt "comforted" to watch the giant finally laid down alongside them together with the sword-slain warriors who had served as its strong arms (31:15–17).

In the final verse, the veil of allegory drops with a sharp thrust aimed right back at the opening listener, Pharaoh: "Which of the trees of Eden can be compared with you in splendor and majesty?" If even a towering cedar like Assyria got chopped down and cast into the Pit, Egypt gets zero exemption: "Yet you too will be brought down with the trees of Eden to the depths of the earth. You will lie among the uncircumcised, with those slain by the sword. This is Pharaoh and all his multitude" (31:18).

## Key Takeaways

**The proud forget that God Himself produced their prosperity.** Assyria's cedar reached the clouds thanks to deep springs and because God declared, "I made it beautiful" (31:4; 31:9). Yet the moment it stood tall, its heart swelled with arrogance (31:10). Turning God-given blessings into fuel for self-exaltation is always step one toward being cut down.

**Recently fallen empires hold up the clearest mirror for today's powers.** When Ezekiel spoke in 587 BC, fearsome Assyria had collapsed barely twenty-five years earlier (Nineveh fell in 612 BC). Memories of Assyria's swift crash were still fresh for Pharaoh and Judah alike. Anyone who watches a bigger tree fall and still imagines himself immortal is living in denial (31:2; 31:18).

**The shelter offered by earthly empires is temporary and brittle.** While the cedar stood tall, birds, beasts, and great nations crowded beneath its canopy (31:6). The instant the axe swung, "all the peoples of the earth left its shade and abandoned it" (31:12). Earthly power attracts crowds during boom times, yet never holds up a roof once judgment hits.

**Before death and God's judgment, the mightiest monarch stands equal to any mortal.** Chapter 31's sobering peak sits in 31:14: no matter how high into the clouds a tree climbs or how deeply watered its roots, every earthly giant is ultimately "consigned to death, to the depths of the earth, among the sons of men." Unbreakable sovereignty belongs to God alone.

## Deeper Meaning

**The world-tree motif bridges Ezekiel 31 and Nebuchadnezzar's dream in Daniel.** Just years after Ezekiel's poem, King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon—the very axe God used to fell these empires—dreamed of himself as a colossal tree at earth's center: "its top reached the sky," "the beasts of the field found shade under it, and the birds of the air nested in its branches," until a holy watcher came down from heaven crying, "Cut down the tree and chop off its branches" (Daniel 4:10–14). Pride's tragedy replays from empire to empire: the woodsman who cuts down yesterday's proud cedar easily grows into tomorrow's proud cedar until he humbles himself and acknowledges that the Most High rules over human kingdoms.

**Sheol welcoming the fallen tyrant echoes Isaiah 14's taunt against Babylon's king.** Watching the cedar crash into the underworld while other fallen "trees of Eden" take bitter comfort seeing the colossus brought down to their level (31:15–17) parallels Isaiah's biting satire: Sheol rouses ancient kings from their thrones to greet Babylon's fallen monarch with the sneer, "You too have become weak like us!" (Isaiah 14:9–15). Likewise, borrowing "the garden of God" in Eden to depict splendor followed by a fall mirrors the exact artistry Ezekiel just used for Tyre's king in chapter 28 (28:13).

**Worldly proud cedars set against God's "tender cedar sprig" and the "mustard seed."** Earlier in Ezekiel, before depicting proud Assyria chopped down here in chapter 31, God promised in chapter 17 to break off a tiny "tender sprig" from a cedar's crest and plant it on Israel's high mountain so it grows into a noble cedar where "birds of every kind will nest in it"—proving God "brings down the tall tree and exalts the low tree" (17:22–24). In the New Testament, Jesus takes up that very imagery in the parable of the mustard seed: the Kingdom of Heaven begins not as a sky-scraping empire, but tiny as a mustard seed, growing into a tree "so that the birds of the air come and nest in its branches" (Matthew 13:31–32). The only kingdom able to shelter all nations forever grows not from arrogance, but from the humility of Christ.

## Notes & Nuances

**Does 31:3 name the empire "Assyria" or a species of tree?** This is an intriguing Hebrew textual question. The Hebrew word for "Assyria" (*Ashur*) looks almost identical to *te'ashur*, meaning "cypress" or "pine," which appeared back in 27:6. Because of this, a few modern translations render 31:3 as a cypress or cedar of Lebanon, reading the whole poem directly about Egypt without naming Assyria. However, the BSB and most major translations (alongside leading scholars) retain "Assyria"—for Assyria truly was the mightiest Near Eastern empire, having fallen recently in 612 BC, offering an unanswerable historical object lesson to Pharaoh in 31:2 and 31:18 (just as Nahum held up Egypt's fallen Thebes as a warning to Assyria's Nineveh).

**Why did Egyptians dread "lying among the uncircumcised" (31:18)?** People often assume circumcision belonged exclusively to Israel, yet ancient historians (like the Greek writer Herodotus) and Egyptian archaeology confirm that ancient Egyptians—especially priests, nobility, and Pharaohs—also practiced circumcision and viewed preserving an intact mummified body inside a grand tomb as essential for the afterlife. Sentencing Pharaoh to die by the sword and lie in a common pit with "the uncircumcised" (31:18)—a refrain repeated heavily in chapter 32—aimed straight at Egypt's deepest religious pride.

**Why does 31:16 say the other underworld trees were "comforted" when the cedar fell?** "Comfort" here is not spiritual peace, but grim, darkly human irony common in ancient poetry: nations once trampled down into the grave by Assyria's empire watch the very bully who crushed them get dragged down to share the same pit, feeling bitter satisfaction that justice has leveled everyone alike.

**Historical timing right on the brink of Jerusalem's collapse.** The date "eleventh year, third month, first day" (31:1) lands around late June 587 BC (or 586 BC on some exile reckonings)—barely weeks before Babylon breached Jerusalem's walls in the fourth month of that same year. At that knife-edge hour, the oracle of the felled cedar aimed to extinguish any final illusion that Egypt could work a miracle to rescue the dying capital.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, explained the cedar of Lebanon flourishing in "the garden of God" (31:3; 31:8–9) as Scripture's portrait of the dazzling prosperity the Creator allowed Assyria to reach in history—until sky-high pride brought down merciless judgment. **Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew a searching lesson from 31:9–10: everything lovely the cedar possessed came straight from God's hand ("I made it beautiful"), yet human sin turns God's very blessings into fuel for conceit. Holding up Assyria's crash before Pharaoh, Henry noted, reflects God's wise pedagogy: whoever refuses to learn humility from another's downfall ends up becoming the next warning lesson for those who follow (31:14; 31:18). *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 31)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's familiar pastoral observations on verses 9–10 (abusing blessings for pride) and Assyria's lesson for Pharaoh are well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Walther Zimmerli**, Swiss Old Testament scholar, praised chapter 31 as masterful poetry adapting the ancient Near Eastern "world-tree" motif—a cosmic tree rooted in the abyss, reaching heaven, and sheltering all creatures. Yet Zimmerli stressed how Ezekiel transforms myth through Israelite faith: the tree is no immortal deity, merely a creature God made fair (31:9), and the moment pride oversteps bounds, God cuts it down to prove every empire is mortal flesh bound for the Pit (31:14). *(Ezekiel 2, Hermeneia, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Zimmerli's analysis of the ancient Near Eastern cosmic-tree motif demythologized under the LORD's sovereignty in Hermeneia volume 2 is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, carefully unpacked chapter 31's historical argument. He strongly defended reading "Assyria" in 31:3 because the chapter's persuasive punch relies on citing a real historical giant Pharaoh could never deny: Assyria had dwarfed Egypt and even conquered Egypt, yet now lay buried in the grave. Greenberg also traced how rich imagery of deep waters, Eden, and sheltering shade contrasts sharply with cold abandonment when the felled trunk lies stripped in the wilderness and tossed into Sheol. *(Ezekiel 21–37, Anchor Bible, 1997)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's defense of reading Assyria in verse 3 and analysis of literary contrasts in chapter 31 in Anchor Bible volume 2 are well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, Canadian-American Old Testament professor, spotlighted the tight bond between chapter 31's arrogant cedar and chapter 17's tender cedar sprig (17:22–24). While every empire crowning itself a sky-scraping cedar (Assyria, Egypt) gets chopped down for pride (31:10–14), only the cedar planted by the LORD Himself on Israel's high mountain endures forever as a refuge for all creatures. Block also clarified the keen religious disgrace felt by ancient Egyptians at Pharaoh being sentenced to lie among "the uncircumcised" in 31:18. *(The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 25–48, NICOT, 1998)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Block's pairing of chapter 31 with chapter 17 and Egyptian cultural context for 31:18 in NICOT volume 2 are well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->
