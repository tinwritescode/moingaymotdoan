# Ezekiel 13 — False Prophets and the Whitewashed Wall

## Story

At the close of the previous chapter, God vowed to put an end to every false vision and flattering divination in the house of Israel (12:24). In chapter 13, the word of the LORD comes to Ezekiel again, addressing him as "son of man" and sending him to prophesy directly against those claiming to be prophets of Israel. They are people who "prophesy out of their own imagination" and "follow their own spirit and have seen nothing." God compares them to "jackals among the ruins": when they should have climbed up into the breaks in the wall to repair it and rebuild defenses so the nation could stand firm in battle on the day of the LORD, they did nothing but prowl among the rubble. They see false visions and speak lying divinations, yet still brazenly announce "Declares the LORD" when He never sent them—and then urge people to expect their hollow promises to come true (13:1–7).

Facing such shameless impersonation, the Lord GOD declares His hand against those lying prophets. He pronounces a threefold sentence of exclusion: they will have no place in the council of His people, their names will not be inscribed in the register of the house of Israel, and their feet will never again enter the land of Israel. Through that judgment, the people will know that He is the Lord GOD (13:8–9).

God lays out exactly how they misled His people: they keep proclaiming, "Peace, when there is no peace." He paints a vivid picture: whenever the people throw up a flimsy wall, these prophets hurry over to "cover it with whitewash," coating the outside with fresh plaster so it looks solid. God instructs Ezekiel to tell those whitewashers straight out that the wall is guaranteed to collapse. God's fury will break over it like torrential rain, pounding hailstones, and violent windstorms ripping the wall apart and flattening it to the ground until its very foundations lie exposed. Once the wall crashes down, bystanders will mock them: where is all that whitewash you smeared on it? The falling masonry will crush the whitewashers right inside the rubble. Both wall and plasterers will be wiped out—that is the exact fate awaiting Israel's prophets who spin dreams of fake peace for Jerusalem (13:10–16).

Having finished the sentence against the male prophets, God tells Ezekiel to turn toward "the daughters of your people"—women who likewise invent prophecies out of their own imagination. They sew magic charm bands onto wrists and craft veils sized for heads of every height, practicing sorcery to "ensnare the souls" of God's people like hunters trapping birds. Even more despicably, they profane God's holy name among His people for a pitiful fee: "handfuls of barley and scraps of bread." Feeding lies to a crowd eager to hear falsehood, they invert justice altogether—putting to death people who should not die and promising life to those who should not live (13:17–19).

The Lord GOD declares that He despises their magic bands and veils. With His own hand He will rip them off their arms and heads, setting free the trapped souls to fly loose like birds escaping a snare. God puts His finger on this group's gravest crime: with their lies they have "disheartened the righteous, even though I have caused them no grief," while "encouraging the wicked" so they stay comfortable and never turn from their evil ways to save their lives. Consequently, all their lying visions and divinations will come to a permanent end; God will snatch His people out of their grip, and then they will know that He is the LORD (13:20–23).

## Key Takeaways

**Speaking in God's name what God never said is a grave offense.** The false prophets' sin was not making an innocent mistake or misreading the weather; it was fabricating messages out of their own heads and stamping "Declares the LORD" across them (13:6–7). Counterfeiting God's authority lures people into pinning their hopes on a mirage and robs them of their chance to change course before disaster hits.

**True shepherds mend the breach; counterfeits only paint over the cracks.** When a nation teeters on ruin, a true prophet's calling is to step into the breach to intercede for the people and call them back to God (13:5). False prophets pick the easier, more popular route: slapping fresh whitewash onto a crumbling wall and shouting "Peace!" (13:10). A pretty coat of plaster adds zero strength when a real storm arrives.

**Spiritual deception does not merely fool people; it turns right and wrong upside down.** The female sorceresses commit two cruelties at once: they inject dread and grief into upright hearts while emboldening the wicked to keep marching down the road to ruin (13:22). Any religious message that troubles honest consciences while letting wrongdoers sleep soundly certainly does not come from God.

**Falsehood survives through a partnership between deceiver and willing listener.** In 13:19, God does not fault the women alone for dishonoring Him over a few handfuls of barley; He also calls the people straight out as those "who listen to lies." False prophets multiply whenever crowds happily pay to hear soothing lullabies.

**God personally rescues His people from spiritual manipulation.** Even though these women deploy charms, veils, and curses to "hunt souls" of fearful victims, God declares He will tear every snare apart Himself and release His people to fly free (13:20–21). No occult spell can keep captive those who belong to the living God.

## Deeper Meaning

**Ezekiel and Jeremiah speaking with one voice before Jerusalem's fall.** From Babylon, Ezekiel exposes prophets crying "Peace, when there is no peace" (13:10), while during those exact same years in Jerusalem, Jeremiah sounds the identical charge against those dressing the people's wound lightly and chanting, "Peace, peace, when there is no peace" (Jeremiah 6:14). Jeremiah likewise unmasks prophets speaking visions of their own minds instead of standing in God's council to turn the people from evil (Jeremiah 23:16; 23:21–22)—exemplified by the false prophet Hananiah, who died that very year after making the nation trust in a lie (Jeremiah 28:15–17). Over a century earlier, Micah had described prophets who proclaimed "Peace!" whenever someone fed them, yet declared war on anyone who put nothing in their mouths (Micah 3:5)—matching the bartered prophecies for barley and bread scraps here (13:19).

**Standing in the breach as the hallmark of true ministry.** The rebuke that Israel's prophets "did not go up to the breaks or build up the wall" (13:5) directly recalls Moses' defining act: when Israel sinned with the golden calf, Moses "stood in the breach before Him" to plead that God's wrath not wipe out the nation (Psalms 106:23). Later in this very book, God revisits that image with deep sorrow: He looked for just one person to stand in the gap before Him to save the land from destruction, yet found no one, while prophets kept slapping whitewash on the people's sins (22:28–30). In stark contrast to jackals prowling ruins, Ezekiel had already been stationed as a watchman charged with sounding an honest alarm (3:17).

**Blotted out of Israel's register and the Book of Life.** The threefold penalty on false prophets in 13:9—exclusion from the people's council, erasure from Israel's register, and denial of entry into the land—strikes straight at covenant belonging. In the Old Testament, the "register of the house of Israel" or God's book represents the roll of the living who share in God's community; Moses once interceded so intensely that he offered to have his own name blotted out of God's book if Israel could not be forgiven, to which God replied He blots out only those who sin against Him (Exodus 32:32–33). Being struck from the register reminds readers that outward religious titles can never substitute for integrity before God.

**The whitewashed wall and the house built on sand.** Concluding the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus pictured the foolish builder whose house stood on sand: "The rain fell, the torrents raged, and the winds blew and beat against that house, and it fell—and great was its collapse" (Matthew 7:26–27). The torrential rain, hailstones, and gales demolishing the deceitfully whitewashed wall in Ezekiel 13 resonate unmistakably in Jesus' warning (13:11–14). Just moments earlier in the same sermon, Jesus also cautioned His listeners to watch out for false prophets arriving in sheep's clothing while inwardly ravenous wolves (Matthew 7:15).

**A warning ringing across every era into the New Testament.** Mosaic law had long forbidden all divination, sorcery, and spells, prescribing the strictest penalty for anyone presuming to speak falsely in the LORD's name (Deuteronomy 18:10–12; 18:20–22). That same vigilance carried straight into the early Church: the apostle John urged believers not to believe every spirit, but to test them because many false prophets have gone out into the world (1 John 4:1), while Paul foretold a time when people would refuse sound doctrine and gather teachers to say whatever their itching ears want to hear (2 Timothy 4:3–4). And God's promise to rip apart the snares so souls fly free like birds in 13:20 echoes the beloved psalm of deliverance: "We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowler" (Psalms 124:7).

## Notes & Nuances

**What does "whitewash" or "untempered mortar" (13:10–15) refer to?** Older translations often rendered the unusual Hebrew term as "untempered mortar," while modern translations such as the BSB render it "whitewash" (cf. 22:28). Scholars understand it as a thin limewash or cosmetic coat slapped onto a dry-stacked, poorly built stone wall lacking real mortar. From outside it looks neat, bright, and sturdy—until the first heavy storm washes the coating clean off and brings the wall crashing down. It is an apt image for shallow, reassuring prophecies that paper over cracks instead of fixing foundations.

**What were the "magic charm bands" and "veils" in 13:18–21?** Older translations rendered these obscure Hebrew terms literally as "pillows" and "kerchiefs," which can sound like bedroom linens to modern ears. Ancient Near Eastern cultural studies show these were cloth amulets or padded bands tied around wrists and elbows alongside long head-veils tailored to each client's height. In Babylonian and Canaanite folk magic, practitioners tied cords and knots to "bind" or "capture" another person's soul—claiming power to harm or shield anyone for a price. That is why God labels the entire business "ensnaring souls like birds" (13:20).

**This chapter does not deny that women can serve as true prophets.** Scripture honors genuine prophetesses used by God, including Miriam, Deborah, and Huldah. Ezekiel does not condemn the women in 13:17 because they are women; he condemns them for the exact same sin committed by the male prophets in the chapter's first half—fabricating messages out of their own minds—compounded by commercialized occult sorcery. Notably, the Hebrew text deliberately refrains from even calling them "prophetesses," referring to them simply as "the daughters of your people" who play at prophesying.

**Why compare false prophets to "jackals (or foxes) among the ruins"?** In Israel, jackals and desert foxes lurked inside broken city walls, burrowing dens that further weakened crumbling stonework while scavenging strictly for themselves. Placing that image right beside the charge that they "did not go up to the breaks" (13:4–5) creates biting irony: instead of guarding the flock and repairing the wall, false prophets were the very scavengers undermining it from within.

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century Church Father and biblical scholar, read the "jackals among the ruins" and whitewashed wall of chapter 13 as a direct warning for the Church of his day against heretical teachers. **John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, examined closely the collusion between liar and listener in this chapter. The false prophets' cries of "Peace!" are deadly, he pointed out, precisely because they scratch the itch of human self-deception: people want to be lulled asleep in sin, so they cheer anyone willing to whitewash their conscience. For Calvin, chapter 13's core lesson is that servants of the Word must dare to speak unwelcome truth rather than flatter the crowd. *(Commentaries on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of the Prophet Ezekiel, on chapter 13)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's central focus on flattering cries of false peace and audience complicity in chapter 13 is well remembered; specific lecture number not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, highlighted the sharp contrast in verses 19 and 22 concerning the women practicing false prophecy. They proved both base—trading God's holy name for handfuls of barley and scraps of bread—and cruel, reversing the entire purpose of God's Word: instead of comforting the righteous and waking the wicked, they saddened upright hearts and emboldened sinners. In Henry's eyes, that serves as a clear test for telling true preaching from counterfeit preaching in every age. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 13)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral remarks on verses 19 and 22 of chapter 13 are well remembered; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, shed light on the rare ancient vocabulary of chapter 13 using Mesopotamian language and custom.
