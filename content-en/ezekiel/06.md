# Ezekiel 6 — Prophecy Against the Mountains of Israel

## Story

After chapters 4 and 5 focused on the siege and fall of Jerusalem, the prophetic lens now widens across the entire country. The word of the LORD comes to Ezekiel, commanding him to set his face toward the mountains of Israel and prophesy against them. Across that rugged landscape, God's word addresses every contour of the terrain—mountains, hills, ravines, and valleys—to announce an inescapable decree: "Behold, I Myself will bring a sword against you, and I will destroy your high places" (6:1–3).

The coming sword targets the sprawling network of idolatrous shrines dotting the hilltops. God declares that their altars will become desolate, their incense altars will be smashed, and their slain will be cast down right in front of the idols they used to bow before. Even more chillingly, He will lay the dead bodies of the Israelites in front of their idols and scatter their bones all around their altars, rendering those shrines permanently defiled. Wherever they live, towns will lie in ruins, high places will become wasteland, altars will be shattered, idols demolished, incense altars chopped down, and everything their hands have made wiped out. As the slain fall right in their midst, the recurring refrain of the entire book rings out for the first time in this chapter: "then you will know that I am the LORD!" (6:4–7).

Right in the middle of a verdict that seems to sweep everything away, an unexpected opening appears with a single turn: "Yet." Even when the people are scattered among the nations, God will spare a remnant—some who escape the sword (6:8).

In the foreign lands where they have been carried captive, those survivors will begin to remember the LORD. They will realize how deeply He has been grieved by "their adulterous hearts that turned away from Me, and by their eyes that lusted after their idols." Out of that bitter awakening, instead of blaming circumstances, they will "loathe themselves" for all the evil and detestable things they committed. Only then will they truly know that He is the LORD, acknowledging that His warning to bring this calamity upon them "was not an empty threat" (6:9–10).

After that brief glimpse of a remnant, the oracle swings back with fierce intensity to the crisis awaiting the nation. God commands Ezekiel to make a striking physical gesture: "Clap your hands, stomp your feet, and say, 'Alas!'" over all the wicked abominations of the house of Israel. No distance can shield them from the threefold scourge: whoever is far away will die of plague, whoever is near will fall by the sword, and whoever survives inside the besieged city will die of famine. In this way, God will spend His fury upon them (6:11–12).

The oracle closes with a sweeping view from one end of the land to the other. When the slain lie scattered among their idols around their altars—on every high hill, on every mountaintop, under every green tree and every leafy oak, the very spots where they offered fragrant incense to their idols—they will know that He is the LORD. God declares that He will stretch out His hand against them, turning every place they inhabit into a desolate wasteland stretching "from the wilderness to Diblah." The chapter ends on a fourth sounding of the refrain, sealing the verdict: "Then they will know that I am the LORD" (6:13–14).

## Key Takeaways

**Sin did not stay confined to Jerusalem; it saturated the entire land.** While chapters 4 and 5 targeted the capital and its temple, chapter 6 reveals that betrayal spread to every ridge, hillside, ravine, and shady tree across Israel (6:3; 6:13). The most beautiful spots in nature were turned into incense altars for false gods. Judgment therefore covers every region where the people dwell, leaving no quiet countryside untouched.

**Idols are utterly helpless in the hour of disaster.** The image of corpses piled in front of idols and human bones scattered around altars shatters the empty promises of false worship (6:4–5; 6:13). People burned incense to idols hoping for harvests, peace, and protection; yet when the sword strikes, those statues of wood and stone are smashed to pieces themselves—how could they ever save their worshipers?

**True repentance begins when we see how our sin grieves God.** In 6:9, the awakening of the surviving exiles goes far deeper than regret over losing home and country. They remember the grief caused to God when their hearts strayed from Him, and from that realization they "loathe themselves" for the evil they have done. Merely fearing punishment is not repentance; real repentance is born when the heart grasps the pain it has inflicted on the One who loves it.

**The ultimate goal of judgment is that people may know God.** In a short chapter of just fourteen verses, the declaration "know that I am the LORD" rings out four times (6:7; 6:10; 6:13; 6:14). God does not act out of pointless cruelty. Through ruin and loss, He strips away every illusion so His people may recognize who truly rules history and alone deserves worship.

**Even in sweeping judgment, God remembers to preserve a remnant.** That turning point "Yet" in 6:8 cuts through wrath like a thin yet unbreakable thread of grace. Amid sword, famine, and plague, some survive into exile—not to escape accountability, but to remember God, repent, and carry the seed of the nation's future.

## Deeper Meaning

**Direct echoes of the Sinai covenant.** Nearly every image in chapter 6 is lifted straight from the ancient covenant warnings of Leviticus: God had warned long before that if Israel persisted in defiance, He would destroy their high places, cut down their incense altars, and pile their dead bodies upon the lifeless forms of their idols (Leviticus 26:30–31). Even the description of idol shrines "on every high hill, on every mountaintop, under every green tree" repeats almost word for word the sites Moses commanded Israel to obliterate when entering the promised land (6:13; Deuteronomy 12:2). Not only did Israel fail to remove Canaanite idolatry, they adopted it wholesale as their own way of life.

**Human bones on the altars: recalling King Josiah's unfinished reform.** Scattering human bones around altars (6:5) carried intense shock value for Jewish readers, since Mosaic law declared contact with human bones ritually defiling. Roughly thirty years before Ezekiel's time, the faithful King Josiah had swept through the high places, dug bones out of tombs, and burned them on pagan altars to defile them permanently so they could never be used for worship again (2 Kings 23:16; 23:20). Yet after Josiah died, the people rebuilt the shrines anyway. Now God Himself finishes what Josiah's reform could not complete—at a far more agonizing cost.

**The recognition formula from the Exodus returns to Israel itself.** The refrain "they will know that I am the LORD" first rose to prominence in Exodus, when God sent plagues so that proud Pharaoh and Egypt would be forced to acknowledge who He was (Exodus 7:5; 14:4). Tragically, by Ezekiel's day God's own covenant people had grown every bit as hardhearted as Pharaoh, forcing God to aim that same refrain at Israel. Across the book of Ezekiel, this formula appears more than sixty times, serving as a crimson thread connecting chapters of judgment to chapters of promised renewal.

**The oracle against the mountains in chapter 6 will be reversed in chapter 36.** One of the finest symmetries in Ezekiel is the pairing of two prophecies addressed to "the mountains of Israel." In chapter 6, God commands the prophet to face the mountains and prophesy *against* them, turning hills, ravines, and valleys into corpse-strewn wastelands (6:2–3; 6:14). Thirty chapters later, after judgment has passed, God tells him to prophesy once more *to* those very mountains, hills, ravines, and valleys—only now God turns toward them in mercy, making the hills put forth branches and bear fruit while towns fill with people again and prosper beyond their former state (36:1–4; 36:8–11). Judgment is never God's final word.

**Godly sorrow that leads to repentance.** The reaction of "loathing themselves" in 6:9—repeated later in chapters promising restoration (20:43; 36:31)—is not hopeless self-hatred, but the sign of an awakened conscience. In the New Testament, the apostle Paul distinguishes sharply between two kinds of sorrow: worldly sorrow brings death, whereas "godly sorrow brings repentance that leads to salvation without regret" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Amid the wreckage of exile, the seed of genuine renewal sprouts from that very pain.

## Notes & Nuances

**The imagery of corpses and bones around altars feels deeply unsettling.** Modern readers naturally recoil from verses 6:4–5 and 6:13, with dead bodies tossed before idols and bones strewn over the hills. Yet this passage is not depicting gratuitous brutality; it speaks in the concrete ritual vocabulary of the Old Testament. High-place idolatry regularly involved moral corruption, social exploitation, and even child sacrifice. Scattering bones over the altars pronounced permanent ritual defilement to end that false worship decisively, while exposing the utter helplessness of the gods Israel had trusted.

**Verse 6:9 has two notably different translations regarding whose heart is broken.** Many translations, including the BSB, render the Hebrew verb passively—"how I have been grieved" (or "broken")—expressing God's deep sorrow over His people's unfaithfulness. Other modern translations read the Hebrew verb actively: the exiles will remember how the LORD "crushed their adulterous hearts," using disaster to shatter their stubborn infatuation. The difference depends on reading the Hebrew verb as passive or active. Either way, both converge on the same truth: the people's betrayal came face-to-face with God's holiness and was completely overcome.

**Comparing idolatry to "adulterous hearts" and "lusting" (6:9).** Old Testament prophets—especially Hosea, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel—consistently portray the covenant between God and Israel as a faithful marriage (Hosea 4:12–13). Chasing after idols is therefore no dry procedural error, but relational betrayal—like an unfaithful spouse walking out on a devoted partner. This stark imagery will grow even more intense in chapters 16 and 23 and should always be read within that covenant-marriage framework.

**"Incense altars" (6:4; 6:6) and the place name "Diblah" (6:14).** The Hebrew term rendered "incense altars" in modern versions such as the BSB refers to small altars or stands used to burn incense at pagan shrines; older translations often connected the word to the root for solar heat and translated it as "sun images." Meanwhile, "Diblah" in 6:14 appears nowhere else in Scripture. Most scholars believe it reflects a very early scribal mix-up between two nearly identical Hebrew letters (*D* and *R*), so the original name was "Riblah"—a key military base at the northern edge of Syria where Babylon's king later tried Judah's captive king (2 Kings 25:6). If so, "from the wilderness to Riblah" means from the southern desert to the far northern frontier, encompassing the entire land.

**What emotion lies behind clapping hands and stomping feet in 6:11?** In the ancient world—and elsewhere in Ezekiel—clapping and stomping can express a rival's malicious gloating (25:6), yet they can also express horror, grief, and intense shock at catastrophe (21:14). Paired with the cry "Alas!", Ezekiel's gesture here is not glee over his countrymen's deaths, but a cry of anguish and stunned dismay at both their towering sin and the ruin rushing toward them.

## What Commentators Say

**Jerome**, 4th–5th century biblical scholar and Church Father, read the oracle against the mountains of Israel on two parallel levels in his commentary on Ezekiel. Historically, he saw it as the verdict pronounced on idolatrous shrines across the hills of Judah and Samaria ahead of Babylon's invasion. Spiritually, he regarded the "mountains" and "high places" as emblems of proud thoughts and false doctrines exalting themselves against the knowledge of God, all destined to be brought low by His word. *(Commentary on Ezekiel, on chapter 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Jerome's dual historical and allegorical reading of the mountains and high places in chapter 6 is well remembered; exact book and wording not checked in the original commentary -->

**John Calvin**, the 16th-century Reformer in Geneva, asked why God told Ezekiel to address senseless mountains rather than speak directly to people. He explained that Israel had become so deaf and calloused that God turned to summon the hills by name—the very sites that witnessed their idolatry—in order to put them to shame. On verse 9, Calvin placed special emphasis on "loathing themselves": in his view, this is an indispensable mark of genuine repentance, since someone who only fears the rod still loves sin, whereas someone truly returning to God recoils in disgust from their past unfaithfulness. *(Commentaries on the First Twenty Chapters of the Book of the Prophet Ezekiel, on chapter 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Calvin's reasoning about addressing lifeless mountains to rebuke hardness of heart and his exposition of self-loathing in true repentance in chapter 6 are well remembered; exact lecture wording not checked on ccel.org -->

**Matthew Henry**, the English pastor who died in 1714, drew attention to the interplay between righteous wrath and quiet mercy in the middle of the chapter. He noted that while three plagues—sword, famine, and pestilence—pursue the guilty whether near or far, the turning word "Yet" in verse 8 opens a door of grace for a remnant. In Henry's view, hardship in exile became the bitter medicine God used to wake their spiritual memory, helping them see how their sin had grieved Him and bringing them back to know Him. *(Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ezekiel 6)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Matthew Henry's characteristic pastoral themes regarding the remnant and the awakening effect of affliction in chapter 6; exact text not checked on ccel.org -->

**Moshe Greenberg**, 20th-century Jewish biblical scholar, analyzed the tight verbal links between Ezekiel 6 and the covenant curses in Leviticus 26. He also highlighted the Hebrew term Ezekiel repeatedly uses for "idols" throughout the chapter—a sharply derogatory word linked in sound and nuance to dung or filth—showing the priest-prophet's utter revulsion at God's people bowing down to lifeless slabs of matter. *(Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible, 1983)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Greenberg's analysis of the Leviticus 26 background and the derogatory Hebrew word for idols in chapter 6 is well remembered; specific page numbers not checked -->

**Daniel I. Block**, American Old Testament professor, pointed out that the whole of chapter 6 is structured around four recurrences of the refrain "they will know that I am the LORD," turning the oracle into a showdown between the living God and the powerless idols on the hills.
