# Jude 1 — Contending for the Faith Delivered Once for All

## Story

Jude, calling himself a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James, writes to those who are called, loved by God the Father, and kept in Jesus Christ, praying that mercy, peace, and love may be multiplied to them (1:1–2).

At first he had intended to write about the salvation they all share, yet an urgent crisis forced him to change course: he writes instead to urge them to "contend earnestly for the faith entrusted once for all to the saints." The reason is alarming: certain ungodly people have slipped into the church unnoticed—people marked out long ago for condemnation, who twist God's grace into a license for immorality and deny Jesus Christ, our only Master and Lord (1:3–4).

Even though his readers already know these stories, Jude calls up three sobering examples of judgment to wake them up. First, the Lord rescued Israel out of Egypt, yet afterward destroyed those who refused to believe in the wilderness. Second, the angels who refused to stay within their proper domain and abandoned their dwelling are held in eternal chains beneath darkness, awaiting the great day of judgment. Third, Sodom, Gomorrah, and the surrounding towns indulged in sexual immorality and pursued unnatural flesh, standing as a public warning of the punishment of eternal fire (1:5–7; Numbers 14:26–35; Genesis 19:1–25).

The intruders in the church follow that very same path: caught up in their own dreamings, they defile their bodies, reject authority, and slander glorious beings. Even the archangel Michael, when arguing with the devil over Moses' body, did not dare pronounce a slanderous verdict against him, saying only, "The Lord rebuke you!" Yet these people mock whatever they cannot understand, while the things they know by bare instinct—like mindless animals—become the very tools of their own destruction (1:8–10; cf. Zechariah 3:2).

Jude pronounces woe on them through three notorious Old Testament figures: they have walked the path of Cain (jealousy leading to fratricide), rushed headlong into the error of Balaam for money, and perished in Korah's rebellion (defying God's appointed leadership). Then he paints them with a rapid-fire burst of images drawn from nature: they are hidden reefs and blemishes at the church's love feasts, shepherds shamelessly fattening only themselves; waterless clouds swept past by the wind; autumn trees bare of fruit, twice dead and pulled up by the roots; wild sea waves foaming up their own shame; wandering stars for whom the blackest darkness has been reserved forever (1:11–13; Genesis 4:3–8; Numbers 16:1–35).

Jude cites a well-known prophecy attributed to Enoch, seventh from Adam: the Lord is coming with tens of thousands of His holy ones to pass judgment on all and convict every ungodly person of every wicked deed and every harsh word spoken against Him. These troublemakers are chronic grumblers, always complaining about their lot, driven by their own cravings, talking big and flattering people whenever it serves their own advantage (1:14–16).

At verse 17, the letter shifts sharply to a warm, constructive tone. Jude reminds his readers of what the apostles themselves foretold: in the last times scoffers will arise who follow their own ungodly desires. These are the people causing divisions—worldly-minded and devoid of the Spirit. As for believers, their task is clear: build yourselves up in your most holy faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, keep yourselves in God's love, and wait for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life. Toward those shaken by the false teachers, Jude calls for wisdom tailored to each situation: show mercy and gentle help to those who are wavering, rescue others by snatching them straight out of the fire, and show mercy tempered with caution toward those trapped even deeper, hating even the clothing stained by the flesh (1:17–23).

The letter closes with one of the grandest doxologies in the entire New Testament, praising the One who is able to keep them from stumbling and present them unblemished in His glorious presence with overflowing joy—the only God our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord (1:24–25).

## Key Takeaways

**Christian faith is a complete treasure handed down to be guarded, not reinvented.** Verse 1:3 calls the gospel the faith entrusted to the saints "once for all." Contending for the faith does not mean being quarrelsome or eager to pick fights; it means standing firm to protect the core truth about Christ whenever someone bends grace into permission to sin (1:3–4).

**Turning grace into an excuse for immoral living is a denial of Jesus Himself.** The false teachers in Jude probably never stood up and announced they were leaving the faith; they were still sitting right there at the church's love feasts (1:12). Yet whenever anyone uses grace to justify immorality and rebellion, they are in practice rejecting Jesus Christ's right to be Lord (1:4).

**Defending the truth goes hand in hand with building yourself up and rescuing the straying.** Jude never tells the church to spend all its energy attacking false teachers. The surest way to stand firm is to build yourself up in faith, pray in the Spirit, stay anchored in God's love—and then reach out a steady hand to pull back those who are wavering or slipping toward the fire (1:20–23).

## Deeper Meaning

**Two Old Testament triads of warning.** Jude arranges his historical warnings in memorable sets of three: first, three groups that faced judgment—unbelieving Israel in the wilderness (Numbers 14:26–35), angels abandoning their proper place (Genesis 6:1–4), and Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis 19:1–25)—followed by three individuals embodying three roots of sin: Cain's violent envy, Balaam's greedy pursuit of gain, and Korah's arrogant rebellion against God-given leadership (Numbers 16:1–35). In doing so, Jude shows that the intruders' sin is nothing new; it is simply traveling down well-worn tracks of ancient ruin.

**Snatching people from the fire echoes the vision of Joshua the high priest.** Both the archangel Michael's words "The Lord rebuke you!" (1:9) and the picture of snatching people from the fire while hating stained clothing (1:23) echo Zechariah chapter 3, where the LORD rebukes Satan, calls Joshua the high priest a burning stick snatched from the fire, and removes his filthy garments to clothe him in clean robes (Zechariah 3:1–5).

**The closing doxology places the church's security firmly in God's hands.** Right after telling believers to "keep yourselves in the love of God" (1:21), Jude finishes with the confident assurance that God Himself is "Him who is able to keep you from stumbling" (1:24). Human faithfulness always rests securely inside God's keeping power.

## Notes & Nuances

**Jude and 2 Peter 2 share remarkable similarities.** Comparing Jude 1:4–16 with 2 Peter 2:1–18 reveals almost the same sequence of examples and vivid metaphors: fallen angels chained in darkness, Sodom and Gomorrah, people who despise authority and slander glorious beings, the way of Balaam, blemishes at the love feasts, waterless clouds driven by the wind, and blackest darkness waiting ahead. Most scholars think Jude was written first and that 2 Peter 2 drew on it and adapted it for its own setting—omitting the two extra-biblical references in Jude 1:9 and 1:14–15 while adding Noah and Lot. Whichever came first, the overlap shows how widespread and serious the threat of grace-distorting false teachers was across the first-century Church.

**Why does Jude quote two sources outside the Bible (1:9; 1:14–15)?** The account of the archangel Michael disputing with the devil over Moses' body (1:9) does not appear in the Old Testament, coming instead from an ancient Jewish work known as the *Assumption of Moses*, while Enoch's prophecy (1:14–15) quotes from *1 Enoch*, a book widely read among Jews of that day. Quoting them does not mean Jude treated either book as Scripture on par with the Old Testament; rather, much like Paul quoting pagan Greek poets (Acts 17:28; Titus 1:12), he draws on stories and sayings familiar to his audience to illustrate truths that align with Scripture.

**Who is the author, Jude?** He introduces himself as "a brother of James" and "a servant of Jesus Christ" (1:1). In the New Testament, the man known simply as James—the leader of the Jerusalem church—was Jesus' own half-brother (Matthew 13:55; Galatians 1:19). Just like his brother in the letter of James, Jude never trades on his family tie to Jesus to claim authority, humbly calling himself instead a servant of the Lord.

**Richard Bauckham**, the British New Testament scholar, closely examined how Jude employs first-century Jewish methods of biblical interpretation to match ancient prophecies with present events. He argues that Jude is not an angry, hastily dashed-off note, but a tightly crafted composition built around triads—and that Jude served as the source later adapted by 2 Peter 2. *(Jude, 2 Peter, Word Biblical Commentary, 1983, introduction and commentary on Jude)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: analysis of Jude's triadic structure, Jewish exegetical method, and priority over 2 Peter is Bauckham's classic contribution in the WBC volume -->

**Augustine**, the fifth-century bishop of Hippo, addressed Jude's quotation of *1 Enoch* in 1:14–15. He pointed out that writings bearing Enoch's name contained much that was obscure and were never accepted into the canon by either the Jewish people or the Church; Jude's quotation of one truthful saying from it does not turn the entire book into Scripture. *(City of God, book 15, chapter 23 and book 18, chapter 38)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: in City of God 15.23 and 18.38, Augustine directly discusses the book of Enoch and why it is not canonical despite being quoted by Jude -->

**John Calvin**, the sixteenth-century Geneva Reformer, drew attention to the careful pastoral distinctions in 1:22–23 for dealing with people led astray by false teachers. In his view, church leaders must never use a single rigid approach for everyone: some need gentle guidance because they are merely confused, while others standing at the edge of the abyss must be pulled back decisively as if snatched from a fire. *(Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, on Jude chapter 1 verses 22–23)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's pastoral comment distinguishing different groups needing rescue in Jude 1:22–23 is very clear in his commentary -->

**Thomas R. Schreiner**, the American New Testament scholar, highlights the balance between 1:21 ("keep yourselves in the love of God") and 1:24 ("Him who is able to keep you from stumbling"). Placed side by side, he notes, those two verses sum up the entire Christian life: believers must earnestly keep themselves faithful, yet the only reason they do not collapse before the finish line is that God Himself keeps them. *(1, 2 Peter, Jude, New American Commentary, 2003, on Jude chapter 1 verses 20–25)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recalling Schreiner's analysis of the pairing between human responsibility in 1:21 and divine preservation in 1:24, though uncertain of exact wording -->
