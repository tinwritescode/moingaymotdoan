# 2 Corinthians 6 — The Temple of the Living God

## Story

As God's fellow workers, Paul urges the Corinthians not to receive God's grace in vain. Scripture records God's promise: "In the time of favor I heard you, and in the day of salvation I helped you." Paul drives the point home immediately: "Behold, now is the time of favor; now is the day of salvation!" (6:1–2; Isaiah 49:8). Isaiah's oracle originally addressed God's servant as Israel stood on the brink of deliverance from exile; Paul declares that promised hour has arrived in Christ, so the Corinthians must not hesitate to embrace the appeal for reconciliation at the end of chapter 5.

He and his companions put no stumbling block in anyone's path so their ministry will not be discredited. Instead, in everything they commend themselves as servants of God: through great endurance in troubles, hardships, and distresses; in beatings, imprisonments, and riots; in hard work, sleepless nights, and hunger; and through purity, knowledge, patience, and kindness, in the Holy Spirit, in genuine love, in truthful speech, and in the power of God—wielding weapons of righteousness for the right hand and for the left (6:3–7). An ancient soldier held a sword in his right hand for offense and a shield in his left for defense; Paul borrows the picture to show that whether advancing or defending, the minister's sole armor is righteousness.

He follows with a rapid-fire cascade of contrasts: through glory and dishonor, bad report and good report; regarded as impostors, yet true; as unknown, yet well known; as dying, and yet we live on; as punished, yet not killed; as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; as poor, yet making many rich; "as having nothing, and yet possessing everything!" (6:8–10). These rhythmic paired lines read like a triumphant hymn capturing the paradox of following Christ: looking empty-handed to the world while brimming with abundance before God.

Shifting abruptly from elevated eloquence to warm personal tenderness, Paul calls them directly by name: "We have spoken freely to you, Corinthians; our hearts are open wide." There is no restriction in Paul's affection for them; the squeeze is inside their own hearts. Speaking as a father to his children, he asks for a fair exchange: open your hearts wide to him as well (6:11–13). The guarded coolness of part of the congregation grieves Paul deeply; refusing to coerce them by rank, he simply asks that an open heart be met with an open heart.

Next comes an urgent, solemn warning: "Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers." What partnership can righteousness have with wickedness? What fellowship can light have with darkness? What harmony is there between Christ and Belial, or what does a believer share with an unbeliever? What agreement exists between the temple of God and idols? For we are "the temple of the living God," just as God has said: He will dwell with them and walk among them, and He will be their God and they will be His people. Therefore the Lord commands them to come out from among them, be separate, and touch no unclean thing; then He will welcome them, be a Father to them, and they will be His sons and daughters (6:14–18; Leviticus 26:11–12; Isaiah 52:11; 2 Samuel 7:14). Being unequally yoked draws on the Old Testament statute banning plowing with an ox and a donkey harnessed together (Deuteronomy 22:10); Belial was a Jewish name of the era for the Wicked One or Satan. Because 6:14–18 and 7:1 shift abruptly—with 7:2 picking right back up from the plea for open hearts in 6:13—many scholars propose this section was an independent Pauline fragment (or preexisting warning) inserted here, while many others see it fitting directly into Paul's plea against compromising with Corinthian idolatry.

## Key Takeaways

**God's grace calls for a response right now.** Urging the Corinthians not to accept God's grace in vain because "now is the day of salvation" (6:1–2), Paul insists that being reconciled to God is never something to postpone into an indefinite future, but an urgent decision today.

**Authentic ministry proves itself through endurance and integrity, never easy popularity.** Paul's list stretches from beatings and prison to patience, kindness, and truthful speech (6:4–7). Christ's servant walks through both honor and shame, possessing everything before God even when appearing empty-handed to the world (6:8–10).

**Strained relationships in the church heal only when both sides open their hearts.** Paul lays his own heart bare to the Corinthians; the blockage comes not from narrowness on his side, but from their own guarded hearts (6:11–13).

**Being the temple of the living God leaves no room for compromise with idols.** The warning "Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers" (6:14) summons the church to clear boundaries: belonging to Christ cannot be blended with practices that contradict light and righteousness (6:15–18).

## Deeper Meaning

**Isaiah's promise of rescue from exile has become present reality in Christ.** Quoting God's promise to His Servant regarding "the time of favor" and "the day of salvation" (Isaiah 49:8), Paul announces that the day has arrived right now (6:2). The greatest exodus is no longer leaving Babylon, but being reconciled to God through Jesus.

**The church becomes a living temple fulfilling God's promise to dwell among His people.** In the Old Testament, God promised to set His dwelling and walk among Israel (Leviticus 26:11–12). Paul had already told the Corinthians that their congregation is God's temple indwelt by the Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16), and here he invokes that dignity to call them to holiness (6:16).

**The promise once spoken to David's royal heir now embraces sons and daughters alike.** In 2 Samuel, God pledged regarding David's son, "I will be his Father, and he will be My son" (2 Samuel 7:14). Quoting that vow in 6:18, Paul deliberately expands it into the plural and explicitly includes women: "you will be My sons and daughters." Under the new covenant, full adoption as God's children belongs equally to men and women who believe.

**The yoke metaphor draws on Old Testament law concerning mismatched partnerships.** Moses' law forbade plowing with an ox and a donkey yoked together (Deuteronomy 22:10) because two animals of unequal strength and nature cannot pull a straight furrow. Paul adopts that vivid image to describe the mismatch of tying a believer's spiritual allegiance to pagan idolatry (6:14–16).

## Notes & Nuances

**"Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers" in 6:14 does not mean cutting off everyday contact with non-Christians.** Earlier Paul explicitly clarified that he was not telling believers to withdraw from all unbelievers, since then "you would have to leave this world" (1 Corinthians 5:9–10), and he urged Christians married to willing unbelievers not to divorce them (1 Corinthians 7:12–13). In the context of 6:14–18, the primary focus is refusing spiritual entanglement with idolatry and binding alliances that pull believers away from fidelity to Christ. Pastors wisely apply its principle to decisions like marriage or close business partnerships, yet it must never justify despising or isolating unbelievers.

**Scholars offer two main explanations for the abrupt transition at 6:14–18 and 7:1.** As noted above, because 7:2 smoothly resumes the appeal for open hearts in 6:13 and this section features unusual vocabulary like "Belial," many scholars suspect an earlier or separate unit was placed here. Conversely, many interpreters argue that the Corinthians' narrowed hearts toward Paul (6:12) stemmed precisely from lingering entanglements with rivals or pagan customs, making this call to purity sit right where it belongs.

**"Having nothing, and yet possessing everything" in 6:10 is not praising poverty for its own sake.** Paul is not claiming Christians must be penniless to be spiritual. He is portraying the harsh cost of his apostolic calling amid slander and pressure, proving that true wealth before God is never measured by earthly bank accounts or status.

**Receiving "God's grace in vain" in 6:1 must be read in context.** Paul is not claiming God's grace fails or breaks down, but warning that someone can hear the gospel, claim it verbally, and yet live out of step with the reconciliation and holiness that grace produces.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, delights in the string of paradoxes in 6:8–10. The apostles look "poor, yet making many rich," not by handing out gold coins, but by imparting the riches of faith and knowledge of God—a treasure no thief can steal and one that multiplies the more freely it is shared. *(Homilies on 2 Corinthians, Homily 12)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Chrysostom preaching vividly on spiritual riches in 6:8–10; uncertain whether it is Homily 12 or 13 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Protestant Reformer, interprets 6:14 with calm realism: Paul never bans ordinary civic interaction with unbelievers, without which human life in this world would be impossible. What is barred is taking on a shared yoke—voluntarily entering entangling alliances that pollute faith, especially participating in pagan worship rites of that era. *(Commentary on Second Corinthians, on chapter 6 verse 14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's clear distinction between ordinary social life and idolatrous entanglement on 6:14 is very consistent in his commentary -->

**The Qumran community**, a Jewish sect living beside the Dead Sea from roughly the first century BC through the first century AD, left texts such as the *Community Rule* and *War Scroll* filled with stark contrasts between children of light and children of darkness, naming the evil power Belial and calling their purified community God's temple. These contemporary Jewish writings reveal how deeply the language of separation in 6:14–18 is rooted in first-century Jewish piety. *(Dead Sea Scrolls, Community Rule)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the parallels between 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1 and Qumran vocabulary regarding Belial, light/darkness, and community-as-temple are a landmark discovery of twentieth-century scholarship -->

**Victor Furnish**, twentieth-century American New Testament scholar, points to the dense cluster of rare terms and sharp break in flow across 6:14–18 and 7:1, concluding that the passage displays strong signs of a non-Pauline or pre-existing text inserted during editorial compilation. Even so, he notes that whoever placed it here did so to reinforce the call to live worthily as God's temple. *(II Corinthians, Anchor Bible, 1984, on chapter 6 verse 14 to chapter 7 verse 1)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Furnish's Anchor Bible commentary presents one of the fullest arguments treating 6:14–7:1 as an interpolation outside the original flow -->

**C. K. Barrett**, twentieth-century British New Testament scholar, remains cautious toward theories assigning this passage to another author.
