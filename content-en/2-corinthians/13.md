# 2 Corinthians 13 — Examine Yourselves in the Faith

## Story

Paul warns them once more: this will be his third visit to them, and he cites the Old Testament rule that "Every matter must be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses." Just as he warned when present on his second visit, he warns again while absent—both those who sinned earlier and everyone else—that when he returns he will not spare them. Are they demanding proof that Christ is speaking through him? They will see that proof, because Christ is not weak toward them, but is powerful among them. Although He was crucified in weakness, He lives by God's power; likewise, Paul and his coworkers are weak in Him, yet by God's power they will live with Him to deal with the Corinthians (13:1–4; Deuteronomy 19:15). Jesus Himself had applied the rule of two or three witnesses to church discipline (Matthew 18:16); some take Paul to mean a fair hearing once he arrives, while others hear a quiet play on his three visits serving as three witnesses.

Then Paul turns the test straight back onto them: instead of cross-examining him, "Examine yourselves to see whether you are in the faith." Test yourselves: do you not realize that Jesus Christ is in you—unless, of course, you fail the test? As for himself and his coworkers, he trusts they will recognize that he has not failed the test (13:5–6). The Corinthians had demanded proof validating Paul's apostleship; his reply cuts to the bone: their own living faith is the clearest evidence of his God-given ministry—disqualifying him comes dangerously close to disqualifying their own faith.

Even after such a firm warning, Paul's pastoral heart softens immediately: he prays to God that they will do nothing wrong—not so that Paul will look vindicated or get a chance to flex disciplinary authority, but simply so that they will do what is right, even if that leaves Paul looking untested. For he cannot do anything against the truth, only for the truth. He is glad whenever he is weak as long as they are strong; his one prayer is for their full restoration (13:7–9). If the Corinthians put things right before he arrives, he will never need to display stern power—and to Paul, looking "weak" while seeing the church healthy is far better.

That is why he writes these candid lines while still away, so that when he arrives he will not have to be severe in exercising the authority the Lord gave him—authority granted "for building you up, not for tearing you down" (13:10). Repeating the principle from chapter 10, that sentence sums up the heartbeat of the whole letter: even at his sharpest, every word aims to build up the church.

The storm clouds of the last four chapters clear into warm farewells. Calling them brothers, Paul leaves five crisp charges: rejoice, aim for full restoration, encourage one another, be of one mind, live in peace—and the God of love and peace will be with you. Greet one another with a holy kiss; all the saints where he is writing send their greetings. Then he closes with the most famous benediction in all his letters: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with all of you!" (13:11–14; 1 Corinthians 16:20). Setting Jesus Christ, God the Father, and the Holy Spirit side by side on equal footing, this parting blessing stands as one of the earliest New Testament witnesses to Trinitarian faith and is still pronounced at the close of Christian worship services worldwide (cf. Numbers 6:24–26).

## Key Takeaways

**The cross is the blueprint for Christian ministry.** The entire closing section distills into 13:4: Christ "was crucified in weakness, yet He lives by God's power." Paul's own life follows that exact cadence—weak in Him, yet alive with Him. The Corinthians craved an unscarred superstar apostle; Paul shows them an apostle shaped like his Lord.

**The decisive test is whether Christ truly dwells in us.** Paul does not invite them to tally up whether they are morally impressive enough. His question cuts deeper: "Do you not realize that Jesus Christ is in you?" (13:5). Authentic faith is proved not by never stumbling, but by Christ genuinely living and working inside us.

**Longing for others' good even at the cost of one's own image.** Paul prays they will walk in righteousness "even though we may seem to have failed" (13:7). If they repent before he lands in Corinth, he gets no showcase of apostolic force, and critics might jeer that he only talks tough on paper. He gladly accepts that: "We are glad whenever we are weak and you are strong" (13:9).

**Peace is the home where God dwells.** Five brief imperatives close the letter: rejoice, mend what is torn, comfort one another, think together in unity, live at peace—followed by the promise, "and the God of love and peace will be with you" (13:11). A church torn by strife (12:20) is invited into the very character of God.

## Deeper Meaning

**The weakness of God.** Back in his first letter, Paul already told Corinth that "the weakness of God is stronger than man's strength" (1 Corinthians 1:25). Philippians traces the same arc: Christ "became obedient to death—even death on a cross," and therefore "God exalted Him to the highest place" (Philippians 2:8–9). Verse 13:4 takes that exact story and maps it onto the servant's life.

**Self-examination.** The charge "test yourselves" (13:5) has deep roots in Scripture. Amid the ruins of Jerusalem, Lamentations urges, "Let us examine and test our ways, and turn back to the LORD" (Lamentations 3:40). Paul similarly told the Corinthians ahead of the Lord's Supper, "Each one must examine himself" (1 Corinthians 11:28). True self-examination never aims at despair, but at returning home.

**Mending torn nets.** The Greek word behind "restoration" in 13:9 and "aim for perfection" in 13:11 is the exact verb used when James and John sat in their boat "mending their nets" (Matthew 4:21). Paul used the same word when telling Spirit-led believers to "restore" a fallen brother gently (Galatians 6:1). His dream for Corinth is not punishment, but mending torn fellowship so it can catch fish again.

**Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.** The benediction of 13:14 names all three persons in a single breath, echoing Jesus' Great Commission to baptize disciples "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19). Believers enter Christian life under those three names and leave worship week by week blessed under those same three names.

**The God of peace.** Paul frequently ends letters promising that the God of peace will accompany his readers (Philippians 4:9). In Romans he spells out our share in it: "as far as it depends on you, live at peace with everyone" (Romans 12:18). Peace is simultaneously God's gift and a daily discipline believers practice.

## Notes & Nuances

**"Examine yourselves" is not a call to live in perpetual dread.** Some readers treat 13:5 as a command to doubt their salvation every morning. In Greek, however, Paul's question expects an affirmative answer: they *do* know Christ is in them—and knowing that confirms the one who brought them the gospel is no counterfeit apostle (13:6). Still, the warning carries real weight: Paul never assumes wearing the label "Christian" automatically guarantees belonging to Christ.

**The benediction of 13:14 and the doctrine of the Trinity.** Paul does not draft a formal definition of the Trinity—the word never appears in Scripture, and the church hammered out its precise formulas at fourth-century councils. Yet he invokes Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit on the exact same divine plane as God the Father in a solemn blessing, something no strict Jewish monotheist would ever do with an angel or prophet. Notice the order too: Christ's grace comes first, because through His grace we discover the Father's love and share in the fellowship of the Spirit.

**Verse numbering varies across editions.** Most English Bibles—including the Berean Standard Bible—number fourteen verses with the benediction at 13:14, whereas the standard Greek critical text and several modern versions merge verses 12 and 13 into one, putting the benediction at 13:13. Commentaries therefore cite the exact same blessing as either 13:13 or 13:14.

**Did Paul's third visit actually take place?** Acts reports that Paul traveled through Macedonia and "came to Greece, where he stayed three months" (Acts 20:2–3). Most historians believe he spent those three winter months in Corinth—writing the Letter to the Romans right there from Gaius's home (Romans 16:23). If so, chapter 13 worked: Paul arrived, healed the breach, and looked westward toward Rome and Spain—though Clement's letter to Corinth decades later shows factionalism eventually crept back in.

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva, appeals to 13:5 against scholastic teaching that believers cannot possess assurance of salvation and must live in continual doubt. Paul expects Christians to know Christ dwells within them, and anyone unaware of that does not yet hold true faith—an assurance grounded not in our own sufficiency, but in Christ living in us. *(Commentary on Second Corinthians, on chapter 13 verse 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's appeal to 13:5 against medieval skepticism regarding assurance is widely cited -->

**The Mishnah**, Jewish oral law codified around AD 200, describes how priests pronounced Aaron's threefold blessing (Numbers 6:24–26): in village synagogues it was spoken as three distinct clauses, while in the temple it was spoken as one continuous blessing with the sacred Name pronounced aloud and hands lifted above the head. Paul's closing blessing likewise unfolds in three balanced clauses and came to close Christian worship just as the priestly blessing closed temple worship. *(Mishnah, Sotah, chapter 7, section 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the description of the priestly blessing in villages versus the temple in Sotah chapter 7 is well known; the comparison with 13:14 is editorial -->

**Gordon Fee**, American New Testament scholar, wrote extensively on the Spirit in Paul's letters and notes that 13:14 reveals Paul did not merely theorize about the Trinity—he lived and thought within it: salvation originates in the Father's love, is accomplished through the Son's grace, and is experienced through the fellowship of the Spirit, a triadic pattern underlying much of Paul's writing. *(God's Empowering Presence: The Holy Spirit in the Letters of Paul, 1994)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Fee highlights 13:14 as central to Paul's experiential Trinitarianism in this book; exact wording not recalled -->

**Murray Harris**, New Zealand New Testament scholar, examines "the fellowship of the Holy Spirit," which can mean either the community the Spirit creates among believers or believers' shared participation in the Spirit Himself.
