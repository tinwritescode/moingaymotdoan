# 2 Corinthians 3 — Ministers of a New Covenant

## Story

Paul asks bluntly: is he starting to commend himself all over again? Or does he need "letters of recommendation" to them or from them, like some people do? No—the Corinthians themselves are his letter of recommendation, written on his heart, known and read by everyone. Plainly they are a letter from Christ, delivered through Paul's ministry—written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God; not on tablets of stone, but on tablets of human hearts (3:1–3). Traveling preachers in the ancient world routinely carried endorsement letters from churches, just as the brothers wrote a letter introducing Apollos (Acts 18:27). New rivals arriving in Corinth probably waved impressive letters while belittling Paul; he answers by pointing straight at the Corinthians' own transformed lives. Engraving God's law on hearts of flesh links directly to the prophets' new covenant promises (Jeremiah 31:31–33; Ezekiel 36:26–27).

Picking up his closing question from chapter 2—who is qualified for this ministry?—Paul declares that his confidence comes through Christ before God. Not that he and his coworkers are competent in themselves to claim anything as coming from their own strength; their competence comes from God. He has made them competent as ministers of a new covenant—not of the letter, but of the Spirit, "for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (3:4–6). "The letter" here does not mean literal reading as opposed to allegory; it means a written code chiseled onto stone that supplies no power to obey it—it can only expose sin and pronounce death, whereas the Spirit of the new covenant breathes life.

From there Paul compares the two ministries. The ministry that brought death, engraved in letters on stone, came with such glory that the Israelites could not gaze steadily at Moses' face because of its radiance—fading though it was. How much more glorious, then, will the ministry of the Spirit be! If the ministry that brought condemnation was glorious, how much more glorious is the ministry that brings righteousness! Compared with this surpassing glory, what once had glory now has no glory at all: if what was fading away came with glory, how much greater is the glory of what lasts forever (3:7–11; Exodus 34:29–35). Arguing from the lesser to the greater was a classic method of Jewish scriptural exposition in Paul's day.

Holding such a bold hope, Paul and his coworkers speak with open, fearless frankness. They are not like Moses, who put a veil over his face to keep the Israelites from staring at the end of what was fading away. Yet their minds were hardened: down to the very day Paul writes, whenever the old covenant is read, that same veil remains unlifted, because only in Christ is it taken away. Every time Moses is read aloud, a veil still covers their hearts; yet "whenever anyone turns to the Lord, the veil is taken away" (3:12–16; Exodus 34:34). Verse 14 is the rare place in the New Testament where the phrase "old covenant" appears for the Scriptures of the earlier covenant read in the synagogues.

Paul sums up the chapter in two unforgettable sentences: "Now the Lord is the Spirit, and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom." With unveiled faces, all believers contemplate the Lord's glory as in a mirror and are being transformed into His image, "from glory to glory," which comes from the Lord, who is the Spirit (3:17–18). Ancient mirrors were polished bronze—a famed specialty of Corinth—not crystal-clear glass, yet capable of casting a real reflection. The Greek verb in verse 18 carries a rich double nuance of both *beholding* in a mirror and *reflecting* like a mirror: believers gaze on Christ's glory and gradually reflect that very glory outward as they grow like Him.

## Key Takeaways

**The strongest credential for ministry is never paperwork, but changed lives.** Others rely on ink-and-paper letters of recommendation, while Paul points straight to the Corinthians themselves: their lives reshaped by the Holy Spirit are a living letter anyone can read (3:1–3).

**Written rules show what is right, yet cannot give the power to obey.** Saying "the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (3:6) does not belittle the Old Testament. It contrasts an external stone law code with the Spirit's inner work transforming the human heart.

**The glory of the new covenant eclipses the old because it brings life and lasts forever.** The Sinai covenant shone brightly enough to make Moses' face glow, yet it remained a temporary ministry exposing guilt; the Spirit's ministry brings righteousness and never fades (3:7–11).

**Turning to the Lord brings freedom and steady transformation.** Once Christ removes the veil from the heart, believers no longer need to hide or live in fear (3:16–17). Fixing their unveiled eyes on the Lord's glory day by day, they are gradually changed into His likeness "from glory to glory" (3:18).

## Deeper Meaning

**Old Testament prophecies of a new covenant and hearts of flesh have come true in the church.** Writing on "tablets of human hearts" (3:3) weaves together two landmark prophetic promises: God's vow to write His law on His people's hearts (Jeremiah 31:33) and His promise to replace hearts of stone with hearts of flesh and put His Spirit within them (Ezekiel 36:26–27). Paul announces that centuries of prophetic longing are unfolding right now in Corinth.

**The contrast between letter and Spirit runs like a golden thread through Paul's theology.** "The letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (3:6) matches Romans closely, where Paul declares believers released from the law to serve God "in the new way of the Spirit, and not in the old way of the written code" (Romans 7:6). God's law is good in itself, yet without the Spirit it only exposes human helplessness.

**Moses' shining face at Sinai is reread in the light of Christ.** In Exodus, Moses wore a veil while speaking to Israel, yet removed it every time he entered the LORD's presence (Exodus 34:29–35). Paul takes that exact detail of lifting the veil before the Lord (3:16) to depict what happens when anyone turns to Christ: every barrier vanishes.

**Reading Moses in verse 3:15 mirrors weekly synagogue worship across the empire.** Every Sabbath, Jewish communities in city after city gathered to hear the five books of Moses read aloud, just as James noted at the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15:21). Paul looks at those familiar gatherings with ache in his heart, grieving that so many countrymen hear Moses week after week without recognizing the One to whom Moses pointed.

**The goal of salvation is transformation into Jesus' likeness.** Being "transformed into His image, from glory to glory" (3:18) connects directly with God's eternal purpose in Romans: that believers be "conformed to the image of His Son" (Romans 8:29). That transformation never happens overnight, but grows step by step by the Spirit's steady work.

## Notes & Nuances

**Verse 3:6 is never an excuse to dismiss careful, literal Bible reading.** People have sometimes cited "the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" to downplay serious study of Scripture's wording or claim allegorical flights always beat plain meaning. In context (3:3–7), "the letter" refers to the Mosaic law etched on stone apart from the Spirit, not to a method of reading texts.

**Verses 3:14–15 must be read carefully to guard against anti-Jewish prejudice.** Tragically, Christians in later centuries sometimes twisted Paul's words about "hardened minds" and a veil over Moses to demean Jewish people. Yet Paul himself was a Jew writing out of deep anguish for his kinsmen (Romans 9:1–3), and right in 3:16 he holds open hope that whenever they turn to the Lord, the veil lifts. Scholars also note that Paul is describing blindness toward recognizing Christ in Scripture—never claiming God has cast off Israel (Romans 11:1–2).

**Why does Paul say Moses veiled his face so Israel wouldn't see the glow fading?** Exodus 34:29–35 never states that Moses' radiance was fading; it simply says the people were afraid of his shining face, so he put on a veil. Some commentators think Paul inferred its temporary nature from Moses needing to return repeatedly to the tabernacle; others note that the Greek word for "end" can also mean "goal"—meaning Israel could not see the true destination (*telos*) of that glory, namely Christ.

**Does "Now the Lord is the Spirit" in 3:17 blur Jesus and the Holy Spirit into one person?** Plucked out of context, the sentence can sound as if Paul confuses Christ with the Spirit. Yet the very second half of 3:17 says "the Spirit of the Lord," preserving a clear distinction. Most scholars note that "the Lord" opening 3:17 picks up the word "Lord" just quoted from Exodus 34:34 in 3:16: the living "Lord" to whom believers turn for the veil to be removed works directly through the Holy Spirit in the new covenant.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, built an entire landmark treatise around 3:6. Opposing Pelagius's claim that merely knowing God's law enables humans to do good on their own, Augustine argued that law alone remains bare "letter," exposing guilt and deepening condemnation, while God's grace and love poured into hearts by the Holy Spirit supply the life that enables real obedience. *(On the Spirit and the Letter)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's entire treatise De spiritu et littera is structured around 2 Corinthians 3:6 in his debate with Pelagius -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Protestant Reformer, likewise reads 3:6 in covenantal terms rather than as literal versus allegorical interpretation. Even the gospel itself, he notes, if heard merely as dead words without the Spirit touching the heart, produces no life; conversely, the Old Testament genuinely conveyed life to faithful ancients who received it by the Spirit. *(Commentary on Second Corinthians, on chapter 3 verse 6)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Calvin's covenantal reading of 3:6 rejecting Origen's allegorical split and stressing the Spirit's work is famous -->

**Richard Hays**, American New Testament scholar, reads 2 Corinthians 3 as the master key to Paul's interpretation of the Old Testament. Rather than discarding Israel's Scriptures, Paul rereads them from inside a Spirit-transformed community: the church as a living letter (3:2–3) shows how the Old Testament shines clearly when read in Christ's light. *(Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul, 1989, chapter 4)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the chapter analyzing 2 Corinthians 3 is one of the best-known sections of Hays's Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul -->

**Daniel Boyarin**, American Jewish scholar of Talmud and antiquity, treats the letter-versus-Spirit contrast in 3:6 as the decisive turn where Paul breaks with traditional Jewish reading of Torah. By shifting focus from Israel's concrete observance to a universal spiritual meaning in Christ, Paul simultaneously opened the door to all nations and created deep tension with Jewish particularity. *(A Radical Jew: Paul and the Politics of Identity, 1994)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Boyarin uses the letter/Spirit polarity in 2 Corinthians 3 as a central lens for Paul's hermeneutics in this book; exact chapter not recalled -->
