# 2 Corinthians 4 — Treasure in Jars of Clay

## Story

Having received this new covenant ministry through God's mercy, Paul and his companions do not lose heart. They have renounced secret and shameful ways; they do not practice deceit, nor do they distort the word of God. Instead, by setting forth the truth plainly, they commend themselves to everyone's conscience in the sight of God (4:1–2). Paul is quietly answering rumors in Corinth suggesting he spoke evasively or held something shady back.

If their gospel is veiled, it is veiled only to those who are perishing—unbelievers whose minds "the god of this age" has blinded, so that they cannot see the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God. Paul does not preach himself, but Jesus Christ as Lord, while presenting himself and his coworkers simply as servants of the Corinthians for Jesus' sake. For God, who once said, "Let light shine out of darkness," made His light shine in their hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ (4:3–6; Genesis 1:3). "The god of this age" is Paul's stark name for Satan. Light flashing out of darkness recalls both the first day of creation and Paul's own blinding encounter with heavenly light on the Damascus road (Acts 9:3–5).

Yet they carry "this treasure in jars of clay," so that everyone may see that this all-surpassing power belongs to God and not to them. They are hard pressed on every side, yet not crushed; perplexed, yet not in despair; persecuted, yet not forsaken; struck down, yet not destroyed. They always carry around in their body the death of Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be revealed in their body. While still alive, they are constantly handed over to death for Jesus' sake, so that His life may shine through their mortal flesh; in short, "death is at work in us, but life is at work in you" (4:7–12). Ancient clay jars were cheap, fragile, everyday pots easily cracked and tossed aside, even when people hid gold coins or valuable scrolls inside them. Paul flips Corinthian prestige upside down: his scarred, unimpressive body does not discredit his ministry—it spotlights God's power.

Sharing the same spirit of faith expressed in Scripture—"I believed, therefore I have spoken"—Paul and his companions likewise believe and therefore speak. They know with certainty that the One who raised the Lord Jesus from the dead will also raise them with Jesus and present them together with the Corinthians in His presence. All this hardship is for their benefit, so that as grace reaches more and more people, thanksgiving may overflow to the glory of God (4:13–15; Psalms 116:10). That psalm was originally the cry of someone freshly rescued from the cords of death who refused to stop trusting God—matching the near-death crisis Paul described in chapter 1.

Therefore they do not lose heart: though their outer self is wasting away, "our inner self is being renewed day by day." This light and momentary affliction is producing for them an eternal weight of glory far beyond all comparison—because they fix their eyes not on what is seen, but on what is unseen, since what is seen is temporary while what is unseen is eternal (4:16–18). Paul calls his beatings, imprisonments, and perils "light and momentary" not because they barely hurt, but because placed on the scales beside eternal glory, they weigh almost nothing.

## Key Takeaways

**Gospel servants preach Jesus Christ, never themselves.** Declaring "we do not proclaim ourselves, but Jesus Christ as Lord" (4:5), Paul sets every piece in its rightful place: Christ stands at the center, while apostles serve simply as humble servants for His sake.

**Human frailty is the very showcase where God's strength shines brightest.** God deliberately stores the priceless gospel inside fragile "jars of clay" so nobody confuses the container with the treasure inside (4:7). Being pressed, perplexed, and struck down without collapsing proves the sustaining power belongs to God rather than human polish (4:8–9).

**Apostolic ministry mirrors the exact shape of Jesus' cross and resurrection.** Paul accepts daily exposure to death for the gospel so that Jesus' resurrection life shines out through his mortal body and pours into the Corinthians (4:10–12).

**Keeping our eyes on eternity guards us from losing heart amid present decline.** The outer body inevitably wears down under age and hardship, yet the inner person grows fresher day by day (4:16). Weighing passing sorrows against boundless glory ahead supplies strength to keep going (4:17–18).

## Deeper Meaning

**Coming to faith is nothing less than an act of new creation.** Paul reaches all the way back to God's opening word in Genesis 1:3 to explain how anyone recognizes Jesus (4:6). Light breaking into a darkened heart is never a human self-discovery; it is a creative miracle spoken by God Himself.

**Jesus is the clearest image of the invisible God.** Verse 4:4 calls Christ "the image of God," matching Paul's great confession in Colossians (Colossians 1:15). Back in chapter 3, divine glory glowed temporarily on Moses' face; now God's glory shines unmistakably in the face of Jesus Christ (4:6).

**Both John's Gospel and Paul recognize Satan as the temporary ruler of this fallen age.** Here Paul names the blinding power "the god of this age" (4:4), paralleling Jesus calling Satan "the prince of this world" who is about to be driven out (John 12:31). That title never makes Satan God's equal; it simply exposes his temporary grip over those who refuse the light.

**Sharing Christ's death to experience His resurrection power marks every faithful disciple.** Carrying Jesus' dying in the body so His life shines through (4:10–11) walks in step with Paul's longing in Philippians: to know the power of Christ's resurrection and "the fellowship of His sufferings" (Philippians 3:10).

**The scale balancing present suffering against coming glory recurs across Paul's letters.** Calling present pain "light and momentary affliction" working an eternal weight of glory (4:17) echoes Romans directly: present sufferings are not worth comparing with the glory that will be revealed in us (Romans 8:18).

## Notes & Nuances

**Calling Satan "the god of this age" in 4:4 does not imply two rival gods of equal rank.** In the early centuries, certain heretical movements trying to split the Old Testament Creator from Jesus' Father seized on this phrase. A few church fathers like Irenaeus even tried repunctuating the sentence to avoid misunderstanding. Most commentators agree Paul means Satan: he wields only a passing, borrowed sway within this broken age and never stands remotely equal to the Creator.

**Suffering never "earns" glory like spiritual wages.** When 4:17 says affliction "is producing for us an eternal weight of glory," Paul is not teaching that pain buys heaven. Read alongside 4:14–16, glory flows entirely from grace and resurrection; enduring hardship for Christ matures the inner person and fits us for the glory God freely gives.

**Contrasting "what is seen" and "what is unseen" in 4:18 is not Platonic contempt for physical matter.** Greek philosopher Plato treated the physical world as inferior or shadowy compared with invisible ideals. Paul does not share that view—right back in 4:14 he affirmed bodily resurrection. "What is seen" here means passing present circumstances, whereas "what is unseen" means the enduring reality of God's coming kingdom not yet visible to our eyes.

**Why does Paul's quotation of Psalm 116:10 in 4:13 differ slightly from Hebrew translations of the psalm?** In Hebrew, Psalm 116:10 reads, "I believed, therefore I said, 'I am greatly afflicted,'" whereas the ancient Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) rendered the opening words cleanly as "I believed, therefore I spoke." Paul quotes directly from the Septuagint, keeping the exact heart of the psalmist's faith: even when crushed near death, the believer refuses to stop bearing witness to God.

## What Commentators Say

**Irenaeus**, second-century bishop of Lyon in Gaul, discussed 4:4 carefully while refuting Marcionites and Gnostics. Because those opponents used "the god of this age" to argue that an evil deity made the world, Irenaeus proposed repunctuating the sentence so it read that God blinded the unbelieving minds "of this age." **John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, remarks on the "jars of clay" in 4:7 that if the apostles had been mighty, wealthy, and untouchable, onlookers would have credited human clout for the gospel's advance. **Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, drew deeply on passages like 4:7–12 for what he called the "theology of the cross." Rather than displaying Himself through worldly glitz, God chooses to conceal His power beneath its opposite: life hidden inside death, glory hidden inside disgrace, priceless treasure hidden inside a cracked clay jar. *(Heidelberg Disputation, 1518)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident this captures the heart of Luther's theology of the cross in the Heidelberg Disputation; uncertain whether he cites 2 Corinthians 4:7 directly in that 1518 text -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Protestant Reformer, views 4:16 as God's medicine against discouragement for every believer. The more our outer body and earthly condition wear away, the more God uses those very blows to strengthen and refresh our inner spiritual life—provided we keep our gaze fixed on eternal realities rather than tallying present losses. *(Commentary on Second Corinthians, on chapter 4 verses 16–18)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Calvin's pastoral emphasis on inner renewal amid outward decay at 4:16–18; exact wording not checked -->

**Michael Gorman**, American New Testament scholar, regards 4:7–12 as one of the clearest expressions of "cruciformity"—cross-shaped spirituality—in Paul's letters. Paul does not merely preach the cross as abstract doctrine, but embodies Jesus' own death and resurrection inside his daily life so that Christ's life flows out to the church. *(Cruciformity: Paul's Narrative Spirituality of the Cross, 2001)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: 2 Corinthians 4:7–12 is a foundational text for Gorman's concept of cruciformity in this book -->
