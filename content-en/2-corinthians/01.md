# 2 Corinthians 1 — The God of All Comfort

## Story

Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, together with Timothy our brother, writes to the church of God in Corinth and to all the saints throughout Achaia, greeting them with grace and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ (1:1–2). Achaia was the Roman province of southern Greece, with Corinth as its capital. This letter was written roughly a year after 1 Corinthians, right after Paul's relationship with the church had weathered a painful crisis.

He opens by blessing God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, "the Father of compassion and the God of all comfort." God comforts Paul in all his troubles so that he in turn can comfort others in any trouble with the comfort he himself has received from God. Just as the sufferings of Christ overflow into Paul's life, so also through Christ his comfort overflows. If Paul is afflicted, it is for their comfort and salvation; if he is comforted, it is for their comfort, producing patient endurance of the same sufferings he endures. His hope for them is unshaken: just as they share in his sufferings, they will also share in his comfort (1:3–7). Unlike most of his letters, which begin with thanksgiving for his readers, Paul here opens with a Jewish-style blessing that immediately introduces the letter's twin themes: affliction and comfort.

Next he openly shares the crisis he endured in the province of Asia. He and his companions were crushed "far beyond our ability to endure, so that we despaired even of life." Inwardly they felt they had already received the sentence of death—so that they would not rely on themselves, but on God, who raises the dead. He delivered them from so deadly a peril, He will deliver them again, and on Him they have set their hope that He will continue to deliver them. Paul also honors the Corinthians' part through prayer: as many join in praying for grace on his behalf, many will likewise give thanks for the answer (1:8–11). Paul does not specify what happened in Asia (western modern Turkey, centered on Ephesus); some think of a near-fatal illness, others of the silversmiths' riot or violent persecution in Ephesus (Acts 19:23–29).

His boast is the clear testimony of his conscience: he has conducted himself in the world—and especially toward the Corinthians—with godly holiness and sincerity, not relying on worldly wisdom but on God's grace. His letters hold no hidden subtext beyond what they read and understand. He hopes they will come to understand him completely, just as they already understand him in part, so that on the day of the Lord Jesus he will be their boast just as they will be his (1:12–14).

Because of that confidence, he had earlier planned to visit them twice: stopping in Corinth on his way to Macedonia, and then returning from Macedonia to Corinth so they could help send him on to Judea, giving them a double blessing. When his travel plans changed, was it out of fickleness or fleshly scheming, making him say "Yes, yes" and "No, no" in the same breath? As surely as God is faithful, his message to them has never been both "Yes" and "No." The Son of God, Jesus Christ—preached among them by Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy—was never "Yes" and "No," but in Him it has always been "Yes." All of God's promises find their "Yes" in Christ, and through Him we utter our "Amen" to the glory of God. It is God Himself who establishes both Paul and them in Christ, anointed them, set His seal of ownership upon them, and put His Spirit in their hearts as a "pledge" of what is to come (1:15–22). Paul had outlined an itinerary at the close of 1 Corinthians and later altered it (1 Corinthians 16:5–8), leading critics in Corinth to brand him unreliable and cast doubt on his gospel as well. Silvanus is Silas, who worked alongside Timothy and Paul when the church was planted (Acts 18:5). A "pledge" (*arrabōn*) was a commercial down payment guaranteeing that the full balance would follow: the Spirit is the down payment on believers' eternal inheritance.

Finally, Paul states plainly why he held back from returning: he calls God as witness upon his own soul that it was strictly to spare them—to avoid another painful confrontation—that he did not yet come back to Corinth. He is not trying to lord it over their faith, but to work alongside them for their joy, because by faith they already stand firm (1:23–24).

## Key Takeaways

**Suffering is never wasted when it equips us to comfort others.** God comforts Paul not merely to ease his personal pain, but so he can pass that very comfort along to others walking through trouble (1:3–4). Those who have walked through dark valleys and been lifted up speak with quiet weight when sitting beside someone else in pain.

**God sometimes allows us to reach the end of our strength so we stop leaning on ourselves.** In Asia, Paul felt crushed beyond endurance, staring straight at death (1:8–9). Coming that close to the edge stripped away every illusion of self-sufficiency, leaving him no anchor except the God who raises the dead.

**Every promise of God comes true in Jesus.** Changing travel plans did not make Paul double-tongued; far more importantly, the Savior he preaches is never "Yes" and "No" (1:19–20). In Jesus, God has spoken His decisive "Yes," and the church echoes back its "Amen."

**Church leaders do not own or rule over people's faith.** Explaining why he delayed returning to Corinth, Paul clarifies that he has no desire to dominate their faith, only to labor as a fellow worker for their joy (1:24). Apostolic authority exists to build up, never to bully.

## Deeper Meaning

**The Old Testament God of comfort now reveals Himself as the Father of Jesus.** In Isaiah, the LORD announces Himself as the One who comforts His frightened, exiled people (Isaiah 51:12). Paul opens this letter with that very title, now anchored directly to "the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1:3): God's mercy is no longer distant, but has come near in His Son.

**Suffering and comfort flow together through union with Christ.** Paul calls his trials "the sufferings of Christ" (1:5). In Romans he similarly calls believers fellow heirs with Christ, sharing in His sufferings so that we may also share in His glory (Romans 8:17). Hardship for the gospel is never proof that God has abandoned us; it is proof we are walking the same road Christ walked.

**Resurrection power is already active in present-day life.** Feeling he had already received "the sentence of death," Paul leaned on "God, who raises the dead" (1:9). Just as Abraham trusted that God could raise the dead when offering Isaac (Hebrews 11:19), Paul discovered that resurrection power is not reserved solely for the final day, but lifts believers through present perils today.

**Every Old Testament promise converges in one person.** Looking back over Israel's entry into the promised land, Joshua affirmed that not one of all the LORD's good promises had failed (Joshua 21:45). Paul carries that praise further: every promise God ever made across the ages finds its complete "Yes" in Jesus (1:20).

**The Holy Spirit is God's seal and down payment on our future inheritance.** The twin images of being sealed and given "the Spirit in our hearts as a pledge" here (1:22) reappear word for word in Ephesians, where the Spirit is the seal and guarantee of our inheritance (Ephesians 1:13–14). Receiving the Spirit today gives us a genuine foretaste of eternal life, like firstfruits promising the full harvest (Romans 8:23).

## Notes & Nuances

**We do not know for certain what crisis struck Paul in Asia.** Paul tells us only that he was crushed beyond strength and despaired of life (1:8–9), without giving details. Some link it to the silversmiths' riot in Ephesus (Acts 19:23–29), while others suspect a severe illness or an unrecorded imprisonment and near-execution outside the narrative of Acts. Knowing the exact incident is unnecessary for grasping the spiritual lesson Paul draws from it.

**Changing plans is not the same as breaking promises.** Critics in Corinth seized on Paul's altered itinerary (1 Corinthians 16:5–8) to accuse him of speaking out of both sides of his mouth, insinuating that his message was equally shaky. Paul shows that truthfulness in the spirit of Jesus' words (Matthew 5:37) is not stubborn rigidity regardless of circumstances, but acting in integrity for others' genuine good (1:17; 1:23).

**Second Corinthians sits inside a complex sequence of letters and visits.** Between 1 Corinthians and 2 Corinthians, Paul paid a brief, distressing "painful visit" to Corinth (2:1) and followed it with a stern "severe letter" written through many tears (2:4). Remembering that painful history makes clear why Paul's tone in chapter 1 blends deep tenderness with careful self-defense.

**"Amen" is much more than a polite sign-off to prayers.** In 1:20, Paul uses the Hebrew word *Amen* (meaning "truly, surely so") for the congregation's spoken response to God's promises. That liturgical habit stretched straight from Old Testament worship (Nehemiah 8:6) into the gatherings of the earliest church.
