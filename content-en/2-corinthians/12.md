# 2 Corinthians 12 — My Grace Is Sufficient for You

## Story

Compelled to keep boasting even though nothing is to be gained by it, Paul turns to visions and revelations from the Lord—precisely the sort of badge his rivals prized. Yet he tells his own story as if speaking about someone else: he knows a man in Christ who fourteen years ago was caught up to the third heaven, into "Paradise"—whether in the body or out of the body he does not know, only God knows—and heard inexpressible things, things that a human being is not permitted to tell. About such a man he will gladly boast, yet about himself he will boast in nothing except his weaknesses. Even if he chose to boast, he would not be a fool, because he would be telling the truth; yet he holds back so no one will credit him with more than what they see in him and hear from him (12:1–6). In first-century Jewish cosmology, the heavens had multiple tiers with the third heaven representing God's dwelling; "Paradise" was originally a Persian word for a royal walled garden, adopted for the blessed realm in God's presence (Luke 23:43). Paul had kept this extraordinary experience completely private for fourteen years and spoke of it only under pressure.

To keep him from becoming conceited because of the surpassing greatness of these revelations, "a thorn in my flesh" was given to him—a messenger of Satan to torment him and keep him humble. Three times he pleaded with the Lord to take it away, yet the Lord answered: "My grace is sufficient for you, for My power is perfected in weakness." Therefore Paul gladly boasts all the more about his weaknesses, so that Christ's power may rest upon him. For Christ's sake he delights in weaknesses, insults, hardships, persecutions, and difficulties, "for when I am weak, then I am strong" (12:7–10). Paul never identifies the thorn; commentators across the centuries have proposed a chronic physical ailment—such as an eye disease (Galatians 4:13–15), malaria, or epilepsy—or a relentless human adversary dogging his ministry. Because Paul left it unnamed, the Lord's answer has brought deep comfort to generations of believers living with hardships God chooses not to remove.

Paul shuts down his "fool's speech" and confronts them directly: the Corinthians themselves drove him to it. They ought to have been the ones commending and defending him, because even though he is nothing, he is not in the slightest inferior to those "super-apostles." Every sign of a true apostle was carried out among them with utmost perseverance—along with signs, wonders, and miracles. How were they ever treated as inferior to the other churches, except that Paul refused to be a financial burden to them? He slips in a wry parting jab: "Forgive me this wrong!" (12:11–13; Romans 15:18–19).

Now he is ready to visit them for "the third time," and he still will not burden them financially: he is not seeking their possessions, but them. Children do not save up for their parents, after all—parents save up for their children. Paul will most gladly spend and be completely spent for their souls, even if the more deeply he loves them, the less he is loved in return. Some critics muttered that while he declined direct pay, he was a crafty schemer catching them by trickery—insinuating he was skimming from the Jerusalem collection. Paul fires straight back: did any of the delegates he sent ever take advantage of them? He urged Titus to visit along with another brother; did Titus exploit them in any way? Did Paul and Titus not walk in the very same Spirit and in the very same footsteps (12:14–18)? This third visit counts after his founding stay and the brief painful visit between the two letters.

He reminds them not to imagine he has been defending himself before their tribunal all this time: he speaks in Christ before God, and every word aims to build up his beloved friends. He fears that when he arrives he may not find them as he wishes, and they may not find him as gentle as they wish. He dreads uncovering quarreling, jealousy, outbursts of anger, factions, slander, gossip, arrogance, and disorder. He fears that when he returns, God may humble him before them once more, leaving him to grieve over many who sinned earlier and still have not repented of the impurity, sexual immorality, and debauchery in which they indulged (12:19–21). That closing worry echoes the exact twin struggles Paul wrestled with throughout 1 Corinthians: bitter rivalry and sexual license.

## Key Takeaways

**Extraordinary spiritual experiences are never meant for bragging.** Paul was swept all the way up to the third heaven, yet he stayed silent about it for fourteen years and spoke of it as "a man" other than himself (12:2). He wants people to judge him not by unverifiable ecstasies, but by "what he sees in me or hears from me" (12:6). Everyday character, not dramatic visions, is the real measure of a servant of God.

**"No" is still a real answer from God.** Three times Paul begged for the thorn to be lifted, and the Lord left it in place (12:8). Yet the Lord did not stay silent; He answered with an unshakable promise: "My grace is sufficient for you" (12:9). God does not always rewrite our circumstances, yet He is always enough right inside them.

**Christ's strength needs empty space to shine.** "My power is perfected in weakness" (12:9). People convinced of their own sufficiency leave no room for God's power. Consequently, Paul completely reframes insults and hardships: no longer embarrassments to hide, they become the very arena where Christ's strength rests on him—"for when I am weak, then I am strong" (12:10).

**Real shepherds seek people, not pockets.** Paul addresses the Corinthians like a parent talking to children: "I am not seeking your possessions, but you" (12:14). He willingly pours out everything and spends himself entirely for them, even when deeper love is repaid with cooler affection (12:15). Genuine love does not wait to be repaid before giving.

**A true pastor weeps over the flock's sin.** Paul does not fear arriving in Corinth to face personal insults; he dreads arriving to find stubborn rivalry and immorality and having to "mourn over many who sinned earlier and have not repented" (12:20–21). He calls that being "humbled" by God—for a shepherd feels the flock's sin as his own heartbreak.

## Deeper Meaning

**Praying three times.** Three times Paul pleaded for his thorn to be removed. In Gethsemane, Jesus likewise prayed, "let this cup pass from Me," and went away to pray "a third time, saying the same thing once more" (Matthew 26:39; Matthew 26:44). That cup was not taken away—and through it salvation came. Paul's prayer walks straight down his Master's road.

**Words kept sealed.** Paul heard "inexpressible things, things that man is not permitted to tell" (12:4). Similarly, when John lifted his pen to write what he heard, a voice from heaven commanded, "Seal up what the seven thunders have said, and do not write it down" (Revelation 10:4). God reveals everything we need to believe and obey, yet not everything He discloses is meant for broadcast.

**Kept small so God receives the glory.** Before battling Midian, God told Gideon his army was too big, "lest Israel boast against Me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me'" (Judges 7:2). Paul earlier reminded Corinth that "God chose the weak things of the world to shame the strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27). The thorn guarded Paul from ever imagining his own hand had done it.

**Satan strikes, yet only inside God's leash.** The thorn is called "a messenger of Satan, to torment me," yet its purpose is "to keep me from becoming conceited" (12:7). Job's story unfolds the same way: Satan was allowed to strike Job, yet God drew the line: "only spare his life" (Job 2:6). What the enemy intends for ruin, God overrules for safekeeping.

**Giving not only the gospel, but oneself.** Paul's willingness to "be spent for your souls" (12:15) matches his words to the Thessalonians: longing to share with them "not only the gospel of God, but our own lives as well" (1 Thessalonians 2:8). That mirrors Christ Himself, who gave not merely His teaching, but His very self.

## Notes & Nuances

**"A man in Christ" is unmistakably Paul himself.** Although he tells the story in the third person, he immediately explains that a thorn was given to *him* to keep *him* from pride "because of the surpassing greatness of the revelations" (12:7). Virtually all interpreters agree he is speaking of himself, using the third person to keep humble distance from an honor he refuses to flaunt.

**Who sent the thorn, and what was it?** Verse 12:7 uses the passive—"there was given me a thorn"—without naming the giver. Though it operates as a messenger of Satan, it accomplishes a holy aim: keeping Paul humble. Most readers therefore see God sovereignly permitting and overruling it. Alongside proposals of chronic illness or relentless opponents, medieval interpreters often took it as carnal temptation (influenced by the Latin translation "goad of the flesh"), while the Old Testament also calls hostile adversaries "thorns in your sides" (Numbers 33:55). Paul leaves it unnamed—and perhaps that is the best gift of all.

**Remaining unhealed is never proof of weak faith.** Some teachings insist that anyone with enough faith will be cured of every illness. Yet Paul—who performed "signs, wonders, and miracles" (12:12)—prayed three times and saw his thorn remain. He also had to leave Trophimus behind in Miletus "sick" (2 Timothy 4:20). God certainly heals, as He showed mercy to Epaphroditus (Philippians 2:27), yet when He does not heal, He has not abandoned His child.

**Paul worked miracles, yet never rested his ministry on them.** Listing "the marks of a true apostle," he puts "great perseverance" ahead of signs and wonders (12:12). He does not deny performing miracles, yet refuses to hinge his credibility on spectacle. In wonder-chasing Corinth, Paul insists that the primary mark of an apostle is steadfast endurance under trial.

## What Commentators Say

**The Tosefta**, an ancient Jewish compilation accompanying the Mishnah, tells of four sages who entered the heavenly "orchard" (Hebrew *pardes*, cognate with *Paradise*): Ben Azzai looked and died; Ben Zoma looked and lost his mind; Elisha ben Abuyah fell into apostasy; only Rabbi Akiva entered in peace and departed in peace. The tale reflects how first-century Jews knew of heavenly ascents and treated them with profound caution—a sobriety mirrored in Paul's guarded account of Paradise. *(Tosefta, Hagigah, chapter 2)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: the tale of the four sages entering pardes in Tosefta Hagigah chapter 2 and Talmud Hagigah 14b is famous; many scholars connect it with Paul's reserve -->

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century Greek preacher, dismissed the notion that the thorn was a demon-induced headache. Pointing out that "Satan" means adversary, he understood "messenger of Satan" as human opponents dogging Paul—men like Alexander the coppersmith and other persecutors whom God allowed to harass Paul so he stayed humble. *(Homilies on 2 Corinthians, Homily 26)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom rejecting headaches and identifying the thorn with human persecutors is widely cited; homily number may vary by one -->

**Augustine**, bishop of Hippo around AD 400, devoted the entire closing book of his Genesis commentary to 12:2–4. He distinguishes three modes of vision—bodily sight, imaginative mental sight (like dreams), and pure intellectual sight beyond images—arguing that the third heaven signifies this highest intellectual vision, where Paul's uncertainty about being in or out of the body reflects rising completely beyond bodily senses. *(The Literal Meaning of Genesis, Book 12)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Book 12's treatment of 12:2–4 and the three kinds of vision is one of Augustine's best-known discussions -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, rejected the widespread medieval reading of the thorn as sexual temptation. Instead, he understood it as the persecutions and trials Paul endured for the gospel, along with the agonizing inward struggles that weigh upon gospel preachers. *(Commentary on Galatians, 1535, on chapter 4 verse 13)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: recall Luther dismissing sexual temptation as the thorn when commenting on Galatians 4:13; exact details about inward struggles uncertain -->

**Gershom Scholem**, Israeli scholar of Jewish mysticism, viewed 2 Corinthians 12 as one of the earliest surviving witnesses to Jewish *Merkabah* ("chariot") mysticism, in which visionaries ascended through layered heavens to behold God's throne. Paul's vocabulary—third heaven, Paradise, and forbidden words—closely parallels rabbinic traditions describing heavenly ascents. *(Jewish Gnosticism, Merkabah Mysticism, and Talmudic Tradition, 1960)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: confident Scholem links 2 Corinthians 12 to Merkabah mysticism; uncertain whether this exact formulation is in this book or Major Trends -->
