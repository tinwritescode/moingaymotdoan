# 2 Corinthians 5 — An Eternal Home and the Ministry of Reconciliation

## Story

Paul continues unfolding the hope that outlasts death: if our "earthly tent"—this mortal body—is dismantled, we have an eternal building from God in heaven, not built by human hands. Meanwhile, inside this tent we groan under its burden, longing to be clothed with our heavenly dwelling so that we will be found clothed rather than "naked." We do not wish to be unclothed, but to be further clothed, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life. God Himself has prepared us for this very purpose and has given us the Spirit as a pledge and guarantee (5:1–5). The Greek word for "earthly house" is literally *tent*—an image close to Paul's heart, since he had worked as a tentmaker in Corinth (Acts 18:3). Unlike Greek philosophy, which hoped for the soul to escape the body forever, Paul longs for a permanent resurrection body from heaven (1 Corinthians 15:53–54).

Because of that guarantee, they live with unwavering courage. Knowing that to be at home in the body means being away from the Lord—"for we walk by faith, not by sight"—they stay confident and would even prefer to be away from the body and at home with the Lord. Whether at home in the body or away from it, their overarching aim is to please Him. For we must all appear before "the judgment seat of Christ," so that each one may receive what is due for the things done in the body, whether good or bad (5:6–10). To Corinthian readers, "judgment seat" (*bēma*) instantly evoked the raised stone platform in the center of their city forum where Proconsul Gallio had once heard charges against Paul (Acts 18:12), as well as the podium where winners at the regional games received their crowns.

Knowing the fear of the Lord, Paul and his coworkers strive to persuade people. What they are is plain to God, and Paul hopes it is equally plain to the Corinthians' consciences. He is not commending himself to them all over again, but giving them reason to be proud of him so they can answer critics who boast in outward appearance rather than in what is in the heart. If he seems "out of his mind," it is for God; if he is in his right mind, it is for them. For Christ's love compels him, convinced that one died for all, and therefore all died; and He died for all so that those who live should no longer live for themselves, but for Him who died for them and was raised again (5:11–15). Opponents in Corinth may have mocked Paul as eccentric or intense, while they themselves favored polished exteriors and flashy speech.

From now on, therefore, Paul regards no one from a worldly, fleshly point of view; even though he once regarded Christ that way, he does so no longer. "Therefore if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away. Behold, the new has come!" (5:16–17). Before meeting the risen Lord, Paul had sized up Jesus through Pharisaic outward eyes as a cursed, crucified pretender; now in Christ, his entire vision of Jesus and of every human being has been remade, fulfilling God's promise through Isaiah to do a "new thing" (Isaiah 43:18–19).

All this newness comes from God, who reconciled us to Himself through Christ and gave us the ministry of reconciliation: that God was reconciling the world to Himself in Christ, not counting people's trespasses against them, and entrusting to the apostles the message of reconciliation. They are therefore "ambassadors for Christ," as though God were making His appeal through them; on Christ's behalf, Paul implores them: be reconciled to God. He condenses the mystery of redemption into one breathtaking sentence: "God made Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God" (5:18–21). An ambassador represented an emperor in negotiating terms of peace. Verse 21 echoes Isaiah's portrait of the sinless servant who gave his life as a guilt offering for sin (Isaiah 53:9–10).

## Key Takeaways

**Believers do not dread death because an eternal dwelling already awaits them.** Our present body is like a temporary tent bound to wear out, yet God has prepared a lasting resurrection body from heaven and given the Spirit as His down payment (5:1–5). Armed with that promise, Christians walk by faith rather than by what is immediately visible (5:7).

**Hope for eternity never breeds apathy today; it drives us to please the Lord.** Knowing everyone will stand before "the judgment seat of Christ" to answer for life lived in the body (5:10), Paul strives to walk transparently before God and earnestly persuade others.

**Christ's love redirects both why we live and how we view every person.** Because Jesus died for all, believers no longer live for themselves, but for the One who died and rose on their behalf (5:14–15). Consequently, we stop judging other people—or Christ Himself—by shallow worldly surfaces (5:16).

**God takes the initiative to reconcile the world to Himself and commissions the church as His peacemakers.** Peace with God is never something humanity climbs up to earn; God Himself acted in Christ, refusing to count our sins against us (5:18–19). He placed our sin onto the sinless One so we might receive His righteousness (5:21).

## Deeper Meaning

**Life swallowing up death links directly to the resurrection triumph of 1 Corinthians.** Longing for what is mortal to be swallowed up by life (5:4) echoes the victory cry at the close of Paul's earlier letter: when the mortal puts on immortality, "Death has been swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). For Paul, salvation is never a soul escaping a body, but the whole person clothed in new life.

**Longing to depart and be with Christ is a steady theme across Paul's ministry.** Saying he would "prefer to be away from the body and at home with the Lord" (5:8) mirrors his words from prison to the Philippians: desiring to depart and be with Christ, which is far better (Philippians 1:23). For a believer, death is no dead end, but stepping closer into the Lord's presence.

**Every Christian will one day appear before the Lord's tribunal.** Standing before "the judgment seat of Christ" (5:10) parallels Paul's reminder in Romans that we will all stand before God's judgment seat, leaving no place for judging or despising fellow believers (Romans 14:10). This court does not strip away salvation, but evaluates each believer's faithfulness.

**Being in Christ means stepping into the new creation foretold by the prophets.** In 5:17, the original Greek is even crisper: if anyone is in Christ—new creation! The old has passed away. That declaration fulfills God's word through Isaiah urging His people not to dwell on former things because He is doing a brand-new thing (Isaiah 43:18–19).

**The great exchange of 5:21 connects straight to Isaiah's Suffering Servant.** Writing that God made "Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf" (5:21) recalls the innocent servant laying down his life as a guilt offering (Isaiah 53:10), and parallels Galatians where Christ became a curse for us to redeem us (Galatians 3:13).

## Notes & Nuances

**What happens between physical death and the final resurrection of the body?** Verses 5:1–8 are among the most closely studied passages on the intermediate state. Paul expresses reluctance to be "naked"—a disembodied soul (5:3–4)—while simultaneously declaring that leaving the body means being immediately "at home with the Lord" (5:8). Most interpreters understand that upon death believers immediately enter peaceful fellowship with Christ while continuing to await the full clothing of resurrection bodies at His return.

**"The judgment seat of Christ" in 5:10 does not contradict salvation by grace.** Hearing that each person receives what is due for good or bad done in the body sometimes makes readers fear losing salvation. Yet Paul is addressing people already in Christ: just as in 1 Corinthians 3:12–15, that day reveals and rewards believers' faithfulness and work rather than revoking forgiving grace.

**Verse 5:16 does not mean Paul ignored Jesus' earthly life.** When he says that though he once "regarded Christ from a worldly point of view," he does so no longer, some have mistakenly thought Paul cared little for Jesus' historical deeds. Grammatically, "from a worldly point of view" modifies *how* Paul looked, not Christ Himself: before conversion Paul judged Jesus by worldly optics as a crucified failure; now he sees Him with eyes opened by resurrection and new creation.

**Saying Christ was "made to be sin" in 5:21 never implies Jesus became a sinner.** In the very same sentence Paul affirms that He "knew no sin." Interpreters offer two closely aligned explanations: either Jesus bore the full legal liability and penalty of our sins before God, or the word "sin" here carries the Old Testament sense of "sin offering." Both guard Jesus' utter sinlessness completely.

## What Commentators Say

**Augustine**, fourth- and fifth-century bishop of Hippo in North Africa, explains "made to be sin" in 5:21 from Old Testament usage, where a single word meant both *sin* and *sin offering*. God appointed the sinless One to become the sacrifice for sin on our behalf so that we might receive His righteousness. *(Enchiridion, chapter 41)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Augustine's explanation of 2 Corinthians 5:21 as "sin offering" in Enchiridion chapter 41 is famous -->

**Martin Luther**, sixteenth-century German Reformer, treasured 5:21 as the "wondrous exchange" at the heart of the gospel. Through union with Christ by faith, Christ takes all our sin, death, and condemnation as His own, while we receive all His righteousness, life, and grace as ours. *(The Freedom of a Christian, 1520)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: the wondrous exchange is one of Luther's most famous themes in this treatise and his Galatians commentary, closely linked to 2 Corinthians 5:21 though uncertain whether he explicitly quotes it in 1520 -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Protestant Reformer, stresses that in 5:18–19 God Himself takes the initiative to reconcile the world to Himself. Reconciliation does not twist an unwilling God from hatred into love; rather, God's pre-existing love opens the way of reconciliation through Christ. *(Commentary on Second Corinthians, on chapter 5 verses 18–19)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: God's loving initiative in reconciliation is a major emphasis of Calvin in both his 2 Corinthians commentary and the Institutes; exact wording not checked -->

**N. T. Wright**, British New Testament scholar, reads 5:21 tightly within the surrounding discussion of apostolic ministry in 5:18–20. In his view, "the righteousness of God" here refers first to God's own covenant faithfulness; as apostles preach reconciliation and suffer for the gospel, their lives and ministry embody that faithfulness. While many scholars maintain the classic reading of imputed righteousness given to every believer, Wright's reading keeps verse 21 grounded in the mission of reconciliation. *(Justification, 2009; Paul and the Faithfulness of God, 2013)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: reading 2 Corinthians 5:21 in terms of covenant faithfulness and apostolic ministry is one of Wright's most famous and debated proposals -->

**Murray Harris**, New Zealand New Testament scholar, examines the apparent tension in 5:1–8 between shrinking from "nakedness" and longing to be "away from the body and at home with the Lord." Paul has not abandoned bodily resurrection, Harris argues, but holds two convictions together: death immediately ushers believers into closer presence with Christ, yet our deepest hope remains being clothed with our new heavenly body. *(The Second Epistle to the Corinthians, NIGTC, 2005, on chapter 5 verses 1–8)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: Harris's analysis of the intermediate state in 2 Corinthians 5:1–8 is a noted strength of his commentary; exact line-by-line wording not verified -->
