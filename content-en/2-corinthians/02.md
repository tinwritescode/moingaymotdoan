# 2 Corinthians 2 — Forgiveness and the Aroma of Christ

## Story

Paul continues explaining why he delayed returning: he made up his mind not to pay them another painful visit. For if he grieves them, who is left to make him glad except the very people he has grieved? He wrote his earlier letter so that when he came he would not be distressed by those who ought to make him rejoice, confident in all of them that his joy is the joy of them all. He wrote that letter "out of great distress and anguish of heart and with many tears"—not to make them sorrowful, but so they would recognize the overflowing love he has for them (2:1–4). These lines uncover two dramatic episodes between 1 and 2 Corinthians: a brief, painful visit during which Paul was publicly insulted, followed by a tear-stained "severe letter" carried to Corinth by Titus. That letter has not survived, though some scholars have speculated that part of it lies preserved in the final four chapters of 2 Corinthians.

Turning to the individual who caused that pain, Paul says the offender has not grieved him alone, but to some degree—not to exaggerate—the whole church. The punishment inflicted by the majority is enough for him. Now they ought to forgive and comfort him, so that he will not be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow. Paul therefore urges them to reaffirm their love for him (2:5–8). Older interpreters often identified this offender with the man living with his stepmother in 1 Corinthians 5:1–5, yet most modern scholars believe this was a different person who directly insulted Paul during his painful visit while the congregation initially failed to defend him (7:12).

Paul adds that he wrote that stern letter also as a test, to see whether they would be obedient in everything. Anyone they forgive, he forgives as well. And what he has forgiven—if there was anything to forgive—he has forgiven for their sake in the presence of Christ, "in order that Satan should not outwit us. For we are not unaware of his schemes" (2:9–11). Church discipline aims to win a fallen member back, never to crush them into despair; if a church clings to bitterness and refuses to forgive, Satan walks away the winner.

Paul picks up his travel story: when he arrived in Troas to preach the gospel of Christ and found a wide-open door in the Lord, he still had no peace of mind because he did not find his brother Titus there. So he said goodbye to the believers in Troas and crossed over to Macedonia (2:12–13). Troas was the seaport in northwest Asia Minor where ships sailed across to Greece (Acts 16:8). Paul was aching for Titus's report on how the Corinthians had received the tearful letter; his travel narrative abruptly pauses right here and resumes only at 7:5–8, making room for a sustained defense of his apostolic ministry.

Right in the middle of that anxiety, Paul bursts into thanksgiving to God, who always leads him and his coworkers in triumphal procession in Christ and through them spreads everywhere the fragrance of knowing Him. Before God, they are "the aroma of Christ" among those who are being saved and those who are perishing: to the perishing, an odor from death to death; to those being saved, a fragrance from life to life. "And who is qualified for such a task?" Unlike so many others, Paul and his coworkers do not peddle the word of God for profit; instead, in Christ they speak before God with sincerity, as people commissioned by God (2:14–17). Verse 14 evokes a Roman general's triumphal parade, with incense rising along the route: to the victors that scent meant glory and life, while to bound captives marched toward execution it signaled looming death. The sobering question "Who is qualified for such a task?" launches Paul's exposition of new covenant ministry across the next three chapters.

## Key Takeaways

**Church discipline fulfills its purpose only when it leads to forgiveness and restoration.** The man who hurt the congregation has already received sufficient rebuke; keeping him frozen out in cold condemnation would sink him into despair (2:6–8). Real love confronts sin firmly, yet also knows exactly when to stop and welcome a repentant offender home.

**Refusing to forgive hands Satan an open victory.** Paul warns the whole church that Satan excels at exploiting lingering resentment and hard hearts to tear fellowship apart (2:10–11). Forgiveness is never weakness; it disarms the enemy's sharpest weapon.

**An open door for ministry never outweighs love for people.** Even with a thriving gospel opportunity right in front of him in Troas, Paul could not rest until he heard news from Titus about Corinth (2:12–13). For Paul, real people always mattered more than impressive ministry metrics.

**Proclaiming Jesus never leaves listeners neutral.** The gospel spreads like a potent fragrance: to open hearts who believe, it smells of life; to those who reject it, the very same aroma becomes the scent of death (2:15–16). Because that calling carries life-and-death weight, messengers dare never hawk God's word like street peddlers chasing profit (2:17).

## Deeper Meaning

**Church discipline always seeks to win a brother back.** Paul's call to forgive and comfort in 2:7 mirrors Jesus' instruction for confronting sin: the entire point is to "win your brother over" (Matthew 18:15). Paul similarly instructed the Galatians to restore anyone caught in a trespass gently while watching themselves lest they too be tempted (Galatians 6:1).

**Lingering anger and unforgiveness both give the devil a foothold.** Here Paul urges forgiveness "in order that Satan should not outwit us" (2:11), just as in Ephesians he warns believers not to let the sun set on their anger and "do not give the devil a foothold" (Ephesians 4:26–27). In the New Testament, spiritual warfare unfolds right inside how Christians treat one another day by day.

**The aroma imagery links gospel ministry to Old Testament sacrifices and Christ's self-offering.** In Leviticus, burnt offerings ascended from the altar as a pleasing aroma to the LORD (Leviticus 1:9). Ephesians describes Christ's own sacrifice as a fragrant offering to God (Ephesians 5:2). Paul borrows that language to show that a faithful Christian's life and ministry likewise rise as sweet incense before God (2:15).

**Jesus Himself inevitably divides listeners into two opposite responses.** Holding the infant Jesus in the temple, aged Simeon prophesied that this child was destined to cause the falling and rising of many (Luke 2:34). So it is no surprise that "the aroma of Christ" brings life to some and death to others (2:15–16): the exact same sunlight guides open eyes while blinding sore ones.

## Notes & Nuances

**Is the offender in 2:5–11 the same incestuous man from 1 Corinthians 5?** Older tradition commonly merged the two cases. Most modern scholars see clear differences: 1 Corinthians 5 dealt with public immorality involving a stepmother, whereas here Paul speaks of someone who personally wronged him and grieved the church (7:12), triggering the tearful letter. Whichever individual is in view, the principle balancing firm discipline with ready forgiveness holds equally true.

**Verse 2:14 can be pictured in two slightly different ways.** Traditional translations read "always leads us in triumph," implying God grants Paul victory. In ancient Greek, however, the verb specifically referred to a Roman general parading defeated captives in his triumphal procession: on that reading, Paul pictures himself as conquered by Christ and led on display everywhere to showcase the Conqueror's glory. Both readings agree that victory belongs wholly to God, not to Paul's own power.

**Why does the story about looking for Titus break off abruptly after 2:13?** Mid-sentence about leaving Troas for Macedonia in anguish (2:12–13), Paul launches into a long thanksgiving and defense of apostolic ministry running all the way to 7:4, before picking up his reunion with Titus in Macedonia at 7:5. Some scholars propose that 2:14–7:4 was originally a separate Pauline apology edited into the letter; others view it as a natural emotional overflow—remembering the relief of finally finding Titus leads Paul to clear up every misunderstanding about his ministry before finishing the story.

**Who are the "many" peddling God's word in 2:17?** The Greek verb evokes crooked tavern keepers watering down wine for profit. Paul is glancing toward traveling preachers arriving in Corinth for money and prestige, diluting the stark message of the cross to please crowds—rivals he will confront head-on in chapters 10 through 13.

## What Commentators Say

**John Chrysostom**, fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, illustrates the paradox of 2:15–16 with an everyday comparison: sunlight is wholly good, yet to inflamed eyes it brings sharp pain. Likewise, the gospel is purely the fragrance of life; if it smells of death to some, the fault lies not in the fragrance, but in the corruption of those who refuse it. *(Homilies on 2 Corinthians, Homily 5)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Chrysostom's analogy of sunlight and diseased eyes on 2 Corinthians 2:15–16 is famous in this homily series -->

**John Calvin**, sixteenth-century Protestant Reformer, warns on 2:7–11 that Satan deploys two opposite traps to wreck a church: first lulling congregations into laxity so they tolerate sin, and then pushing them into merciless rigidity so they drown a repentant sinner in despair. Pastoral wisdom knows exactly when discipline has done its work. *(Commentary on Second Corinthians, on chapter 2 verses 7–11)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: identifying Satan's twin extremes in church discipline is a hallmark of Calvin's exposition of 2 Corinthians 2 -->

**Victor Furnish**, twentieth-century American New Testament scholar, closely examines the Greek verb in 2:14 and concludes the true picture is not Paul waving a victory banner, but God leading Paul as a conquered captive in Christ's triumphal procession. That image dovetails with all of 2 Corinthians, where God's strength shines through apostolic weakness and suffering. *(II Corinthians, Anchor Bible, 1984, on chapter 2 verse 14)* <!-- confidence: high | verified: no | reason: Furnish's analysis of thriambeuō as leading captives in triumph is a landmark contribution of his Anchor Bible commentary -->

**Scott Hafemann**, American New Testament scholar, builds on the image of condemned captives marching to death in 2:14–16 to explain the weighty question "Who is qualified for such a task?" in 2:16. Apostolic ministry is not effortless prestige, but a death march for the gospel—a calling nobody could possibly bear unless God supplies sufficiency, as chapter 3 will answer. *(2 Corinthians, NIV Application Commentary, 2000, on chapter 2 verses 14–16)* <!-- confidence: medium | verified: no | reason: this is Hafemann's core thesis in both Suffering and the Spirit and NIVAC; unsure of exact wording in NIVAC -->
